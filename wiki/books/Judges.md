# Judges

## Overview

Judges begins where Joshua ends, with a death: *After the death of Joshua, the children of Israel
asked of Yahweh, saying, "Who should go up for us first against the Canaanites, to fight against
them?"* (1:1). Joshua closed on a land divided and a people sworn to serve Yahweh; Judges opens on
the same land still to be taken, tribe by tribe, and on the question of who will lead now that no
single leader stands in Joshua's place. Its answer, for most of the book, is a series of
deliverers raised up one crisis at a time, and its closing verdict is the refrain *In those days
there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes* (21:25).

[[Judah Goes Up First]] (1:1–7) puts the question to Yahweh and receives a tribe, not a man:
*Judah shall go up*. Judah takes Simeon his brother with him, as the two lots of Joshua 19 were
already joined, and they defeat the Canaanites and Perizzites at Bezek. Their captive, Adoni-Bezek,
gives the book its first theological sentence from the mouth of a Canaanite king: *As I have done,
so God has done to me*. The same question, *who shall go up for us first*, and the same answer,
*Judah*, return at 20:18 — but there the enemy is Benjamin, a brother tribe. The book's first and
last wars are framed by one inquiry.

[[Judah's Conquests and Achsah's Springs]] (1:8–21) carries Judah through Jerusalem, which it takes
and burns, to Hebron, Debir, Zephath renamed Hormah, and three Philistine cities. It repeats from Joshua
15 Caleb's gift of Achsah to Othniel, the man who will be the first judge, and her request for springs.
Then the run stops at the valley, *because they had chariots of iron* (1:19), though *Yahweh was with
Judah*. And the pericope ends with the first *did not drive out*: the Jebusites stay in Jerusalem with
Benjamin *to this day* (1:21). The burned city of verse 8 is still unpossessed in verse 21. The rest of
chapter 1 becomes a list of such sentences.

[[The Tribes That Did Not Drive Out]] (1:22–36) is that list. The house of Joseph takes Bethel, once
Luz, through a man who shows its spies the way in and is spared, as Rahab was; he leaves and builds a
new Luz in the land of the Hittites. Then Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, and Naphtali each *did not
drive out* the Canaanites of their cities, who were *determined* to stay. Where Israel grew strong they
became forced laborers. In Asher and Naphtali the relation is reversed, and Israel *lived among the
Canaanites*. Last, the Amorites press Dan out of the valley. Where Joshua 17:12 said Manasseh *couldn't*,
Judges 1:27 says *did not*. The chapter ends without a verdict; Judges 2:1–5 supplies one.

[[The Angel at Bochim]] (2:1–5) is that verdict. Yahweh's angel comes up from Gilgal and speaks in
Yahweh's first person: *I brought you out of Egypt … I will never break my covenant with you*. Israel
was to make no covenant with the land's inhabitants and to break their altars, *but you have not
listened to my voice*. So Yahweh *will not drive them out*, and their gods will be *a snare*. The
verb is *gāraš*, the verb of his promise in Exodus 23, not the *yāraš* of the tribes in chapter 1.
The people weep, name the place *Bochim*, *weepers*, and sacrifice. The same idiom of weeping returns
at Bethel in the book's last chapter (21:2).

[[Another Generation Arose]] (2:6–23) starts the book a second time. Joshua sends the people home,
dies, and is buried, in words taken from Joshua 24:28–31, and then *another generation arose after
them, who didn't know Yahweh*. The verb Joshua 24:31 used of the elders, *known*, returns negated for
their children. What follows is the cycle in general terms before any judge is named: Israel serves
the Baals and the Ashtaroth; Yahweh *sold them* to their enemies; Yahweh, *grieved because of their
groaning*, raises up judges who save them; and when each judge dies they turn back, *more corruptly
than their fathers*. Their sin is described in the words of the golden calf, *they quickly turned
away from the way*. Yahweh's last word on it is the same sentence as Bochim's, *I also will no longer
drive out*, with a new purpose added: the nations stay *that by them I may test Israel*.

[[The Nations Left to Test Israel]] (3:1–6) closes the introduction. It names the nations left: *the
five lords of the Philistines, all the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites who lived on Mount
Lebanon*, the same open edges as Joshua 13:2–6. It gives the test again, *to know whether they would
listen to Yahweh's commandments*, and a new purpose, *to teach them war* to a generation that *had not
known all the wars of Canaan*. Then it reports the answer before any judge appears. Israel *lived
among* six peoples, the list of Exodus 3:8 in the same order. *They took their daughters to be their
wives, and gave their own daughters to their sons and served their gods* (3:6). The verbs are those of
Deuteronomy 7:3–4, which forbade exactly this.

[[Othniel the First Judge]] (3:7–11) is the cycle of 2:11–19 carried out once, with names. Israel
*forgot Yahweh* and served *the Baals and the Asheroth*; Yahweh *sold them* to Cushan Rishathaim, *king
of Mesopotamia*, for eight years; they *cried*, and Yahweh *raised up a savior*, Othniel, Caleb's
kinsman, already known from Kiriath Sepher. *Yahweh's Spirit came on him*, in the clause used of
Balaam (Numbers 24:2); *he judged Israel*, went to war, and *his hand prevailed*. *The land had rest
forty years*, in Joshua's word for rest from war, and then he died. Nobody speaks, and nothing goes
wrong. The judges after him each add something to the pattern.

[[Ehud and Eglon]] (3:12–30) is the first judge story told at length. Israel *again* does evil, and
this time Yahweh does not sell them but *strengthened Eglon the king of Moab*, with the verb of
Pharaoh's hardened heart. Moab, with Ammon and Amalek, takes *the city of palm trees* and rules
eighteen years. The savior is *Ehud the son of Gera, the Benjamite, a left-handed man*, a son of the
right hand who is restricted in his right hand. He carries Israel's tribute to a *very fat* king,
gains a private audience with *a secret message* and then *a message from God*, and kills him with a
hidden sword. The servants, finding the doors locked, assume the king is *covering his feet* and wait
*until they were ashamed*. Ehud blows the horn, Israel holds the fords of the Jordan, *no man escaped*,
and the land rests eighty years. No Spirit comes on him, he is never said to judge, and the narrator
neither praises nor blames the deception.

[[Shamgar and the Ox Goad]] (3:31) is a single verse: *After him was Shamgar the son of Anath, who
struck six hundred men of the Philistines with an ox goad. He also saved Israel.* Almost none of the
cycle is there. No sin, oppressor, cry, Spirit, judging, or rest is mentioned, and Yahweh is not
named. Only the verb *saved* remains, and *also* counts him with Othniel and Ehud. He is the first in
the book to fight the Philistines, the enemy Samson will only *begin* to save Israel from (13:5). The
next verse dates the relapse from *when Ehud was dead* (4:1). The Song of Deborah remembers *the days
of Shamgar* as a time of empty roads (5:6).

[[Deborah and Barak]] (4:1–16) dates the next relapse from Ehud's death. Yahweh *sold* Israel to
*Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor*, the name and city of the king Joshua killed, and his
captain Sisera's *nine hundred chariots of iron* oppress them twenty years. The deliverer is not raised
up after the cry; she is already there. *Deborah, a prophetess, … judged Israel at that time*, under
her palm between Ramah and Bethel, and Israel came to her for judgment. She sends Barak of Kedesh
Naphtali with Yahweh's command. He will go only if she goes, and she tells him *Yahweh will sell Sisera
into a woman's hand*. Heber the Kenite's tent is pitched near Kedesh. On her word Barak comes down
Mount Tabor, *Yahweh confused Sisera* and his chariots, the verb of the sea and of Gibeon, and not a man
of the army is left. Sisera escapes on foot.

[[Jael and Sisera]] (4:17–24) follows him to the tent of Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite, whose house is
at peace with Jabin. She welcomes him, *don't be afraid*, covers him, gives him milk, and while he sleeps
drives a tent peg through his temple. Barak arrives second and is shown the body: the *woman's hand* of
4:9 was Jael's, not Deborah's. The scene is told in the shape of Eglon's death, a lone killing found
afterwards, *behold*, dead. The narrator gives the victory to God, *God subdued Jabin the king of
Canaan*, and Israel's hand presses until Jabin is destroyed. The rest-formula waits until after the
Song.

[[The Song of Deborah]] (5:1–31) tells the same war again as a poem, *Then Deborah and Barak the son of
Abinoam sang on that day*, as Exodus sings the sea after narrating it. It begins with Yahweh marching
out of Seir while the earth trembles and Sinai quakes, and remembers *the days of Shamgar* as a time of
empty roads, until Deborah *arose a mother in Israel*. It names the tribes one by one: Ephraim, Benjamin,
Machir, Zebulun, Issachar, and Naphtali came; Reuben, Gilead, Dan, and Asher stayed, each asked *why*.
*The kings of Canaan fought at Taanach*, *the stars fought* from heaven, and *the river Kishon swept
them away*. Yahweh's angel curses Meroz for not coming; Jael is *blessed above women*, and Sisera falls
*at her feet*. The last scene is his mother at her lattice, imagining plunder. The Song never names
Jabin, and it lists six tribes where the prose had two; the wiki records those differences rather than
harmonizing them. *Then the land had rest forty years.*

[[Midian's Oppression and the Prophet]] (6:1–10) opens the cycle again, without the *again* of 3:12 and
4:1: Israel does evil, and Yahweh gives them *into the hand of Midian seven years*. This oppressor is a
migration, not a kingdom. Every year *when Israel had sown*, the Midianites, the Amalekites, and *the
children of the east* come with their herds, tents, and camels *as locusts for multitude*, and strip the
land *until you come to Gaza*, while Israel hides in dens and caves. It is the first oppression told as
hunger, in the images of the eighth plague and of Deuteronomy 28. Israel cries, and the narrator says so
twice; but the first answer is not a savior. *Yahweh sent a prophet*, unnamed, who recites the exodus
in the words of the Ten Words, quotes *You shall not fear the gods of the Amorites, in whose land you
dwell* from Joshua 24:15, and ends on Bochim's charge, *you have not listened to my voice*, with no
sentence after it. The cry that once brought rescue at once now brings a rebuke first.

[[The Call of Gideon]] (6:11–24) brings the second answer, a deliverer. Yahweh's angel sits under the
oak at Ophrah while Gideon threshes wheat in a winepress *to hide it from the Midianites*, and greets
him, *Yahweh is with you, you mighty man of valor!* Gideon answers with the prophet's history turned
into a complaint: *Where are all his wondrous works which our fathers told us of … But now Yahweh has
cast us off*. He is the first judge whose call is narrated, and it follows Moses' call at the bush: a
sending, *Haven't I sent you?*, an objection of smallness, *my family is the poorest in Manasseh*, the
promise *Surely I will be with you*, and a sign. Fire from the rock consumes his offering. He fears
death for having seen the angel *face to face*, is told *Peace be to you … You shall not die*, and
builds an altar he names *Yahweh is Peace*.

[[Gideon Throws Down Baal's Altar]] (6:25–32) turns the deliverer's first task homeward. *That same
night* Yahweh tells him to throw down *the altar of Baal that your father has* and cut down the Asherah
by it, and to offer his father's second bull on a new altar to Yahweh, burned with the Asherah's wood.
It is the first time in Judges that anyone obeys Bochim's *You shall break down their altars* (2:2),
and the altar is an Israelite's. Gideon does it with ten servants, by night, *because he feared his
father's household and the men of the city*. In the morning the town demands *Bring out your son, that
he may die*, the law's procedure against idolaters (Deuteronomy 17:5) turned against the man who
destroyed an idol's altar. Joash refuses: *If he is a god, let him contend for himself*, and names his
son *Jerub-Baal*, *Let Baal contend against him*.

[[The Fleece]] (6:33–40) brings the enemy into one place, Midian, Amalek, and the children of the east encamped
in the valley of Jezreel. *Yahweh's Spirit came on Gideon*; the Hebrew says it *clothed* him. He blows
the ram's horn as Ehud did, and Abiezer, Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali gather. Asher comes this
time, though it stayed home from Deborah's war. Then Gideon asks God to confirm the promise *save
Israel by my hand, as you have spoken*: dew on a fleece and dry ground, and then the reverse. He
introduces the second request with Abraham's words at Sodom, *Don't let your anger be kindled … but this
once*, and calls it *a trial*, the verb Judges had used for God testing Israel. God does both and says
nothing.

[[Gideon's Three Hundred]] (7:1–8) turns the request around. *Jerubbaal, who is Gideon*, camps at the
spring of Harod with Midian north of him by the hill of Moreh. Yahweh says the army is *too many … lest
Israel brag against me, saying, "My own hand has saved me"*. Gideon had asked God to save Israel *by my
hand*. The fearful are sent home by the law of Deuteronomy 20:8, twenty-two thousand of them. Then
Yahweh tests the ten thousand left at the water, with the smelter's verb, and keeps the three hundred
who lapped. *By the three hundred men who lapped will I save you*. Gideon says nothing in the whole
scene, and the text gives no reason why those three hundred were chosen.

[[The Dream of the Barley Loaf]] (7:9–15) happens *That same night*. Yahweh says *I have delivered it into
your hand*, and then offers a sign before Gideon asks: *if you are afraid to go down, go with Purah your
servant*. He goes with Purah. At the edge of a camp lying *like locusts for multitude* and with camels *as
the sand which is on the seashore* (Joshua 11:4's words), he hears a Midianite tell a dream of a barley
loaf that knocks a tent flat. His fellow interprets it: *the sword of Gideon the son of Joash … God has
delivered Midian into his hand*. Gideon worships, the only time Judges uses that verb of worshiping
Yahweh. Then he gives his men Yahweh's own sentence: *Arise; for Yahweh has delivered the army of Midian
into your hand!* The fear of 7:3 is now in the enemy's camp. As at Jericho (Joshua 2:9, 24), the spies
hear it from the enemy's own mouth.

[[The Sword of Yahweh and of Gideon]] (7:16–25) is the battle, and no Israelite strikes a blow in the
camp. The three hundred carry horns, and torches hidden in empty jars. At *the beginning of the middle
watch* they blow, break the jars, and shout *The sword of Yahweh and of Gideon!*, Gideon's own *For
Yahweh and for Gideon!* with the Midianite's word *sword* added. *They each stood in his place*, and
*Yahweh set every man's sword against his fellow*. It is Jericho's horns and shout without priest or
ark, and with a man's name in the cry. The tribes pursue, and Ephraim, summoned late, holds the waters
of the Jordan as Ehud's men held the fords (3:28) and kills the princes Oreb and Zeeb. Their heads are
brought to Gideon, and Ephraim's complaint at being called late follows at once (8:1). Psalm 83 and
Isaiah 9–10 later remember it as *the day of Midian*.

[[Ephraim, Succoth, and Penuel]] (8:1–21) is the aftermath, and Yahweh does not speak in it. Ephraim
*contends* with Gideon, the *rîb* of his name, for not being called to the fight; he answers with a
proverb of the grape harvest, *Isn't the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of
Abiezer?*, and *their anger was abated*. Then he crosses the Jordan with the three hundred, *faint, yet
pursuing*, after *Zebah and Zalmunna, the kings of Midian*. Succoth and Penuel, the towns of Jacob's
return, refuse his men bread until the kings are taken. He takes them at Karkor, has a young man of
Succoth write down its seventy-seven elders, *taught* them with thorns and briers, breaks down Penuel's
tower, and kills its men. Last, the reader learns that the kings had killed his brothers at Tabor. *As
Yahweh lives, if you had saved them alive, I would not kill you*. His firstborn Jether is too afraid to
strike, and Gideon kills the kings himself and takes the crescents from their camels. A soft answer to a
strong tribe, punishment for weak towns, and a war that ends as a blood feud.

[[Gideon's Ephod and Death]] (8:22–35) ends his story. Israel offers him a dynasty, *Rule over us, both
you, and your son, and your son's son also*, and he answers *I will not rule over you, neither shall my
son rule over you. Yahweh shall rule over you*. It is the book's plainest statement that Yahweh is
Israel's ruler. Then he asks for the gold earrings of the plunder, seventeen hundred of gold, and makes an
ephod that he puts in Ophrah, where *all Israel played the prostitute with it there; and it became a
snare to Gideon, and to his house*. The land has its last forty years of rest in the book. He has seventy
sons from *many wives*, and a concubine in Shechem bears a son he names *Abimelech*. He dies *in a good
old age*, Abraham's phrase. As soon as he is dead Israel turns back to the Baals and makes *Baal Berith*
its god, and the last verses charge Israel with two failures of memory: it *didn't remember Yahweh*, and
it showed no *kindness to the house of Jerubbaal*.

[[Abimelech Made King at Shechem]] (9:1–6) shows what the missing kindness looks like. Gideon's son by
the Shechem concubine goes to his mother's brothers and has them ask the town, *Is it better for you that
all the sons of Jerubbaal, who are seventy persons, rule over you, or that one rule over you?* He uses
his father's verb, *rule*, and leaves out his father's third ruler, Yahweh. *Remember also that I am your
bone and your flesh*, he says, and Shechem answers *He is our brother*, the words Judah used of Joseph
at Dothan. The town pays him seventy of silver from the house of Baal Berith, and he hires *vain and
light fellows* and kills his seventy brothers *on one stone* at Ophrah. Only Jotham, the youngest,
survives, *for he hid himself*. Then the men of Shechem and the house of Millo make him king *by the oak
of the pillar*. No outside oppressor appears, nobody cries to Yahweh, and God is not named. It is the
first time in Genesis–Judges that anyone is *made king*.

[[Jotham's Fable]] (9:7–21) is the answer. The one son who hid stands on Mount Gerizim, the mountain of
the blessing, and calls to Shechem, *Listen to me, you men of Shechem, that God may listen to you*. It is
the first time God is named in the chapter. He tells the Bible's first fable. The trees go to anoint a
king. The olive, the fig, and the vine refuse to leave their oil, fruit, and wine *to wave back and
forth over the trees*. The bramble accepts: *come and take refuge in my shade; and if not, let fire come
out of the bramble, and devour the cedars of Lebanon*. Jotham asks whether Shechem made Abimelech king
*truly and righteously*, the pair Joshua used at Shechem (Joshua 24:14). He reminds them that *my father
fought for you, and risked his life*, and charges them with the seventy killed *on one stone*. His
curse is fire both ways, from Abimelech on Shechem and from Shechem on Abimelech. Then he flees to Beer
*for fear of Abimelech his brother*. The rest of the chapter carries the curse out.

[[The Fall of Abimelech]] (9:22–57) does. *Abimelech was prince over Israel three years*, with a verb
that is not *king*. Then *God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the men of Shechem*, so that the
blood of the seventy would come back on both. Shechem trusts a newcomer, Gaal the son of Ebed, and at
the vintage festival, in *the house of their god*, curses Abimelech: *Who is Abimelech … that we should
serve him?* Zebul, Abimelech's officer, sets the trap. Gaal is beaten and driven out. Abimelech then
takes the city, kills its people, *beat down the city, and sowed it with salt*, and burns about a
thousand men and women in the stronghold of El Berith. He copies his father's *do as I have done* and
his father's broken tower. At Thebez, as he comes to burn another tower, *a certain woman cast an upper
millstone on Abimelech's head*. He has his armor bearer kill him, *that men not say of me, "A woman
killed him"*, and Israel goes home. The narrator gives the verdict: *Thus God repaid the wickedness of
Abimelech … and the curse of Jotham the son of Jerubbaal came on them*. It is the book's only story
with no outside enemy, no outcry, and no deliverer. God acts only in its first and last verses.

[[Tola and Jair]] (10:1–5) follows with five quiet verses. *After Abimelech*, Tola of Issachar
*arose to save Israel*, from no named enemy, and judged twenty-three years from Shamir in the hill
country of Ephraim. Jair the Gileadite judged twenty-two years. His thirty sons rode thirty donkey
colts and held thirty cities, *called Havvoth Jair to this day*, a name Numbers, Deuteronomy, and
Joshua explain differently. Both men die and are buried in named towns. There is no sin, no enemy,
and no rest. They are the first of the five short notices called the *minor judges*; Ibzan, Elon,
and Abdon follow Jephthah (12:8–15).

[[Israel's Repentance Under Ammon]] (10:6–18) begins the cycle again and tells it at length. Israel
*again did that which was evil* and served the gods of seven peoples. Yahweh sold them to the
Philistines and to Ammon, and Ammon oppressed Gilead eighteen years and crossed the Jordan against
Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim. For the first time the cry has words: *We have sinned against you*.
Yahweh answers in his own voice. He lists seven peoples he saved them from and ends, *Therefore I
will save you no more. Go and cry to the gods which you have chosen*. Israel confesses again, *Do
you to us whatever seems good to you*, puts away the foreign gods, and serves Yahweh, *and his soul
was grieved for the misery of Israel*. Then Ammon camps in Gilead and Israel at Mizpah, and the
princes of Gilead ask one another, *Who is the man who will begin to fight?* They offer him the
headship of Gilead. No deliverer is raised up; the next chapter introduces one.

[[Jephthah Called from Tob]] (11:1–11) gives the answer a name. *Jephthah the Gileadite was a mighty
man of valor, and he was the son of a prostitute*. His half-brothers drove him out, *You will not
inherit in our father's house, for you are the son of another woman*, and in the land of Tob *outlaws*
gathered to him, the *empty men* Abimelech had hired (9:4). When Ammon attacks, the elders of Gilead
come for him and offer him *chief*. He answers, *Didn't you hate me, and drive me out of my father's
house? Why have you come to me now when you are in distress?*, close to Yahweh's *in the time of your
distress* (10:14). They raise the offer to *head over all the inhabitants of Gilead*. He makes Yahweh's
victory the condition, they swear *Yahweh will be witness between us*, and the people make him *head and
chief*. He speaks *all his words before Yahweh in Mizpah*. He is the first deliverer in the book chosen
by a bargain rather than raised up or called by Yahweh.

[[Jephthah's Message to the King of Ammon]] (11:12–28) delays the battle by a speech. Ammon's king
claims *from the Arnon even to the Jabbok, and to the Jordan* and asks for it back *peaceably*.
Jephthah answers with the road out of Egypt: Israel took neither Moab's land nor Ammon's, went around
Edom and Moab, and took Sihon's Amorite land when *Yahweh … delivered Sihon*. *Whoever Yahweh our God
has dispossessed from before us, them will we possess*; let Ammon keep what *Chemosh your god* gives
it. Balak never fought for this land, and Israel has held it *three hundred years*. He ends, *May
Yahweh the Judge be judge today*, the only time Judges gives a speaker the title *the Judge* for
Yahweh. His history follows Numbers' wording and Deuteronomy's theology, adds an embassy to Moab that
neither records, and denies a fight Joshua 24:9 affirms. The king does not listen.

[[Jephthah's Vow]] (11:29–40) gives Yahweh's answer and Jephthah's. *Yahweh's Spirit came on Jephthah*, and before the
battle he vows: *whatever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me … I will offer it up for a burnt
offering*. Yahweh gives Ammon into his hand, twenty cities from Aroer to Minnith, but no years of rest
follow. His daughter, *his only child*, comes out to meet him *with tambourines and with dances*, the
words of the women after Miriam at the sea. *I have opened my mouth to Yahweh, and I can't go back.* She
holds him to it, asks two months to mourn her virginity, and returns, and *he did with her according to
his vow*. The narrator does not name the act, and whether she was burned or dedicated unmarried is
disputed. Her one other memorial is a custom: the daughters of Israel go every year to *recount* her
four days. *Yāḥîd*, *only*, otherwise belongs in the first seven books to Isaac on Moriah; here no voice
stops the father.

[[Shibboleth]] (12:1–7) is the book's first war between tribes. Ephraim makes the complaint it made to
Gideon, *didn't call us to go with you?*, and threatens to burn Jephthah's house. Jephthah, *a man of
strife* with Ammon, says he did call and Ephraim *didn't save* him; the book never narrated that call.
Ephraim mocks the Gileadites as *fugitives of Ephraim*, and Gilead fights, takes the fords of the Jordan,
and kills every man who says *Sibboleth* for *Shibboleth*: *forty-two thousand of Ephraim fell*. The
narrator then gives Ephraim's own men the name *fugitives of Ephraim*. Jephthah's story ends in the form
of the minor judges' notices, with no rest: *judged Israel six years … died, and was buried in the cities
of Gilead*. Gideon's soft answer to the same complaint had turned Ephraim's anger away (8:1–3).

[[Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon]] (12:8–15) closes the middle of the book with three more short notices.
*After him* Ibzan of Bethlehem judged seven years; thirty daughters he *sent outside* and thirty he
*brought in from outside* for his thirty sons. Which Bethlehem, the text does not say: everywhere else in
Judges the town is *Bethlehem Judah*, and Joshua lists another in Zebulun. Elon the Zebulunite, who
bears the name of a Zebulunite clan, judged ten years and was buried in Aijalon *in the land of
Zebulun*, a place spelled with his name's consonants. Abdon the son of Hillel the Pirathonite judged
eight years; his forty sons and thirty grandsons rode seventy donkey colts, and he was buried in Pirathon
*in the land of Ephraim, in the hill country of the Amalekites*. Again no sin, no enemy, no rest, and
every judge buried in a named place. The five minor judges together judge seventy years. The next verse
begins the last cycle: *again … the Philistines forty years* (13:1).

[[The Announcement of Samson's Birth]] (13:1–25) opens that cycle with no outcry, the first time in the book Israel
does not cry to Yahweh before a deliverer is sent. Yahweh's angel appears to the barren, unnamed wife
of Manoah, a Danite of Zorah, and promises a son who will be *a Nazirite to God from the womb* and will
*begin to save Israel out of the hand of the Philistines*. She reports it, adding *to the day of his
death*. Manoah prays for the visitor's return, but the angel comes again to her, and to Manoah repeats
only her rules. He refuses Manoah's meal and his name, *incomprehensible*, and ascends in the flame of
Manoah's offering on the rock, as the angel at Ophrah vanished in Gideon's fire. *We shall surely die,
because we have seen God*, says Manoah; his wife answers that Yahweh would not have accepted their
offering if he meant to kill them. She bears and names Samson, *Yahweh blessed him*, and *Yahweh's
Spirit began to move him … between Zorah and Eshtaol*. He is the only judge whose birth is told, and
the only one promised a beginning rather than rest.

[[Samson's Riddle]] (14:1–20) begins his work with a look: *Samson went down to Timnah, and saw a woman
… of the daughters of the Philistines*. His parents plead for *a woman among your brothers' daughters*
rather than *the uncircumcised Philistines*; he answers *she pleases me well*, in Hebrew *she is right in
my eyes*, the words the book will close on (21:25). The narrator adds that *it was of Yahweh; for he
sought an occasion against the Philistines. Now at that time the Philistines ruled over Israel*. On the
way the Spirit *came mightily on him* and he tears a young lion bare-handed; later he eats honey from its
carcass and tells his parents neither thing. At the seven-day wedding feast he sets thirty Philistine
companions a riddle, *Out of the eater came out food. Out of the strong came out sweetness*. They threaten
to burn his bride and her father's house; she weeps the seven days until he tells her, and she tells
them. The Spirit comes again, he strikes thirty men of Ashkelon to pay the wager, *His anger burned*, and
he goes home. His wife is given to his companion. The pressing and the *Entice* return with Delilah.

[[Samson Against the Philistines at Lehi]] (15:1–20) is the exchange of blows that follows. Refused his wife, Samson
declares himself *blameless* and burns the Philistines' grain with three hundred foxes; they burn his wife
and her father; he avenges them with *a great slaughter* and hides in the cleft of Etam's rock. The
Philistines invade Judah, and three thousand men of Judah, saying *the Philistines are rulers over us*,
bind him with new ropes and hand him over. Both sides speak the formula of Adoni-Bezek: *to do to him as he
has done to us*; *As they did to me, so I have done to them*. At Lehi the Spirit comes on him a third
time, the ropes melt, and he kills a thousand with a donkey's jawbone and sings of it. Then, very
thirsty, he prays for the first time, calling it *this great deliverance* that Yahweh gave; God splits
the hollow place, and the spring is named En Hakkore. *He judged Israel twenty years in the days of the
Philistines*, a closing formula that 16:31 will repeat.

[[Samson at Gaza]] (16:1–3) goes on as if the formula had not been said. Samson goes to Gaza, *saw there a
prostitute, and went in to her*. The Gazites lie in wait all night in the city gate, saying *Wait until
morning light, then we will kill him*; he rises at midnight, pulls up the doors, posts, and bar of the gate,
and carries them on his shoulders to the hill *before Hebron*. No Spirit is named, and no word is spoken to
him or by him. The scene of Rahab's house at Jericho (Joshua 2:1–7) is played again by one man for
himself, and the city he takes the gate from is the city he will be brought back to blind (16:21).

[[Samson and Delilah]] (16:4–22) is the fall the story has leaned toward. *He loved a woman in the valley
of Sorek, whose name was Delilah*, the first time the book says he loved anyone, and the lords of the
Philistines pay her *eleven hundred pieces of silver* each to *Entice him*. Three times he lies and three
times breaks the bindings; pressed daily *to death*, he tells her *all his heart*, the razor and the
Nazirite vow *from my mother's womb*. Shaved asleep on her knees, he wakes to go out *as at other times*,
*But he didn't know that Yahweh had departed from him*. The Philistines put out his eyes, and he grinds at
the prison mill in Gaza. The chapter's last word before Dagon's feast is a beginning: *the hair of his
head began to grow again*. The sum paid for him reappears in the next chapter, stolen and made into an
idol (17:2–3).

## Structure

The book is usually read in three parts, and the pericope plan below follows them:

1. **A double introduction (1:1–3:6).** Chapter 1 surveys the tribes' campaigns and, more and more,
   the cities they *did not drive out*. Chapter 2 retells Joshua's death and lays out the cycle the
   rest of the book will follow: Israel serves the Baals, Yahweh gives them into the hands of
   raiders, he raises up judges, and after each judge dies they turn back (2:11–19). Then come
   the nations left *to test Israel* (3:1–6).
2. **The judges (3:7–16:31).** Othniel, Ehud, Shamgar, Deborah and Barak, Gideon, the
   anti-judge Abimelech, Tola and Jair, Jephthah, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon, and Samson. The cycle
   frays as it goes: the deliverers become more compromised and the tribes less united.
3. **A double epilogue (17:1–21:25).** No judge appears. Micah's shrine and the migration of Dan;
   the outrage at Gibeah and the war against Benjamin. The refrain about having no king frames
   these chapters.

## Key themes
- [[War]] — the tribes' campaigns, and at the end a war of Israel against one of its own tribes.
- [[Retribution]] — *As I have done, so God has done to me* (1:7), the first of the book's
  measure-for-measure reversals; *Thus God repaid the wickedness of Abimelech* (9:56).
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the land allotted in Joshua, still to be taken in Judges.
- [[Brotherhood]] — Judah and Simeon fight together (1:3); *He is our brother*, and seventy brothers killed on one stone (9:3–5); Judah leads against Benjamin (20:18).
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — forced labor in place of driving out (1:28–35).
- [[The Nations]] — the Canaanites and Amorites who stay (1:27–36), and the nations left to test Israel (3:1–6).
- [[Testing]] — the nations left *to test Israel by them* (2:22; 3:1, 4); Gideon's *let me make a trial* with the fleece (6:39); Yahweh's *I will test them for you* at the water (7:4).
- [[Marriage]] — daughters taken and given with the land's peoples (3:6); Ibzan's thirty daughters sent out and thirty brought in (12:9).
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — Deborah, *a prophetess* who judges Israel (4:4); the dream of the barley loaf, told and interpreted in Midian's camp (7:13–14).
- [[Call and Election]] — Gideon, the first judge whose call is narrated (6:11–24).
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — *lest Israel brag against me, saying, "My own hand has saved me"* (7:2); *For Yahweh and for Gideon!* (7:18).
- [[Idolatry]] — Baal served from the start of the cycle (2:11–13); Joash's Baal altar defended by an Israelite town (6:25–32); Gideon's ephod, *a snare* (8:27); Baal Berith (8:33).
- [[The Spirit of God]] — on Othniel (3:10), and *clothing* Gideon (6:34); *Yahweh had departed* from Samson (16:20).
- [[Vengeance]] — Gideon kills the kings of Midian for *my brothers, the sons of my mother* (8:19).
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — Ephraim *contends* with Gideon and is calmed (8:1–3); Jephthah, *a man of strife*, fights it (12:1–6).
- [[Language and Speech]] — *I have opened my mouth to Yahweh* (11:35); *Shibboleth* at the fords (12:5–6).
- [[Signs and Wonders]] — Gideon's signs: fire from the rock (6:21), the fleece twice (6:36–40), and the dream he is sent to overhear (7:9–15).
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — *Yahweh shall rule over you* (8:23); Abimelech *made king* at Shechem (9:6); the trees seek a king (9:8–15); Jair's thirty sons on thirty colts (10:4), Abdon's seventy riders (12:14); the four refrains *there was no king in Israel* (17:6; 18:1; 19:1; 21:25).

- [[Parables]] — Jotham's fable of the trees, the Bible's first (9:8–15).
- [[Curse]] — *Curse Meroz* (5:23); *the curse of Jotham* (9:20, 57).
- [[The Spirit of God]] — and *an evil spirit* sent by God between Abimelech and Shechem (9:23).
- [[Blood]] — the blood of the seventy *laid on Abimelech … and on the men of Shechem* (9:24).
- [[Burial and the Grave]] — Gideon *in the tomb of Joash his father* (8:32); every minor judge buried in a named town (10:2, 5; 12:10, 12, 15).
- [[Redemption]] — *who saved them* (2:16); Tola *arose to save Israel* (10:1); *I will save you no more* (10:13).
- [[Sin]] — *We have sinned against you*, the first quoted confession (10:10, 15).
- [[Judgment]] — *May Yahweh the Judge be judge today* (11:27).
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — Jephthah's vow, *I can't go back* (11:30–39).
- [[Barrenness]] — Manoah's wife, *barren, and childless* (13:2–3).
- [[Prayer]] — Samson calls on Yahweh in thirst, and the spring is opened (15:18–19).
- [[Holiness]] — Samson *a Nazirite to God from the womb* (13:5, 7); the razor at last (16:17–19).
- [[Love]] — *he loved a woman in the valley of Sorek* (16:4).
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — eleven hundred pieces of silver, for Samson (16:5) and for Micah's idol (17:2–3).
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — *I will offer it up for a burnt offering* (11:31).
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — the daughter's two months, and the yearly four days (11:37–40).
- [[Remembrance]] — the road from Egypt told as evidence (11:15–26).
- [[Divine Grief and Relenting]] — *it grieved Yahweh* (2:18); *his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel* (10:16).

## Key people
- [[Judah]] — the tribe named first, at the book's beginning and near its end; binds Samson for the Philistines (15:9–13).
- [[Simeon]] — goes up with Judah into his lot.
- [[Adoni-Bezek]] — the first enemy king, and the first to confess God's repayment.
- [[Joshua (son of Nun)]] — dead before the book begins.
- [[Joseph]] — *the house of Joseph* takes Bethel (1:22–25).
- [[The Canaanites]] — not driven out (1:27–33).
- [[The Amorites]] — press Dan from the valley (1:34–36).
- [[The Philistines]] — their five lords head the nations left (3:3); Samson's enemies.
- [[The Hivites]] — on Mount Lebanon (3:3), and among the six Israel lives among (3:5).
- [[Deborah (prophetess)]] — prophetess and judge (4:4–14).
- [[Barak]] — commander against Sisera (4:6–16).
- [[Sisera]] and [[Jabin (of Deborah's time)]] — the oppressors with nine hundred chariots (4:2–3).
- [[Gideon]] — called at Ophrah to save Israel from Midian (6:11–24); named *Jerub-Baal* (6:32).
- [[Joash (father of Gideon)]] — keeper of Ophrah's Baal altar, who refuses to give up his son (6:25–32).
- [[Purah]] — Gideon's servant, who goes down with him to the edge of Midian's camp (7:10–11).
- [[Oreb]] and [[Zeeb]] — the two princes of Midian, killed by Ephraim (7:25).
- [[Ephraim]] — summoned late against Midian (7:24); quarrels with Gideon (8:1) and Jephthah (12:1).
- [[Zebah]] and [[Zalmunna]] — the kings of Midian, pursued beyond the Jordan and killed by Gideon (8:5–21).
- [[Jether (son of Gideon)]] — Gideon's firstborn, afraid to kill the kings (8:20).
- [[Abimelech (son of Gideon)]] — Gideon's son by a concubine in Shechem (8:31); kills his seventy brothers and is made king at Shechem (9:1–6).
- [[Baal Berith]] — the god Israel takes after Gideon's death (8:33), whose house pays for the killing (9:4).
- [[Jotham (son of Gideon)]] — the youngest son, who hid and survived (9:5); tells the fable of the trees from Gerizim, and flees to Beer (9:7–21).
- [[Gaal]] — the newcomer who stirs Shechem against Abimelech (9:26–41).
- [[Zebul]] — Abimelech's officer and *ruler of the city* (9:28–41).
- [[The Woman of Thebez]] — casts the millstone that kills Abimelech (9:53).
- [[Ibzan]], [[Elon the Zebulunite]], [[Abdon (son of Hillel)]] — the three minor judges after Jephthah (12:8–15).
- [[Tola (son of Puah)]] — of Issachar; *arose to save Israel* and judged twenty-three years (10:1–2).
- [[Jair (the Gileadite)]] — judged twenty-two years; thirty sons on thirty donkey colts (10:3–5).
- [[Issachar]] — Tola's tribe (10:1); *the princes of Issachar were with Deborah* (5:15).
- [[Jephthah]] — the Gileadite made head by contract (11:1–11); argues with Ammon (11:12–28); vows, and keeps the vow (11:29–40); fights Ephraim at the fords, judges six years, and dies (12:1–7).
- [[Jephthah's Daughter]] — his only child, who comes out to meet him and is given up to his vow (11:34–40).
- [[King of Ammon (of Jephthah's time)]] — claims the land from the Arnon to the Jabbok (11:13).
- [[Sihon]], [[Balak]], [[Chemosh]] — the Amorite king, the Moabite king, and the god named in Jephthah's case (11:19–25).
- [[The Maonites]] — named once, among the peoples Yahweh saved Israel from (10:12).
- [[Samson]] — announced before conception, a Nazirite from the womb, to *begin* to save Israel from the Philistines (13:1–25); kills a thousand at Lehi and judges twenty years (15:1–20); carries off the gate of Gaza (16:1–3); betrayed by Delilah, shaved, blinded, and imprisoned (16:4–22).
- [[Manoah]] and [[Manoah's Wife]] — Samson's parents; she sees the angel twice and answers her husband's fear (13:2–24); they object to his Philistine wife (14:3).
- [[Samson's Timnite Wife]] — chosen by sight, pressed for the riddle, and given to his companion (14:1–20); burned with her father (15:6).
- [[Delilah]] — loved by Samson, paid by the Philistine lords to learn his strength (16:4–20).
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — at Bochim (2:1–5), Ophrah (6:11–24), and Manoah's field and altar (13:3–21).
