# Song of Solomon

## Overview

Love poetry associated with [[Solomon]] by its heading, voiced through lovers and
other speakers. Desire, praise, absence, searching, gardens, and renewed invitation
shape its movement. The wiki follows the poems without imposing a continuous biography
or equating every male voice and royal reference with Solomon.

[[Love Better Than Wine]] opens with a woman's desire for kisses, a comparison of love
with wine, fragrant oils and a name, and rejoicing in love. The heading's attribution
is explicit in the WEB; the identities of the lovers are not. [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]]
and [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] are literary-role pages, not independently identified
historical people. No composition date or original performance setting is established.

[[Dark and Lovely]] turns the woman toward an audience, [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]], and
moves from royal rooms to vineyards and pasture. She calls herself dark and lovely, explains
the darkness by the sun and the vineyard work her brothers forced on her, and asks where the
beloved shepherd rests his flock at noon. Royal and pastoral images stand side by side
without being reconciled into one plot.

[[Delight and the Unawakened Love]] is the first full exchange between the lovers. He compares
her to a steed in Pharaoh's chariots and calls her beautiful; she answers with myrrh, henna
from En Gedi, and the same words returned. She is a rose of Sharon and a lily among thorns; he
is an apple tree among the trees. After the banquet hall and the embrace, the woman puts the
daughters of Jerusalem under oath not to awaken love *"until it so desires"* (2:7). That
refrain returns at 3:5 and 8:4 and marks the ends of major units in the plan below.

[[Rise Up, My Love]] begins with *"The voice of my beloved!"* He comes over the hills like a roe,
looks in through the windows and the lattice, and calls her out twice: *"Rise up, my love, my
beautiful one, and come away"*, because winter is past and the land is in blossom. He asks to see
her face and hear her voice; a plural voice asks for the little foxes to be caught. She answers with
the first statement of mutual belonging, *"My beloved is mine, and I am his"* (2:16), which returns
changed at 6:3 and 7:10, and sends him to the mountains of Bether. Whether she goes out is not said.

[[Seeking Him by Night]] turns the roles around. At night on her bed she seeks *"him whom my soul
loves"* and does not find him; she goes through the city's streets and squares, is found by the
watchmen, and then finds him, holds him, and brings him into her mother's house (3:1–4). The phrase
*him whom my soul loves* is said four times and replaces any name. The refrain of 2:7 follows word
for word (3:5). A second night search, at 5:2–8, repeats the watchmen and the failed seeking and
ends without finding him.

[[Solomon's Wedding Procession]] changes the scene completely. *"Who is this who comes up from the
wilderness like pillars of smoke"* (3:6)? The answer is Solomon's carriage, perfumed, guarded by sixty
armed men, and made of Lebanon wood, silver, gold, and purple (3:7–10). The daughters of Zion are told
to see king Solomon, crowned by his mother *"in the day of his weddings"* (3:11). It is the first time
Solomon is present in a scene and the Song's only named wedding, yet no bride is seen and neither
lover speaks. Whether it is the lovers' wedding is left open.

[[You Are All Beautiful]] is the man's first full description of the woman. Framed by *"Behold, you
are beautiful"* and *"You are all beautiful, my love. There is no spot in you"* (4:1, 7), it moves
from her eyes to her breasts in pictures from the land: goats on Mount Gilead, shorn ewes, a scarlet
thread, a pomegranate, David's tower hung with shields, twin fawns among the lilies. He takes up her
line of 2:17 and says he will go to the mountain of myrrh (4:6). Parts of the description return at
6:5–7 and 7:3.

[[A Garden Locked and Opened]] continues his speech and calls her *"my bride"* for the first time; all six of the
Song's uses of *bride* fall in 4:8–5:1. He calls her down from Lebanon, Amana, Senir, and Hermon, says
she has ravished his heart, and returns her opening words: her love is better than wine (4:10; 1:2).
Then she is *"A locked up garden … a sealed fountain"* (4:12), full of fruit and spices and watered
by *"a well of living waters"*. At 4:16 she opens it: *"Let my beloved come into his garden"*. At 5:1
he has come, and an unidentified voice says *"Eat, friends! Drink"*. It is the Song's fullest scene of
union, near the middle of the book.

[[I Opened, but He Had Gone]] follows at once with the opposite. She is asleep, *"but my heart was
awake"*, when he knocks: *"Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled"* (5:2). She hesitates
over her robe and her washed feet, and when she opens, *"my beloved left; and had gone away"* (5:6).
She seeks and calls him in vain. The watchmen of 3:3 find her again, and this time they beat her, and
the keepers of the walls take her cloak (5:7). She asks the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him she is
*"faint with love"* (5:8). It is the second night search, and the first that ends without him.

## Structure

The opening sequence leads from desire and self-description to shared delight and the
first refrain about not awakening love (1:1–2:7). Invitations and a night search follow
(2:8–3:5), then a royal procession and extended praise, ending in garden invitation and
response (3:6–5:1). A second night search, descriptions, renewed belonging, and praise
occupy 5:2–7:9. The final invitations, refrain, seal, family exchanges, and summons form
7:10–8:14. Repeated motifs supply continuity without requiring every scene to form one
chronological plot. The divisions below are editorial judgments based on the stored WEB.

## Key themes

- [[Love]] — desire, invitation, and the value assigned to the beloved (1:2–4); mutual praise
  and the charge not to awaken love before its time (1:9–2:7).
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — wine and fragrance as positive measures of delight (1:2–4).
- [[Names and Naming]] — a name likened to poured oil (1:3).
- [[Praise and Song]] — the heading's song and the shared praise of love (1:1, 4).
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — Solomon's attribution and the king's rooms (1:1, 4).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the woman answers the stare of onlookers (1:5–6); *"Behold, you
  are beautiful"*, said and returned (1:15–16).
- [[Brotherhood]] — angry brothers who set their sister to work (1:6).
- [[Work]] — vineyard keeping and grazing flocks (1:6–8).
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the first adjuration of the daughters of Jerusalem (2:7).
- [[Love]], [[Seeing and Being Seen]], [[Work]] — the spring invitation, *"Let me see your face"*,
  the foxes in the vineyards, and *"My beloved is mine, and I am his"* (2:8–17).
- [[Love]], [[City and Civilization]], [[Light and Darkness]], [[Oaths and Vows]] — the night
  search through the city for *"him whom my soul loves"*, and the second adjuration (3:1–5).
- [[Marriage]], [[Kingship and Empire]], [[Joy and Enjoyment]], [[War]], [[Wealth and Possessions]]
  — Solomon's carriage, armed escort, and wedding crown, *"in the day of the gladness of his heart"*
  (3:6–11).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]], [[Love]], [[War]], [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — the man's description of
  her body, a neck like a tower hung with shields, and *"There is no spot in you"* (4:1–7).
- [[Marriage]], [[Love]], [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — *"my sister, my bride"*, the locked garden opened
  to the beloved, and *"Eat, friends! Drink"* (4:8–5:1).
- [[Love]], [[Oaths and Vows]], [[City and Civilization]], [[Suffering]], [[Nakedness and Covering]],
  [[Light and Darkness]] — the knock at night, the door opened too late, the beating by the watchmen,
  and the daughters adjured to tell him *"I am faint with love"* (5:2–8).

## Key people

- [[Solomon]] — named in the heading (1:1); present, silent, in the wedding procession (3:6–11).
- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — the opening desiring voice.
- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — the beloved addressed; not named in 1:2–4; pictured as a
  shepherd in 1:7; the one who calls her out in spring (2:10–14); the only speaker of 4:1–7; calls
  her *bride* and comes into the garden (4:8–5:1); knocks at night and is gone when she opens
  (5:2–6).
- [[David]] — named only in *"David's tower"* (4:4), a comparison.
- [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] — the Song's recurring addressed audience, first at 1:5;
  adjured at 2:7 and again at 3:5; as *"daughters of Zion"*, summoned to see the king (3:11);
  adjured a third time, to tell him she is faint with love (5:8).
- [[The Watchmen (Song of Solomon)]] — *"who go about the city"*; they find her at 3:3, and at 5:7
  beat her, with *"the keepers of the walls"* who take her cloak.
- Friends and virgins remain unnamed groups in the opening unit; the woman's brothers
  (*"my mother's sons"*, 1:6) are unnamed and have no page. Her mother, whose house she brings him
  into (3:4), has no page. Nor do Solomon's
  sixty mighty men or his unnamed mother (3:7, 11).

## Key places

No geographical place is named in 1:1–4. The king's rooms are an interior without a
stated location; Jerusalem must not be supplied there from later verses.

- [[Jerusalem]] — named in the address *"daughters of Jerusalem"* (1:5; 2:7; 3:5; 5:8), not as a
  setting. The city of the night searches (3:2–3; 5:7) is unnamed.
- [[Kedar]] — the tents of an Arabian people, a comparison for the woman's darkness (1:5).
- [[En Gedi]] — its vineyards' henna, an image for the beloved (1:14).
- [[Sharon]] — *"a rose of Sharon"*, the woman's self-description (2:1).
- [[Bether]] — *"the mountains of Bether"* (2:17); possibly *cleft mountains* rather than a name.
- [[Lebanon]] — the wood of Solomon's carriage (3:9); it recurs through chapters 4–7.
- [[Gilead]] — *"Mount Gilead"*, where the goats of the hair simile come down (4:1).
- [[Zion]] — in *"daughters of Zion"* (3:11), in parallel with Jerusalem. The wilderness the
  procession comes up from (3:6) is unnamed.
- [[Amana]] and [[Hermon]] — with Senir, the heights the bride is called from (4:8). Lebanon is named
  there too, and again for her garments' smell and the garden's streams (4:11, 15).
