# Deuteronomy

## Overview

[[Consecrate the Firstborn of the Herd and Flock]] (15:19–23) dedicates every firstborn male of herd and flock to
Yahweh: it may not be worked or shorn, and the household eats it yearly at the chosen place. A
firstling with a defect is not sacrificed but eaten at home like game, its blood poured out.

[[Release the Servant with Gifts]] (15:12–18) frees a Hebrew man or woman in the seventh year
and forbids sending them away empty: the master gives from flock, threshing floor, and wine press,
remembering Israel's slavery in Egypt. A servant who loves the house may stay for life, his ear
pierced at the door.

[[The Year of Release and the Open Hand]] (15:1–11) releases a brother's debts at the end of every seven years, though not a
foreigner's. It promises that there shall be no poor in a blessed and obedient land, yet says the
poor will never cease, and forbids refusing a loan because the year of release is near.

[[Tithes for Feasting and Provision]] (14:22–29) has the household eat its yearly tithe before
Yahweh at the chosen place, turning it into money if the way is long, so as to learn to fear him.
Every third year the tithe stays in the towns for the Levite, foreigner, fatherless, and widow.

[[A Holy People and Its Food]] (14:1–21) grounds daily life in Israel's status as Yahweh's
children and holy people: no cutting or baldness for the dead, and a food list close to
Leviticus 11, with differences over insects and over meat that dies of itself.

[[Do Not Follow Other Gods]] (12:29–13:18) forbids imitating the nations' rites, which included
burning their children, and adds nothing to the command. A prophet whose sign comes true, a relative
who entices in secret, and a town drawn away to other gods all face death; the town is devoted.

[[The Place Yahweh Will Choose]] (12:1–28) opens the law code with worship. The nations' shrines
are destroyed, and offerings, tithes, and vows go only to the one unnamed place Yahweh will choose
for his name. Ordinary meat may now be eaten at home, but never its blood.

[[Set These Words Before You]] (11:18–32) closes the exhortation begun at 4:44. The household
teaching of chapter 6 returns, possession is promised for love and obedience, and a blessing and a
curse are set before Israel, to be staged on Gerizim and Ebal beside the oaks of Moreh.

[[Remember His Deeds and Guard Your Hearts]] (11:1–17) grounds obedience in what the assembly saw,
from Egypt's signs to the earth swallowing Dathan and Abiram. The land drinks the sky's rain, unlike
foot-watered Egypt, so loyalty or a deceived heart decides between harvest and a shut sky.

[[What Yahweh Requires of Israel]] (10:12–22) joins wholehearted obedience and heart-circumcision
to impartial justice and love for foreigners. Remembered Egypt grounds care; growth from seventy
to a multitude grounds praise. Election does not authorize judicial favoritism.

[[New Tablets and the Journey Onward]] (10:1–11) recalls replacement tablets and heard intercession,
with notices of Aaron’s succession and Levi’s service. The command to depart renews Israel’s
future after the calf; the Moserah death notice remains in tension with Numbers’ Mount Hor.

[[Remember the Calf and Moses' Intercession]] (9:7–29) recalls the calf and other wilderness rebellions as
evidence against merit. Moses’ heard intercession appeals to redemption, the ancestors, and
Yahweh’s ownership of Israel; the specific prayer for Aaron expands the Exodus account.

[[Not Because of Your Righteousness]] (9:1–6) answers fear of stronger nations with Yahweh’s advance,
then denies Israel’s righteousness as the basis for possession. Judgment on the nations and
the ancestral oath explain the gift to a stiff-necked people.

[[Remember the Wilderness and the Giver of Wealth]] (8:1–20) interprets hunger, manna, and
preservation as humbling, testing, and fatherly discipline. The good land’s abundance calls
for gratitude; self-credit and forgetting can end in the same destruction as the nations.


[[Blessing and the Removal of the Nations]] (7:12–26) ties covenant blessing in the land to listening.
Fear of the nations is answered by remembering Egypt; their removal is complete but gradual, and the
snare of their gods reaches the silver and gold on their images.

[[A People Chosen in Love]] (7:1–11) grounds election in love and the ancestral oath rather than size.
Holiness entails separation from rival worship, including destruction and marriage prohibitions;
the warning of destruction also addresses Israel. Covenant faithfulness calls for obedience.

[[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]] (6:10–25) warns that inherited abundance can lead to forgetting the exodus.
Exclusive service, the Massah warning, and the future parent’s explanation join rescue to
obedience for the people’s good and life.


[[Hear Israel and Love Yahweh]] (6:1–9) joins confession, wholehearted love, and
daily household teaching. The commands are for life in the land across generations; words
on the heart become words spoken, bound as signs, and written at household entrances.


[[The People's Request for a Mediator]] (5:23–33) explains the continuing mediation of instruction.
God approves the representatives’ request, wishes for lasting fear and obedience, and keeps
Moses beside him to receive what he must teach for life in the land.

[[The Ten Commandments Recounted]] (5:1–22) binds the living assembly to Horeb’s covenant.
The public words are recalled and written on tablets; Sabbath rest is explicitly grounded
in remembered bondage and rescue, differing from Exodus 20’s creation rationale.


[[The Setting of the Covenant Instruction]] (4:44–49) introduces the law in the valley opposite Beth Peor,
east of the Jordan. The narrator ties the instruction to the exodus journey and the already
conquered kingdoms of Sihon and Og before Moses resumes direct speech.

[[Three Cities Beyond the Jordan]] (4:41–43) records Moses designating Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan
as eastern refuges, so that the unwitting killer without prior hatred may flee and live.
This narrative notice gives concrete locations to the protection prescribed in Numbers 35.

Deuteronomy places Moses’ instruction to Israel beyond the Jordan, in Moab, before his death
and the people’s crossing. Retrospective speeches, covenant commands, blessings and curses,
a song, tribal blessings, and the account of Moses’ death form its major movements.

The introductory frame, [[The Words of Moses Beyond the Jordan]] (1:1–5), dates the address to
the first day of the eleventh month of the fortieth year and places it after the victories over
Sihon and Og. Moses speaks according to Yahweh’s command and begins to declare the law. The
narrator’s introduction and Moses’ direct speech must be distinguished.

Moses' speech then opens, in [[Leave Horeb and Possess the Land]] (1:6–8), not with a law but with
a quotation of Yahweh's word at Horeb: the stay at the mountain is over, the land is listed out to
Lebanon and the Euphrates, and it is *set before you* on the strength of the oath to Abraham,
Isaac, and Jacob. The retrospect that follows measures Israel against that command.

Before the march, [[Judges Appointed for the People]] (1:9–18) recalls how Moses, unable to carry so
many alone, had the tribes supply wise and known men and made them heads and judges, charged to hear
small and great alike *"for the judgment is God's"*. Exodus 18 credits the plan to Jethro;
Deuteronomy tells it as Moses' own proposal, approved by the people.

At Kadesh Barnea, in [[The Spies and the Refusal to Go Up]] (1:19–33), the Horeb command is
repeated, the people themselves propose sending men ahead, and the twelve bring back fruit and a
single verdict, *"It is a good land"*. The people refuse anyway, saying Yahweh brought them out
*"because Yahweh hated us"*. Moses answers with what they had seen — Yahweh fighting for them in
Egypt, carrying them *"as a man does bear his son"*, and going before them in fire and cloud — and
gives the verdict: *"in this thing you didn't believe Yahweh your God"*. The story is told to the
sons in the second person, as if they had stood at Kadesh themselves.

[[The Sentence and the Presumptuous Ascent]] (1:34–46) recalls the exclusion oath, the promises to Caleb, Joshua, and the children, and the failed attempt to fight after Yahweh forbids it. Moses includes his own exclusion; its explanation differs from Numbers 20:12 and remains explicitly flagged. The account closes with unanswered weeping and the stay at Kadesh.

[[Around Seir and Past Moab and Ammon]] (2:1–23) recalls the protected territories of Esau and Lot’s descendants,
the thirty-eight years from Kadesh to Zered, and the end of the condemned military generation.
Historical asides place Israel’s possession beside other peoples’ territorial successions;
provision in the wilderness does not cancel judgment.

[[The Defeat of Sihon Recounted]] (2:24–37) recalls the peaceful embassy, Sihon’s divinely hardened refusal,
the conquest including civilian deaths, and the continued prohibition on approaching Ammon.
The comparison with Edomite accommodation in 2:29 remains in tension with Numbers 20.

[[The Defeat of Og Recounted]] (3:1–11) adds the defeat of Og, sixty fortified cities,
the destruction of civilians, and notices about Hermon’s names and Og’s bedstead. The bedstead’s
dimensions do not establish Og’s height.

[[The Eastern Inheritance and the Charge to Joshua]] (3:12–22) recalls the tribal allocation, the eastern warriors’
obligation to serve until their brothers also rest, and Joshua’s encouragement from the defeats
of the two kings. Possession precedes release from shared responsibility.

[[Moses Pleads to Cross the Jordan]] (3:23–29) recounts Moses’ refused request to cross.
He may see from Pisgah but must strengthen Joshua to lead Israel into the inheritance.
The explanation of his exclusion remains in tension with Numbers 20:12.

[[Hear the Statutes and Remember Horeb]] (4:1–14) turns the retrospect into exhortation. Hearing and
doing the statutes is life and possession; the word may not be added to or reduced; the deaths at Peor
and the survival of the faithful are the proof. Keeping the law is Israel's wisdom before the nations,
and the day at Horeb — fire, darkness, a voice with no form, the covenant on two tablets — must be
remembered and taught to children's children.

[[No Form Seen and No Image Made]] (4:15–31) draws the first command from that memory: Israel saw no
form at Horeb, so it may make no likeness of any creature and worship nothing in the sky. Moses sets
his own sworn exclusion between the warnings. Yahweh is a devouring fire and a jealous God; a
long-settled, image-making Israel will be scattered to serve gods that cannot hear, and from there will
seek and find a merciful God who does not forget the covenant.

[[Has Any People Heard God and Lived]] (4:32–40) closes the first address. Moses challenges Israel to
search all history since creation for anything like Horeb and the exodus: a people that heard God from
the fire and lived, a nation taken out of another by signs and a mighty hand. It was shown so that
Israel would know *there is no one else*. Yahweh's love for the fathers is named for the first time as
the reason for the choice, and the confession leads straight to the charge to keep the statutes.

## Structure

- 1:1–4:43 — introduction, journey recalled, and exhortation.
- 4:44–11:32 — covenant teaching, Horeb recalled, and the call to love and remember.
- 12–26 — instructions for worship, justice, community, and life in the land.
- 27–30 — covenant ceremony, blessings, curses, and return.
- 31–34 — succession, preservation of instruction, song, blessing, and Moses’ death.
