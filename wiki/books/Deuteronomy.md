# Deuteronomy

## Overview

[[The Covenant in Moab and the Hidden Things]] (29:1–29) gathers the inclusive assembly into the Moab covenant.
Witnessed signs coexist with an ungiven knowing heart; presumptuous apostasy brings judgment,
and the ruined land becomes a warning to later observers. Hidden things remain God’s; revealed
law obligates Israel and its children.

[[Curses for Disobedience]] (28:15–68) reverses the blessings through disease, failed harvests,
defeat, siege, and scattering. The closing return to Egypt undoes liberation; unnamed kings
and invaders remain unnamed, and threatened destruction is held beside the covenant-preserving
promise of Leviticus 26:44–45.

[[Blessings for Obedience (Deuteronomy)]] (28:1–14) promises blessing across household, harvest, work,
security, and national standing, conditional on obedience. Holy identity and seasonal rain
join lending without borrowing; the section closes against following other gods.

[[The People Answer Amen to the Curses]] (27:11–26) prescribes six tribes on each mountain,
loud Levitical curses, and the people’s repeated Amen. Specific wrongs culminate in failure
to uphold the law by doing it. Blessing is assigned but only curse formulas are supplied;
the ceremony remains commanded rather than narrated.


[[Stones and an Altar on Mount Ebal]] (27:1–10) moves from the law code to instructions for entry:
plastered inscribed stones, an uncut-stone altar, offerings, and rejoicing on Ebal. Moses speaks
with elders and then priests; present belonging grounds obedience, while the ceremony remains
commanded for after the crossing.

[[A People for Yahweh and a God for Israel]] (26:16–19) closes the law code with reciprocal
declarations: Israel acknowledges Yahweh and undertakes obedience, and Yahweh declares Israel
his possession. Wholehearted keeping of the commands accompanies the promise of honor among
the nations and holiness to him; the earlier promise is reaffirmed.

[[First Fruits and the Tithe Declaration]] (26:1–15) closes the law code with two speeches. Once in the
land, the farmer brings the first of the ground's fruit in a basket to the chosen place and recites
over it: *"My father was a Syrian ready to perish. He went down into Egypt"*, the oppression, the cry,
*"Yahweh heard our voice"*, the mighty hand, and *"this land, a land flowing with milk and honey"*;
then *"Now, behold, I have brought the first of the fruit of the ground"*. He bows down, the only time
in the book that verb is directed to Yahweh, and rejoices with the Levite and the foreigner. In the
third year, after giving the tithe to the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow, he
declares *"I have put away the holy things out of my house"*, kept from mourning and the dead, and
asks Yahweh to *"Look down from your holy habitation, from heaven, and bless"*. Who *"my father"* is
is recorded, not settled.

[[Justice, Family Duty, Honest Weights, and Amalek]] (25:1–19) closes the casuistic laws with six cases.
The judges *"justify the righteous, and condemn the wicked"*, and a beating is *"by number"* and capped
at forty, *"lest … your brother will be degraded in your sight"*. The threshing ox is not muzzled. A
brother marries his brother's childless widow *"that his name not be blotted out of Israel"*; if he
refuses, she loosens his shoe and spits in his face before the elders, and his house is named for it.
A wife who seizes a man's private parts in a fight loses her hand. One weight and one measure, *"perfect
and just"*. And *"Remember what Amalek did to you"*, who struck the stragglers *"when you were faint and
weary"*: *"you shall blot out the memory of Amalek from under the sky. You shall not forget."*

[[Pledges, Wages, Justice, and Gleaning]] (24:6–22) protects what a poor person cannot do without for a
day. No one takes a household's mill in pledge, *"for he takes a life in pledge"*; a man who steals and
sells an Israelite brother dies; Israel follows the priests on leprosy and remembers Miriam. The lender
waits outside for the pledge and returns a poor man's garment at sunset, and it *"shall be righteousness
to you before Yahweh your God"*. The hired man, Israelite or foreigner, is paid the same day. *"Every
man shall be put to death for his own sin."* The widow's clothing is never pledged, and the forgotten
sheaf, the olives, and the grapes are left for the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow, because
*"you were a slave in Egypt"*.

[[Divorce, Remarriage, and the Newlywed]] (24:1–5) gives two laws about the end and the beginning of a marriage. A man
who has sent his wife away with *"a bill of divorce"* *"may not take her again to be his wife"* after she
has married another man, *"after that she is defiled; for that is abomination before Yahweh. You shall
not cause the land to sin"*. The divorce is assumed and its ground, *"some unseemly thing"*, left
undefined. A man newly married stays home a year and *"shall cheer his wife whom he has taken"*. Who or
what defiled the woman is recorded as unsettled.

[[Fugitives, Interest, Vows, and a Neighbor's Produce]] (23:15–25) gives five short laws. A slave who has
escaped to Israel is not handed back, and he *"shall dwell with you … in the place which he shall
choose"*: Deuteronomy's formula for Yahweh's chosen place, used this once with another chooser. No
*qǝdēšâ* or *qādēš* is to be found in Israel, and a prostitute's hire may not pay a vow. No interest
is taken from a brother, though it may be from a *nokrî*. A vow is optional, but once spoken it must
be paid. A passer-by may eat a neighbor's grapes and grain by hand but carry nothing off. Whether the
runaway law covers an Israelite's own slave is recorded as unsettled.

[[The Assembly and the Holy Camp]] (23:1–14) draws two boundaries around a gathering where Yahweh is
present. No man *"emasculated by crushing or cutting"*, no *mamzēr*, and no Ammonite or Moabite *"even
to the tenth generation"* may *"enter into Yahweh's assembly"* — the last because they *"didn't meet you
with bread and with water"* and hired Balaam, whose curse *"Yahweh your God turned … into a blessing to
you, because Yahweh your God loved you"*. The Edomite, *"your brother"*, and the Egyptian, *"because you
lived as a foreigner in his land"*, enter in the third generation. In the war camp the man unclean by
night stays outside until sunset and excrement is buried, *"for Yahweh your God walks in the middle of
your camp"*. That 2:29 has Moab selling Israel food is recorded, not resolved.

[[Marriage Accusations and Sexual Offenses]] (22:13–30) gives seven cases about a woman's status. A husband who *"hates"* his
bride and gives her *"a bad name"* is answered by her parents, who *"spread the cloth before the elders of
the city"*; if he lied he is chastised, fined a hundred shekels, and *"may not put her away all his days"*,
and if the charge is true she is stoned at her father's door, *"because she has done folly in Israel"*.
Adultery brings death on both. A pledged woman in the city is stoned with the man *"because she didn't
cry"*; in the field only the man dies, *"for as when a man rises against his neighbor, and kills him, even
so is this matter"*. The man who seizes an unpledged virgin pays fifty shekels and marries her without
right of divorce, and no man may take his father's wife. Yahweh is not named in the eighteen verses.

[[Care for the Neighbor and Ordered Distinctions]] (22:1–12) gathers ten short laws. The Exodus law
of the enemy's straying ox is restated for *"your brother"*, with the animal kept *"until your brother
comes looking for it"* and a threefold *"you may not hide yourself"*. Men's and women's clothing are
not to be exchanged, *"an abomination to Yahweh your God"*. The mother bird is let go, with the fifth
commandment's promise, *"that it may be well with you, and that you may prolong your days"*. A railing
keeps *"blood"* off a new house. Vineyard, plow team, and cloth are not to mix two kinds, the vineyard's
yield *"defiled"* — Hebrew *become holy* — if they do; and the fringes of Numbers 15 go *"on the four
corners of your cloak"*.

[[Captives, Firstborn Sons, and Public Shame]] (21:10–23) sets four limits inside powers the law does not
remove. A woman taken in war may become a wife only after she has *"bewail[ed] her father and her mother
a full month"*, and if she is dismissed, *"you shall not sell her at all for money … because you have
humbled her"*. A father may not let love for one wife move the firstborn's *"double portion"* from the
hated wife's son, *"the beginning of his strength"*. Both parents bring the *"stubborn and rebellious"*
son to the elders in the gate; the town stones him, and *"All Israel shall hear, and fear"*. A hanged body
is buried the same day, *"for he who is hanged is accursed of God"*, which Galatians 3:13 applies to the
cross.

[[An Unsolved Killing and the Broken-Necked Heifer]] (21:1–9) takes the killing that chapter 19's courts cannot reach: a body
in the field, and *"it isn't known who has struck him"*. The elders and judges measure to the nearest
town, whose elders break the neck of an unworked heifer in a valley *"neither plowed nor sown"*, wash
their hands over it, and say, *"Our hands have not shed this blood, neither have our eyes seen it"*. They
pray, *"Forgive, Yahweh, your people Israel, whom you have redeemed"*, and the innocent blood is put away.
The verb is *kipper*, which Numbers 35:33 allows for the land only by the shedder's blood; the tension is
recorded, not resolved.

[[Laws for Going to War]] (20:1–20) is the law code's one chapter on war. Before battle the priest tells
the army not to fear, *"for Yahweh your God is he who goes with you, to fight for you"*. The officers send
home the man with an undedicated house, an unused vineyard, or a betrothed wife, and the man who is
afraid, *"lest his brother's heart melt as his heart"*. A city far off is offered peace; if it refuses, its
males are killed. The cities of the land are utterly destroyed, *"that they not teach you to follow all
their abominations"*. Fruit trees may not be cut in a siege: *"is the tree of the field man?"* Whether the
offer of peace reaches the land's cities is left open.

[[Landmarks, Witnesses, and False Testimony]] (19:14–21) closes the chapter. A neighbour's landmark,
*"which they of old time have set"*, may not be moved. No single witness can establish *"any iniquity"*;
it takes two or three. A witness suspected of lying is tried *"before Yahweh, before the priests and the
judges"*, and if he is false, *"you shall do to him as he had thought to do to his brother"*, with the
talion as the measure: *"life for life, eye for eye"*.

[[Refuge for the Unwitting Killer]] (19:1–13) turns from the offices to the protection of life. Three
cities in the middle of the land, with the way prepared, receive the man who *"kills his neighbor
unawares, and didn't hate him in time past"* — the ax head flying off the handle — lest *"the avenger of
blood"* overtake him *"because the way is long"*. Three more are added if the border is enlarged. The man
who lay in wait is fetched by his own elders and handed to the avenger: *"Your eye shall not pity him"*.
Whether this means six cities or nine is left open.

[[A Prophet Like Moses]] (18:9–22) ends the list of offices with the prophet. Israel is not to imitate
the nations' child-burning, divination, sorcery, mediums, or consulting of the dead, which are why the
nations are driven out; it is to be *"perfect with Yahweh"*. Instead Yahweh will raise *"a prophet …
like me"* from among the brothers, as he promised at Horeb when the people asked not to hear his voice
again, and put his words in his mouth. The prophet who speaks presumptuously dies, and a word that does
not happen was not Yahweh's. Whether the promise is of an office or of one prophet is left open; Acts
3:22–23 applies it to Jesus.

[[The Portion of the Priests and Levites]] (18:1–8) turns from the king to the priests. *"The priests
the Levites, all the tribe of Levi"* have no portion nor inheritance with Israel; Yahweh is their
inheritance, and they eat the offerings by fire. The priest receives the shoulder, cheeks, and inner
parts of each sacrifice, and the first fruits of grain, wine, oil, and fleece. A Levite from the
towns may come to the chosen place, minister as his brothers do, and eat like portions. Whether this
makes every Levite a priest is left open, as are the different cuts named in Leviticus 7.

[[The Law for the King]] (17:14–20) foresees Israel's request for a king *"like all the nations"*
and grants it: the king must be chosen by Yahweh and be a brother, not a foreigner. He shall not
multiply horses, sending the people back to Egypt for them, nor wives, nor silver and gold. His one
duty is to copy *"this law"* and read it all his life, so that his heart is not lifted above his
brothers.

[[Judges, Worship, and Difficult Cases]] (16:18–17:13) sets judges and officers in every town, bans
partiality and bribes, and commands *"that which is altogether just"*. It forbids an Asherah or a
sacred stone at Yahweh's altar and a defective sacrifice. Death for other gods needs two or three
witnesses, and the case too hard for the towns goes up to the priests and judge at the chosen place.

[[Passover, Weeks, and Booths]] (16:1–17) sets Deuteronomy's festival calendar. Passover is kept in
Abib, but only at the chosen place and not in the towns, with seven days of *"the bread of
affliction"*. Weeks is counted from the first sickle in the grain, and tents follows the harvest of
threshing floor and wine press. At both the household, servants, Levite, foreigner, fatherless, and
widow rejoice before Yahweh, and all males appear three times a year, not empty, giving as Yahweh
has blessed.

[[Consecrate the Firstborn of the Herd and Flock]] (15:19–23) dedicates every firstborn male of herd and flock to
Yahweh: it may not be worked or shorn, and the household eats it yearly at the chosen place. A
firstling with a defect is not sacrificed but eaten at home like game, its blood poured out.

[[Release the Servant with Gifts]] (15:12–18) frees a Hebrew man or woman in the seventh year
and forbids sending them away empty: the master gives from flock, threshing floor, and wine press,
remembering Israel's slavery in Egypt. A servant who loves the house may stay for life, his ear
pierced at the door.

[[The Year of Release and the Open Hand]] (15:1–11) releases a brother's debts at the end of every seven years, though not a
foreigner's. It promises that there shall be no poor in a blessed and obedient land, yet says the
poor will never cease, and forbids refusing a loan because the year of release is near.

[[Tithes for Feasting and Provision]] (14:22–29) has the household eat its yearly tithe before
Yahweh at the chosen place, turning it into money if the way is long, so as to learn to fear him.
Every third year the tithe stays in the towns for the Levite, foreigner, fatherless, and widow.

[[A Holy People and Its Food]] (14:1–21) grounds daily life in Israel's status as Yahweh's
children and holy people: no cutting or baldness for the dead, and a food list close to
Leviticus 11, with differences over insects and over meat that dies of itself.

[[Do Not Follow Other Gods]] (12:29–13:18) forbids imitating the nations' rites, which included
burning their children, and adds nothing to the command. A prophet whose sign comes true, a relative
who entices in secret, and a town drawn away to other gods all face death; the town is devoted.

[[The Place Yahweh Will Choose]] (12:1–28) opens the law code with worship. The nations' shrines
are destroyed, and offerings, tithes, and vows go only to the one unnamed place Yahweh will choose
for his name. Ordinary meat may now be eaten at home, but never its blood.

[[Set These Words Before You]] (11:18–32) closes the exhortation begun at 4:44. The household
teaching of chapter 6 returns, possession is promised for love and obedience, and a blessing and a
curse are set before Israel, to be staged on Gerizim and Ebal beside the oaks of Moreh.

[[Remember His Deeds and Guard Your Hearts]] (11:1–17) grounds obedience in what the assembly saw,
from Egypt's signs to the earth swallowing Dathan and Abiram. The land drinks the sky's rain, unlike
foot-watered Egypt, so loyalty or a deceived heart decides between harvest and a shut sky.

[[What Yahweh Requires of Israel]] (10:12–22) joins wholehearted obedience and heart-circumcision
to impartial justice and love for foreigners. Remembered Egypt grounds care; growth from seventy
to a multitude grounds praise. Election does not authorize judicial favoritism.

[[New Tablets and the Journey Onward]] (10:1–11) recalls replacement tablets and heard intercession,
with notices of Aaron’s succession and Levi’s service. The command to depart renews Israel’s
future after the calf; the Moserah death notice remains in tension with Numbers’ Mount Hor.

[[Remember the Calf and Moses' Intercession]] (9:7–29) recalls the calf and other wilderness rebellions as
evidence against merit. Moses’ heard intercession appeals to redemption, the ancestors, and
Yahweh’s ownership of Israel; the specific prayer for Aaron expands the Exodus account.

[[Not Because of Your Righteousness]] (9:1–6) answers fear of stronger nations with Yahweh’s advance,
then denies Israel’s righteousness as the basis for possession. Judgment on the nations and
the ancestral oath explain the gift to a stiff-necked people.

[[Remember the Wilderness and the Giver of Wealth]] (8:1–20) interprets hunger, manna, and
preservation as humbling, testing, and fatherly discipline. The good land’s abundance calls
for gratitude; self-credit and forgetting can end in the same destruction as the nations.


[[Blessing and the Removal of the Nations]] (7:12–26) ties covenant blessing in the land to listening.
Fear of the nations is answered by remembering Egypt; their removal is complete but gradual, and the
snare of their gods reaches the silver and gold on their images.

[[A People Chosen in Love]] (7:1–11) grounds election in love and the ancestral oath rather than size.
Holiness entails separation from rival worship, including destruction and marriage prohibitions;
the warning of destruction also addresses Israel. Covenant faithfulness calls for obedience.

[[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]] (6:10–25) warns that inherited abundance can lead to forgetting the exodus.
Exclusive service, the Massah warning, and the future parent’s explanation join rescue to
obedience for the people’s good and life.


[[Hear Israel and Love Yahweh]] (6:1–9) joins confession, wholehearted love, and
daily household teaching. The commands are for life in the land across generations; words
on the heart become words spoken, bound as signs, and written at household entrances.


[[The People's Request for a Mediator]] (5:23–33) explains the continuing mediation of instruction.
God approves the representatives’ request, wishes for lasting fear and obedience, and keeps
Moses beside him to receive what he must teach for life in the land.

[[The Ten Commandments Recounted]] (5:1–22) binds the living assembly to Horeb’s covenant.
The public words are recalled and written on tablets; Sabbath rest is explicitly grounded
in remembered bondage and rescue, differing from Exodus 20’s creation rationale.


[[The Setting of the Covenant Instruction]] (4:44–49) introduces the law in the valley opposite Beth Peor,
east of the Jordan. The narrator ties the instruction to the exodus journey and the already
conquered kingdoms of Sihon and Og before Moses resumes direct speech.

[[Three Cities Beyond the Jordan]] (4:41–43) records Moses designating Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan
as eastern refuges, so that the unwitting killer without prior hatred may flee and live.
This narrative notice gives concrete locations to the protection prescribed in Numbers 35.

Deuteronomy places Moses’ instruction to Israel beyond the Jordan, in Moab, before his death
and the people’s crossing. Retrospective speeches, covenant commands, blessings and curses,
a song, tribal blessings, and the account of Moses’ death form its major movements.

The introductory frame, [[The Words of Moses Beyond the Jordan]] (1:1–5), dates the address to
the first day of the eleventh month of the fortieth year and places it after the victories over
Sihon and Og. Moses speaks according to Yahweh’s command and begins to declare the law. The
narrator’s introduction and Moses’ direct speech must be distinguished.

Moses' speech then opens, in [[Leave Horeb and Possess the Land]] (1:6–8), not with a law but with
a quotation of Yahweh's word at Horeb: the stay at the mountain is over, the land is listed out to
Lebanon and the Euphrates, and it is *set before you* on the strength of the oath to Abraham,
Isaac, and Jacob. The retrospect that follows measures Israel against that command.

Before the march, [[Judges Appointed for the People]] (1:9–18) recalls how Moses, unable to carry so
many alone, had the tribes supply wise and known men and made them heads and judges, charged to hear
small and great alike *"for the judgment is God's"*. Exodus 18 credits the plan to Jethro;
Deuteronomy tells it as Moses' own proposal, approved by the people.

At Kadesh Barnea, in [[The Spies and the Refusal to Go Up]] (1:19–33), the Horeb command is
repeated, the people themselves propose sending men ahead, and the twelve bring back fruit and a
single verdict, *"It is a good land"*. The people refuse anyway, saying Yahweh brought them out
*"because Yahweh hated us"*. Moses answers with what they had seen — Yahweh fighting for them in
Egypt, carrying them *"as a man does bear his son"*, and going before them in fire and cloud — and
gives the verdict: *"in this thing you didn't believe Yahweh your God"*. The story is told to the
sons in the second person, as if they had stood at Kadesh themselves.

[[The Sentence and the Presumptuous Ascent]] (1:34–46) recalls the exclusion oath, the promises to Caleb, Joshua, and the children, and the failed attempt to fight after Yahweh forbids it. Moses includes his own exclusion; its explanation differs from Numbers 20:12 and remains explicitly flagged. The account closes with unanswered weeping and the stay at Kadesh.

[[Around Seir and Past Moab and Ammon]] (2:1–23) recalls the protected territories of Esau and Lot’s descendants,
the thirty-eight years from Kadesh to Zered, and the end of the condemned military generation.
Historical asides place Israel’s possession beside other peoples’ territorial successions;
provision in the wilderness does not cancel judgment.

[[The Defeat of Sihon Recounted]] (2:24–37) recalls the peaceful embassy, Sihon’s divinely hardened refusal,
the conquest including civilian deaths, and the continued prohibition on approaching Ammon.
The comparison with Edomite accommodation in 2:29 remains in tension with Numbers 20.

[[The Defeat of Og Recounted]] (3:1–11) adds the defeat of Og, sixty fortified cities,
the destruction of civilians, and notices about Hermon’s names and Og’s bedstead. The bedstead’s
dimensions do not establish Og’s height.

[[The Eastern Inheritance and the Charge to Joshua]] (3:12–22) recalls the tribal allocation, the eastern warriors’
obligation to serve until their brothers also rest, and Joshua’s encouragement from the defeats
of the two kings. Possession precedes release from shared responsibility.

[[Moses Pleads to Cross the Jordan]] (3:23–29) recounts Moses’ refused request to cross.
He may see from Pisgah but must strengthen Joshua to lead Israel into the inheritance.
The explanation of his exclusion remains in tension with Numbers 20:12.

[[Hear the Statutes and Remember Horeb]] (4:1–14) turns the retrospect into exhortation. Hearing and
doing the statutes is life and possession; the word may not be added to or reduced; the deaths at Peor
and the survival of the faithful are the proof. Keeping the law is Israel's wisdom before the nations,
and the day at Horeb — fire, darkness, a voice with no form, the covenant on two tablets — must be
remembered and taught to children's children.

[[No Form Seen and No Image Made]] (4:15–31) draws the first command from that memory: Israel saw no
form at Horeb, so it may make no likeness of any creature and worship nothing in the sky. Moses sets
his own sworn exclusion between the warnings. Yahweh is a devouring fire and a jealous God; a
long-settled, image-making Israel will be scattered to serve gods that cannot hear, and from there will
seek and find a merciful God who does not forget the covenant.

[[Has Any People Heard God and Lived]] (4:32–40) closes the first address. Moses challenges Israel to
search all history since creation for anything like Horeb and the exodus: a people that heard God from
the fire and lived, a nation taken out of another by signs and a mighty hand. It was shown so that
Israel would know *there is no one else*. Yahweh's love for the fathers is named for the first time as
the reason for the choice, and the confession leads straight to the charge to keep the statutes.

The full plan below is provisional beyond the checked opening unit. It follows discourse and
subject changes, joining across chapter boundaries where a unit continues.

## Structure

- 1:1–4:43 — introduction, journey recalled, and exhortation.
- 4:44–11:32 — covenant teaching, Horeb recalled, and the call to love and remember.
- 12–26 — instructions for worship, justice, community, and life in the land.
- 27–30 — covenant ceremony, blessings, curses, and return.
- 31–34 — succession, preservation of instruction, song, blessing, and Moses’ death.
