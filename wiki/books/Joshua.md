# Joshua

## Overview

The book opens on the sentence Deuteronomy closed with: *Moses my servant is dead* (1:2). What
Moses was shown from Nebo, his attendant is now told to cross into and give out. Joshua carries
the Torah's promise of land into narrative: crossing the Jordan, the fall of Jericho and Ai, the
southern and northern campaigns, the distribution of the land by lot, and two farewell
assemblies that put the covenant choice back to Israel. Its own verdict is double — *Nothing
failed of any good thing which Yahweh had spoken* (21:45) and also *there remains yet very much
land to be possessed* (13:1), with tribe after tribe that *didn't drive out* the inhabitants.

[[After the Death of Moses]] (1:1–9) is Yahweh's first word to Joshua. The land is given in the
borders of Deuteronomy 11:24; the presence promised Moses is promised his successor; and the
thrice-repeated *be strong and courageous* is aimed, in its central instance, at keeping the
written *book of the law* — meditated *day and night*, not turned from right or left. Courage in
this book is first of all obedience to a text.

[[The Eastern Tribes Pledge to Cross]] (1:10–18) shows the charge taking hold. Joshua sends the
order down through the officers, holds Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh to Moses' word in the
language of Deuteronomy 3:18–20 — their rest already given, their brothers' still to come — and
receives a pledge of obedience *as … to Moses*, with death for rebellion and Yahweh's own *Only be
strong and courageous* returned to him.

[[Rahab and the Spies]] (2:1–24) brings testimony from inside Jericho: Rahab’s confession and
protection of the spies lead to an oath for her household, and the spies carry her confidence back
to Joshua. The land’s frightened inhabitants now supply the encouragement Israel needs.

[[Crossing the Jordan]] (3:1–17) brings the nation across on dry ground, with the covenant ark leading
and its priests standing in the river. The event confirms Yahweh’s presence with Joshua and
among Israel; conquest remains ahead.

The crossing receives a memorial in [[The Twelve Stones from the Jordan]] (4:1–24).
Stones at Gilgal prompt future children’s questions, Joshua’s authority is confirmed, and
the eastern tribes’ pledge becomes an armed crossing. The explanation joins Jordan to Red Sea
and aims at Israel’s enduring fear and all peoples’ knowledge of Yahweh.

[[The Second Circumcision at Gilgal]] (5:1–9) pauses the advance for circumcision and healing.
The children receive the sign omitted during the wilderness, while the fate of their fathers
recalls judgment within the continuing land promise. Yahweh declares Egypt’s reproach removed.

[[Passover at Gilgal and the End of the Manna]] (5:10–12) records Passover in the land and the cessation of manna
after Israel eats its produce. Wilderness provision gives way to food from Canaan before
Jericho is captured.

[[The Commander of Yahweh's Army]] (5:13–15) places Joshua before the armed commander of Yahweh's army.
He asks for instruction and removes his shoes on holy ground, recalling Moses' encounter
at the bush before the conquest narrative begins.

[[The Fall of Jericho]] (6:1–27) narrates the first city’s destruction, the rescue of Rahab’s
household under oath, and the reservation of metals for Yahweh. Victory carries a warning:
taking devoted property would bring the sanction upon Israel’s own camp.

[[The Sin of Achan]] (7:1–26) makes the warning good. One man of Judah takes a robe, silver, and
gold; Israel is routed at Ai, and Yahweh says Israel itself has *become devoted for destruction*.
The selection narrows to Achan, who confesses — *I saw … I coveted them and took them* — and is
stoned and burned with his household and goods in the valley of Achor, *trouble*, under a heap
of stones. The conquest's first defeat comes from inside the camp.

[[The Capture of Ai]] (8:1–29) answers it. Yahweh speaks first again — *Don't be afraid, and don't
be dismayed*, Moses' words at Kadesh — names an ambush, and turns the rout into the bait. Joshua
holds out the javelin until the city is destroyed, Ai becomes *a heap forever*, and its king lies
under a second heap of stones at the gate. This time the goods and livestock are Israel's by
command.

[[The Law Read at Mount Ebal]] (8:30–35) turns from conquest to altar, inscription, and public reading.
Moses’ written instruction governs Joshua’s leadership, and the whole assembly hears blessing
and curse, including women, little ones, and foreigners.

[[The Gibeonite Deception]] (9:1–27) exposes a failure to seek counsel after the public reading of
the law. A treaty obtained by deception conflicts with the prohibition of covenants with the land’s
peoples; the sworn protection nevertheless preserves Gibeon’s communities under imposed service.

[[The Sun Stands Still at Gibeon]] (10:1–15) turns Gibeon’s treaty into a military commitment.
Joshua rescues the threatened city; hail and the delayed sunset accompany Yahweh’s promised victory.
The return notice at 10:15 stands beside the resumed action at Makkedah and the return at 10:43.

[[The Five Kings in the Cave at Makkedah]] (10:16–27) completes the five kings’ story: imprisonment, public
subjection, execution, display, and burial in their hiding cave. Joshua passes the courage charge
to his commanders; the narrative also preserves a remnant escaping into fortified cities.

[[The Southern Cities Taken]] (10:28–43) extends the victory through six cities and a defeated
relief force. The summary attributes success to Yahweh, while its comprehensive language
must remain alongside later reports of remaining land and renewed action at Hebron and Debir.

[[The Northern Kings at the Waters of Merom]] (11:1–15) brings the northern coalition’s defeat, the burning of Hazor,
and the destruction of inhabitants while other cities and plunder remain. The narrator
closes by tracing Joshua’s obedience to Yahweh’s commands through Moses.

[[The Whole Land Taken]] (11:16–23) summarizes the conquest from Mount Halak to Baal Gad under
Hermon. The war lasted *a long time*; no city but Gibeon made peace, because *it was of Yahweh to
harden their hearts*; the Anakim are cut off, with a remnant in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod. It ends
with *the whole land* given by tribes and *rest from war* — a verdict that 13:1 at once qualifies.

[[The Kings Struck Down]] (12:1–24) closes the conquest half of the book with a register. Moses'
two kings east of the Jordan, Sihon and Og, stand beside Joshua's thirty-one in the west, each
side introduced by the same formula and closed by the same gift of land. The western kings are
unnamed and counted one by one; more than a dozen have no battle in the narrative, and several
of their cities — Jerusalem, Gezer, Taanach, Megiddo, Dor — reappear in the allotment as places
not driven out. A king struck is not a city held.

## Structure

- 1–5 — commission and entry: the charge, Rahab and the spies, the Jordan crossed, Gilgal.
- 6–12 — conquest: Jericho, Achan, Ai, Ebal, Gibeon, the southern and northern campaigns, the
  list of kings.
- 13–21 — allotment: the land remaining, the east bank, Caleb, Judah, Joseph, Shiloh, the
  other tribes, refuge and Levitical cities, and the summary that no promise failed.
- 22–24 — return and renewal: the eastern tribes sent home and their altar, Joshua's farewell,
  the covenant at Shechem, and three burials.
