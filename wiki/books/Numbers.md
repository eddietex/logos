# Numbers

## Overview

[[Drive Out the Inhabitants]] (33:50–56) turns from the itinerary to commands for entry: removal of
inhabitants and idols, possession and allotment, and a warning that Israel can face the same
judgment. The land gift does not make the recipients immune from dispossession.

[[The Stages of the Journey]] (33:1–49) gathers the journey from Egypt to Moab into a written itinerary
attributed to Moses at Yahweh’s command. Aaron’s date and age at death interrupt the stages;
the route is preserved without resolving every difference from the earlier narratives.

[[Reuben, Gad, and the Land Beyond the Jordan]] (32:1–42) has Reuben and Gad, rich in livestock, ask to settle the conquered land east of
the Jordan. Moses hears Kadesh again — *Shall your brothers go to the war, and shall you sit here?* — and
binds them to cross armed *before Yahweh* until every tribe has its inheritance: *be sure your sin will
find you out*. Gad, Reuben, and half of Manasseh receive Sihon's and Og's kingdoms.

[[The War Against Midian]] (31:1–54) carries out the command of 25:17 as Moses' last campaign:
*Yahweh's vengeance on Midian*, five kings and Balaam killed, and Peor laid to *the counsel of Balaam*.
Moses' order to kill the boys and the women who had lain with a man is reported without comment. Most of
the chapter is aftermath: purification, the plunder halved, and the officers' gold laid up as a memorial.

[[Vows of Men and Women]] (30:1–16) follows *besides your vows* (29:39) with the vow law: a man shall not
break his word; a woman's vow in her father's or husband's house stands if he holds his peace and is void
if he forbids it on the day he hears, and Yahweh forgives her. A widow's or divorced woman's vow stands.

[[The Offerings of the Appointed Feasts]] (28:16–29:40) completes the calendar: the animals for
Unleavened Bread, the first fruits, and the seventh month's first, tenth, and fifteenth days, with
seventy bulls across the week of tents. Every set is *besides the continual burnt offering*. On the
first fruits it disagrees with Leviticus 23:18, and the wiki records both.

[[Daily, Sabbath, and New Moon Offerings]] (28:1–15) opens the offering calendar for the counted generation: two
lambs every day, *ordained in Mount Sinai*, two more each Sabbath, and bulls, a ram, lambs, and a
sin-offering goat each new month — each layer *besides the continual burnt offering*. Yahweh calls the
offerings *my food*.

[[Joshua Commissioned]] (27:12–23) restates the Meribah sentence on Moses and answers his request
for a shepherd over the congregation. Joshua, *a man in whom is the Spirit*, is commissioned before
Eleazar, who will inquire for him by the Urim; leadership passes without Moses' direct access.


[[The Petition of Zelophehad's Daughters]] (27:1–11) turns the census notice of a sonless family
into a ruling: daughters inherit before paternal collateral relatives when no son survives.
The public petition is affirmed and becomes a general succession statute.


[[The Second Census]] (26:1–65) counts the new military generation and prepares land
allotment. Levi is separately counted; the closing notice confirms the wilderness sentence
with Caleb and Joshua as exceptions. Named survivors qualify indiscriminate accounts of judgment.


[[Baal Peor and the Zeal of Phinehas]] (25:1–18) follows the blessings with apostasy and plague.
Phinehas’s intervention stops the plague and receives a covenant of peace and enduring
priesthood; the final command turns toward conflict with Midian.


[[Balaam's Last Oracles]] (Numbers 23:27–24:25) completes the attempted curse with a third blessing,
then future oracles of royal victory and the downfall of nations. The Spirit’s gift and the
seer’s constrained speech do not erase his earlier rebuke; historical identifications remain open.


[[Balaam's First Two Oracles]] (Numbers 22:36–23:26) turns the attempted curse into two blessings.
Repeated sacrifices and a changed viewpoint cannot reverse God’s commitment; the oracle’s
claims about repentance and iniquity remain explicitly compared with earlier narratives.


[[Balaam's Donkey]] (22:22–35) interrupts the permitted journey with a deadly encounter.
The donkey sees and avoids the angel before Balaam does; his eyes are opened, his way rebuked,
and his onward speech restricted again to the given word.

[[Balak Sends for Balaam]] (22:1–21) moves the conflict from battle to attempted cursing. Balak’s
embassies cannot override Israel’s blessed status; Balaam departs with permission restricted to
God’s word.

[[Sihon and Og]] (21:21–35) moves from requested transit to possession of Amorite territory. Sihon’s earlier conquest of Moab supplies the historical background; Yahweh’s assurance before Og frames the second victory. The quoted Heshbon poem preserves a different voice within the conquest narrative.

[[The Journey to Moab]] (21:10–20) follows the camps toward Moab, cites the Book of the
Wars of Yahweh, and records the well song at Beer. Promised water and the leaders’
digging stand together; the unit ends before the confrontation with Sihon.

[[The Brass Serpent]] (21:4–9) joins deadly judgment to rescue on the detour around Edom.
Moses intercedes for his accusers; the bitten person who looks at the commanded sign lives.

[[Arad and Hormah]] (21:1–3) turns an attack and captivity into a vow and victory.
Yahweh hears Israel; the Canaanites and their cities are destroyed, and the place
is named Hormah, recalling the earlier defeat while leaving the naming tension open.

[[The Death of Aaron]] (20:22–29) brings the Meribah sentence to its consequence for Aaron.
His garments pass to Eleazar on Mount Hor, and Israel mourns for thirty days; priestly
service continues beyond the death of its first holder.


[[Edom Refuses Passage]] (20:14–21) turns from water within the camp to access through Edom.
Kinship, remembered rescue, and guarantees of payment do not secure passage; Israel
withdraws before armed resistance.


[[The Waters of Meribah]] (20:1–13) records Miriam’s death at Kadesh and a water crisis.
Water is supplied, but Moses and Aaron are barred from bringing Israel into the land
for failing to believe and sanctify Yahweh before the people.

[[The Red Heifer]] (19:1–22) supplies purification from human-death contamination:
stored ashes and water restore cleanness, while refusal of purification defiles the sanctuary.
The rite’s preparers themselves undergo temporary uncleanness.

[[The Duties and Portions of Priests and Levites]] (18:1–32)
joins sanctuary responsibility to material provision: priestly portions and Levite tithes
support assigned service, while the Levites themselves give the best tenth to Aaron.

[[Aaron's Rod Buds]] (17:1–13) confirms Aaron’s selection through a rod bearing buds, blossoms,
and almonds. Retained as a warning to prevent death, it leaves Israel asking fearfully how
anyone can approach the sanctuary and live.

[[Aaron Stands Between the Dead and the Living]] (16:41–50) turns the defended
priesthood toward rescue: Aaron’s incense stops the plague among his accusers, though 14,700
have died. The repeated complaint leads to atonement as well as judgment.


[[The Rebellion of Korah]] (16:1–40) joins a challenge to priestly access with an accusation
of failed leadership. Intercession preserves the larger congregation, earth and fire judge the
rebels, and their censers become an altar memorial guarding Aaron’s priesthood.

[[The Tassels]] (15:37–41) adds a visible reminder: looking at garment fringes is to lead
to remembering and doing all the commandments, with holiness grounded in the God of the exodus.

[[The Sabbath Breaker]] (15:32–36) brings a concrete case after the high-hand law:
a man gathering sticks is held for a ruling, then stoned outside the camp as commanded.
His motive is not narrated, and the precise uncertainty despite the earlier Sabbath death
sanction is not explained.

Numbers takes Israel from the camp at Sinai to the plains of Moab. It opens with the army counted
around the tabernacle and closes with the next generation counted on the edge of the land. Between
those two censuses the first generation refuses the land and dies in the wilderness, as 26:64–65
says outright. The Hebrew name of the book is taken from its first verse, *in the wilderness*.

[[The Census in the Wilderness of Sinai]] (1:1–54) sets the frame. A month after the tabernacle was raised, Moses and Aaron
count 603,550 men of fighting age by tribe, and the Levites are set apart to carry and guard the
tabernacle instead. Israel is arranged as a marching army with a sanctuary at its center. [[The Camp Around the Tent]] (2:1–34) then gives
every tribe a side and a turn, four camps of three around the Tent, in one order for rest and march.
[[The Generations of Aaron and the Levites Given]] (3:1–13) names the priestly line, cut to two sons,
and gives the tribe of Levi to Aaron, taken by Yahweh in place of every firstborn in Israel.
[[The Levites and the Firstborn Counted]] (3:14–51) numbers
the Levites from a month old, gives each house its side and charge, and sets 22,273 firstborn against
them, with five shekels each for the 273 left over.
[[The Service of Kohath, Gershon, and Merari]] (4:1–49) then counts the working men of thirty to
fifty and gives each house its load; the priests must wrap the holy things first, so that the
Kohathites who carry them *may live, and not die*.
[[The Unclean Sent Out and Wrongs Repaid]] (5:1–10) then clears the camp of the unclean, because Yahweh dwells in it, and requires the
guilty to confess and repay with a fifth added.
[[The Suspected Wife]] (5:11–31) takes a husband's unprovable jealousy to the priest, where an oath, a
curse written into water, and the outcome are left to Yahweh.
[[The Nazirite Vow]] (6:1–21) opens holiness to either man or woman by a vow: nothing from the vine, no
razor, and no contact with the dead, even for a parent, until the days are fulfilled and the hair goes
on the fire.
[[Yahweh Bless You and Keep You]] (6:22–27) closes the section with the words Aaron and his sons are to
speak: three lines, each naming Yahweh, by which they put his name on Israel and he blesses.
[[The Offerings of the Twelve Princes]] (7:1–89) then steps back to the day the tabernacle was finished: six
wagons for the Levites, twelve identical gifts over twelve days in camp order, and Moses hearing the voice
from between the cherubim.
[[The Lamps and the Levites Cleansed]] (8:1–26) has Aaron light the lamps, then has the Levites shaved,
cleansed, and offered as a wave offering for Israel, to serve from twenty-five to fifty.
[[The Passover in the Second Year]] (9:1–14)
keeps the feast at Sinai, and a question from men unclean by the dead yields a second date in the second month.
[[The Cloud over the Tabernacle]] (9:15–23) then gives the rule of every journey before the first one starts:
the camp moves when the cloud rises, stays while it stays, and calls both *the commandment of Yahweh*.
[[The Two Silver Trumpets]] (10:1–10) puts that rule into sound: signals for assembly and march, a statute for the
priests, and a memorial before God in the wars and feasts of the land.
[[The March from Sinai]] (10:11–36) then moves at last: the cloud rises, the twelve tribes go in order with the tabernacle in two
stages, Hobab is asked to be Israel's eyes, and Moses cries out as the ark sets out and rests.
[[The Fire at Taberah]] (11:1–3) follows at once: an unspecified complaint, Yahweh's fire at the edge of the camp, a cry to Moses,
and a prayer with no recorded words.
[[The Seventy Elders and the Quail]] (11:4–35) sets two complaints side by side: the people crave meat, Moses says the burden is too heavy and asks to die,
and Yahweh shares the Spirit on Moses with seventy elders, sends quail on a wind, and strikes with a plague at the graves of craving.
[[Miriam and Aaron Against Moses]] (12:1–16) brings the dispute into the family: Yahweh sets Moses above the prophets, *mouth to mouth*, Miriam is shut out seven days, and the people reach Paran.
[[The Twelve Spies]] (13:1–33) sends a man from each tribe to search the land for forty days; they bring back a cluster on a pole, Caleb says *we are well able*, and the others bring an evil report that ends in *grasshoppers*.
[[The People Refuse the Land]] (14:1–38) gives the answer: the people weep and propose a captain to take them back to Egypt, Moses turns down a second offer of a new nation and wins pardon,
and Yahweh swears that the counted generation will die in the wilderness, forty years for forty days, while Caleb, Joshua, and the little ones go in.
[[The Defeat at Hormah]] (14:39–45) reverses it: the people say *we have sinned*, go up without the ark or Moses against his warning, and are beaten down by the Amalekite and the Canaanite.
[[Offerings in the Land and Sins of Ignorance]] (15:1–31) answers with law for the land they were just refused: flour, oil, and wine with every animal, the first of the dough, and offerings for error, with one law for native and stranger, and no offering for the one who sins *with a high hand*.

The book mixes narrative with law, and it is not in strict date order: 7:1 and 9:1 go back to the first
month. It names Moses as the writer of the journey list (33:2), but says nothing about the book as a
whole.

## Structure

- **At Sinai (1:1–10:10)** — census, camp, Levites, purity, dedication, and the signals for moving.
- **Sinai to Kadesh (10:11–12:16)** — the march, and the complaints that begin at once.
- **Kadesh and the wilderness years (13:1–19:22)** — the spies, the refusal, the sentence, Korah,
  and the laws around them.
- **Kadesh to Moab (20:1–25:18)** — Meribah, the deaths of Miriam and Aaron, the Transjordan
  victories, Balaam, and Baal Peor.
- **The plains of Moab (26:1–36:13)** — the second census, inheritance, offerings, Midian, the
  eastern tribes, and the borders of the land.
