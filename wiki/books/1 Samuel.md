# 1 Samuel

## Overview

1 Samuel takes Israel from the last of the judges to its first king. It opens on a barren woman at Shiloh
and closes on a dead king on the wall of Beth Shan. Three lives fill it, overlapping: [[Samuel]], the
prophet and last judge, asked of Yahweh and given back to him; [[Saul]], the king the people ask for; and
[[David]], the shepherd anointed while Saul still reigns. In the English canon it follows [[Ruth]], which
ended on the name *David* (Ruth 4:22). Judges had ended *In those days there was no king in Israel*
(Judges 21:25). This book narrates how one came, and at what cost. In the Hebrew Bible, 1 and 2 Samuel are
one book; the division is reported here, not argued.

[[Hannah Asks Yahweh for a Son]] (1:1–28) opens the book in a family, as Ruth did, not at a battle. Elkanah
of the hill country of Ephraim has two wives: Peninnah has children, and Hannah, whom he loves, has none,
because *Yahweh had shut up her womb* (1:5). Year after year at Shiloh, her rival provokes her. Elkanah asks
*Am I not better to you than ten sons?* (1:8). Hannah prays silently at the doorpost of the temple and vows
that if Yahweh will *look at the affliction of your servant* and give her a son, she will give him to Yahweh
*all the days of his life* (1:11). Eli the priest takes her for drunk, then blesses her. *Yahweh remembered
her* (1:19), and she names the boy Samuel, *Because I have asked him of Yahweh* (1:20). Once he is weaned,
she brings him to Eli at Shiloh: *As long as he lives he is given to Yahweh* (1:28). The verb *šāʾal*,
*ask*, runs through the chapter. Its passive participle, *šāʾûl*, *asked*, is spelled with the same
letters as the name of the king the people will ask for (1:28; 9:2, WLC).

[[The Song of Hannah]] (2:1–11) turns the private story into a hymn. Hannah *prayed, and said*, and asks
for nothing. She names no one in her own story; *the barren has borne seven* (2:5) is one reversal in a
list that runs through soldiers, the hungry, the dead, and the poor raised *out of the dust* to *the
throne of glory* (2:4–8). She takes the Rock and *Yahweh kills, and makes alive* from the Song of Moses
(Deuteronomy 32:4, 39), and shares a line with Psalm 113:7–8. The song ends on *his king* and *his
anointed* (2:10), the first royal use of *māšîaḥ* in the books stored so far, sung before Israel has a
king. David's song near the end of 2 Samuel closes on the same pair (22:51), and the two are commonly
read as framing the books. Elkanah goes home, and *the child served Yahweh before Eli the priest* (2:11).

[[The Sins of Eli's Sons]] (2:12–26) sets the proud of the song before the reader. Eli's sons, *wicked
men* who *didn't know Yahweh* (2:12), the words used of Pharaoh (Exodus 5:2), take the worshippers' meat
by fork and by force before the fat is burned for Yahweh (2:13–16): *the men despised the offering of
Yahweh* (2:17). They lie with the women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting (2:22). Eli rebukes
them, *if a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?*, and they do not listen, *because Yahweh
intended to kill them* (2:25). The narrative alternates the sons with the boy: Samuel ministers in a linen
ephod, Hannah brings him a robe each year and bears five more children, and *the child Samuel grew on, and
increased in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men* (2:18–21, 26). The house brought low and the child
raised up stand at the same altar.

[[A Man of God Against the House of Eli]] (2:27–36) brings the sentence. An unnamed man of God recalls
that Yahweh chose Eli's ancestral house in Egypt to serve his altar, and asks *Why do you kick at my
sacrifice … and honor your sons above me?* (2:29). The promise that the house would *walk before me
forever* is withdrawn: *those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me will be cursed* (2:30).
No one in the house will grow old; both sons will die *in one day* as the sign (2:34); and Yahweh will
*raise me up a faithful priest* with *a sure house*, who *will walk before my anointed forever* (2:35).
The sign falls at Aphek (4:11), and Kings records the end of the sentence when Solomon removes Abiathar,
*that he might fulfill Yahweh's word, which he spoke concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh* (1 Kings 2:27).

[[Yahweh Calls Samuel]] (3:1–4:1a) turns from the stranger's oracle to the child's call. *Yahweh's word was
precious in those days* (3:1). At night, with Eli's eyes growing dim and *God's lamp* not yet out, Yahweh
calls Samuel where the ark is, and three times the boy runs to Eli, because he *didn't yet know Yahweh*
(3:2–7). Eli understands and teaches him the answer, *Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears* (3:9). The
first word Samuel receives is the sentence on Eli's house, confirmed with an oath that its iniquity *shall
not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever* (3:14); Eli, hearing it from the boy, says *It is
Yahweh* (3:18). Yahweh lets *none of his words fall to the ground*; *all Israel from Dan even to
Beersheba* knows Samuel as *a prophet of Yahweh*; and *the word of Samuel came to all Israel* (3:19–4:1a).

[[The Ark Is Captured]] (4:1b–22) carries the sentence out. Israel camps at Ebenezer against the Philistines
at Aphek, loses four thousand men, and the elders, asking *Why has Yahweh defeated us?*, send to Shiloh for
*the ark of Yahweh's covenant … that it may come among us, and save us* (4:3). Hophni and Phinehas come with
it. Israel's *great shout* frightens the Philistines, who know these are *the gods that struck the
Egyptians*, but they fight and win: thirty thousand fall, *God's ark was taken; and the two sons of Eli,
Hophni and Phinehas, were slain* (4:11), the sign of 2:34 fulfilled. A Benjamite runner brings the news to
Shiloh; at the mention of the ark Eli falls backward from his seat and dies, *an old man, and heavy*, having
*judged Israel forty years* (4:18). Phinehas' wife dies in childbirth, naming her son Ichabod: *The glory has
departed from Israel; for God's ark has been taken* (4:22). Samuel does not appear in the chapter.

[[The Ark Among the Philistines]] (5:1–12) follows the glory into exile. The Philistines set the ark *by
Dagon* in his house at Ashdod, and on two mornings Dagon is found *fallen on his face to the ground before
Yahweh's ark*, the second time with his head and hands *cut off on the threshold* (5:3–4). Then *Yahweh's
hand was heavy on the people of Ashdod*, with tumors (5:6); the ark is carried to Gath, which suffers *a
very great confusion*, and on to Ekron, which cries that it has come *to kill us and our people* (5:9–10).
The lords decide to send it *to its own place*, and *the cry of the city went up to heaven* (5:11–12). The
*heavy* hand, *kāḇēḏ*, shares its root with the *glory*, *kāḇôḏ*, that had departed (4:21–22, WLC), and the
tumors and *confusion* are nouns of Deuteronomy's curses (7:23; 28:20, 27). No Israelite appears in the
chapter.

[[The Ark Returns to Israel]] (6:1–7:1) sends it home. After *seven months* the Philistine priests and
diviners advise that the ark must not go back *empty* but with *a trespass offering*, five golden tumors and
five golden mice, that the lords may *give glory to the God of Israel*, and they warn against hardening their
hearts *as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened their hearts* (6:3–6). They test the cause: unyoked milk cows,
their calves shut up at home, either go to Beth Shemesh or prove *It was a chance* (6:7–9). The cows go
straight, *lowing as they went* (6:12). Beth Shemesh rejoices, the Levites set the ark on a great stone, and
the cows become a burnt offering (6:13–15); then Yahweh strikes its men *because they had looked into
Yahweh's ark* (6:19). *Who is able to stand before Yahweh, this holy God?* (6:20). The ark is fetched to
Kiriath Jearim, to Abinadab's house *on the hill*, and his son Eleazar is consecrated to keep it (7:1). It
does not return to Shiloh.

[[Samuel Judges Israel at Mizpah]] (7:2–17) closes the ark narrative with Samuel. After *twenty years*, *all
the house of Israel lamented after Yahweh* (7:2), and Samuel, silent since his call, makes return conditional:
*put away the foreign gods and the Ashtaroth … and serve him only* (7:3). They do; at Mizpah they pour out water
before Yahweh, fast, and confess *We have sinned against Yahweh*, and *Samuel judged the children of Israel*
(7:6). When the Philistines come up, Samuel offers a suckling lamb and *cried to Yahweh for Israel; and Yahweh
answered him*: *Yahweh thundered with a great thunder … and confused them* (7:9–10), as Hannah sang (2:10). He
names a stone *Ebenezer*, *Yahweh helped us until now*, the name of the camp where the ark was lost (4:1; 7:12).
The Philistines are *subdued*, the verb of the judges' victories, and *Yahweh's hand was against the Philistines
all the days of Samuel* (7:13), a summary the Philistine wars of chapters 9–14 do not obviously fit. The chapter
ends on a judge's notice: a yearly circuit of Bethel, Gilgal, and Mizpah, and a house and altar at Ramah
(7:15–17). Deliverance has come without a king, and the next chapter asks for one.

[[Israel Asks for a King]] (8:1–22) makes the request. *When Samuel was old, he made his sons judges*, and Joel
and Abijah, at Beersheba, *took bribes, and perverted justice* (8:1–3), as Eli's sons had abused the altar.
*All the elders of Israel* come to Ramah: *make us a king to judge us like all the nations* (8:5), the words
of Deuteronomy 17:14. It displeases Samuel; Yahweh tells him three times to *listen to their voice*, *for they
have not rejected you, but they have rejected me as the king over them* (8:7). Samuel describes *the way of the
king*, *mišpaṭ hammeleḵ*, in one verb, *he will take*: sons, daughters, the best fields, a tenth of seed and
flock, *and you will be his servants*; *you will cry out in that day because of your king whom you will have
chosen*, *and Yahweh will not answer you* (8:11–18), the reverse of Mizpah's *Yahweh answered him* (7:9). The
people refuse: *we will have a king … that our king may judge us, and go out before us, and fight our battles*
(8:19–20). Yahweh says *make them a king*, and Samuel sends everyone home (8:22).

[[Saul Comes to the Seer]] (9:1–25) brings the king in from Benjamin, looking for donkeys. Kish, *a mighty man of
valor*, has a son, Saul, *an impressive young man*, taller than all the people (9:1–2). Sent after his father's lost
donkeys, Saul searches four districts and is ready to turn back when his servant proposes *a man of God* in the city:
*All that he says surely happens* (9:6). The narrator explains that *he who is now called a prophet was before called
a seer* (9:9). Young women going out to draw water, as at the wells of Genesis, send them on to the sacrifice at the
high place (9:11–13). The day before, Yahweh had told Samuel: *Tomorrow about this time I will send you a man out of
the land of Benjamin, and you shall anoint him to be prince over my people Israel … for I have looked upon my people,
because their cry has come to me* (9:16), the words of the burning bush (Exodus 3:7, 9). Saul asks Samuel where the
seer lives; Samuel tells him the donkeys are found and *all that is desirable in Israel* is for him. Saul answers as
Gideon did: *Am I not a Benjamite, of the smallest of the tribes of Israel?* (9:21; Judges 6:15). Samuel seats him at
the head of thirty guests and gives him the portion *kept for you* (9:22–24). The king the people asked for is also
the one Yahweh sends, and the chapter does not say how the two fit.

[[Samuel Anoints Saul]] (9:26–10:16) does in private what chapter 9 prepared. At dawn, at the edge of the city, with
the servant sent ahead, Samuel pours *the vial of oil* on Saul's head, kisses him, and asks, *Hasn't Yahweh anointed
you to be prince over his inheritance?* (10:1). He gives three signs: two men at Rachel's tomb with news of the
donkeys, three pilgrims to Bethel who give him two loaves, and a band of prophets at *the hill of God, where the
garrison of the Philistines is*, among whom *Yahweh's Spirit will come mightily on you … and you will be turned into
another man* (10:2–6). He tells Saul to *do what is appropriate for the occasion; for God is with you*, and also to
wait seven days for him at Gilgal (10:7–8), and the text does not say how the two orders fit. *God gave him another
heart*, the signs all happen, and Saul prophesies, so that people ask *Is Saul also among the prophets?* (10:9–12),
a proverb the book will explain a second time (19:24). Asked by his uncle what Samuel said, Saul tells him only
about the donkeys: *concerning the matter of the kingdom … he didn't tell him* (10:16).

[[Saul Chosen by Lot at Mizpah]] (10:17–27) makes public what the anointing kept private. Samuel calls the people
*to Yahweh to Mizpah*, recalls the exodus, and charges them: *you have today rejected your God, who himself saves
you* (10:18–19). Then he has them stand before Yahweh by tribes. Benjamin is *chosen*, then the family of the
Matrites, then *Saul the son of Kish*, with the verb that found Achan in Joshua 7. But Saul *could not be found*,
and Yahweh answers that *he has hidden himself among the baggage* (10:20–22). Brought out, he is taller than all
the people, and Samuel asks *Do you see him whom Yahweh has chosen …?*, the language of Deuteronomy 17:15. The
people shout *Long live the king!* (10:23–24). Samuel writes *the regulations of the kingdom* in a book and lays
it up before Yahweh (10:25). Saul goes home to Gibeah with men *whose hearts God had touched*, while *worthless
fellows*, the phrase once used of Gibeah's men (Judges 19:22), ask *How could this man save us?* *But he held his
peace* (10:26–27). The next chapter answers their question.

[[Saul Rescues Jabesh Gilead]] (11:1–15) gives the answer. Nahash the Ammonite besieges Jabesh Gilead and offers
terms: *that all your right eyes be gouged out. I will make this dishonor all Israel* (11:2). The elders ask for
seven days to find someone *to save us* (11:3). Their messengers reach *Gibeah of Saul*, and Saul, coming in
behind his oxen, hears them; *God's Spirit came mightily on Saul … and his anger burned hot* (11:6), the verb and
clause of Samson (Judges 14:19). He cuts a yoke of oxen in pieces and sends them *throughout all the borders of
Israel*, as the Levite of Gibeah had sent his concubine (Judges 19:29), and *the dread of Yahweh* brings Israel
out *as one man* (11:7). In three companies, in the morning watch, Saul breaks the Ammonite camp (11:11). The
people want the doubters put to death; Saul refuses, *for today Yahweh has rescued Israel* (11:13). At Gilgal they
*renew the kingdom* and make Saul king *before Yahweh* (11:14–15), the third time he is made king. The town Israel
destroyed at the end of Judges is the first town its king saves. Samuel's farewell will name Nahash as the reason
the people asked for a king (12:12), against the order of chapters 8–11.

[[Samuel's Farewell Address]] (12:1–25) closes the section. Samuel begins *I have listened to your voice* (12:1),
the words Yahweh had told him to obey, and puts his own career on trial: *Whose ox have I taken? Whose donkey …?*
The people clear him, with Yahweh and *his anointed*, the king, as witnesses (12:3–5). Then he pleads with them
*concerning all the righteous acts of Yahweh*: Moses and Aaron, Sisera, the Philistines, Moab, and the deliverers
*Jerubbaal, Bedan, Jephthah, and Samuel* (12:6–11). When Nahash came, they said *a king shall reign over us; when
Yahweh your God was your king* (12:12). King and people stand under one condition (12:14–15). At wheat harvest
Samuel calls, *Yahweh sent thunder and rain*, the idiom of the hail in Egypt (Exodus 9:23), and the people ask him
to pray *that we not die* (12:17–19). He answers *Don't be afraid*: *Yahweh will not forsake his people for his
great name's sake*, and *far be it from me that I should sin against Yahweh in ceasing to pray for you* (12:20–23).
The speech shares its form with Joshua's farewells (Joshua 23–24), and ends *you will be consumed, both you and
your king* (12:25).

[[Saul's Sacrifice at Gilgal]] (13:1–15) opens the account of the reign and forfeits its dynasty. The regnal
formula of 13:1 lacks its numbers in the Hebrew, *a son of a year … and two years he reigned* (WLC); the WEB
supplies *thirty* and *forty-two*. Jonathan, named here for the first time, strikes the Philistine garrison at
Geba, and *all Israel heard that Saul had struck* it (13:3–4). The Philistines camp at Michmash *as the sand which
is on the seashore*, and Israel hides in caves and pits or crosses the Jordan (13:5–7). At Gilgal Saul waits *seven
days, according to the time set by Samuel*, the order of 10:8; Samuel does not come, the people scatter, and Saul
offers the burnt offering. Samuel arrives as he finishes: *What have you done?* Saul gives his reasons, *I haven't
entreated the favor of Yahweh. I forced myself* (13:11–12). *You have done foolishly. You have not kept the
commandment of Yahweh your God*; the kingdom that *would have* been established *forever* *will not continue*, and
Yahweh *has sought for himself a man after his own heart* as *prince over his people* (13:13–14). The text names
the commandment, not the altar, as the fault. Saul keeps the throne and loses the house, with six hundred men left
(13:15).

[[No Smith in Israel]] (13:16–23) pauses before the battle. Saul and Jonathan hold Geba; the Philistine *raiders*,
*hammašḥîṯ*, the word of *the destroyer* at the Passover (Exodus 12:23, WLC), go out from Michmash in three
companies and meet no one (13:17–18). The narrator explains why: *there was no blacksmith found throughout all the
land of Israel; for the Philistines said, "Lest the Hebrews make themselves swords or spears"* (13:19). Israel goes
down to the Philistines to have its plowshares and mattocks sharpened, at a stated price (13:20–21), and *in the day
of battle* only Saul and Jonathan carry sword or spear (13:22). The text names no metal; the pair *sword and spear*
returns in David's *Yahweh doesn't save with sword and spear* (17:47).

[[Jonathan's Attack at Michmash]] (14:1–23) is the battle the pause prepared. Jonathan and his armour-bearer go over
to the Philistine garrison without telling Saul, who sits under a pomegranate tree at Gibeah with six hundred men
and Ahijah, Eli's great-grandson, wearing the ephod (14:1–3). Jonathan's reason is *It may be that Yahweh will work
for us; for there is no restraint on Yahweh to save by many or by few* (14:6). He sets a sign, receives it in the
Philistines' taunt, climbs *on his hands and on his feet*, and twenty men fall (14:8–14). Then *the earth quaked*, a
*trembling of God* runs through the camp, and the Philistines turn their swords on each other (14:15, 20). Saul
counts the people, calls for *God's ark*, and breaks off the inquiry (14:17–19); deserters and men who had hidden
join the pursuit (14:21–22). The narrator closes in the words of Exodus 14:30: *So Yahweh saved Israel that day*
(14:23). The Masoretic *ark* of 14:18, while 7:2 places the ark at Kiriath Jearim, is an open question; the
Septuagint is commonly reported to read *ephod* there (recalled, not checked).

[[Saul's Rash Oath]] (14:24–46) turns the victory into a near-execution. Saul *had adjured the people*, *Cursed is the
man who eats any food until it is evening, and I am avenged of my enemies* (14:24), and the army goes hungry through
the pursuit. Jonathan, who *didn't hear*, tastes honey and says *My father has troubled the land* (14:27–29), with the
verb of Achan's sentence (Joshua 7:25). At evening the faint people eat plunder *with the blood*, against Leviticus
19:26; Saul stops it at a great stone and builds *the first altar that he built to Yahweh* (14:32–35). He asks God
whether to pursue by night, and *he didn't answer him that day* (14:37), as Samuel had warned (8:18). Saul swears the
guilty man will die *though it is in Jonathan my son*; the lot takes Jonathan, and Saul swears again (14:39–44). The
people answer with their own oath, *there shall not one hair of his head fall to the ground; for he has worked with
God today*, and *rescued*, *pāḏâ*, *Jonathan* (14:45). The pursuit is abandoned (14:46). Jephthah's daughter had died
under her father's vow (Judges 11:39); Jonathan is redeemed from his father's oath.

[[Saul's Wars and Household]] (14:47–52) steps out of the story to sum up the reign. *When Saul had taken the
kingdom*, *lāḵaḏ*, the verb by which the lot had taken him (10:20–21), he fought *on every side*, Moab, Ammon, Edom,
*the kings of* [[Zobah]], and the Philistines, and *wherever he turned himself, he defeated them*; he struck the
Amalekites and *delivered Israel out of the hands of those who plundered them*, the phrase of Judges 2:16
(14:47–48). Then his house: sons [[Jonathan (son of Saul)]], [[Ishvi (son of Saul)]], and [[Malchishua]];
daughters [[Merab]] and [[Michal]]; his wife [[Ahinoam (wife of Saul)]]; and his commander [[Abner]] son of [[Ner]],
his cousin by 14:51 with 9:1, though 1 Chronicles 8:33 makes Ner Kish's father (14:49–51). It ends on *severe war
against the Philistines all the days of Saul* and a king who takes every mighty man into his service (14:52), as
Samuel warned (8:11), and as David will be taken (16:18–22). Placed before chapter 15, the summary gives Saul's
reign its full military credit while the sentence of 13:13–14 stands; David's notice in 2 Samuel 8 uses the same
frame with Yahweh as the one who gives victory. See
[[Saul's Wars and David's Wars - 1 Samuel 14.47-52 and 2 Samuel 8]].

[[Saul Rejected over Amalek]] (15:1–35) removes the kingship itself. Samuel brings Yahweh's word, *I remember what
Amalek did to Israel … when he came up out of Egypt*, the charge of Deuteronomy 25:17, and the order: *strike Amalek,
and utterly destroy all that they have* (15:2–3). Saul spares the Kenites for their old *kindness*, strikes Amalek
*from Havilah as you go to Shur*, and takes Agag alive; *Saul and the people spared Agag, and the best*, *and were not
willing to utterly destroy them* (15:6–9). Yahweh tells Samuel *It grieves me that I have set up Saul to be king*,
*niḥamtî*, the word of the flood (15:11; Genesis 6:7, WLC). At Gilgal Saul says *I have performed the commandment of
Yahweh*, and Samuel hears *the bleating of the sheep* (15:13–14). Saul says the best was kept *to sacrifice*; Samuel
answers *to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams*, and *Because you have rejected
Yahweh's word, he has also rejected you from being king* (15:22–23). Saul confesses, *because I feared the people, and
obeyed their voice*, in Pharaoh's words, *please forgive my sin* (15:24–25; Exodus 10:16–17). The skirt of Samuel's
robe tears in his hand: *Yahweh has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today, and has given it to a neighbor of yours
who is better than you*, and *the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent* (15:27–29), in a chapter that ends
*Yahweh grieved that he had made Saul king* (15:35). Samuel kills Agag *before Yahweh in Gilgal* and does not see Saul
again while he lives (15:33–35). See [[The Torn Robe - 1 Samuel 15.27-28, 24.4-5, 28.17, and 1 Kings 11.29-31]].

[[Samuel Anoints David]] (16:1–13) finds the man the two sentences on Saul had left unnamed. Yahweh ends Samuel's
mourning, *How long will you mourn for Saul … ? Fill your horn with oil, and go*, and sends him to Jesse of
Bethlehem, *for I have provided a king for myself among his sons*, literally *I have seen*, *rāʾîṯî* (16:1, WLC).
Afraid of Saul, Samuel goes with a heifer and a real sacrifice as cover (16:2–5). He takes the eldest, Eliab, for
*Yahweh's anointed*, and is told *Don't look on his face, or on the height of his stature … for man looks at the
outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart* (16:6–7), the noun of 13:14's *after his own heart*. Seven sons
pass, and *Yahweh has not chosen these* (16:10). The youngest is *keeping the sheep*; brought in, he is *ruddy, with a
handsome face*, a word used elsewhere in the Hebrew files checked only of Esau at birth (Genesis 25:25). Samuel
anoints him with *the horn of oil … in the middle of his brothers*, and *Yahweh's Spirit came mightily on David from
that day forward*, the first time the narrative names him (16:11–13). He is anointed, not crowned; Saul reigns until
chapter 31. 16:10 and 17:12 give Jesse eight sons, 1 Chronicles 2:15 seven, with *David the seventh*; both counts are
recorded. See [[Ruddy - Esau and David]] and
[[Taken from Following the Sheep - 1 Samuel 16.11, 2 Samuel 7.8, and Psalm 78.70-72]].

[[David Plays the Harp for Saul]] (16:14–23) turns from the new anointed to the old. *Now Yahweh's Spirit departed
from Saul, and an evil spirit from Yahweh troubled him* (16:14). His servants propose a harpist, and one of them has
already *seen* the man: *a son of Jesse the Bethlehemite who is skillful in playing, a mighty man of valor, a man of
war, prudent in speech, and a handsome person; and Yahweh is with him* (16:18). David comes from the sheep with
Jesse's gift of bread, wine, and a young goat; Saul *loved him greatly*, makes him his armor bearer, and asks to keep
him (16:19–22). When David plays, *Saul was refreshed, and was well, and the evil spirit departed from him* (16:23).
One verb, *sārâ*, *departed*, opens and closes the pericope (WLC). Saul does not know whom he has taken into his
house. In 17:55–58 he will ask whose son David is; the two accounts are recorded, not reconciled. See
[[An Evil Spirit from God - Abimelech, Saul, and Ahab's Prophets]] and
[[The Harp of David - Jubal, the Prophets, and the Sweet Psalmist]].

[[Goliath Defies Israel]] (17:1–11) opens the Philistine war in Judah's lowland. The Philistines camp between Socoh
and Azekah, at Ephes Dammim; Saul and Israel camp in the valley of Elah; *there was a valley between them* (17:1–3).
Out comes *a champion … named Goliath, of Gath*, six cubits and a span, and the narrator itemizes his bronze, his
iron spearhead, and his shield bearer (17:4–7). *Champion* is *ʾîš habbēnayim*, *the man of the between* (WLC). He
offers single combat for servitude, *Choose a man for yourselves*, and *I defy the armies of Israel today!* (17:8–10).
*Saul and all Israel … were dismayed, and greatly afraid* (17:11). *Ḥāṯaṯ*, *dismayed*, occurs in the Hebrew files
checked otherwise only in the commands *Don't be afraid, neither be dismayed* of Deuteronomy and Joshua (WLC). Neither
*Yahweh* nor *God* is named in the pericope. The king asked for to *go out before us, and fight our battles* (8:20),
taller than all the people (9:2), hears and is afraid. 2 Samuel 21:19 in the Hebrew has Elhanan kill Goliath the
Gittite; the WEB adds *brother* from 1 Chronicles 20:5; both accounts are recorded. See
[[Afraid and Dismayed - Deuteronomy 1.21, Joshua 1.9, and 1 Samuel 17.11]] and
[[Goliath and Elhanan - 1 Samuel 17 and 2 Samuel 21.19]].

[[David Comes to the Camp]] (17:12–40) introduces David again, *the son of that Ephrathite of Bethlehem Judah*, the
youngest of eight, going *back and forth from Saul* to the sheep (17:12–15). Jesse sends him with bread to *see how
your brothers are doing* (17:17–18), an errand shaped like Jacob's sending of Joseph (Genesis 37:14). At the camp he
hears *the same words* (17:23), and his first words in the book name the God no one else has named:
*who is this uncircumcised Philistine, that he should defy the armies of the living God?* (17:26). Eliab charges him
with pride; Saul calls him *a youth* against *a man of war* (17:28, 33). David answers from the flock, *Yahweh who
delivered me out of the paw of the lion … will deliver me out of the hand of this Philistine*, and Saul answers *Go!
Yahweh will be with you* (17:37). Dressed in Saul's bronze helmet and mail, the armor Goliath wears, David takes it
off, *for I have not tested them*, and goes with a staff, five smooth stones, and a sling (17:38–40). See
[[Sent to See His Brothers - Joseph at Shechem and David at Elah]] and
[[To Defy the Living God - 1 Samuel 17 and 2 Kings 19]].

[[David Kills Goliath]] (17:41–54) is over in two verses. Goliath sees David and *disdained him* for his youth and his
looks (17:42), the looks of 16:12, and curses him *by his gods* (17:43). David answers *You come to me with a sword,
with a spear, and with a javelin; but I come to you in the name of Yahweh of Armies* (17:45), and gives the fight its
purpose: *that all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel, and that all this assembly may know that Yahweh
doesn't save with sword and spear; for the battle is Yahweh's* (17:46–47), the principle that 13:22 set up. One stone
*sank into his forehead, and he fell on his face to the earth* (17:49), as Dagon had fallen before the ark (5:4). The
narrator sums up, *there was no sword in the hand of David* (17:50), and then David beheads him with his own sword
(17:51). The Philistines flee to Gath and Ekron and their camp is plundered (17:51–53). David brings the head to
Jerusalem, the only time 1 Samuel names the city, which in the narrative is still Jebusite, and the armor to his tent
(17:54); neither detail is explained. The fall, the head, and the armor recur at Saul's death (31:4, 9–10). See
[[Yahweh Doesn't Save with Sword and Spear - 1 Samuel 13.22, 17.47, and Judges 5.8]] and
[[The Head and the Armor - Goliath at Elah and Saul at Gilboa]].

[[Jonathan Loves David]] (17:55–18:5) goes back to the moment David went out. Saul asks Abner *whose son is this
youth?*, Abner swears he does not know, and David, the head still in his hand, answers *I am the son of your servant
Jesse the Bethlehemite* (17:55–58). After 16:18–22, where Saul sent to Jesse for him by name, the question does not
fit; the two accounts are recorded, not reconciled. Then *the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and
Jonathan loved him as his own soul* (18:1), the verb *qāšar* that Judah used of Jacob and Benjamin (Genesis 44:30) and
that Saul will use for conspiracy (22:8). Saul keeps David and does not let him go home; Jonathan makes a covenant with
him and gives him his robe, his clothing, his sword, his bow, and his sash (18:2–4). Saul's clothing David had taken
off (17:39); Jonathan's he keeps, and one of the two swords of 13:22 is now his. David *behaved himself wisely* and is
set over the men of war, *good in the sight of all the people* (18:5). See
[[Whose Son Is This Youth - 1 Samuel 16.18-22 and 17.55-58]] and
[[His Life Is Bound Up in the Boy's Life - Nephesh Tied to Nephesh]].

[[Saul's Jealousy of David]] (18:6–16) turns the victory into a grudge. The women come out of all the cities of Israel
with tambourines and dances, in the scene of Miriam and Jephthah's daughter, and sing to one another *Saul has slain
his thousands, and David his ten thousands* (18:6–7). Saul hears the second number: *What can he have more but the
kingdom?* (18:8), and from that day he *watched David* (18:9). The next day the evil spirit *came mightily* on him,
the verb of the Spirit's rush at 10:6 and 16:13, and while David plays as he did day by day Saul throws the spear,
*I will pin David even to the wall*, and David escapes twice (18:10–11). The narrator gives the reason: *Saul was afraid
of David, because Yahweh was with him, and had departed from Saul* (18:12). Yahweh *departed*, *sār*, and Saul
*removed* David, the same verb *sûr*: he makes him a captain over a thousand, and David goes out and comes in before
the people, the phrase of Moses' prayer for a shepherd (Numbers 27:17). He *behaved himself wisely*, Saul *stood in
awe of him*, and *all Israel and Judah loved David* (18:13–16). The song is quoted twice more by Philistines (21:11;
29:5). See [[Saul Has Slain His Thousands - 1 Samuel 18.7, 21.11, and 29.5]].

[[David Marries Michal]] (18:17–30) has Saul stop throwing the spear and start using his daughters. He offers his
*elder daughter Merab*, *Only be valiant for me, and fight Yahweh's battles*, and the narrator opens his mind: *Don't let
my hand be on him, but let the hand of the Philistines be on him* (18:17). David answers *Who am I … that I should be
son-in-law to the king?*, the *mî ʾānōḵî* of Moses at the bush (18:18; Exodus 3:11), and Merab is *given to Adriel the
Meholathite* instead (18:19). Then *Michal, Saul's daughter, loved David*; it pleases Saul, *that she may be a snare to
him* (18:20–21), the word for the nations' gods in Exodus 23:33 and Judges 2:3. Through his servants, *secretly*, he
names the price, *no dowry except one hundred foreskins of the Philistines, to be avenged of the king's enemies*, to
*make David fall by the hand of the Philistines* (18:22–25). David, *a poor man, and little known* (18:23), brings two
hundred, and *Saul gave him Michal his daughter as wife* (18:27), in the words of Laban giving Rachel (Genesis 29:28).
The elder given and the younger loved repeat Genesis 29; the bride-price of foreskins answers Genesis 34. *Saul saw
and knew that Yahweh was with David; and Michal, Saul's daughter, loved him*; *Saul was David's enemy continually*
(18:28–29). See [[A Bride-Price of Foreskins - Shechem, Saul, and David]] and
[[The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban]].
[[Jonathan Speaks for David]] (19:1–7) has Saul say aloud what chapter 18 kept in his thoughts. He *spoke to Jonathan
his son, and to all his servants, that they should kill David*, and the same verse answers: *But Jonathan, Saul's son,
greatly delighted in David* (19:1), the delight Saul's servants had falsely announced (18:22). Jonathan tells David to
hide and goes out with his father to *the field* (19:2–3). His plea is the first speech in the book for David, and it
argues from law and record, not from love: *Don't let the king sin against his servant*; *he put his life in his hand,
and struck the Philistine, and Yahweh worked a great victory*; *You saw it, and rejoiced. Why then will you sin
against innocent blood, to kill David without a cause?* (19:4–5), the *dām nāqî* of Deuteronomy 19:10. *Saul listened
to the voice of Jonathan* and swore *As Yahweh lives, he shall not be put to death*, and David was *in his presence,
as before* (19:6–7). The oath holds for three verses (19:10). See [[As Yahweh Lives - The Three Oaths of Saul]] and
[[My Life in My Hand - Jephthah, David, and the Medium of Endor]].

[[Michal Helps David Escape]] (19:8–17) breaks the oath in one night. *There was war again*, David wins *with a great
slaughter*, and the scene of 18:10–11 returns: *An evil spirit from Yahweh was on Saul, as he sat in his house with his
spear in his hand*, and David playing (19:8–9). The third spear goes into the wall; David *slipped away* and *fled, and
escaped that night* (19:10). Saul sends messengers to David's house *to watch him, and to kill him in the morning*, and
Michal, *David's wife*, warns him: *If you don't save your life tonight, tomorrow you will be killed* (19:11). She *let
David down through the window*, in the words of Rahab at Jericho (Joshua 2:15), lays *the teraphim* in the bed with *a
pillow of goats' hair at its head*, and tells the messengers *He is sick* (19:12–14). Saul's *Why have you deceived me
thus, and let my enemy go?* is Jacob's question to Laban (Genesis 29:25) and the medium's to Saul at Endor (28:12), and
Michal answers with a threat she puts in David's mouth (19:17). A daughter of the man in power uses household gods to
cover her husband's flight from her father, as Rachel did (Genesis 31:19–35). The verb *escape*, *mālaṭ*, comes four
times in eight verses. See [[Let Down Through the Window - Rahab and Michal]],
[[Why Have You Deceived Me - Laban, the Gibeonites, Michal, and the Medium]], and
[[The Teraphim - Household Gods in Genesis and After]].


## Structure

1. **Samuel and the house of Eli** (1–3) — Hannah's prayer and song, the corruption of Eli's sons, and
   Yahweh's word to the boy at Shiloh.
2. **The ark** (4–7) — the ark lost to the Philistines, its journey through their cities, its return, and
   Samuel's victory at Mizpah.
3. **A king like the nations** (8–12) — the people's demand, Saul anointed in secret and chosen by lot,
   his first victory, and Samuel's farewell.
4. **Saul rejected** (13–15) — the sacrifice at Gilgal, Jonathan at Michmash, the rash oath, and Amalek.
5. **David rises in Saul's house** (16–20) — the anointing in Bethlehem, Goliath, Jonathan's love, and
   Saul's jealousy.
6. **David a fugitive** (21–27) — Nob, Adullam, Keilah, the wilderness, Saul twice spared, Nabal and
   Abigail, and refuge with Achish of Gath.
7. **The end of Saul** (28–31) — the medium at Endor, David sent away from the Philistine army,
   Ziklag, and Gilboa.
