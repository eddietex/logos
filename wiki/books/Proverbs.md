# Proverbs

## Overview

Proverbs is the Old Testament's handbook of wisdom, and it says what it is for before it says
anything else. Its first seven verses are a statement of purpose — *"to know wisdom and
instruction"* (1:2) — addressed in the same breath to the simple, the young, and the already
wise, and closed by the sentence the whole tradition treats as its motto: *"The fear of Yahweh is
the beginning of knowledge"* (1:7). See [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]]. The first lecture follows at once, and its first
lesson is not about sex or money but about company: a father warning his son against a gang's
invitation to ambush the innocent, and showing him the ambush turned round on its makers (1:8–19). See
[[Do Not Consent to Sinners]]. Then Wisdom herself speaks, in the street and the city gates, in the
voice of a prophet: she calls, is refused, laughs at the refusers' calamity, and will not answer
when they call too late (1:20–33). See [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]]. The father's second lecture
answers her: one long conditional sentence — *if* you seek her as silver, *then* you will understand
the fear of Yahweh, *for* Yahweh gives wisdom — which then names the two dangers wisdom rescues from,
men of perverse speech and the *"strange woman"* who forgets *"the covenant of her God"*, and ends on
the upright dwelling in the land (2:1–22). See [[If You Seek Her as Silver]]. The third lecture is six
couplets of command and reason — keep my teaching, bind kindness and truth, *"Trust in Yahweh with all
your heart"*, fear Yahweh, honour him with first fruits — whose rewards are long life, favour, straight
paths, health, and full barns; and then, set apart by a paragraph marker, the sixth: *"whom Yahweh
loves, he reproves; even as a father"* (3:1–12). See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]].

It is an anthology, and its headings say so. The book names Solomon three times (1:1; 10:1;
25:1), but it also contains *"the words of the wise"* (22:17), further *"sayings of the wise"*
(24:23), *"the words of Agur the son of Jakeh"* (30:1), and *"the words of king Lemuel; the
revelation which his mother taught him"* (31:1). Its second Solomonic collection was *"copied out"*
by *"the men of Hezekiah king of Judah"* (25:1), two and a half centuries after Solomon on the
usual chronology, so the book as received is the work of an editorial tradition and not of one
pen. The headings are the wiki's evidence for its structure; nothing about authorship is claimed
beyond what they state. See
[[The Wisdom of Solomon - 1 Kings 3-4 and the Headings of Proverbs]].

The wiki reaches Proverbs after [[Job]], and the order matters for reading it. Job put the
doctrine of [[Retribution]] on trial, and Proverbs is where much of that doctrine is stated in its
classic form — the righteous flourish, the wicked fall, diligence prospers and laziness starves.
**Read after Job, Proverbs is not naïve but generic**: its sentences describe how the world
ordinarily runs, which is exactly the thing Job's friends mistook for a verdict on a particular
man. Proverbs itself knows the limit — *"Answer a fool according to his folly"* stands next to its
opposite (26:4–5), and the book is full of *"better than"* sayings that admit the righteous poor
and the wicked rich exist. The pages for each pericope keep both books in view.

The next poem praises wisdom's incomparable worth and life-giving gifts (3:13–18), then
grounds that praise in Yahweh's founding of earth and heavens by wisdom and understanding
(3:19–20). The tree of life moves Eden's image into instruction; the creation setting
anticipates the fuller speech in chapter 8. See [[Wisdom's Worth and the Founding of the Earth]].
The promises of riches and peace remain in tension with Job's righteous suffering.

The next instruction makes wisdom's peaceful paths concrete: Yahweh guards the son's
steps and sleep; the son must give due good promptly, respect a neighbor's trust, avoid
baseless strife, and refuse to envy violence (3:21–35). Friendship, blessing, grace, and
glory close the contrasts. See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Job's ruined household and
terrified nights remain explicit counterexamples to a universal prosperity inference.

A father next recalls the instruction of his own childhood and passes it to his sons
(4:1–9). Wisdom is to be acquired, retained, loved, and embraced; she preserves and crowns
the learner. See [[Get Wisdom]]. The unnamed speakers are not securely identified as
Solomon and David, and the honor promised remains in tension with Job's removed crown
(Job 19:9). Instruction is handed on through a household as well as learned from observation.

The father then sets two roads before the son (4:10–19). He has already led him in *"the way of
wisdom"*; six commands keep him off *"the path of the wicked"*, whose walkers cannot sleep until
they have done evil. The lecture ends on light: the righteous path brightening like dawn, the
wicked way so dark they *"don't know what they stumble over"*. See [[The Two Paths]]. Job 24:13–17
draws the same night-loving wicked but cannot see their fall, and the wiki keeps the tension.

The chapter's last lecture moves from the road to the walker (4:20–27). The father's words are
kept *"in the center of your heart"* as life and health, and the heart is guarded above all,
*"for out of it is the wellspring of life"* (4:23); mouth, eyes, and feet follow, on a level path
that turns *"to the right hand nor to the left"*. See [[Keep Your Heart]]. Job 31 swears to the
same keeping of heart, eyes, and step, and Job 2:7 records the body struck anyway.

Chapter 5 gives the strange woman of 2:16–19 a lecture of her own (5:1–23). Her lips *"drip
honey"* but her end is wormwood and her steps *"lead straight to Sheol"*; her door costs the son
honour, years, and labour, all passing to strangers, and a confession of hated instruction made too
late. Against her stands his own wife, pictured as his own cistern and spring: *"Rejoice in the
wife of your youth"* (5:18), and be captivated by her love. Every way of man is before Yahweh's
eyes, and the wicked are held by the cords of their own sin (5:21–22). See
[[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]]. Job 31:9–12 swears to the same door and invokes the same
loss on himself.

Chapter 6 opens with four short units instead of one lecture (6:1–19): the son who has pledged
himself for another's debt, *"trapped by the words of your mouth"*, told to get free at once; the
sluggard sent to the ant, whose *"little sleep, a little slumber"* brings poverty *"as a robber"*;
the worthless man whose mouth, eyes, feet, fingers, and heart all serve evil, broken *"without
remedy"*; and the *"six things which Yahweh hates; yes, seven"*, from haughty eyes to *"he who
sows discord among brothers"*. See [[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]]. The
warning against suretyship stands in tension with Judah's pledge for Benjamin (Genesis 43:9) and
Job's plea for God as guarantor (Job 17:3); the list of seven runs close to the Decalogue's second
table.

The chapter's second half returns to the strange woman, and names her outright as *a man's wife*
(6:20–35). The father's commandment and the mother's teaching, bound on the heart, lead the son when
he walks, watch him when he sleeps, and talk with him when he wakes — Deuteronomy 6:7's round of the
day — for *"the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light"* (6:23). Against her beauty he is told
not to covet (6:25, the tenth commandment's verb), and the argument is fire: *"Can a man scoop fire
into his lap, and his clothes not be burned?"* (6:27). A hungry thief can repay, even sevenfold; the
adulterer *"destroys his own soul"*, and the husband's jealousy *"won't regard any ransom"* (6:35).
See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]]. Proverbs names no court where Leviticus 20:10 sets death, and
the wiki records the two pictures side by side; with 6:16–19, the chapter touches the sixth through
tenth commandments.

Chapter 7 is the strange woman's last lecture, and the only one told as a story (7:1–27). After a
charge to keep the commandments *"as the apple of your eye"*, write them on the heart, and call
wisdom *"my sister"*, the father looks out through his lattice at dusk and sees a young man *"void
of understanding"* walk toward her corner as the night darkens. She meets him in *"the attire of a
prostitute"* though she is a wife, seizes him, and speaks: peace offerings at home and vows paid
today, a bed perfumed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon, and a husband away until the full moon. He
follows *"as an ox goes to the slaughter"*; *"Her house is the way to Sheol"* (7:27). See
[[The Young Man Void of Understanding]]. She stands in Wisdom's streets and uses Wisdom's words, which chapter 8 answers.

Chapter 8 answers at once: *"Doesn't wisdom cry out?"* (8:1–21). Wisdom stands on the heights, where
the paths meet, and at the city gates, and calls *"the sons of mankind"*, the simple and fools
included. Her words have *"nothing crooked or perverse in them"*; her instruction is to be taken
*"rather than silver"*. She defines *"The fear of Yahweh"* as *"to hate evil"* (8:13), claims the
counsel, understanding, and might Job 12:13 gave to God, and says *"By me kings reign"* (8:15). She
loves those who love her, is found by those who seek her (8:17), and fills their treasuries. See
[[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] and
[[Two Voices in the Street - The Strange Woman and Wisdom in Proverbs 7-9]]. The promise of riches
and honour stands, again, against Job's losses.

The speech's second half says where Wisdom comes from (8:22–36). *"Yahweh possessed me in the
beginning of his work"* (8:22), with *qānâ*, the verb of *"Get wisdom"* (4:7), and *rēʾšîṯ*, the
noun of Genesis 1:1. Born before depths and hills, she was *"there"* when the heavens were
established and the sea given its boundary, and beside God as *"the craftsman"* — or *nursling*, on
another reading of *ʾāmôn* — *"rejoicing in his whole world"*, her delight *"with the sons of men"*
(8:30–31). She closes in the father's words, *"Now therefore, my sons, listen to me"* (8:32): *"whoever
finds me, finds life"*, and *"All those who hate me love death"* (8:35–36). See
[[Wisdom at the Creation]] and
[[Possessed in the Beginning - Proverbs 8.22-31, John 1, and Colossians 1]]; the New Testament's
language for Christ at creation resembles the passage without citing it.

Chapter 9 ends the prologue with two feasts (9:1–18). Wisdom *"has built her house"*, carved *"her
seven pillars"*, prepared meat and mixed wine, and sent her maidens to call from the city's heights:
*"Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!"* — *"Come, eat some of my bread … Leave your simple ways,
and live"* (9:4–6). The foolish woman, *"loud, undisciplined, and knows nothing"*, sits at her door in
the same high places and calls the same words — the Hebrew of 9:16 repeats 9:4 — but offers *"Stolen
water"* and *"Food eaten in secret"*, and her guests *"are in the depths of Sheol"* (9:17–18). Between
them, sayings on the scoffer and the wise man and the motto again: *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning
of wisdom"* (9:10), closing chapters 1–9 as 1:7 opened them. See [[The Two Feasts]] and
[[Come, Eat of My Bread - Proverbs 9, Isaiah 55, and the Gospel Banquets]].

The new heading at 10:1 opens the sentence collection: [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]] (10:1–32).
Short contrasts bring wisdom into the household, harvest, choice of words, and response to
correction. Wealth protects and poverty exposes (10:15), but wicked treasure does not save (10:2);
love covers wrongs while deceitful hatred is condemned (10:12, 18). The promises of safety and long
life remain in explicit tension with Job's disaster and his observation of prosperous wicked people
(Job 1:13–22; 3:25; 21:7–13).

[[A False Balance]] (11:1–31) brings righteousness into honest measurement, trustworthy speech,
civic counsel, and the release of goods. It praises generosity without condemning commerce: selling
grain earns blessing where withholding it earns a curse. Righteous fruit becomes a tree of life
(30), while repayment on earth closes the unit (31). The promises of deliverance, especially for
righteous offspring (21), remain explicitly in tension with Job’s lost children and the secure
children of the wicked (Job 1:18–22; 21:7–13).

[[Whoever Loves Correction]] (12:1–28) makes love of correction a test of wisdom, then develops it
through truthful testimony, restrained and healing speech, household honor, diligent work, and care
for an animal’s life. Yahweh’s delight includes truthful conduct. The standing righteous household
and promise of safety remain in explicit tension with Job’s calamity, while the warning that
laziness ends in slave labor cannot explain the imposed labor of Exodus 1. The unit ends on
righteousness as the path of life.

[[Hope Deferred]] (13:1–25) is framed by the son who listens to his father (1) and the father who
does not spare the rod (24), and is the first chapter of the collection with no divine name in the
WEB text. Its title line, *"Hope deferred makes the heart sick"* (12), makes no moral contrast at
all. The lamp of the wicked (9) and the sinner's wealth stored for the righteous (22) are sentences
Job's dialogue already uses (Job 18:5–6; 21:17; 27:16–17), and 13:23's productive field swept away
by injustice is the chapter's own check on reading poverty as a verdict.

[[A Way That Seems Right]] (14:1–35) opens with the wise woman who *"builds her house"* (1), Wisdom's
house of 9:1 brought into an ordinary home, and names Yahweh again after chapter 13's silence: the
fear of Yahweh is *"a secure fortress"* and *"a fountain of life"* (26–27), the latter repeating
13:14's line about the teaching of the wise. Its title line, *"There is a way which seems right to a
man, but in the end it leads to death"* (12), recurs at 16:25. The chapter grounds care for the poor
in creation — oppressing them *"shows contempt for his Maker"* (31) — sets the shunned poor man (20)
beside a ruling that despising him is sin (21), commends being *"slow to anger"* (29) in the WEB
phrase of Exodus 34:6, and admits that *"The heart knows its own bitterness"* (10), a limit Job's
friends did not respect.

[[A Gentle Answer]] (15:1–33) opens on *"A gentle answer turns away wrath"* (1) and is mostly about
speech — the gentle tongue as *"a tree of life"* (4), the *"word at the right time"* (23), the righteous
heart that *"weighs answers"* (28). It names Yahweh nine times, more than any chapter of the collection
so far: his eyes *"are everywhere"* (3), Sheol and Abaddon are before him and so are human hearts (11),
the wicked man's sacrifice is *"an abomination"* while *"the prayer of the upright is his delight"* (8),
and he *"will keep the widow's borders intact"* (25). Its first *"better than"* sayings — *"Better is
little, with the fear of Yahweh, than great treasure with trouble"* (16) — admit within the same chapter
that put *"much treasure"* in the righteous house (6) that the righteous may have little. It ends on
*"The fear of Yahweh teaches wisdom. Before honor is humility"* (33).

[[The Plans of the Heart]] (16:1–33) is framed by plan and outcome: *"The plans of the heart belong to
man, but the answer of the tongue is from Yahweh"* (1), *"A man's heart plans his course, but Yahweh
directs his steps"* (9), and *"The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from Yahweh"*
(33). Its opening verses name Yahweh in all but one of the first nine, and include the hardest line in
the collection, *"even the wicked for the day of evil"* (4), and atonement *"by mercy and truth"* (6).
It holds the first cluster of royal sayings (10–15) — *"In the light of the king's face is life"* —
and the book's best-known line, *"Pride goes before destruction"* (18). Verse 25 repeats 14:12 word for
word.

[[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]] (17:1–28) has no frame, and names Yahweh only twice, and God once more as *Maker*: he *"tests
the hearts"* as the furnace tests gold (3), is the *Maker* whom mockery of the poor reproaches (5), and
abhors both the acquittal of the wicked and the condemnation of the righteous (15). Its densest cluster
is the corrupted court — the bribe that *prospers* its giver (8), the bribe taken *"to pervert the ways
of justice"* (23) in the words of Exodus 23:6–8, and officials flogged *"for their integrity"* (26) —
the collection's plainest admission that judgment can be bought. Around it stand the quiet house (1),
covering an offense (9), the friend who *"loves at all times"* (17), set against Job's friends, and the
fool counted wise when he is silent (28), Job 13:5 made general.

[[A Strong Tower]] (18:1–24) centres on fortification: *"Yahweh's name is a strong tower: the
righteous run to him, and are safe"* (10), the only *"Yahweh's name"* in the book, set against the
rich man's *"strong city"*, high only *"in his own imagination"* (11, repeating 10:15), and the
offended brother *"more difficult than a fortified city"* (19). Most of the chapter is about speech —
the fool airing his opinion (2), the gossip's *"dainty morsels"* (8), answering before hearing (13),
the first litigant who *"seems right"* until questioned (17), and *"Death and life are in the power
of the tongue"* (21). The man who finds a wife *"obtains favor of Yahweh"* (22) in Wisdom's words from
8:35, and the chapter ends on *"a friend who sticks closer than a brother"* (24). It repeats more
earlier and later lines than any chapter so far.

[[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] (19:1–29) ranks integrity and honesty above poverty's
social disadvantages. Friends follow wealth and gifts, while the poor are deserted (4, 6–7); pity
for them is lending to Yahweh (17). The chapter joins restraint of anger, household prudence,
correction, and listening to counsel with the claim that Yahweh's counsel prevails (21). Its safety
promise (23) remains explicitly in tension with Job's God-fearing suffering. The servant's rule over
princes judged inappropriate (10) also sits uneasily beside the wise servant's elevation in 17:2;
different settings are retained rather than turned into a single rule.

[[Wine Is a Mocker]] (20:1–30) tests professions against conduct: sobriety and restraint, faithful love,
integrity, a clean heart, honest weights, and discreet speech. Yahweh makes hearing and sight,
saves the person who waits instead of retaliating, directs steps beyond full human
understanding, and searches inwardly through the human spirit as lamp. The king's terror and
threshing judgment stand beside love and faithfulness sustaining his throne. The blessing on
righteous children remains in tension with Job's bereavement; cleansing blows cannot cancel
Proverbs 17:26's condemnation of unjust punishment.

[[The King's Heart]] (21:1–31) is framed by Yahweh's sovereignty over the powerful: the king's heart
turned like watercourses (1), and no counsel against Yahweh, whose victory the prepared horse cannot
supply (30–31). Between, Yahweh weighs hearts that approve themselves (2) and prefers righteousness
and justice to sacrifice (3, 27). Two better-than sayings flee a contentious wife (9, 19); the bribe
that pacifies wrath is observed, not approved (14); the one deaf to the poor cries unheard (13), a
warning kept in tension with Job's unanswered cries.

[[A Good Name]] (22:1–16) closes the Solomonic collection with wealth at both ends: a good name
above great riches (1), one Maker of rich and poor (2), and two roads to poverty — oppressing the
poor and giving to the rich (16). Between them stand the trained child (6), read as a saying about
formation rather than a guarantee; the borrower who serves the lender (7); the sower of wickedness
(8), kept beside Eliphaz's misuse of the image; the sluggard's lion (13); the adulteress's mouth as a
deep pit (14); and the rod that drives out folly (15). Verse 17 begins the words of the wise.

[[Thirty Excellent Things]] (22:17–23:11) opens *"the words of the wise"* with a preface whose stated
goal is *"that your trust may be in Yahweh"* (22:19), and then changes form: commands with reasons
instead of observations. The block is framed by Yahweh pleading the case of the poor in court
(22:22–23) and of the fatherless, whose *Defender* — Hebrew *gōʾēl* — is strong (23:10–11), and by the
ancient boundary stone (22:28; 23:10). Between stand the hot-tempered companion (22:24–25), the surety
who loses his bed (22:26–27), the skilled worker before kings (22:29), the ruler's *"deceitful food"*
(23:1–3), riches that sprout wings (23:4–5), the stingy host (23:6–8), and the fool not worth answering
(23:9). The WEB's *"thirty"* (22:20) rests on a disputed Hebrew word, and the often-noted Egyptian
parallel, the *Instruction of Amenemope*, is recorded on the passage page as unverified here.

[[Do Not Envy Sinners]] (23:12–24:2) returns to the voice of chapters 1–9 — *"My son"*, the father's
glad heart, the mother *"when she is old"* — and is framed by two prohibitions of envy (23:17; 24:1).
Its argument is the *ʾaḥărît*, the latter end: the God-fearer has *"a future hope"* that *"will not be
cut off"* (23:18), while the wine that *"goes down smoothly"* *"In the end … bites like a snake"*
(23:32) — one Hebrew noun for both. Between come the rod that saves from Sheol (23:13–14), the drunkard
and the glutton (23:20–21), truth bought and not sold (23:23), the prostitute as *"a deep pit"*
(23:27–28), and the book's fullest portrait of drunkenness, which ends with the drinker reaching for
another (23:29–35).

[[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]] (24:3–22) closes the first *"words of the wise"*. It opens on a house built *"through wisdom
… by understanding … by knowledge"* (24:3–4) — the three gifts by which Yahweh founded the world
(3:19–20) and filled Bezalel for the tabernacle (Exodus 31:3) — and turns to what wise strength is
for: not only war won by counsel (24:5–6) but the rescue of *"those who are being led away to death"*
(24:11), with the bystander's *"we didn't know"* answered by *"he who weighs the hearts"*, who will
*"render to every man according to his work"* (24:12). Then honey and a hope not cut off (24:13–14);
the righteous man who *"falls seven times, and rises up again"* (24:16); a ban on rejoicing when an
enemy falls (24:17–18); the fourth prohibition of envying the wicked (24:19–20); and a closing command
to *"fear Yahweh and the king"* (24:21–22).

A short appendix follows under its own heading, *"These also are sayings of the wise"* (24:23–34). It
opens on the court — partiality in judgment, literally *recognizing faces*, the words of Deuteronomy
1:17, and the judge who says *"You are righteous"* to the wicked cursed by the peoples (24:23–25) — then
an honest answer like a kiss (24:26), the field readied before the house (24:27), and a neighbour not
to be witnessed against or paid back: *"I will render to the man according to his work"* (24:29) is
forbidden to the son twelve verses after it was said of God. It ends with a first-person scene to set
beside the window of 7:6–23: a walk past a vineyard gone to thorns, its wall fallen, and the lesson of
6:10–11 received again (24:30–34). See [[The Field of the Sluggard]]. Yahweh is not named in it.

The last Solomonic collection opens under a heading that names its copyists: *"These also are
proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out"* (25:1). See
[[The Glory of Kings]] (25:1–28) and [[Hezekiah]]. Its first saying makes inquiry royal — *"It is the
glory of God to conceal a thing, but the glory of kings is to search out a matter"* (25:2) — and its
next ones teach the subject his place: the wicked removed from the king like dross from silver (25:4–5),
and the courtier told *"Come up here"* rather than put lower (25:6–7), the scene of Jesus' parable of
the wedding guests (Luke 14:7–11). The form changes with the heading: antithetical couplets give way to
comparisons — apples of gold, snow at harvest, rainless clouds, a bad tooth, vinegar on soda. Between
them come settling with a neighbour out of court (25:8–10), the soft tongue that *"breaks the bone"*
(25:15), honey and visits in measure (25:16–17), the false witness in the ninth commandment's own Hebrew
(25:18), bread and water for an enemy, for which *"Yahweh will reward you"* (25:21–22; quoted at Romans
12:20), and a man without self-restraint as *"a city that is broken down and without walls"* (25:28).

The next chapter draws three portraits. [[Answer a Fool]] (26:1–28) names the fool in eleven of its
first twelve verses: honour does not fit him (26:1, 8), a rod is for his back (26:3), and *"a parable
in the mouth of fools"* is a lame leg or a thorn in a drunkard's hand (26:7, 9) — the book's own word
for itself, useless in the wrong mouth. At its head stand two commands that contradict each other on
purpose: *"Don't answer a fool according to his folly … Answer a fool according to his folly"*
(26:4–5). The dog returning to its vomit (26:11) is the line 2 Peter 2:22 quotes, and the portrait
ends below the fool, with the man *"wise in his own eyes"* (26:12). Then the sluggard's last four
sayings (26:13–16), two of them repeated from earlier collections; and the troublemaker — the
meddler, the prankster, the whisperer without whom *"a quarrel dies down"*, and the hater whose
charming speech covers *"seven abominations in his heart"* — ending with the pit that catches its
digger (26:17–28). Yahweh is not named in the chapter.

[[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]] (27:1–27) turns from the fool to the people nearest. It opens on praise
— *"Don't boast about tomorrow; for you don't know what a day may bring"* (27:1), *"Let another man
praise you, and not your own mouth"* (27:2) — and closes that thread with *"man is refined by his
praise"* (27:21), whose first line is 17:3's crucible and furnace. Between come the friend proved by
cost: *"Better is open rebuke than hidden love"*, *"Faithful are the wounds of a friend"* (27:5–6), the
father's friend not forsaken and the near neighbour *"better … than a distant brother"* (27:10), and
*"Iron sharpens iron"* (27:17). Repeats of 22:3 and 20:16 (27:12–13), the contentious wife as a dripping
roof (27:15–16), Sheol, Abaddon, and the human eye *"never satisfied"* (27:20), and the fool ground in a
mortar (27:22) follow. The chapter ends with the collection's only sustained poem: *"Know well the state
of your flocks … for riches are not forever, nor does the crown endure to all generations"* (27:23–27).
Yahweh is not named in it.

[[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]] (28:1–28) brings law, wealth, and rule into one field of
moral testing. The poor may have integrity and discernment, the rich may misjudge themselves, and
even a needy person may oppress. Refusing to hear the law corrupts prayer; confession and
renunciation find mercy. Wicked rule drives people into hiding, while work, generosity, rebuke, and
trust in Yahweh are commended. The promises of boldness and safety remain explicitly in tension with
Job’s affirmed integrity, fear, and suffering; the chapter’s own poor person with integrity prevents
equating wealth with righteousness.

[[Where There Is No Vision]] (29:1–27) closes the collection copied by Hezekiah’s men. Reproof and household
correction stand alongside royal justice for the poor, restraint of anger, and the dangers of
flattery and hasty speech. Revelation is paired with keeping the law (18); fear of people and
royal favor are set against trust and justice from Yahweh (25–26). The servant-to-son saying is
preserved as the WEB gives it, without turning it into an adoption law. Promised safety and the
wicked’s downfall remain in explicit tension with Job’s undeserved suffering and prosperous
wrongdoers.

[[The Words of Agur]] (30:1–14) introduces [[Agur]], son of [[Jakeh]], addressing [[Ithiel]] and
[[Ucal]] in the WEB. Confessed ignorance and questions about creation lead to trust in God’s
flawless words and a prohibition on adding to them. His prayer asks for truth and enough food,
without poverty or riches. The prayer against riches stands explicitly beside Wisdom’s promise
of wealth in 8:18–21. The remaining sayings protect a servant from slander and condemn parental
dishonor, supposed purity, haughty eyes, and the consuming of the poor. The son asked about in
30:4 remains unnamed; the Christian comparison with John 3:13 is distinguished from an explicit
identification in Proverbs.

[[Four Things]] (30:15–33) completes Agur's words with numbered lists in the *three … four* form:
four things never satisfied, led by [[Sheol]] and the barren womb; four ways too wonderful to
understand, set beside the adulteress who wipes her mouth; four reversals the earth cannot bear;
four small creatures that are wise; and four stately in their going. The mocker of parents is left
to the birds (17), and the unit ends with the hand over the mouth, because pressing wrath produces
strife (32–33). The servant who inherits and the unloved wife who marries (23) are held in
explicit tension with Proverbs 17:2 and with Genesis' stories of [[Hagar]] and [[Leah]].

[[The Words of King Lemuel]] (31:1–9) begins a new attribution: [[Lemuel]] receives the instruction
of
[[Lemuel's Mother]]. Her warning about women and royal strength leads to abstinence for the sake
of remembering the law and the justice due to the afflicted. Drink assigned to the dying and
bitter in soul (6–7) remains in explicit tension with the destructive portrait of 23:29–35.
Twice she commands the king to open his mouth for the vulnerable and judge righteously (8–9).
Neither his kingdom nor an identification with Solomon is supplied. The final poem remains next.

## Structure

- **1–9 Prologue and lectures** — the purpose statement (1:1–7), then a sequence of instructions
  addressed to *"my son"*, interleaved with speeches by Wisdom personified, who calls in the
  streets (1:20–33), was with Yahweh *"in the beginning of his work"* (8:22), and spreads a feast
  that competes with Folly's (9:1–18). The section opens and closes on the motto (1:7; 9:10).
- **10:1–22:16 The proverbs of Solomon** — a new heading (10:1) and a change of form: hundreds of
  two-line sentences, most of them antithetical, with no narrative sequence.
- **22:17–24:22 The words of the wise** — a hortatory collection that announces *"thirty excellent
  things"* (22:20), with a further short collection of *"sayings of the wise"* (24:23–34).
- **25–29 The proverbs of Solomon copied by Hezekiah's men** — comparisons and images, many in
  clusters on the fool, the sluggard, and the king.
- **30 The words of Agur** — a confession of ignorance (30:2–4), a prayer, and numerical sayings.
- **31 The words of King Lemuel** — his mother's instruction (31:1–9), and the acrostic poem on the
  *"worthy woman"* (31:10–31) that ends the book.

## Key themes

- [[Kingship and Empire]], [[Judgment]], [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — sober judgment and speaking
  for the mute, poor, and needy (31:4–9).
- [[Wisdom]], [[Oaths and Vows]], [[Remembrance]] — maternal instruction, the son of her vows,
  and the contrast between forgetting law and forgetting misery (31:1–7).

- [[Wisdom]] , [[Faith]] , [[Language and Speech]] — human limits, trustworthy divine words, and
  restraint in adding (30:2–6).
- [[Prayer]] , [[Wealth and Possessions]] , [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] — sufficient provision,
  dangers of riches and poverty, and the poor consumed (30:7–14).
- [[Creation]] , [[Barrenness]] , [[Slavery and Servitude]] , [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the
  insatiable, the wonderful, the intolerable, the small and wise, and wrath pressed into strife
  (30:15–33).


- [[Prophecy and Vision]], [[Law and Obedience]] — revelation and keeping the law against unrestrained life (29:18).
- [[Slavery and Servitude]], [[Adoption]] — the unresponsive servant and the servant who becomes a son (29:19, 21); no legal procedure stated.
- [[Kingship and Empire]], [[Judgment]] — just rule, the poor person’s case, and justice from Yahweh beyond royal favor (29:4, 7, 14, 26).

- [[Law and Obedience]] — law kept or refused shapes allegiance, family honor, and prayer (28:4, 7,
  9).
- [[Forgiveness]], [[Guilt and Conscience]] — confession and renunciation find mercy (28:13); fear
  is not a reliable test of guilt (28:1 beside Job 3:25–26).

- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — available good must not be delayed (3:27–28).
- [[Favor and Grace]] — grace given to the humble (3:34).
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the neighbor’s trust protected; groundless strife refused (3:29–30).

- [[Creation]] — Yahweh founds earth and establishes heavens through wisdom (3:19–20).
- [[Life and Death]] — wisdom as a tree of life to those who retain her (3:18).
- [[Wisdom]] — the book's subject, named in its first purpose clause (1:2)
- [[The Glory of God]] — *"It is the glory of God to conceal a thing, but the glory of kings is to search
  out a matter"* (25:2)
- [[The Fear of God]] — *"the beginning of knowledge"* (1:7) and of wisdom (9:10); *"to hate evil"*
  (8:13)
- [[Folly]] — wisdom's opposite, and the other way the book's reader can go; *"Answer a fool according to his folly"* and its opposite (26:4–5)
- [[Righteousness]] — *"righteousness, justice, and equity"* (1:3), wisdom's moral content
- [[Language and Speech]] — proverb, parable, riddle (1:6): a book about words, made of words

## Key people

- [[Lemuel]] and [[Lemuel's Mother]] — the named king and his unnamed teacher (31:1–9).

- [[Agur]] — named speaker, son of [[Jakeh]], addressing [[Ithiel]] and [[Ucal]] in the WEB (30:1).

- [[Solomon]] — named in the headings at 1:1, 10:1, and 25:1; not named at 8:15–18, where Wisdom
