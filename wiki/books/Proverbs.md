# Proverbs

## Overview

Proverbs is the Old Testament's handbook of wisdom, and it says what it is for before it says
anything else. Its first seven verses are a statement of purpose — *"to know wisdom and
instruction"* (1:2) — addressed in the same breath to the simple, the young, and the already
wise, and closed by the sentence the whole tradition treats as its motto: *"The fear of Yahweh is
the beginning of knowledge"* (1:7). See [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]]. The first lecture follows at once, and its first
lesson is not about sex or money but about company: a father warning his son against a gang's
invitation to ambush the innocent, and showing him the ambush turned round on its makers (1:8–19). See
[[Do Not Consent to Sinners]]. Then Wisdom herself speaks, in the street and the city gates, in the
voice of a prophet: she calls, is refused, laughs at the refusers' calamity, and will not answer
when they call too late (1:20–33). See [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]]. The father's second lecture
answers her: one long conditional sentence — *if* you seek her as silver, *then* you will understand
the fear of Yahweh, *for* Yahweh gives wisdom — which then names the two dangers wisdom rescues from,
men of perverse speech and the *"strange woman"* who forgets *"the covenant of her God"*, and ends on
the upright dwelling in the land (2:1–22). See [[If You Seek Her as Silver]]. The third lecture is six
couplets of command and reason — keep my teaching, bind kindness and truth, *"Trust in Yahweh with all
your heart"*, fear Yahweh, honour him with first fruits — whose rewards are long life, favour, straight
paths, health, and full barns; and then, set apart by a paragraph marker, the sixth: *"whom Yahweh
loves, he reproves; even as a father"* (3:1–12). See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]].

It is an anthology, and its headings say so. The book names Solomon three times (1:1; 10:1;
25:1), but it also contains *"the words of the wise"* (22:17), further *"sayings of the wise"*
(24:23), *"the words of Agur the son of Jakeh"* (30:1), and *"the words of king Lemuel; the
revelation which his mother taught him"* (31:1). Its second Solomonic collection was *"copied out"*
by *"the men of Hezekiah king of Judah"* (25:1), two and a half centuries after Solomon on the
usual chronology, so the book as received is the work of an editorial tradition and not of one
pen. The headings are the wiki's evidence for its structure; nothing about authorship is claimed
beyond what they state. See
[[The Wisdom of Solomon - 1 Kings 3-4 and the Headings of Proverbs]].

The wiki reaches Proverbs after [[Job]], and the order matters for reading it. Job put the
doctrine of [[Retribution]] on trial, and Proverbs is where much of that doctrine is stated in its
classic form — the righteous flourish, the wicked fall, diligence prospers and laziness starves.
**Read after Job, Proverbs is not naïve but generic**: its sentences describe how the world
ordinarily runs, which is exactly the thing Job's friends mistook for a verdict on a particular
man. Proverbs itself knows the limit — *"Answer a fool according to his folly"* stands next to its
opposite (26:4–5), and the book is full of *"better than"* sayings that admit the righteous poor
and the wicked rich exist. The pages for each pericope keep both books in view.

The next poem praises wisdom's incomparable worth and life-giving gifts (3:13–18), then
grounds that praise in Yahweh's founding of earth and heavens by wisdom and understanding
(3:19–20). The tree of life moves Eden's image into instruction; the creation setting
anticipates the fuller speech in chapter 8. See [[Wisdom's Worth and the Founding of the Earth]].
The promises of riches and peace remain in tension with Job's righteous suffering.

The next instruction makes wisdom's peaceful paths concrete: Yahweh guards the son's
steps and sleep; the son must give due good promptly, respect a neighbor's trust, avoid
baseless strife, and refuse to envy violence (3:21–35). Friendship, blessing, grace, and
glory close the contrasts. See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Job's ruined household and
terrified nights remain explicit counterexamples to a universal prosperity inference.

A father next recalls the instruction of his own childhood and passes it to his sons
(4:1–9). Wisdom is to be acquired, retained, loved, and embraced; she preserves and crowns
the learner. See [[Get Wisdom]]. The unnamed speakers are not securely identified as
Solomon and David, and the honor promised remains in tension with Job's removed crown
(Job 19:9). Instruction is handed on through a household as well as learned from observation.

The father then sets two roads before the son (4:10–19). He has already led him in *"the way of
wisdom"*; six commands keep him off *"the path of the wicked"*, whose walkers cannot sleep until
they have done evil. The lecture ends on light: the righteous path brightening like dawn, the
wicked way so dark they *"don't know what they stumble over"*. See [[The Two Paths]]. Job 24:13–17
draws the same night-loving wicked but cannot see their fall, and the wiki keeps the tension.

The chapter's last lecture moves from the road to the walker (4:20–27). The father's words are
kept *"in the center of your heart"* as life and health, and the heart is guarded above all,
*"for out of it is the wellspring of life"* (4:23); mouth, eyes, and feet follow, on a level path
that turns *"to the right hand nor to the left"*. See [[Keep Your Heart]]. Job 31 swears to the
same keeping of heart, eyes, and step, and Job 2:7 records the body struck anyway.

Chapter 5 gives the strange woman of 2:16–19 a lecture of her own (5:1–23). Her lips *"drip
honey"* but her end is wormwood and her steps *"lead straight to Sheol"*; her door costs the son
honour, years, and labour, all passing to strangers, and a confession of hated instruction made too
late. Against her stands his own wife, pictured as his own cistern and spring: *"Rejoice in the
wife of your youth"* (5:18), and be captivated by her love. Every way of man is before Yahweh's
eyes, and the wicked are held by the cords of their own sin (5:21–22). See
[[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]]. Job 31:9–12 swears to the same door and invokes the same
loss on himself.

Chapter 6 opens with four short units instead of one lecture (6:1–19): the son who has pledged
himself for another's debt, *"trapped by the words of your mouth"*, told to get free at once; the
sluggard sent to the ant, whose *"little sleep, a little slumber"* brings poverty *"as a robber"*;
the worthless man whose mouth, eyes, feet, fingers, and heart all serve evil, broken *"without
remedy"*; and the *"six things which Yahweh hates; yes, seven"*, from haughty eyes to *"he who
sows discord among brothers"*. See [[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]]. The
warning against suretyship stands in tension with Judah's pledge for Benjamin (Genesis 43:9) and
Job's plea for God as guarantor (Job 17:3); the list of seven runs close to the Decalogue's second
table.

The chapter's second half returns to the strange woman, and names her outright as *a man's wife*
(6:20–35). The father's commandment and the mother's teaching, bound on the heart, lead the son when
he walks, watch him when he sleeps, and talk with him when he wakes — Deuteronomy 6:7's round of the
day — for *"the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light"* (6:23). Against her beauty he is told
not to covet (6:25, the tenth commandment's verb), and the argument is fire: *"Can a man scoop fire
into his lap, and his clothes not be burned?"* (6:27). A hungry thief can repay, even sevenfold; the
adulterer *"destroys his own soul"*, and the husband's jealousy *"won't regard any ransom"* (6:35).
See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]]. Proverbs names no court where Leviticus 20:10 sets death, and
the wiki records the two pictures side by side; with 6:16–19, the chapter touches the sixth through
tenth commandments.

Chapter 7 is the strange woman's last lecture, and the only one told as a story (7:1–27). After a
charge to keep the commandments *"as the apple of your eye"*, write them on the heart, and call
wisdom *"my sister"*, the father looks out through his lattice at dusk and sees a young man *"void
of understanding"* walk toward her corner as the night darkens. She meets him in *"the attire of a
prostitute"* though she is a wife, seizes him, and speaks: peace offerings at home and vows paid
today, a bed perfumed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon, and a husband away until the full moon. He
follows *"as an ox goes to the slaughter"*; *"Her house is the way to Sheol"* (7:27). See
[[The Young Man Void of Understanding]]. She stands in Wisdom's streets and uses Wisdom's words, which chapter 8 answers.

Chapter 8 answers at once: *"Doesn't wisdom cry out?"* (8:1–21). Wisdom stands on the heights, where
the paths meet, and at the city gates, and calls *"the sons of mankind"*, the simple and fools
included. Her words have *"nothing crooked or perverse in them"*; her instruction is to be taken
*"rather than silver"*. She defines *"The fear of Yahweh"* as *"to hate evil"* (8:13), claims the
counsel, understanding, and might Job 12:13 gave to God, and says *"By me kings reign"* (8:15). She
loves those who love her, is found by those who seek her (8:17), and fills their treasuries. See
[[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] and
[[Two Voices in the Street - The Strange Woman and Wisdom in Proverbs 7-9]]. The promise of riches
and honour stands, again, against Job's losses.

The speech's second half says where Wisdom comes from (8:22–36). *"Yahweh possessed me in the
beginning of his work"* (8:22), with *qānâ*, the verb of *"Get wisdom"* (4:7), and *rēʾšîṯ*, the
noun of Genesis 1:1. Born before depths and hills, she was *"there"* when the heavens were
established and the sea given its boundary, and beside God as *"the craftsman"* — or *nursling*, on
another reading of *ʾāmôn* — *"rejoicing in his whole world"*, her delight *"with the sons of men"*
(8:30–31). She closes in the father's words, *"Now therefore, my sons, listen to me"* (8:32): *"whoever
finds me, finds life"*, and *"All those who hate me love death"* (8:35–36). See
[[Wisdom at the Creation]] and
[[Possessed in the Beginning - Proverbs 8.22-31, John 1, and Colossians 1]]; the New Testament's
language for Christ at creation resembles the passage without citing it.

## Structure

- **1–9 Prologue and lectures** — the purpose statement (1:1–7), then a sequence of instructions
  addressed to *"my son"*, interleaved with speeches by Wisdom personified, who calls in the
  streets (1:20–33), was with Yahweh *"in the beginning of his work"* (8:22), and spreads a feast
  that competes with Folly's (9:1–18). The section opens and closes on the motto (1:7; 9:10).
- **10:1–22:16 The proverbs of Solomon** — a new heading (10:1) and a change of form: hundreds of
  two-line sentences, most of them antithetical, with no narrative sequence.
- **22:17–24:22 The words of the wise** — a hortatory collection that announces *"thirty excellent
  things"* (22:20), with a further short collection of *"sayings of the wise"* (24:23–34).
- **25–29 The proverbs of Solomon copied by Hezekiah's men** — comparisons and images, many in
  clusters on the fool, the sluggard, and the king.
- **30 The words of Agur** — a confession of ignorance (30:2–4), a prayer, and numerical sayings.
- **31 The words of King Lemuel** — his mother's instruction (31:1–9), and the acrostic poem on the
  *"worthy woman"* (31:10–31) that ends the book.

## Key themes

- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — available good must not be delayed (3:27–28).
- [[Favor and Grace]] — grace given to the humble (3:34).
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the neighbor’s trust protected; groundless strife refused (3:29–30).

- [[Creation]] — Yahweh founds earth and establishes heavens through wisdom (3:19–20).
- [[Life and Death]] — wisdom as a tree of life to those who retain her (3:18).
- [[Wisdom]] — the book's subject, named in its first purpose clause (1:2)
- [[The Fear of God]] — *"the beginning of knowledge"* (1:7) and of wisdom (9:10); *"to hate evil"*
  (8:13)
- [[Folly]] — wisdom's opposite, and the other way the book's reader can go
- [[Righteousness]] — *"righteousness, justice, and equity"* (1:3), wisdom's moral content
- [[Language and Speech]] — proverb, parable, riddle (1:6): a book about words, made of words
- [[Sexual Sin]] — the strange woman, introduced at 2:16–19 and developed in chapters 5–7; named
  *a man's wife* at 6:26, 29
- [[Marriage]] — called *"the covenant of her God"* (2:17); the wife of your youth as one's own well
  (5:15–19)
- [[Love]] — *"Be captivated always with her love"* (5:19)
- [[Retribution]] — the ordinary run of things, stated as sentences; see the Overview on reading
  it after Job
- [[Faith]] — *"Trust in Yahweh with all your heart"* (3:5)
- [[Walking with God]] — the way of wisdom and the path of the wicked (4:10–19)
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the dawning path and the dark way (4:18–19); *"the commandment is a
  lamp"* (6:23)
- [[Healing]] — the father's words as *"health to their whole body"* (4:22)
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — eyes fixed straight ahead (4:25)
- [[Work]] — the ant and the sluggard (6:6–11)
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — suretyship for another's debt, and poverty as a robber (6:1–11)
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — haughty eyes, first of the seven abominations (6:17)
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — *"he who sows discord among brothers"* (6:19)
- [[Theft and Restitution]] — the hungry thief who restores sevenfold (6:30–31)
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — *"By me kings reign, and princes decree justice"* (8:15)
- [[Prayer]] — *"Those who seek me diligently will find me"* (8:17), against 1:28
- [[Vengeance]] — the wronged husband's *"day of vengeance"*, which no ransom buys off (6:34–35)
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]], [[Oaths and Vows]] — *"Sacrifices of peace offerings are with me.
  Today I have paid my vows"* (7:14), the opening of a seduction
- [[Deception]] — a wife in *"the attire of a prostitute"*, *"with crafty intent"* (7:10)
- [[Suffering]] — Yahweh's reproof of the son he loves (3:11–12), the discipline Hebrews 12 quotes

## Key people
- [[Solomon]] — named in the headings at 1:1, 10:1, and 25:1; not named at 8:15–18, where Wisdom
  claims the kingship and the riches and honour 1 Kings 3 gives him
- [[David]] — named once, as Solomon's father (1:1)
- [[God]] — *Yahweh*, named in the motto (1:7) and throughout

## Key places
- [[Sheol]] — the insatiable realm of the dead, which the gang of 1:12 proposes to imitate, and to
  which the strange woman's house sinks (2:18; 5:5; 7:27; 9:18)
- [[Egypt]] — *"the yarn of Egypt"* on the strange woman's couch (7:16)
