# Job

## Overview

Job is the Bible's long argument about undeserved suffering, and it is built as a trap for the
reader's assumptions. A prose prologue tells us something none of the characters know: Job is
suffering *because* he is blameless, not despite it. Then forty poetic chapters unfold in which
five men reason about his case from the evidence available on the ground — and every one of them,
Job included, reasons without the one fact that would settle it.

The mechanism of that withholding is worth stating early, because it is unique in Scripture. Other
books show the heavenly court to a human being — Micaiah sees it, Isaiah sees it, Zechariah sees it —
and the seer then reports what he saw. **Job 1:6–12 is shown to no one.** There is no vision, no
dream, and no prophet; the narrator simply tells the reader, and then keeps the information out of
the story for forty-one chapters. The accuser who asks the question that starts everything leaves at
2:7 and is never named again by anyone, in heaven or on earth, and the epilogue attributes the whole
affair to Yahweh without mentioning him (42:11). See [[The Divine Council]] and [[Satan]].

The book's central target is the doctrine of **retribution**: the conviction that suffering is
payment for sin, so that a man's circumstances are a readable index of his standing with God.
That doctrine is not a straw man. It is the working assumption of much of Deuteronomy and
Proverbs, it is what Job's three friends say, and a great deal of what they say is quotable and
true in isolation. Job's protest is not that God is unjust in general but that in *his* case the
doctrine returns a false verdict, and he refuses to stop saying so — refuses to buy peace by
confessing sins he did not commit.

What makes the book more than a debate is that Job addresses God directly while the friends only
talk about him. Job's speeches oscillate violently between accusing God of assaulting him and
appealing to God for vindication, sometimes within a single breath, and out of that contradiction
come the book's most famous lines: the witness in heaven (16:19), the living Redeemer (19:25),
the wish that his words were written in a book. When God finally answers out of the whirlwind, he
answers neither the friends' theology nor Job's charge. He asks questions about wild goats and
storehouses of snow, and Job withdraws — not because he has been argued down, but because he has
been addressed.

The epilogue then delivers the verdict the poem withheld: God tells Eliphaz, *"My wrath is kindled
against you… for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has"*
(42:7). The man who accused God of hunting him like a beast is the one commended; the men who
defended God's justice are the ones who need Job's intercession. Every page of this wiki's
treatment of the friends' speeches is written knowing that sentence is coming.

## Structure

- **1–2 Prologue (prose)** — Job introduced, the heavenly court, two catastrophes, three friends.
- **3 Job's opening lament** — the poetry begins; Job curses the day he was born. **He does not curse
  God**, which is the accuser's prediction failing in the verse that most resembles its fulfilment,
  and the speech is addressed to nobody — not to God and not to the three men who answer it.
- **4–14 First cycle of speeches** — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar, each answered by Job. Job's first
  reply (6–7) is where he first names God as his assailant and, at 7:7, **first speaks to God** —
  the turn the friends never make in any of their speeches.
  His second (9–10) is where the dialogue becomes a lawsuit: Bildad is conceded in one line, the
  friends are not addressed again until chapter 12, and Job asks for the trial he says cannot be
  held — *"there is no umpire between us"* (9:33).
  Zophar's reply (11) closes the friends' first round at its harshest — *"God exacts of you less than
  your iniquity deserves"* (11:6) — and with its most generous offer, the last future any friend
  holds out to Job.
  Job's reply (12–14) ends the cycle and is its longest speech: the friends' wisdom taken from them in
  a hymn where God overthrows every human authority, the friends dismissed as men who lie for God,
  *"I know that I am righteous"* (13:18), and a prayer that grants hope to a felled tree and asks,
  of a man, *"If a man dies, shall he live again?"* (14:14).
- **15–21 Second cycle** — the same order, the consolations withdrawn, the accusations sharper.
  This is also where the friends stop arguing: chapters 18 and 20 are two set-piece portraits of
  the wicked man's ruin that answer nothing Job has said, and Job settles both at once in
  chapter 21 — the one speech in which he takes up the friends' subject instead of his own, speaks to
  them throughout and never to God, and puts their portraits to a count: the wicked grow old, keep
  their children, die *"in an instant"*, and are buried with a vigil and a crowd.
- **22–27 Third cycle** — the pattern breaks down; Bildad's speech is six verses and Zophar has none.
  Eliphaz opens it by finally naming Job's crimes (22:6–9) — the canonical list of mercy negated
  clause by clause, and supported by nothing — and closes it with the dialogue's warmest offer, whose
  last line, *"He will even deliver him who is not innocent … through the cleanness of your hands"*
  (22:30), the epilogue fulfils with Eliphaz as the guilty man and Job as the intercessor.
  Job's reply (23–24) answers neither the charges nor the offer: it seeks a God who cannot be found
  in any direction — *"When he has tried me, I shall come out like gold"* (23:10) — and then gives
  every one of Eliphaz's invented crimes a real, unpunished defendant, ending in a dare (24:25) that
  neither of the remaining friends takes up.
  Bildad's reply (25) is six verses and has no *you* in it: a hymn to God's dominion and a descent
  from moon and stars to *"man, who is a worm"* (25:6) — Job's own 9:2 and his own worm handed back as
  a definition, and the last word any of the friends speaks.
  Job's reply (26–27) is spoken twice over in the place where Zophar should answer — *"Job again took
  up his parable"* (27:1). It mocks Bildad's help, outbids his hymn with one that runs from Sheol to
  the heavens and ends *"How small a whisper do we hear of him!"* (26:14), and swears the oath the
  whole case rests on: *"As God lives, who has taken away my right … Until I die I will not put away my
  integrity from me"* (27:2, 5). Its last section (27:13–23) recites the wicked man's ruin in words
  that contradict Job's own chapter 21 — Zophar's lost third speech, on one reconstruction.
- **28 Interlude** — the hymn to wisdom: *"Where shall wisdom be found?"* No heading follows 27:1, so
  as received it is Job's; but it has no *I*, no *you* and no complaint, and it reads as a pause between
  the failed debate and the speakers who will try to go past it. The miner finds every precious thing
  in the earth and brings the hidden to light (28:1–11); wisdom is found nowhere, priced above the gold
  of Ophir and the topaz of Cush, and known to Death only by rumour (28:12–22); God alone knows its
  place, having seen it when he measured the wind and the rain (28:23–27). Then: *"the fear of the
  Lord, that is wisdom. To depart from evil is understanding"* (28:28) — **1:1's description of Job,
  made into a definition.** The hymn calls wise the man the friends call guilty, and its rain and
  lightning (28:26) are the whirlwind's (38:25), in the same Hebrew.
- **29–31 Job's final defense** — remembrance, present misery, and an oath of innocence.
  Chapter 29 is the remembrance, and the only speech of Job's with no complaint and no addressee:
  *"Oh that I were as in the months of old, as in the days when God watched over me"* (29:2). God's
  lamp on his head and his children around him; the princes silent at the gate; and at the centre
  the reason — *"I put on righteousness, and it clothed me"* (29:14), eyes to the blind, a father to
  the needy, the widow singing — **Eliphaz's charge sheet of 22:6–9 answered item by item** before
  chapter 31 swears to it. It ends with Job *"as one who comforts the mourners"* (29:25), the
  friends' own verb.
  Chapter 30 is its negative, turned three times on *"But now"* (30:1, 9, 16): the young men who hid
  from him now mock him, the princes' silence has become spitting, and the man who heard every cry at
  the gate cries to God and is not answered — *"I cry to you, and you do not answer me"* (30:20), **his
  last direct address to God before 40:4**. At its centre, *"I have become like dust and ashes"* (30:19),
  Abraham's pair and the words of 42:6; at its end, *"my harp has turned to mourning"* (30:31).
  Chapter 31 is the oath that closes the case: a chain of *"If I have …"* clauses, each denying a sin
  under a curse on himself, from the look (*"I made a covenant with my eyes"*, 31:1) to the slave's
  lawsuit (*"Didn't one fashion us in the womb?"*, 31:15), the poor of Eliphaz's charge sheet, gold, the
  sun and moon, and sin hidden *"like Adam"* (31:33). He signs it — *"Behold, here is my signature! Let the
  Almighty answer me!"* (31:35) — and *"The words of Job are ended"* (31:40). **The friends do not
  reply; God does, and mentions none of it.**
- **32–37 Elihu** — a fourth speaker, unannounced and unmentioned afterwards.
  Chapters 32–33 bring him on in prose — *"Elihu the son of Barachel, the Buzite, of the family of Ram"*
  (32:2), the only pedigree in the book — angry four times, at Job *"because he justified himself rather
  than God"* and at the friends *"because they had found no answer, and yet had condemned Job"*
  (32:2–5). A young man's preface claims wisdom by *"a spirit in man"* against age (32:8–9), tells the
  friends there was *"no one who convinced Job"* — 9:33's *mōkîaḥ* — and bursts like new wineskins
  (32:19). Then, to Job by name, which no friend ever used: a man of clay without terror (33:6–7), Job
  quoted back word for word (33:11 from 13:27), and God who *"speaks once, yes twice"* — by dream and by
  pain — to turn a man from the pit; and *"an angel, an interpreter, one among a thousand"*, at whose word
  God says *"I have found a ransom"* (33:23–24). It ends *"I desire to justify you"* (33:32). **No one
  answers.**
  Chapters 34–35 are his second and third speeches, and the turn from the new to the familiar. To
  *"wise men"* as a jury, he quotes Job's oath (*"God has taken away my right"*, 34:5, from 27:2) and puts
  in his mouth the wicked's question Job had disowned (*"It profits a man nothing"*, 34:9, from 21:15);
  restates Bildad's 8:3 (*"far be it from God"*, 34:10–12); grounds God's justice in his holding every
  breath (*"all flesh would perish together"*, 34:14–15); and wishes Job *"tried to the end"* (34:36). To
  Job, he returns 7:20 and Eliphaz's 22:2–3 (35:6–8), and says the oppressed go unanswered because no one
  asks *"Where is God my Maker, who gives songs in the night?"* (35:10). Both speeches end on Job's words
  *"without knowledge"* (34:35; 35:16) — **the charge God opens with at 38:2**.
  Chapters 36–37 are the fourth speech, headed *"Elihu also continued"* (36:1), and the best of him. It
  puts his one new idea in a line — *"He delivers the afflicted by their affliction, and opens their ear in
  oppression"* (36:15) — and turns it on Job (*"you have chosen this rather than affliction"*, 36:21).
  Then, from *"Behold, God is great, and we don't know him"* (36:26), a hymn to God in the weather: rain
  from the *ʾēd* of Genesis 2:6 (36:27), thunder as *"the voice of his majesty"* (37:4), snow, ice, and a sky
  like cast metal, and Job examined by name in God's own grammar — *"Do you know …? Can you …?"*
  (37:14–18). **It names the whirlwind's subjects and reaches the opposite conclusion**: no one can make
  his case before God (37:19–20), *"We can't reach the Almighty"* (37:23) — and the next verse is
  *"Then Yahweh answered Job out of the whirlwind"* (38:1).
- **38–41 Yahweh answers out of the whirlwind** — two speeches, ending in Behemoth and Leviathan.
  The first (38:1–40:5) opens with the narrator's first *Yahweh* since 2:7 and the storm-word of Job's
  9:17, and its first sentence uses chapter 3's darkness against him: *"Who is this who darkens counsel by
  words without knowledge?"* (38:2). Then the trial Job asked for, on his terms (13:22) with the roles
  reversed — *"I will question you, then you answer me!"* (38:3) — and more than fifty questions with no
  word of the wager, the friends, Elihu, or Job's suffering. The earth's foundation, with the sons of God
  singing (38:4–7); the sea born from the womb and shut in by doors, chapter 3's womb and doors (38:8–11);
  the dawn that shakes the wicked out (38:12–15); the gates of death; the houses of light and darkness, and
  *"you were born then"* (38:21); rain *"on a land where no man is"* (38:26); the stars of 9:9 under law;
  then food for the lioness and the raven's crying young (38:39–41), and a gallery of creatures outside
  human use — goat, wild donkey, wild ox, ostrich, war-horse, hawk — ending on the eagle: *"Where the slain
  are, there he is"* (39:30). God names Job the *mōkîaḥ*, the umpire of 9:33 (40:2), and Job answers with
  his hand on his mouth: *"I have spoken once … Yes, twice, but I will proceed no further"* (40:5).
  **Silence, not repentance — and God speaks again.**
  The second (40:6–41:34) repeats the heading and the summons, and asks the one question either speech asks
  about justice: *"Will you condemn me, that you may be justified?"* (40:8) — Job's own *"Do not condemn me"*
  (10:2) turned round. It offers Job God's robe (*"Array yourself with honor and majesty"*, 40:10) and his work —
  bring every proud man low — and concedes that if he can, *"your own right hand can save you"* (40:14). Then
  two creatures: **Behemoth**, *"which I made as well as you"*, *"the chief of the ways of God"* (40:15, 19),
  Wisdom's title in Proverbs 8:22; and **Leviathan**, the monster Job called on at 3:8, thirty-four verses long
  — no hook can land him, no covenant bind him, no weapon touch him, and *"None is so fierce that he dare stir
  him up. Who then is he who can stand before me? Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?"*
  (41:10–11), the line Paul quotes at Romans 11:35. His eyes are *"the eyelids of the morning"* (41:18; 3:9), and
  he is *"king over all the sons of pride"* (41:34). **No combat, no reason, no word of the wager — and the
  speeches end.**
- **42 Epilogue** — Job's retraction, the friends rebuked, and restoration.

## Key themes

- [[Suffering]]
- [[Retribution]]
- [[Integrity]] — *tummâ*, the word of 2:3 and 2:9 and of Job's oath at 27:5; the thing the second
  test is designed to find the price of
- [[The Fear of God]]
- [[Wisdom]]
- [[Hope]]
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]]
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — the rite at 1:20, the ashes at 2:8, a funeral held over a living man at
  2:12–13, and then the whole of chapter 3, where the rite's silence turns into its speech
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the book's controlling image, and the one Job and God both reach for
  first: chapter 3 asks for a day to be given back to darkness, and 38:2 opens the whirlwind by
  accusing Job of *darkening counsel*
- [[Righteousness]]
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the two oaths that carry Job's case, *"As God lives"* at 27:2 and the self-curses
  of chapter 31, signed at 31:35
- [[Sin]]
- [[Judgment]]
- [[Life and Death]]
- [[Creation]] — the whirlwind's subject: the earth's foundation, the sea's doors, the houses of light and
  darkness, and the stars of 9:9 under law (38:4–33)
- [[Providence]] — rain *"on a land where no man is"* (38:26), and the lioness and the raven fed (38:39–41)
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — the proud Job is told to bring low (40:11–12), and Leviathan, *"king over all
  the sons of pride"* (41:34)

## Key places

- [[Uz]]
- [[Tema]] — the oasis whose caravans die at the dry wadi of 6:19; not to be confused with Teman
- [[Teman]] — Eliphaz's, and the only one of the three friends' homelands that can be located
- [[Shuah]] — Bildad's; usually the Shuah of Genesis 25:2, sometimes Assyrian Sūḫu, certainly neither
- [[Buz]] — Elihu's clan; Uz's brother among Nahor's sons (Genesis 22:21)
- [[Naamah]] — Zophar's, unattested anywhere else and unlocated
- [[Sheol]]
- [[Ophir]] — the byword for fine gold, thrown in the wadi at 22:24 and outpriced by wisdom at 28:16
- [[Cush]] — the WEB's *Ethiopia*; its topaz is the last precious thing named against wisdom (28:19)
- [[The Jordan]] — swelling to Behemoth's mouth (40:23); perhaps any river in flood
