# Job

## Overview

Job is the Bible's long argument about undeserved suffering, and it is built as a trap for the
reader's assumptions. A prose prologue tells us something none of the characters know: Job is
suffering *because* he is blameless, not despite it. Then forty poetic chapters unfold in which
five men reason about his case from the evidence available on the ground — and every one of them,
Job included, reasons without the one fact that would settle it.

The book's central target is the doctrine of **retribution**: the conviction that suffering is
payment for sin, so that a man's circumstances are a readable index of his standing with God.
That doctrine is not a straw man. It is the working assumption of much of Deuteronomy and
Proverbs, it is what Job's three friends say, and a great deal of what they say is quotable and
true in isolation. Job's protest is not that God is unjust in general but that in *his* case the
doctrine returns a false verdict, and he refuses to stop saying so — refuses to buy peace by
confessing sins he did not commit.

What makes the book more than a debate is that Job addresses God directly while the friends only
talk about him. Job's speeches oscillate violently between accusing God of assaulting him and
appealing to God for vindication, sometimes within a single breath, and out of that contradiction
come the book's most famous lines: the witness in heaven (16:19), the living Redeemer (19:25),
the wish that his words were written in a book. When God finally answers out of the whirlwind, he
answers neither the friends' theology nor Job's charge. He asks questions about wild goats and
storehouses of snow, and Job withdraws — not because he has been argued down, but because he has
been addressed.

The epilogue then delivers the verdict the poem withheld: God tells Eliphaz, *"My wrath is kindled
against you… for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has"*
(42:7). The man who accused God of hunting him like a beast is the one commended; the men who
defended God's justice are the ones who need Job's intercession. Every page of this wiki's
treatment of the friends' speeches is written knowing that sentence is coming.

## Structure

- **1–2 Prologue (prose)** — Job introduced, the heavenly court, two catastrophes, three friends.
- **3 Job's opening lament** — the poetry begins; Job curses the day he was born.
- **4–14 First cycle of speeches** — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar, each answered by Job.
- **15–21 Second cycle** — the same order, the consolations withdrawn, the accusations sharper.
  This is also where the friends stop arguing: chapters 18 and 20 are two set-piece portraits of
  the wicked man's ruin that answer nothing Job has said, and Job settles both at once in
  chapter 21.
- **22–27 Third cycle** — the pattern breaks down; Bildad's speech is six verses and Zophar has none.
- **28 Interlude** — the hymn to wisdom: *"Where shall wisdom be found?"*
- **29–31 Job's final defense** — remembrance, present misery, and an oath of innocence.
- **32–37 Elihu** — a fourth speaker, unannounced and unmentioned afterwards.
- **38–41 Yahweh answers out of the whirlwind** — two speeches, ending in Behemoth and Leviathan.
- **42 Epilogue** — Job's retraction, the friends rebuked, and restoration.
