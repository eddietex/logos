# Ecclesiastes

## Overview

A book of inquiry into what human beings gain from their work within a world they cannot
master. Its heading identifies [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] as the son of [[David]], king in
[[Jerusalem]], without naming [[Solomon]]. The wiki keeps the textual speaker distinct from
an asserted historical author. No composition date is established here.

The opening poem, [[Vanity of Vanities]], asks about gain, describes recurring natural movements and
passing generations, and ends with forgotten predecessors and successors. Its claim of vanity
sets a question for the book, not a license to omit the later affirmations of enjoyment,
wisdom's relative worth, and accountability to God.

[[The Burden of Wisdom]] turns to the first person. The Preacher, king over Israel in Jerusalem,
searches out all that is done under the sky by wisdom, and calls the task a heavy burden God has
given (1:13). This is the book's first mention of God. The results are two verdicts of vanity
and chasing after wind, a saying that the crooked cannot be made straight (1:15), and a saying
that much wisdom brings much grief (1:18).

[[The Trial of Pleasure]] tests the opposite instrument. With wisdom still guiding him (2:3), the
Preacher tries mirth, wine, great works, gardens, servants, treasure, and music, withholding his
heart from no joy. His heart rejoiced, and that was his portion (2:10), yet the verdict is vanity,
a chasing after wind, and *"no profit under the sun"* (2:11) — the first direct answer to the
question of 1:3. God is not mentioned in the unit.

[[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]] returns to wisdom, madness, and folly. Wisdom excels folly as light
excels darkness (2:13), yet one event happens to them all (2:14): the wise man dies like the fool and
is forgotten like him (2:16). The Preacher asks why he was then more wise (2:15) and reports that he
hated life (2:17). Both findings, the ranking and the common end, stand side by side.

[[The Labor Left to Another]] (2:18–26) turns hatred toward labor whose product must pass to
an unknown successor. Skill cannot secure its future, and its present cost is sleepless grief.
Yet eating, drinking, and enjoying good in labor come from God's hand (2:24); God gives wisdom,
knowledge, and joy, and directs the sinner's accumulation to the one who pleases him (2:26).
The closing vanity verdict remains alongside this affirmation.

[[A Time for Every Purpose]] (3:1–15) sets fourteen pairs of times beside the recurring
question of labor's profit. God makes everything beautiful in its time, yet humans cannot
find out his whole work (11). Enjoyment in labor is his gift (13); his work endures and calls
forth fear (14). Order, burden, limited knowledge, and present enjoyment remain together.

[[From Dust to Dust]] (3:16–22) observes wickedness where justice belongs and affirms God's
judgment. Humanity's shared breath, death, and dust with animals expose its limits; the WEB
asks about the spirits' destinations. Rejoicing in works remains a portion without knowledge
of what follows. The question here and 12:7's later affirmation remain distinct.

[[The Tears of the Oppressed]] (4:1–3) sees tears without comfort and power with oppressors.
The Preacher praises the dead above the living and the one who has not yet been above both,
for not seeing evil. The claim remains beside 3:22's enjoyment and 3:17's affirmed judgment;
no rescue is narrated here.

[[Two Are Better Than One]] (4:4–12) finds all labor and achievement to be the envy of a
neighbor, rejects the fool's folded hands, and prefers a quiet handful to two handfuls with
labor. A man with neither son nor brother labors without end and asks for whom. Against him,
two are better than one: they lift, warm, and defend each other, and a threefold cord holds.

[[The Youth and the Old King]] (4:13–16) ranks a poor and wise youth above an old and foolish
king who no longer takes admonition. A youth comes out of prison to reign and draws countless
people, yet those who come after will not rejoice in him. Wisdom keeps its ranking; the
following it wins is vanity.

[[Let Your Words Be Few]] (5:1–7) turns from observation to the book's first direct commands,
and they concern worship. Drawing near to listen is better than the sacrifice of fools; words
before God should be few, because God is in heaven; a vow is to be paid without delay, not
excused as a mistake. Many dreams and many words are vanities, *"but you must fear God"* (7) —
the fear that 3:14 named and 12:13 will make the conclusion.

[[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]] (5:8–20) returns to oppression and follows
wealth through dissatisfaction, lost sleep, misfortune, and empty-handed departure. Yet
life's days, wealth, and the power to enjoy it are gifts of God (18–20). Possession and
enjoyment are distinguished without explaining away the injustice of the opening.

[[Riches Without Enjoyment]] (6:1–9) distinguishes God's giving of wealth from the power
to enjoy it, now explicitly withheld (2). Neither a hundred children nor two thousand
years outweigh good unexperienced: the stillborn child has more rest (3–6). Unfilled
appetite leads to questions about wisdom's gain and a preference for sight over wandering
desire, followed by the vanity refrain (7–9). No cause is given for the withheld power.

[[Who Knows What Is Good]] (6:10–12) closes this part of the inquiry with human limits:
what has been was named long ago, the person cannot contend with the mightier, many words
create vanity, and life passes like a shadow. God is a plausible but unnamed referent of
the mightier party. Questions about good and what follows remain unanswered beside the
earlier positive judgments about enjoyment (3:12–13; 5:18).

[[The House of Mourning]] (7:1–14) answers with *better than* sayings: a good name above
perfume, the day of death above birth, mourning above feasting, sorrow above laughter, the
wise man's rebuke above the fool's song. Wisdom can be corrupted by a bribe (7), yet it
defends and preserves life (12). God has made the crooked (13) and set prosperity beside
adversity (14). The preference for mourning stands beside the book's commended enjoyment.

[[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]] (7:15–29) reports a righteous man perishing and
a wicked man living long (15), warns against being overly righteous or overly wise and
against being too wicked (16–17), and grounds the counsel in the fear of God (18). No one
does good without sin (20). Wisdom proves far off (23–24); the Preacher finds a woman more
bitter than death (26), one man among a thousand (28), and one unqualified finding: God made
man upright, but they search for many schemes (29).

[[The King and the Limits of Power]] (8:1–9) commends wisdom's effect on the face and
obedience to the king because of the oath to God. The king's word is supreme (4), but no
person controls the day of death (8), and human dominion can cause harm (9). Knowing the
time and procedure (5) stands beside not knowing what will be (7); protection for keeping
the commandment stands beside heavy misery and harmful rule.

**Reading tensions.** The book can affirm wisdom over folly (2:13) while questioning its lasting
advantage in death (2:14–16); commend enjoyment as God's gift (3:12–13) while describing oppression
without comfort (4:1–3); and insist on judgment (12:14) while reporting reversed outcomes
(8:14). These statements will remain visible together as their pericopes are ingested.
This overview and the queue are based on a reading of all twelve chapters of stored WEB;
only 1:1–8:9 currently has ingested passage pages.

## Structure

- 1:1–11 — heading, verdict of vanity, and the poem of recurrence.
- 1:12–2:26 — royal inquiry through wisdom, pleasure, mortality, and labor.
- 3:1–6:12 — appointed times, injustice, companionship, worship, wealth, and enjoyment.
- 7:1–10:20 — sayings and observations testing wisdom against death, power, and uncertainty.
- 11:1–12:8 — action despite uncertainty, joy in youth, aging, and the return of the vanity refrain.
- 12:9–14 — an assessment of the Preacher and the concluding charge to fear God.

These groupings describe the present text; they are not a reconstruction of its composition.

## Key themes

- [[Kingship and Empire]], [[Oaths and Vows]], and [[Law and Obedience]] — the royal command
  and the oath to God (8:2–5), held beside harmful dominion (8:9).
- [[Wisdom]], [[Life and Death]], and [[War]] — fitting timing without foreknowledge and
  no control over the day of death (8:5–8). See [[The King and the Limits of Power]].

- [[Righteousness]], [[Retribution]], and [[Sin]] — the righteous who perish and the wicked
  who live long (7:15), the overly righteous (16), and no one without sin (20).
- [[The Fear of God]] and [[Image of God]] — the one who fears God comes out of them all
  (7:18); God made man upright (29).
- [[Sexual Sin]] and [[Language and Speech]] — the woman more bitter than death (7:26); the
  servant's curse not to be listened for (21–22).

- [[Mourning and Lament]], [[Life and Death]], and [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — the house of
  mourning above feasting, sorrow above laughter, and joy commanded in prosperity (7:1–14).
- [[Providence]] — the crooked God has made; prosperity and adversity side by side (7:13–14).

- [[Names and Naming]] — naming long ago within an account of human limits (6:10);
  no namer is identified. See [[Who Knows What Is Good]].

- [[Burial and the Grave]], [[Blessing]], and [[Life and Death]] — many children and years
  without good or burial; the stillborn comparison tests outward flourishing (6:1–9).

- [[Wealth and Possessions]], [[Joy and Enjoyment]], and [[Nakedness and Covering]] — harmful
  wealth and empty-handed departure beside enjoyment as God's gift (5:8–20).

- [[The Fear of God]], [[Oaths and Vows]], and [[Language and Speech]] — few words before God,
  paid vows, and the book's first command to fear God (5:1–7).

- [[Kingship and Empire]] and [[Remembrance]] — the foolish king, the youth from prison, and
  the successor later generations will not rejoice in (4:13–16).

- [[Envy]], [[Folly]], and [[Brotherhood]] — labor as rivalry, the fool's folded hands, and the
  companion who lifts the one who falls (4:4–12).

- [[Suffering]], [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]], and [[Compassion and Mercy]] — oppression
  without comfort (4:1–3), held beside the book's affirmations of enjoyment.

- [[Judgment]] — corrupt justice observed, divine judgment affirmed (3:16–17).
- [[Testing]] — humanity brought to recognize its likeness to animals (3:18–20).

- [[Providence]] — God’s enduring work exceeds the observer’s knowledge (3:11–15).
- [[The Fear of God]] — the purpose named for God’s enduring work (3:14).

- [[Vanity]] — the opening verdict and its recurring tests.
- [[Work]] — the question of gain and the possibility of enjoyment.
- [[Wisdom]] — valuable, yet unable to master every outcome.
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — tested and found without profit (2:1–11), then received from God (2:24–26).
- [[Life and Death]] — generations pass; the wise man dies just like the fool (2:16).
- [[Remembrance]] — what survives in the minds of those who come after.
- [[Creation]] — the world that continues as its observers pass.
- [[Light and Darkness]] — wisdom excels folly as light excels darkness (2:13).

## Key people

- [[God]] — giver of burden and enjoyment; his work endures (3:10–15); in heaven, owed paid vows,
  and to be feared (5:1–7); gives life, wealth, and power to enjoy it (5:18–20), but also
  gives wealth without that power (6:2), with no reason supplied; makes the crooked and
  sets prosperity beside adversity (7:13–14); to be feared (7:18); made man upright (7:29).

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — the speaker named by title.
- [[David]] — named in the heading.
- [[Solomon]] — traditional association, not an explicit name in the book.
