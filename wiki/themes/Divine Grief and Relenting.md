# Divine Grief and Relenting

## Definition / overview

Whether God can change his mind, and whether he can be hurt — two questions the Bible raises in the
same verse and never fully separates.

The word at the centre of it is **nāḥam**. In the stem used of God it covers a range English cannot
hold in one word: *to regret*, *to relent*, *to be moved to pity*, *to change one's course of
action*, and — in a different stem — *to comfort*. Genesis 6:6 uses it of God for the first time in
Scripture: *"Yahweh was sorry that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him in his heart."*
Ten generations earlier, Lamech had used the same root of his son, hoping Noah would *comfort* them
in their toil (5:29). The book gets the human hope and the divine regret out of one root, and puts
them four verses apart.

The second half of 6:6 is the part most often passed over and is at least as remarkable. *"It
grieved him in his heart"* — *wayyitʿaṣṣēb ʾel-libbô*, from **ʿāṣab**, the root of the *pain* God
assigned to the woman in childbearing and the *toil* he assigned to the man in the field (3:16–17).
The word for what the fall cost humanity is the word for what humanity's corruption costs God. It is
the only place in Genesis that reports an emotion located in God's *heart*, and it stands three
verses after the report that human hearts were *"continually only evil"*. Two hearts are examined in
this paragraph, and only one of them is condemned.

**The problem, stated plainly.** Numbers 23:19 says *"God is not a man, that he should lie, nor a
son of man, that he should repent [nāḥam]."* 1 Samuel 15:29 says *"the Strength of Israel will not
lie nor repent, for he is not a man, that he should repent."* And 1 Samuel 15 says that twice, in a
chapter that also says, twice, that Yahweh *did* repent of making Saul king (15:11, 15:35). The
tension is not manufactured by later theology; it is inside a single chapter of the Hebrew Bible.

**Four approaches, and what each costs.**

1. **Anthropomorphism.** God is described in human terms because human terms are the only ones
   available, and the language reports a real change in God's *relation* to the world rather than a
   change in God. This is the classical consensus — Philo, Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin — and it fits
   the "does not repent" texts without strain. Its cost is that it must treat some of Scripture's
   most vivid statements about God as accommodations, and it can slide into treating the grief of
   6:6 as merely a figure of speech, which flattens a verse the narrator plainly meant to land.
2. **A distinction between kinds of divine speech.** God does not go back on his character or his
   sworn oaths (which is what Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 are about, both in contexts of
   irrevocable words) but announced intentions are conditional by nature. Jeremiah 18:7–10 states
   this as explicit policy: if a nation under an announced judgment turns, *"I will relent of the
   disaster"*, and vice versa. Jonah's whole complaint is that he knew this in advance (Jonah 4:2).
   This is probably the most textually grounded option, and it handles most cases; it is less
   obviously an answer to 6:6, which is regret over the past rather than relenting about the future.
3. **Relational or covenantal responsiveness.** God is genuinely affected by his creatures — the
   grief is real grief, the responses are real responses — while his purposes and knowledge are
   unchanged. Much of modern theology sits here, and it takes 6:6 at face value in a way (1) does
   not.
4. **Open theism.** The future is genuinely open to God, and *"I am sorry that I have made them"*
   means what it appears to mean. Genesis 6:6 is one of this position's primary texts. Its cost is
   the large body of Scripture asserting foreknowledge and settled purpose, and it remains a
   minority position.

Two things worth holding whichever way one leans. The verse never says God was **wrong** — regret
over an outcome is not an admission of error about a decision. And in the same paragraph, the God
who grieves is the God who favors: 6:6 and 6:8 are two verses apart, and both are about what is
happening in God rather than in the world.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 6:6–7** — the first use of *nāḥam* of God, twice in two verses, as the ground of the
  flood. See [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- **Genesis 8:21** *(not yet ingested)* — the counterpart, and the theme's most important turn: God
  restates the verdict of 6:5 almost word for word (*"the imagination of man's heart is evil from
  his youth"*) and draws the opposite conclusion — *"I will not again curse the ground any more for
  man's sake"*. The same fact about humanity produces the flood in chapter 6 and the promise never
  to repeat it in chapter 8.
- **Exodus 32:9–14** — Moses argues, and *"Yahweh repented of the evil which he said he would do to
  his people"*. The pattern's clearest case of intercession changing an announced outcome.
- **Numbers 23:19** — *"God is not a man … that he should repent"*, spoken by Balaam about a
  blessing that cannot be revoked.
- **1 Samuel 15:11, 29, 35** — the chapter that contains both sides of the question, unreconciled.
- **2 Samuel 24:16; 1 Chronicles 21:15** — the destroying angel stopped: *"Yahweh relented of the
  disaster"*.
- **Jeremiah 18:7–10; 26:3, 13, 19** — relenting stated as a general principle of how God deals with
  nations, and used as an argument for repentance.
- **Jonah 3:9–10; 4:2** — Nineveh spared, and a prophet furious about it, quoting Exodus 34:6 back
  at God as the reason he fled.
- **Joel 2:13; Amos 7:1–6** — *"he relents from sending calamity"* as part of the creed, and a
  prophet twice stopping a judgment by asking.
- **Hosea 11:8–9** — the most concentrated statement of divine anguish in the prophets: *"My heart
  is turned within me, my compassion is aroused"*, followed immediately by *"for I am God, and not
  man"* — the same contrast as Numbers 23:19, drawn to the opposite effect.
- **Isaiah 63:9–10; Ephesians 4:30** — God afflicted in his people's affliction, and the Spirit
  grieved; the New Testament's continuation of the *ʿāṣab* side of Genesis 6:6.
- **Luke 19:41; John 11:35** — Jesus weeping over a city and at a grave, which is where Christian
  theology has usually located the answer to the question this verse raises.
