# City and Civilization

## Definition / overview

Human beings building things that outlast them — walls, trades, crafts, institutions — and what the
Bible makes of the fact that all of it starts in the line of a murderer.

Genesis 4:17–22 is the origin story of civilization, and it fits in six verses. Cain builds the
first city and names it for his son. Five generations later Lamech's three sons each found a domain
of human practice: Jabal *"the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock"*, Jubal
*"the father of all who handle the harp and pipe"*, Tubal Cain *"the forger of every cutting
instrument of brass and iron."* Economy, art, technology. See [[Jabal]], [[Jubal]],
[[Tubal Cain]].

Three things are worth holding together, because most readings of this passage drop one of them.

**1. These are goods.** Not neutral facilities that could go either way — actual goods, and ones
the Bible goes on to depend on. The patriarchs live in tents with livestock for the whole second
half of Genesis. The temple runs on harps. Metal tools are the substrate of every trade in the
ancient world, agriculture first. Whatever Genesis is doing here, it is not describing the invention
of vice.

**2. They come from the cursed line.** Cain was sentenced to wander and built a city; his
descendants made the things human life is furnished with. The narrative places the whole inventory
in the family it has just watched commit the first murder, four verses before that family's head
sings about killing a man.

**3. Genesis does not resolve this.** No verdict is entered on the city, the tent, the harp, or the
forge. Two over-readings are common and both are over-readings: that the passage means to taint
culture with Cain's guilt (which cannot survive the patriarchs living Jabal's life and David
playing Jubal's instrument), and that it means to celebrate human progress (which cannot survive
where it is placed). What the text actually does is refuse to sort the world into a clean half and a
dirty one. The people who make the world habitable are the same people who make it dangerous, and
Genesis reports both without arbitration.

**The strand this opens.** The city becomes one of the Bible's largest running images, and it stays
double all the way through. Babel is the second city and the first collective project, and it is
scattered (11:1–9) — not for being a city but for what it was built to secure. Sodom is a city.
So is Jerusalem, the city God chooses and puts his name in. Babylon is the city that stands for
every human order set against him. And the last thing the Bible shows is a city — but one that
*"comes down out of heaven from God"* (Revelation 21:2) rather than being built, which is the
resolution Genesis 4 withholds: the thing humanity started making east of Eden is finally given
rather than achieved.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 4:17** — Cain builds the first city, named for his son Enoch, in defiance of or in
  submission to a sentence to wander. See [[Enoch (city)]].
- **Genesis 4:20–22** — herding, music, and metalwork founded in three verses, all by Lamech's
  children, with no evaluation attached.
- **Genesis 9:20** — the register of founded crafts resumes on the far side of the flood, and adds
  viticulture: *"Noah began to be a farmer, and planted a vineyard."* The first entry not attributed to
  Cain's line, and the pattern is unchanged — Genesis names the origin of a craft and shows its cost in
  the same paragraph, exactly as 4:22's forging of bronze and iron is followed two verses later by the
  Song of the Sword. The book has now derived herding, music, metalwork, the city, and wine, and has
  passed a verdict on none of them. See [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] and
  [[The Vine and the Cup]].
- **Genesis 10:8–12** — eight cities at once, and the first *kingdom*. [[Nimrod]] founds [[Babel]],
  Erech, Accad, and Calneh in [[Shinar]], and — on the WEB's reading of 10:11 — [[Nineveh]], Rehoboth
  Ir, Calah, and Resen in [[Assyria]]. The register that began with one city built by a murderer now
  takes in the capitals of both empires that will destroy Israel and Judah, and Genesis still passes
  no verdict; it names the origin and moves on to the next name in the list. This is where
  civilization stops being a set of crafts and becomes a polity — see [[Kingship and Empire]] and
  [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the second city in Scripture and the first collective one: brick and bitumen
  because the plain has no stone, a tower with its head in the heavens, and a stated purpose — a name,
  and no scattering. God comes down, confuses the language, and *"They stopped building the city."*
  The pattern holds exactly: the manufacturing process is explained approvingly, the ambition is
  quoted, and no verdict on building as such is entered anywhere in the nine verses. What Genesis
  stops is not the city but what the city was for. See [[The Tower of Babel]] and
  [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]]. Babylon's whole later career as *the* city against God begins here;
  see [[Babel and Babylon the Great]].
- **Genesis 13:10–18** — the theme's first human choice between the two ways of living, and Genesis
  still declines to grade it. Lot takes the well-watered plain, then *"the cities of the plain"*, then
  *"moved his tent as far as Sodom"* (13:12), and by 19:1 he is sitting in its gate; Abram is given
  the whole land and pitches under trees at [[Hebron]]. The narrator's only editorial is about the
  men of Sodom (13:13), not about cities. Hebrews 11:9–10 reads the contrast as deliberate and then
  reverses it — the tent-dweller is the one *"looking for the city which has foundations, whose
  builder and maker is God"*. See [[Abram and Lot Separate]] and [[Sodom]].
- **Genesis 19:1–29** — the theme's first destroyed city, and the only one Genesis takes the reader
  inside. Three of Genesis's cities are now on the page and none of them ends well: Enoch, built by
  a fratricide (4:17); Babel, abandoned in confusion (11:8); Sodom, burned. **What the chapter
  indicts is specifically urban, and it is the crowd.** The offence is committed by *"the men of the
  city … both young and old, all the people from every quarter"* (19:4) — a whole population acting
  as one body against two strangers — and the sentence falls on the town rather than on individuals.
  Genesis's picture of the city is not that building is wrong but that a city can act, and that when
  it does there is nobody left to appeal to. Lot's own arc completes here: tent to gate to a cave
  above [[Zoar]], the one man in Genesis who moves into a city and the one who has to be dragged out
  of one. See [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: the cities of refuge as civilization
  domesticating blood vengeance (Numbers 35); Jerusalem as the chosen city (2 Samuel 5, Psalms
  46, 48, 122); Babylon as the archetype of the city against God (Isaiah 13, Jeremiah 50–51,
  Revelation 17–18); Jeremiah 29:7, on seeking the welfare of the city you are exiled to;
  *"he looked for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God"* (Hebrews 11:10);
  the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21–22).

- **Proverbs 1:20–21** — [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]]: wisdom located in the city, not apart
  from it — the street, the squares, the noisy corners, and *"the entrance of the city gates"*, where
  the city's business and judgment were done. The gang of 1:11 *lurks*; Wisdom shouts where the
  crowds are.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:10–11, 14

The city rejoices when the righteous prosper and is elevated by their blessing, but wicked speech
overturns it. A multitude of counselors guards the nation against collapse. These sayings give civic
life a positive possibility alongside the earlier narratives of violent or presumptuous cities; no
particular city is named.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### The strong city — Proverbs 18:10–11, 19

Three fortifications in one chapter: the name of Yahweh as *"a strong tower"* (10), the rich man's
*"strong city"* and *"unscalable wall"* that exist *"in his own imagination"* (11), and the offended
brother *"more difficult than a fortified city"* (19). Babel's city and tower (Genesis 11:4) are the
background the wiki reads them against. See
[[The Name of Yahweh Is a Strong Tower - Proverbs 18.10-11, Psalm 61.3, and Genesis 11.4]].

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

A little city with few men, besieged by a great king, is delivered by one poor
wise man's wisdom (9:14–15). The city is unnamed and is not identified with a known place. As in
Proverbs 21:22 and Ecclesiastes 7:19, the city's fate turns on wisdom rather than numbers or
walls; unlike them, the story records the city's failure to remember its deliverer.

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The city at night — Song of Solomon 3:1–5

The woman gets up at night and goes about an unnamed city, *"in the streets and in the squares"*
(3:2), looking for the one she loves. The city has watchmen *"who go about the city"* (3:3), and
they find her before she finds him. The city is not identified, and the wiki does not make it
Jerusalem on the strength of the *daughters of Jerusalem* in 3:5. The same streets and squares are
where Proverbs puts Wisdom and the forbidden woman; see
[[Seeking in the Streets and Squares - Song of Solomon 3.1-4 and Proverbs 1, 7, and 8]].

See [[Seeking Him by Night]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 3:1–5; comparison sources are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The keepers of the walls — Song of Solomon 5:7

The same unnamed city, at night, the second time. The watchmen *"who go about the city"* find her in
the same words as 3:3, and this time *"They beat me. They bruised me. The keepers of the walls took my
cloak away from me"* (5:7). The city now has walls and men to keep them, *šōmǝrê haḥōmôt* (Sefaria),
and its order falls on the woman who is out in it alone. The poem gives no reason and passes no
judgment on the watchmen. See [[The Watchmen (Song of Solomon)]].

See [[I Opened, but He Had Gone]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 5:2–8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### Cities as measures of beauty — Song of Solomon 6:4–10

Tirzah and Jerusalem provide positive comparisons for the woman's beauty (6:4).
The city that supplied streets, watchmen, and danger in the night searches is not the
only urban image available to the Song. Named cities can also serve love's admiration.
Neither comparison sets the scene in one of them or identifies the unnamed search city.

See [[My Dove Is Unique]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 6:4–10; comparisons use the stored WEB
Song 1:5–6; 4:1–9 and Proverbs 31:28–31 where noted.

## Key passages

- [[I Opened, but He Had Gone]] — Song of Solomon 5:7; the watchmen beat her and the keepers of the
  walls take her cloak.

- [[Seeking Him by Night]] — Song of Solomon 3:1–5; streets, squares, and watchmen in a city at night.

- [[The Forgotten Wise Man]] — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; a little city delivered by a poor wise man and then forgetful of him.

- [[A Strong Tower]] — Proverbs 18:10–11, 19.

- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:10–11, 14.

- [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] — the first city and the three founders of human craft
- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — the first vineyard, and the register of crafts resumed
  after the flood
- [[The Table of Nations]] — eight cities in five verses, and the first kingdom
- [[The Tower of Babel]] — the first city built by everybody, and the only one in Scripture whose
  construction God stops
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — Genesis 14: five cities of the plain lose everything they
  have to a bigger power in an afternoon, and the tent-dweller in the hills is the one who gets it
  back. The chapter also puts two cities' kings side by side — [[Salem]], whose king brings out bread
  and wine, and [[Sodom]], whose king asks

- [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] — Genesis 19: the first city Genesis destroys and the
  only one it takes the reader inside. The offence is committed by the town acting as one body, and
  the sentence falls on the town

- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:30: the arc run backwards in one verse. The only man
  Genesis followed all the way into a city leaves the last surviving one out of fear and lives in a
  cave, and what he founds there is not a city but two nations. Genesis's cities run Enoch, Babel,
  Sodom; its one urban biography ends underground
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:7–10, 21–25; **the gate and the square as the seat of justice and
  honour**, the only city in the Hebrew book of Job. The elders rise, the princes fall silent, and the
  man who sits there is honoured for what he does for the powerless — the city at its best, seen
  from the judge's seat. See [[Uz]].
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:11; the storage cities [[Pithom]] and [[Rameses]], **the first cities
  Israel builds, and built for someone else**. See [[Come, Let Us - Babel's Bricks and Pharaoh's]]
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:7–12; **Babel's bricks and Babel's scattering, in Egypt.**
  *Ləbēnîm* five times, and *"the people were scattered abroad throughout all the land"* —
  *wayyāpeṣ*, the form of Genesis 11:8. Babel built *"lest we be scattered"*; Egypt scatters its
  brickmakers to find stubble. See [[Come, Let Us - Babel's Bricks and Pharaoh's]]
- [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]] — Proverbs 1:20–21; Wisdom calls in the city's streets, squares, and gates
- [[My Dove Is Unique]] — Song of Solomon 6:4–10; cities as measures of beauty.
