# City and Civilization

## Definition / overview

Human beings building things that outlast them — walls, trades, crafts, institutions — and what the
Bible makes of the fact that all of it starts in the line of a murderer.

Genesis 4:17–22 is the origin story of civilization, and it fits in six verses. Cain builds the
first city and names it for his son. Five generations later Lamech's three sons each found a domain
of human practice: Jabal *"the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock"*, Jubal
*"the father of all who handle the harp and pipe"*, Tubal Cain *"the forger of every cutting
instrument of brass and iron."* Economy, art, technology. See [[Jabal]], [[Jubal]],
[[Tubal Cain]].

Three things are worth holding together, because most readings of this passage drop one of them.

**1. These are goods.** Not neutral facilities that could go either way — actual goods, and ones
the Bible goes on to depend on. The patriarchs live in tents with livestock for the whole second
half of Genesis. The temple runs on harps. Metal tools are the substrate of every trade in the
ancient world, agriculture first. Whatever Genesis is doing here, it is not describing the invention
of vice.

**2. They come from the cursed line.** Cain was sentenced to wander and built a city; his
descendants made the things human life is furnished with. The narrative places the whole inventory
in the family it has just watched commit the first murder, four verses before that family's head
sings about killing a man.

**3. Genesis does not resolve this.** No verdict is entered on the city, the tent, the harp, or the
forge. Two over-readings are common and both are over-readings: that the passage means to taint
culture with Cain's guilt (which cannot survive the patriarchs living Jabal's life and David
playing Jubal's instrument), and that it means to celebrate human progress (which cannot survive
where it is placed). What the text actually does is refuse to sort the world into a clean half and a
dirty one. The people who make the world habitable are the same people who make it dangerous, and
Genesis reports both without arbitration.

**The strand this opens.** The city becomes one of the Bible's largest running images, and it stays
double all the way through. Babel is the second city and the first collective project, and it is
scattered (11:1–9) — not for being a city but for what it was built to secure. Sodom is a city.
So is Jerusalem, the city God chooses and puts his name in. Babylon is the city that stands for
every human order set against him. And the last thing the Bible shows is a city — but one that
*"comes down out of heaven from God"* (Revelation 21:2) rather than being built, which is the
resolution Genesis 4 withholds: the thing humanity started making east of Eden is finally given
rather than achieved.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 4:17** — Cain builds the first city, named for his son Enoch, in defiance of or in
  submission to a sentence to wander. See [[Enoch (city)]].
- **Genesis 4:20–22** — herding, music, and metalwork founded in three verses, all by Lamech's
  children, with no evaluation attached.
- **Genesis 9:20** — the register of founded crafts resumes on the far side of the flood, and adds
  viticulture: *"Noah began to be a farmer, and planted a vineyard."* The first entry not attributed to
  Cain's line, and the pattern is unchanged — Genesis names the origin of a craft and shows its cost in
  the same paragraph, exactly as 4:22's forging of bronze and iron is followed two verses later by the
  Song of the Sword. The book has now derived herding, music, metalwork, the city, and wine, and has
  passed a verdict on none of them. See [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] and
  [[The Vine and the Cup]].
- **Genesis 10:8–12** — eight cities at once, and the first *kingdom*. [[Nimrod]] founds [[Babel]],
  Erech, Accad, and Calneh in [[Shinar]], and — on the WEB's reading of 10:11 — [[Nineveh]], Rehoboth
  Ir, Calah, and Resen in [[Assyria]]. The register that began with one city built by a murderer now
  takes in the capitals of both empires that will destroy Israel and Judah, and Genesis still passes
  no verdict; it names the origin and moves on to the next name in the list. This is where
  civilization stops being a set of crafts and becomes a polity — see [[Kingship and Empire]] and
  [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the second city in Scripture and the first collective one: brick and bitumen
  because the plain has no stone, a tower with its head in the heavens, and a stated purpose — a name,
  and no scattering. God comes down, confuses the language, and *"They stopped building the city."*
  The pattern holds exactly: the manufacturing process is explained approvingly, the ambition is
  quoted, and no verdict on building as such is entered anywhere in the nine verses. What Genesis
  stops is not the city but what the city was for. See [[The Tower of Babel]] and
  [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]]. Babylon's whole later career as *the* city against God begins here;
  see [[Babel and Babylon the Great]].
- **Genesis 13:10–18** — the theme's first human choice between the two ways of living, and Genesis
  still declines to grade it. Lot takes the well-watered plain, then *"the cities of the plain"*, then
  *"moved his tent as far as Sodom"* (13:12), and by 19:1 he is sitting in its gate; Abram is given
  the whole land and pitches under trees at [[Hebron]]. The narrator's only editorial is about the
  men of Sodom (13:13), not about cities. Hebrews 11:9–10 reads the contrast as deliberate and then
  reverses it — the tent-dweller is the one *"looking for the city which has foundations, whose
  builder and maker is God"*. See [[Abram and Lot Separate]] and [[Sodom]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: Sodom (Genesis 18–19); the cities of refuge as civilization
  domesticating blood vengeance (Numbers 35); Jerusalem as the chosen city (2 Samuel 5, Psalms
  46, 48, 122); Babylon as the archetype of the city against God (Isaiah 13, Jeremiah 50–51,
  Revelation 17–18); Jeremiah 29:7, on seeking the welfare of the city you are exiled to;
  *"he looked for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God"* (Hebrews 11:10);
  the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21–22).
