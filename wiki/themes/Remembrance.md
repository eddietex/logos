# Remembrance

## Definition / overview

### The covenant at Shechem — Joshua 24:1–28

The covenant is grounded in a remembered history, told twice: first by Yahweh in the first person (2–13), then by the people in their own words (16–18). Yahweh appeals to what *your eyes saw* (7), and the people answer with *those great signs in our sight* (17). The stone set up at the end *has heard all Yahweh's words* (27) and stands as a memory that can testify.

See [[The Covenant at Shechem]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 24:1–28; comparisons as cited.

### The altar of witness — Joshua 22:10–34

A monument is built for a question in *time to come* (24, 27–28). Joshua 4's stones prompt
children to ask what Yahweh did. This altar answers a question the builders fear: whether the
eastern tribes have *portion in Yahweh*. See
[[A Witness Between Us - Galeed, the Jordan Altar, and the Shechem Stone]].

See [[The Altar of Witness by the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 22:10–34; comparisons as cited.

### Caleb’s inheritance — Joshua 14:6–15

Caleb’s appeal makes the commission, failed report, and oath at Kadesh govern the present allocation at Gilgal (6–10). Remembering here creates a claim on present leadership.

See [[Caleb Claims Hebron]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 14:6–15; comparisons as cited.


### The five kings at Makkedah — Joshua 10:16–27

The stones sealing the cave remain to the narrator’s day (27), preserving a physical trace of the
kings’ fate. No children’s question or commemorative rite is prescribed here. The displayed defeat
also becomes a precedent for future courage (25).

See [[The Five Kings in the Cave at Makkedah]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 10:16–27.


### The law read at Ebal — Joshua 8:30–35

Inscribed stones and public reading preserve and communicate Moses’ instruction after his death (32–35). Little ones hear alongside adults; unlike Joshua 4’s memorial stones, these stones bear a written copy of the law.

See [[The Law Read at Mount Ebal]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 8:30–35.

### The capture of Ai — Joshua 8:1–29

Two *to this day* markers in two verses: Ai a heap *to this day* (28), and the stones over its king
*that remain to this day* (29). With Gilgal's twelve stones (4:20–24) and Achor's heap (7:26), the
book has now set up memorials of crossing, of sin, and of victory in stone, and the altar stones of
Ebal follow (8:30–32).

See [[The Capture of Ai]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 8:1–29; WLC (morphhb) where Hebrew is named.

### The sin of Achan — Joshua 7:1–26

A great heap of stones over Achan remains to this day (26), a memorial of judgment answering the
twelve memorial stones at Gilgal (4:1–24). A second heap follows over the king of Ai (8:29).

See [[The Sin of Achan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 7:1–26; WLC (morphhb) where Hebrew is named.

### The twelve stones — Joshua 4:1–24

The stones are a lasting memorial designed to provoke children’s questions (6–7, 21–23). Fathers
must explain the deliverance; the object preserves an occasion for testimony rather than
interpreting itself.
See [[The Twelve Stones from the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 4:1–24.

### Remember the word — Joshua 1:10–18

*Remember the word which Moses the servant of Yahweh commanded you* (13, *zākôr*, MAM via Sefaria). What is to be remembered is an agreement, and Joshua recites it in the words of Deuteronomy 3:18–20. Memory here binds the living to a promise their own tribes made.

See [[The Eastern Tribes Pledge to Cross]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 1:10–18.

### The Song of Moses — Deuteronomy 31:30–32:47

Israel must ask fathers and elders about former generations (7), yet forgets the God who gave it birth (18). The song preserves the memory of care and betrayal; the closing instruction requires transmission to children in obedience (46).

See [[The Song of Moses]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:30–32:47.


### The book beside the ark — Deuteronomy 31:24–29

The enduring book complements the enduring song of 31:19–21. Preservation allows testimony to outlast Moses even when the people abandon the way he taught (26–29).

See [[The Book Beside the Ark]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:24–29.


### The song as witness — Deuteronomy 31:14–23

The song will survive in descendants' mouths as a witness against Israel (19–21). Retaining a
text in memory is distinguished from remaining faithful to the God it names: the people may
preserve the testimony that accuses them.

See [[A Song to Witness Against Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:14–23.

### Courage and succession — Deuteronomy 31:1–8

The defeat of Sihon and Og becomes a precedent for the future (4). Moses gives the whole people the remembered-victory argument previously addressed to Joshua in 3:21–22.

See [[Be Strong as Joshua Takes the Lead]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:1–8; Deuteronomy 31:16–18 and 34:7 where compared.

### Return and the circumcised heart — Deuteronomy 30:1–10

Return begins when Israel *shall call them to mind among all the nations* (1). Memory of blessing and curse in exile is the first step back to Yahweh.

See [[Return Restoration and the Circumcised Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 30:1–10.

### The covenant in Moab — Deuteronomy 29:1–29

Egypt’s signs, forty years of preserved clothing, and the victories over Sihon and Og ground the present summons (2–9). Remembered experience does not itself guarantee understanding (4).

See [[The Covenant in Moab and the Hidden Things]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 29:1–29.

### The calf and intercession — Deuteronomy 9:7–29

Israel must remember its provocations (7), while Moses asks Yahweh to remember Abraham, Isaac, and
Jacob (27). Memory both humbles the people and supplies the intercessor’s appeal.

See [[Remember the Calf and Moses' Intercession]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 9:7–29.


### Wilderness and wealth — Deuteronomy 8:1–20

Remembering the whole journey (2) and the giver of productive power (18) counters forgetting through
abundance (11–14). Memory takes practical form in obedience and blessing God after eating.

See [[Remember the Wilderness and the Giver of Wealth]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 8:1–20.


### Fullness and remembrance — Deuteronomy 6:10–25

Fullness threatens memory of the deliverer (10–12). The future child’s question is answered by a
first-person account of slavery and rescue (20–23), making remembrance part of explaining the law.

See [[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 6:10–25.

### No form and no image — Deuteronomy 4:15–31

*Be careful, lest you forget the covenant* (23); in exile, Yahweh will not *forget the covenant of your fathers* (31). Israel's memory of Horeb (15) is the premise of the image ban.

See [[No Form Seen and No Image Made]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:15–31.

### Statutes and Horeb — Deuteronomy 4:1–14

*Lest you forget the things which your eyes saw* (9): memory is guarded like the soul and passed to children and grandchildren. Two seen events are summoned — the deaths at Peor (3) and the day at Horeb (10–13).

See [[Hear the Statutes and Remember Horeb]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:1–14.

### Eastern inheritance — Deuteronomy 3:12–22

Moses turns recollection of the two defeated kings into assurance for Joshua about kingdoms ahead
(21–22). The past is used to equip a successor for a future Moses will not lead.

See [[The Eastern Inheritance and the Charge to Joshua]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:12–22.

### Og’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 3:1–11

The retelling expands Numbers 21:33–35 with fortified cities, geographical names, and Og’s bedstead
(4–11). Remembered victory supplies encouragement without concealing the destruction of inhabitants.

See [[The Defeat of Og Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:1–11.

### Sihon’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 2:24–37

Moses recounts the embassy, hardening, battle, and bounded conquest as the explanation of Israel’s
present possession (24–37). Numbers 21:21–26 shares the narrative outline but omits the hardening
and the explicit description of women and little ones in Sihon’s cities.

See [[The Defeat of Sihon Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:24–37.

### The sentence and the ascent — Deuteronomy 1:34–46

Moses tells the generation in Moab the sentence and defeat in the second person. The recollection
makes the old refusal and the later presumption warnings for the listeners who will inherit.

See [[The Sentence and the Presumptuous Ascent]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:34–46; Numbers 14 and 20 for comparisons.

### The spies and the refusal — Deuteronomy 1:19–33

Moses argues from what the people remember: the wilderness *which you saw* (19), Egypt *before your eyes* (30), the carrying *where you have seen* (31). The refusal is told to the sons in the second person, as if they had refused.

See [[The Spies and the Refusal to Go Up]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:19–33.

### Judges appointed — Deuteronomy 1:9–18

Moses recalls his own words and acts *at that time* (9, 16, 18): the proposal, the people's consent, the appointment, and the charge. The retelling omits Jethro, whom Exodus 18 credits with the plan.

See [[Judges Appointed for the People]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:9–18.

### Leave Horeb and possess the land — Deuteronomy 1:6–8

Moses' speech begins by recalling a word Yahweh spoke at Horeb almost forty years earlier (6). He compresses the departure into a single remembered command and gives no date, cloud, or angel; recollection selects as well as preserves.

See [[Leave Horeb and Possess the Land]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:6–8.

### Moses’ address in Moab — Deuteronomy 1:1–5

The opening dates the address after Sihon and Og’s defeat and places an eleven-day route beside the
fortieth year (2–4). The frame invites historical recollection before the speech supplies its
account.

See [[The Words of Moses Beyond the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:1–5.

### The stages of the journey — Numbers 33:1–49

The journey is preserved as a written record attributed to Moses at Yahweh’s command (2). Selective notices of departure, water, and Aaron’s death punctuate repeated travel stages.

See [[The Stages of the Journey]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 33:1–49.

### The second census — Numbers 26:1–65

Genealogical entries preserve warnings about Dathan, Abiram, Korah, Nadab, and Abihu, as well as
named survivors; counting also remembers.

See [[The Second Census]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 26.


### The journey to Moab — Numbers 21:10–20

The narrator cites the Book of the Wars of Yahweh for the Arnon landscape (14–15), then records
Israel’s well song (17–18). Written citation and communal song preserve memory; the fragment does
not disclose the cited book’s full contents or author.

See [[The Journey to Moab]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:10–20.


### The budding rod — Numbers 17:1–13

Aaron’s rod is put back before the testimony as a token against rebellion (10). Like the altar
covering from the censers in Numbers 16:38–40, an object preserves a warning beyond the event;
this object bears fruit rather than the marks of the rebels’ destruction.

See [[Aaron's Rod Buds]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 17.

### The rebellion of Korah — Numbers 16:1–40

The bronze censers become an altar covering and a memorial warning against repeating the
unauthorized approach (36–40). Unlike the garment fringes of Numbers 15:37–41, this reminder is made
from the instruments of a judged act.

See [[The Rebellion of Korah]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 16:1–40; comparisons as cited.

Remembering as divine faithfulness, human obligation, and the preservation or loss of a person's
place in a community's memory. The foundational Genesis pattern is what God does when the story
turns; Ecclesiastes 1:11 adds the frailty of human recollection. These are distinct questions.

The Hebrew verb is **zākar**, and the first thing to say about it is that the English word
*remember* is a poor fit and quietly ruins the verses it appears in. In English, remembering is a
mental event that presupposes forgetting: I remembered because I had let it slip. Scripture never
says God forgot and later recovered, and it never treats his remembering as recollection. **Zākar
with God as its subject is a verb of action.** It means to turn toward, to take up again, to act on
a commitment already made. Its object is usually a person under pressure or a covenant already
sworn, and what follows it is never a thought — it is always an intervention.

Genesis 8:1 is the first occurrence with God as subject, and it is placed at the exact structural
centre of the flood narrative:

> **1** God remembered Noah, all the animals, and all the livestock that were with him in the ship;
> and God made a wind to pass over the earth. The waters subsided.

Nothing else in the account can turn the flood. The ark's construction cannot, Noah's righteousness
cannot, the passage of time cannot — the water is an un-creation, and only the one who withdrew the
world's order can restore it. What turns it is that God calls someone to mind, and the wind follows
in the same verse. See [[The Waters Recede]] and [[The Flood as Un-Creation]].

**The grammar of the pattern.** Once the verb is noticed it is visible everywhere, and it almost
always runs in the same three beats: a situation with no way out of itself, *God remembered X*, and
then an action that changes the situation immediately. *"God remembered Rachel… and opened her
womb"*. *"God remembered his covenant… and God saw the children of Israel"*, and the exodus starts.
*"Yahweh remembered her"*, and Hannah conceives. The verb is the hinge, and the sentence after it is
the plot.

**The human counterpart is a command.** What God does by remembering, Israel is repeatedly told to
do by remembering: *"Remember the Sabbath day"* (Exodus 20:8), *"you shall remember that you were a
slave in the land of Egypt"* (Deuteronomy 5:15, and six more times in Deuteronomy), *"remember
Yahweh your God, for it is he who gives you the power to get wealth"* (Deuteronomy 8:18). Here too
the word is not primarily cognitive. To remember the exodus is to keep the feast, free your slaves,
and leave the gleanings; to forget it is not amnesia but apostasy. The same verb governs both sides
of the covenant, and the whole sacrificial and festal calendar exists to make remembering something
a nation does with its body.

**And it is what the desperate ask for.** The shortest prayers in Scripture are requests to be
remembered. Samson: *"Lord Yahweh, remember me, please, and strengthen me, please, only this once"*
(Judges 16:28). Hezekiah on his sickbed: *"Remember now, Yahweh, I beg you, how I have walked before
you"* (Isaiah 38:3). Job, wanting to be hidden in Sheol until the wrath passes and then recalled:
*"that you would appoint me a set time and remember me"* (14:13) — a request that only makes sense
if remembering is what raises the dead. And the last of them, from a cross: *"Jesus, remember me
when you come into your kingdom"* (Luke 23:42). None of these are asking God to think about them.
They are asking him to do what he did in Genesis 8:1.

**The negative form is the deepest promise in the prophets.** If remembering is acting, then *not*
remembering is the deliberate refusal to act on what is remembered — which is what forgiveness is.
*"I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more"* (Jeremiah 31:34), quoted
twice in Hebrews as the heart of the new covenant. Isaiah 43:25 puts both halves in one sentence:
*"I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake; and I will not remember your sins."*
The one thing God undertakes to forget is the only thing anyone would want him to.

## Development across Scripture

### Passover and the end of manna — Joshua 5:10–12

The Passover continues in the new land (10). Its memorial meaning comes from Exodus 12;
Joshua reports the observance without repeating the institution or its explanatory speeches.

See [[Passover at Gilgal and the End of the Manna]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 5:10–12; comparisons as cited.

### Reading to all Israel — Deuteronomy 31:9–13

Recurring public hearing gives children who have not known a way to learn (13). This complements household teaching in Deuteronomy 6:6–9; transmission is scheduled for the community as well as practiced at home.

See [[Read the Law to All Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:9–13.

### Fringes without the reason — Deuteronomy 22:12

*"You shall make yourselves fringes on the four corners of your cloak with which you cover
yourself."* Numbers 15:39 gave the fringes their purpose, *"that you may look on it, and remember
all Yahweh's commandments"*. Deuteronomy restates the law without the purpose, the blue cord, or
Numbers' word *ṣîṣit*, using *gǝdilîm* instead (WLC). See
[[Fringes on the Four Corners - Numbers 15.37-41 and Deuteronomy 22.12]].

See [[Care for the Neighbor and Ordered Distinctions]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 22:1–12.

### The day you came out, and the slave you were — Deuteronomy 16:1–17

The unleavened bread is eaten *"that you may remember the day when you came out of the land of Egypt all the days of your life"* (16:3). At weeks: *"You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt"* (12). The Passover's memory is the night of leaving; the feast of weeks remembers the slavery itself, as the household's servants rejoice beside it (11). See [[You Were a Slave in Egypt - The Remembrance Refrain of Deuteronomy]].

See [[Passover, Weeks, and Booths]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 16:1–17.

### Remember you were a slave — Deuteronomy 15:12–18

The second of five *"remember that you were a slave"* clauses in Deuteronomy (5:15; 15:15; 16:12; 24:18, 22). Each grounds a command that protects someone dependent; here, the servant released with gifts. See [[You Were a Slave in Egypt - The Remembrance Refrain of Deuteronomy]].

See [[Release the Servant with Gifts]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 15:12–18.

### Words laid up and handed on — Deuteronomy 11:18–32

The words are to be laid up in heart and soul, bound on hand and forehead, and written on door posts and gates (18, 20). The children Moses said had not seen (11:2) are now the ones to be taught (19).

See [[Set These Words Before You]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 11:18–32.

### Remembered deeds and the rain-fed land — Deuteronomy 11:1–17

Moses addresses those who saw, not their children who did not (2, 7). Signs in Egypt, the sea, the wilderness, and the fate of Dathan and Abiram are listed as the content of what must be known "this day" (2–6).

See [[Remember His Deeds and Guard Your Hearts]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 11:1–17.

### What Yahweh requires — Deuteronomy 10:12–22

Israel’s former foreignness in Egypt becomes the reason to love foreigners (19). Seen divine deeds and the growth from seventy to a multitude furnish the reasons for praise (21–22). Remembering produces care, not only gratitude.

See [[What Yahweh Requires of Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 10:12–22.

### Blessing and removal — Deuteronomy 7:12–26

Memory is the cure for fear. When Israel says the nations are too many, it must *remember well what
Yahweh your God did to Pharaoh, and to all Egypt*: what its own eyes saw is evidence for what Yahweh
will do next (17–19).

See [[Blessing and the Removal of the Nations]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:12–26.

### Wholehearted love and daily instruction — Deuteronomy 6:1–9

Words are carried through the daily round of sitting, walking, lying down, and rising (6–9).
Teaching, bodily signs, and writing at household entrances sustain remembrance; the command does
not wait for a child to ask a question.

See [[Hear Israel and Love Yahweh]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 6:1–9.

### The commandments recounted — Deuteronomy 5:1–22

Remembering bondage and deliverance produces a concrete duty to give others rest (15). This differs from Exodus 20:8–11, where remembering the day is connected with creation.

See [[The Ten Commandments Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 5:1–22.


### Sihon and Og — Numbers 21:21–35

The poem preserves the memory of Moab’s defeat and Sihon’s power (27–30), while the surrounding
prose records his fall. The text does not identify its author or securely assign every poetic
speaker.

See [[Sihon and Og]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:21–35.


### The tassels — Numbers 15:37–41

The fringe makes remembrance a practice of looking that leads to doing (39–40). This is
human remembrance of commandments, distinct from God remembering people or covenant.

See [[The Tassels]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 15:37–41.

### Covenant warnings and remembered promises — Leviticus 26:14–46

*Zākar* four times, promised ahead of time for exile: the covenant with Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham,
the land (42), and the covenant of the first generation (45). No rescue follows at once, against the
usual pattern. See [[God Remembered - Noah, Abraham, Rachel, and Israel]].

See [[Covenant Warnings and Remembered Promises]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Lamps and weekly bread — Leviticus 24:1–9

Pure frankincense accompanies each row as the bread’s memorial offering (7). This
is a ritual memorial before Yahweh, distinct from the explicit teaching of later
generations in the preceding feast of shelters (23:43); neither sense erases the other.

See [[The Lamps and the Bread Before Yahweh]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 24:1–9.

### Booths and remembered deliverance — Leviticus 23:33–44

Seven days in temporary shelters teach later generations what Yahweh did at the
departure from Egypt (42–43). Like the instruction attached to Unleavened Bread
(Exodus 13:8), a repeated practice carries the history of deliverance.

See [[The Feast of Booths]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:33–44; comparisons as cited.

### Trumpets and atonement — Leviticus 23:23–32

The first day of the seventh month is a memorial of trumpet blowing in the WEB (24).
The verse does not identify an event commemorated or say who is to remember; this memorial
should not be supplied with an unstated object from other remembrance passages.

See [[Trumpets and the Day of Atonement]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:23–32; contextual references as cited.

### Field and community — Leviticus 19:19–37

Israel’s past as foreigners and its departure from Egypt supply reasons for present love and
honest dealing (34, 36). This is a thematic appeal to remembered experience, without an assertion
about a Hebrew verb.

See [[Holy Living in Field and Community]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19.


- **Genesis 8:1** — *"God remembered Noah"*: the first occurrence with God as subject, at the apex
  of the flood chiasm, and the turn on which the world's survival hangs. See [[The Waters Recede]].
- **Genesis 9:15–16** — *"I will remember my covenant"*: the rainbow is explicitly a memory aid, and
  the striking thing is whose. It is set in the sky so that *God* will see it and remember, not so
  that people will. Every purpose clause in 9:14–16 has him as its subject — *I bring a cloud*, *I
  will remember*, *I will look at it, that I may remember* — and Noah is never told to watch for it.
  Which makes the sign the visible, permanent form of the act 8:1 turned on: the verb that saved the
  world once is here attached to an object and given a trigger. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and
  [[The Bow in the Cloud]].
- **Genesis 19:29** — *"God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the middle of the overthrow"*:
  a man rescued because of somebody else's standing. The verse is the closest parallel in Genesis to
  8:1 — both use *wayyizkōr ʾĕlōhîm* to close a total judgment by naming the one person on the other
  side of it, and in both the remembering *is* the rescue rather than a feeling that precedes it.
  What is distinctive here is that the person remembered is not the person saved: Abraham is forty
  miles away, is never told, and is not the one in danger. See
  [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]].
- **Genesis 30:22** — *"God remembered Rachel, and God listened to her, and opened her womb."* **The
  fourth and last *wayyizkōr ʾĕlōhîm* in Genesis, and the only one whose object is a woman in her own
  right** rather than a covenant or a righteous man interceding for someone else. The verse triples the
  attribution — *remembered*, *listened*, *opened* — **in a chapter where a fertility drug has just
  changed hands and is never mentioned again.** See [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]].
- **Genesis 40:14, 23; 41:9** — **the verb handed to a human being, and dropped.** Joseph asks a fellow
  prisoner to *remember* him and to *cause Pharaoh to remember* him — *zəkartanî* and *wəhizkartanî*,
  **the root twice in one verse, in two stems** — and the chapter's last line is *"the chief cup bearer
  didn't remember Joseph, but forgot him."* **The negative and its positive twin, which Hebrew does not
  usually bother with**, as the last seven words of a chapter. **Genesis has used *zākar* with God as its
  subject four times and never once without an act in the following clause**; here it is asked of a man
  and produces two years of silence, until 41:9's *"I remember my faults today"*. See
  [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]].
- **Exodus 2:24; 6:5** — *"God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob"*:
  four hundred years of silence end in this verb, and the exodus follows from it.
- **Exodus 20:8; Deuteronomy 5:15; 8:18; 15:15; 16:12** — the command in its human form: remember
  the Sabbath, remember that you were slaves, and let the memory determine how you treat other
  people.
- **Numbers 15:39–40** — tassels on the garment *"that you may see it, and remember all the
  commandments"*: memory built into clothing.
- **Judges 16:28; 1 Samuel 1:11, 19** — Samson's last prayer, and Hannah's request answered in the
  same words she used.
- **Job 14:13** — *"that you would… appoint me a set time and remember me"*: Job asking to be hidden
  in Sheol and then recalled, which is as close as the book comes to asking for resurrection. See
  [[Sheol]].
- **Psalm 8:4** — *"What is man, that you think of him?"*, the question the verb raises when a
  human being is on the receiving end of it.
- **Psalm 25:6–7; 79:8; 137:7** — remember your mercies, do not remember the sins of my youth, and
  remember what Edom did: the same verb turned to petition, to pardon, and to vengeance.
- **Psalm 105:8, 42; 106:45** — *"He has remembered his covenant forever"*, as the summary of the
  entire national history.
- **Isaiah 43:25; Jeremiah 31:34** — the promise not to remember, which is what forgiveness is.
- **Jeremiah 2:2** — *"I remember for you the kindness of your youth"*: God recalling Israel's first
  love as the ground of an appeal.
- **Malachi 3:16** — *"a book of memory was written before him"*, for those who feared Yahweh.
- **Luke 1:54, 72** — the Magnificat and the Benedictus both close on it: *"in remembrance of
  mercy"*, *"to remember his holy covenant"*. The incarnation is announced as an instance of the
  pattern.
- **Luke 22:19; 1 Corinthians 11:24–25** — *"Do this in memory of me"*: the human command, given
  again, and given a meal to carry it, as the exodus had one.
- **Luke 23:42** — *"Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom"*, and an answer given the
  same day.
- **Acts 10:4, 31** — Cornelius's prayers and alms *"gone up for a memorial before God"*.
- **Hebrews 8:12; 10:17** — Jeremiah 31:34 quoted twice, as the clause that makes the new covenant
  new.
- **Revelation 18:5** — *"God has remembered her iniquities"*: the pattern's other edge, and proof
  that the verb is about action rather than sentiment.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:8–11

Remembering the Sabbath has an enacted form: the hearer keeps it holy by interrupting the six-day
labor pattern for the entire household (20:8–11). The stated basis in this version is God's creation
rest. Deuteronomy 5:15 instead makes remembered servitude and rescue the explicit Sabbath rationale,
a difference kept in [[The Ten Commandments Retold - Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5]]. See
[[The Ten Commandments]].

### God causes his name to be remembered — Exodus 20:24

The verb of *"Remember the Sabbath day"* (20:8) returns four verses after the commandments end, with
God as its subject. In the WLC, *ʾazkîr*, the WEB's *"record"*, carries the lemma of *zākar*.
**Israel is told to remember a day; God undertakes to make his name remembered in places**, and
promises to come and bless there (20:24). See [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]].

### Experience turned into obligation — Exodus 23:1–19

The remembered experience of being aliens grounds protection of the alien (23:9); departure from
Egypt in Abib grounds Unleavened Bread (23:15). Memory is thus expressed both in treatment of others
and in an annual observance. This is a thematic comparison of the WEB passages, not a claim that
both verses use the Hebrew verb discussed in this page's overview.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The priestly garments — Exodus 28:1–43

The engraved stones are *"stones of memorial"*, and Aaron bears the names *"for a memorial
before Yahweh continually"* (Exodus 28:12, 29). The memorial is directed at God, not at Israel;
the text does not say that God would otherwise forget. See [[The Priestly Garments]].

### The incense altar, the ransom, and the holy oil — Exodus 30:1–38

The census ransom is *"a memorial for the children of Israel before Yahweh"* (*ləzikkārôn*, 30:16,
MAM). As with the twelve names worn *"for a memorial"* on Aaron's shoulders (28:12), the memorial
is before God; the text does not say what it reminds him of beyond the atonement for Israel's
souls. See [[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Moses asks God to remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel and the sworn offspring and land promise
(32:13). The people’s attribution of their rescue to the calf (32:4) is a failure to remember the
deliverer rightly. Psalm 106:21 later explicitly describes forgetting God; see [[The Golden Calf Retold - Deuteronomy 9, Psalm 106, and Acts 7]]. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

The month Abib remains the appointed time for Unleavened Bread because Israel came out of Egypt
then (34:18). Covenant renewal does not replace that earlier rescue with a different founding
event. Its memory is given a recurring practice, although the unit does not narrate the feast
being held. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

Exodus 30:16 calls the census money a memorial before Yahweh; 38:25–28 specifies the silver’s use in
sockets and pillar fittings. Reading these together gives the memorial a material destination.
Chapter 38 does not restate the memorial language or assign a symbolic meaning to each socket.

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The priestly garments made — Exodus 39:1–31

The making report retains the memorial purpose of the shoulder stones (39:7), alongside
the twelve tribal names on the breastplate (39:14). Exodus 28:12, 29 supplies the fuller account
of Aaron bearing names before Yahweh. The memorial objects now exist; the passage does not yet
describe their use or suggest that God had forgotten the tribes.

See [[The Priestly Garments Made]]. Source: cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Don't forget my teaching — Proverbs 3:1

*"My son, don't forget my teaching"* (3:1, *ʾal-tiškāḥ*) follows directly on the strange woman who
*"forgets the covenant of her God"* (2:17, *šāḵēḥâ*). Remembering is the son's first duty in the
lecture, and it is kept by the heart (*yiṣṣōr libbeḵā*). Hebrews 12:5 introduces its quotation of the
same lecture's 3:11–12 by telling its readers they *"have forgotten the exhortation"*. See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]]. Source: Proverbs 3 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Remembered teaching passed to sons — Proverbs 4:1–9

The father recalls his own childhood and repeats what his father taught him (4:3–4).
The next learner must not forget or depart from these words (4:5). This is human memory
serving continued practice, distinct from God's covenant remembrance described above.
Exodus 13:8–16 likewise joins household speech to remembered experience, though its
subject is deliverance and observance rather than wisdom's acquisition. See
[[Wisdom Handed On - Proverbs 4 and Exodus 13]].

See [[Get Wisdom]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:7

The memory of the righteous is blessed, while the name of the wicked rots. This is human remembrance
and reputation, rather than a scene of God remembering a covenant. The saying extends the contrast
of righteous and wicked beyond their immediate actions into the way they are remembered.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Lemuel’s mother — Proverbs 31:5–7

Human forgetting receives opposite evaluations in adjacent sayings. The king must not drink
and forget the law, because another person’s justice is at stake (5). The sufferer is offered
drink so that poverty and misery no longer occupy memory (7). This is a contrast in human
awareness and responsibility, distinct from the earlier stories of God remembering a covenant.
The passage does not claim that forgotten poverty has ceased to exist.

See [[The Words of King Lemuel]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9 and the comparisons documented
there.

### The opening of Ecclesiastes — 1:1–11

Ecclesiastes 1:11 denies remembrance to the former and to the later among those who come
after. **Tension:** Proverbs 10:7 calls the memory of the righteous blessed, distinguishing it
from the wicked person's name. The Preacher's opening gives no corresponding moral exception.
One can distinguish a community's present recognition from remembrance across generations,
but that is an interpretation, not an explicit qualification within 1:11. This is human
forgetting; the passage does not say that God forgets his commitments. See
[[Profit and Remembrance - Proverbs and Ecclesiastes 1]].

See [[Vanity of Vanities]]. Sources: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and the comparison texts
verified on that passage page.


### No memory of the wise — Ecclesiastes 2:16

*"For of the wise man, even as of the fool, there is no memory for ever"* (2:16). Ecclesiastes 1:11
denied memory to generations in general; 2:16 denies it to the wise in particular, so wisdom does
not secure a name. **Tension:** this sharpens the conflict with Proverbs 10:7, where the memory of the
righteous is blessed. The conflict is recorded on
[[Profit and Remembrance - Proverbs and Ecclesiastes 1]] and not resolved.

See [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:12–17.

### Those who come after — Ecclesiastes 4:16

*"Yet those who come after shall not rejoice in him"* (4:16) applies 1:11's forgetting to a ruler's
popularity, and if *"all them over whom he was"* is read as *those who were before them* (an
alternative recorded on the passage page), the verse runs from earlier to later generations as 1:11
does. Compare 9:15's poor wise man, whom no one remembered. See [[The Youth and the Old King]]. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 1:11; 4:16; 9:15.

### Wealth and enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 5:8–20

In the WEB, the recipient of joy will not often reflect on the days of his life, because
God occupies his heart with joy (20). This differs from 1:11 and 2:16, where later people
fail to remember their predecessors. Here less reflection belongs to the experience of
present enjoyment; it is not a promise of an enduring public name or a command to forget
one's obligations. This comparison concerns the WEB's sense, not verified Hebrew vocabulary.

See [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20;
comparisons as identified on the passage page.

### Riches without enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

The stillborn child's name is covered with darkness (4). Read beside 1:11's forgotten
generations and 2:16's forgotten wise person and fool, this adds a life with no public
story of accomplishment. The connection concerns the WEB's image of a hidden name;
no shared Hebrew verb is asserted. The child nevertheless has more rest than the
unsatisfied long-lived man (5), so remembered achievement is not the deciding good.

See [[Riches Without Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9; comparison
sources are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Forgotten in the city — Ecclesiastes 8:10–17

The stored WEB describes the wicked as forgotten in the city where they acted (10).
The passage therefore continues the problem of human memory without promising an
enduring reputation. Burial and forgetting coexist; the text used here does not say
that these people were praised. No assertion about a Hebrew variant is made.

See [[The Wicked Buried and the Work Beyond Finding]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:10–17;
comparisons are identified on the passage page.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

The dead have no more reward, for their memory is forgotten (5). The claim extends the
forgetting of wise and fool in 2:16 without exempting the righteous. **Tension:** Proverbs
10:7 calls the righteous person's memory blessed. Present honor and eventual forgetting
can be distinguished, but Ecclesiastes does not state that qualification. The claim is
about the dead's memory, not God's forgetting a covenant.

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

A poor wise man delivers a besieged city, *"yet no man remembered that same poor man"*
(9:15). The single case realizes the general statements of 1:11, 2:16, and 9:5. A proposed
reading in which he was never consulted, rather than forgotten afterward, is recorded on the
passage page as unverified against the Hebrew. See
[[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]].

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Remembering darkness and the Creator — Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8

The two objects of remembrance belong together: the many days of darkness (11:8)
and the Creator in youth (12:1). Remembering here is the living person's task, not
later generations preserving a reputation. **Tension:** 5:20 describes the recipient
of joy reflecting little on life's days, while 11:8 directs recollection of darkness.
These differ as description and instruction; neither cancels the other.

See [[Remember Your Creator]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8; comparison sources
identified on the passage page.

### The meal offering — Leviticus 2

The burned portion is called a memorial (2:2, 9, 16). The text describes its ingredients and burning without saying who remembers what; this should not be collapsed into a narrated act of God remembering someone. See [[The Meal Offering]].

### Altar fire and priestly portions — Leviticus 6:8–23

The handful of flour and oil, with all its frankincense, is burned as a memorial (15), continuing Leviticus 2. The verse does not specify a psychological act of remembering or explain the term’s mechanism.

See [[The Altar Fire and the Priests’ Meal Offerings]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6:8–23; comparisons documented there.

### Remembered at a signal — Numbers 10:9–10

Here Israel is the one remembered: *wənizkartem*, *you will be remembered* (WLC), before Yahweh, at the sound of an alarm. Verse 10 calls the trumpets a *zikkārôn*. Unlike the Passover, this memorial does not recall a past event. The verses place the people themselves before God.

See [[The Two Silver Trumpets]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 10.

### We remember the fish — Numbers 11:5

*Zākarnû*, *we remember* (WLC), and what follows is a list of foods eaten *for nothing*. This memory leaves out the slavery that Exodus 1–5 describes. The wiki reads it as selective memory, and the text answers it with its own account of the manna (7–9).

See [[The Seventy Elders and the Quail]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 11.

### A memorial in the Tent — Numbers 31

The officers' gold is brought into the Tent of Meeting *for a memorial for the children of Israel before
Yahweh* (54), the phrase used of the census silver at Exodus 30:16.

See [[The War Against Midian]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 31.

### Remember Miriam, remember Egypt, leave the forgotten sheaf — Deuteronomy 24:9, 18–22

*Zākôr*, the infinitive absolute, occurs in Deuteronomy at 7:18 (Pharaoh), 24:9 (Miriam), and 25:17
(Amalek), and 24:9 and 25:17 share their frame word for word (WLC). The chapter also uses the slave
refrain twice (18, 22). Between them stands the one sheaf Israel is told to leave forgotten (19), with
*šākaḥ*, the verb of Deuteronomy's warnings not to forget Yahweh (6:12). See [[Pledges, Wages, Justice, and Gleaning]] and
[[Remember What Yahweh Did to Miriam - Numbers 12, Deuteronomy 24, and Micah 6]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 24:6–22.

### Remember Amalek, and do not forget — Deuteronomy 25:17–19

*"Remember what Amalek did to you by the way as you came out of Egypt"* (25:17) opens with the same
Hebrew words as *"Remember what Yahweh your God did to Miriam"* (24:9), *zākôr ʾēt ʾăšer ʿāśâ*. The
chapter ends with Israel told to *"blot out the memory (zēker) of Amalek from under the sky"* and also
*"You shall not forget"* (19). What is erased is Amalek's memory under the sky; what is kept is what
Amalek did. That is the wiki's reading of the knot, not a resolution of it. See [[Justice, Family Duty, Honest Weights, and Amalek]],
[[Remember What Yahweh Did to Miriam - Numbers 12, Deuteronomy 24, and Micah 6]], and
[[Amalek - From a Concubine's Son to Israel's First Enemy]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 25.

### The history said aloud — Deuteronomy 26:5–13

The first-fruits confession is remembrance as a script: *"My father was a Syrian ready to perish. He
went down into Egypt"*, then *"The Egyptians mistreated us"*, *"we cried"*, *"Yahweh brought us
out"*, *"he has brought us into this place"* (5–9). The speaker was not in Egypt and says *us*. The
third-year declaration answers the last word of chapter 25, *"You shall not forget"*, with *"neither
have I forgotten them"* (26:13). See [[First Fruits and the Tithe Declaration]] and
[[My Father Went Down into Egypt - Numbers 20.15-16 and Deuteronomy 26.5-9]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 25:19; 26:5–13.

- [[The Covenant with Noah]] — the same verb attached to an object and given a trigger: *"I will look
  at it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant"* (9:16)
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — Job wanting his cry to have a permanent
  record and a witness on file, which is the same need approached from underneath
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — the theme at its darkest, as a sentence: *"His memory shall perish
  from the earth. He shall have no name in the street"* (18:17). In a world with no developed
  afterlife, a remembered name and a surviving line are what a man has instead of immortality, and
  Bildad's verdict takes both
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — the answer, one chapter later: *"Oh that my words were now
  written! … That with an iron pen and lead they were engraved in the rock forever!"* (19:23–24).
  Job wants a record rather than a memorial, because a record can still be entered as evidence — and
  the book the reader is holding is the outcome of that exchange
- [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]] — Genesis 21:1, the theme's sister verb. *Pāqad*,
  *visited*, enters Scripture here and does for Sarah what *zākar* did for Noah at 8:1 and for
  Abraham at 19:29: it names the moment God turns back toward something he had left alone for a long
  time. The two verbs run in parallel through the Old Testament and meet in the exodus, where God
  both *remembers* his covenant (Exodus 2:24) and *visits* his people (Exodus 3:16; 4:31). See
  [[Yahweh Visited Sarah - Paqad and the Visitations of God]]

- [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] — Genesis 25:8, a promise kept in its own
  vocabulary with nobody pointing at it. *Śêbâ ṭôbâ*, *a good old age*, occurs twice in Genesis: at
  15:15, where God promises it in the dark, and here, where it is paid. **The narrator does not say
  that it is the same phrase.** And the formula the verse ends on — *gathered to his people* — is
  itself a remembrance device, naming a man's dead as the thing he arrives at
- [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] — Genesis 28:18–19, 22; **a stone set up so that a night has a
  monument, and a name that outlives the event by a thousand years.** The *maṣṣēbâ* is the theme's
  purest object: an altar is equipment, but **a standing stone does nothing except stay where it is
  put.** Jacob raises four of them and three mark a departure, a boundary, or a return. And the
  memory holds better than the meaning — the town is still called *house of God* when Jeroboam puts a
  calf in it and when Amos has to make a pun to say it has come to nothing. See
  [[Bethel from Jacob's Pillar to Jeroboam's Calf]] and
  [[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]]
- [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]] — Genesis 30:22, the verb's fourth and last appearance in Genesis
  with God as subject, **and the one that completes the set the book has been building**: he remembers
  Noah and the flood turns (8:1), he remembers Abraham and Lot is pulled out (19:29), he remembers his
  covenant and the exodus begins (Exodus 2:24) — **and he remembers Rachel, and the twelfth tribe's
  father is born.** The pattern's three beats run exactly as the theme describes: a situation with no
  way out of itself, *God remembered X*, and an action in the same verse. **What is distinctive here is
  the object.** Noah is a righteous man, Abraham is an intercessor forty miles away, the covenant is an
  oath — **Rachel is simply a woman who has waited**, and the sentence turns toward her by name
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:14, 23; **the theme's negative, and the Bible's first
  request to be remembered.** Every other one in Scripture is addressed to God — Samson at the pillars,
  Hannah at Shiloh, Job wanting to be hidden in Sheol and recalled, Nehemiah four times, and the man
  dying beside Jesus. **Joseph's is the only one aimed at a human being, and the only one that fails.**
  The chapter puts the request and the failure nine verses apart and **states the failure twice over**,
  *lōʾ-zākar … wayyiškāḥēhû*, as its closing line. **What the theme says about God is exactly what the
  chapter shows a man unable to supply**: the verb is an act, and the act does not come.
  Ecclesiastes 9:15 tells the same outcome as an example story — *"yet no man remembered that same poor
  man"* ([[The Forgotten Wise Man]]). See [[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]]
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:9, 30, 51, **the answer to 40:23, and it is grudging in every
  direction.** *"I remember my faults today"* (41:9) is ***mazkîr***, **the causative of the verb Joseph
  gave the cupbearer at 40:14** (*wəhizkartanî*, *cause Pharaoh to remember me*) — **and the object is
  not Joseph, it is the speaker's own offences.** He then describes Joseph to Pharaoh as *a young man, a
  Hebrew, a slave*, **without using his name.** Whether *my faults* means his old offence against the
  king or his two-year silence **is left open by the text.** And **the chapter's other memory verb is
  *šākaḥ*, twice**: the plenty *"will be forgotten"* in the famine (41:30), and **Joseph thanks God for
  making him forget his father's house** (41:51) — *"forgetting is this cycle's standing form of
  loss"*, and here it is received as a mercy. See
  [[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]]
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:9, 21; **two memories drive the chapter and neither is affectionate.** *"Joseph
  remembered the dreams"* precedes the charge of espionage; **the brothers remember the pit** and
  describe a scene Genesis never narrated — *"he begged us, and we wouldn't listen."* **After 40:23's
  *"didn't remember"* and 41:9's *"I remember my faults today"*, this is the cycle's third consecutive
  chapter organised by *zākar* and its failures**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:9, 13, 26–27; **a chapter full of recitation and empty of
  remembering.** [[Joseph]] wants his father told *"of all my glory in Egypt, and of all that you have
  seen"* (45:13), and the brothers do it — *"they told him all the words of Joseph"* (45:27) — **and
  the telling does not work.** **Nobody is asked to recall anything**: not the pit, not the dreams
  (which go unmentioned although 45:9's *"God has made me lord of all Egypt"* states their content as
  fact), not the coat. **Joseph recites the past once, in four words, in order to close it**; see
  [[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]]
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:26; ***"to this day"*** — **the seventh and last time the
  narrator of Genesis says it**, after Moab and Ammon (19:37–38), Yahweh Will Provide (22:14),
  [[Beersheba]] (26:33), the sinew of the hip (32:32) and [[Rachel]]'s pillar (35:20). **Six of the
  seven explain a name or a custom. The seventh explains a tax**: *"Joseph made it a statute
  concerning the land of Egypt to this day, that Pharaoh should have the fifth."* **The clause dates
  the telling rather than the event**, and it is the only thing in Genesis that the narrator reports
  as still legally in force
- [[Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh]] — Genesis 48:3–7, 15–16, 22; **the pericope is almost entirely
  recollection, and the book lets it stand uncorrected.** **A theophany at Luz, quoted inexactly**
  (48:3–4, against 35:11–12); **a death on the road to Ephrath thirty years back** (48:7); **a lifetime of
  being shepherded and a rescue from all evil** (48:15–16); **and a battle against an Amorite that Genesis
  never narrates** (48:22). **[[Jacob]] is the only character in the book given a long speech that is
  nothing but memory**, and **the narrator checks none of it**
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 7:7, ***"Oh remember that my life is a breath"*** — *zəkōr*,
  **Job's first word to God in the poetry**, and the verb of *"God remembered Noah"* (Genesis 8:1).
  What Job asks God to remember is that he will soon not exist: *"you will seek me diligently, but I
  shall not be"* (7:21). Seven chapters later the request becomes a wish to be hidden in Sheol and
  remembered afterwards (14:13)
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 10:9, ***zəkor-nāʾ***, *"Remember, I beg you, that you have fashioned me as clay"*:
  Job's second request to be remembered, and this time what he asks God to recall is God's own
  workmanship.
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:6, 16; God *forgetting* part of the iniquity, and Job promised that he will
  *"remember it like waters that have passed away"* — **Job's own image of the friends as a failing
  wadi (6:15), turned into a promise that grief will drain off the same way**
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 14:13; *"that you would appoint me a set time, and remember
  (tizkərēnî) me!"* — the verb of Genesis 8:1, *"God remembered Noah"*, asked for by a man who wants to
  be hidden in Sheol until the wrath is over and then recalled. And its opposite: *"His sons come to
  honor, and he doesn't know it"* (14:21), the dead man who cannot remember the living. See
  [[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]]
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:1–25; ***"Oh that I were as in the months of old"*** (29:2). **The
  whole chapter is a memory held up as evidence**: God's lamp and friendship, the children around
  him, the gate silent, the widow singing. Remembrance here is not nostalgia but testimony — the
  "before" that chapter 30's *"But now"* (30:1) is measured against, and the record chapter 31 swears
  to. Against Bildad's wicked man, who has *"no name in the street"* (18:17), Job remembers his seat
  there (29:7).
- [[Now They Mock Me]] — Job 30:1, 9, 16; ***"But now"*** — *wəʿattâ*, three times. **Memory's other
  half**: chapter 29 held up the past, and chapter 30 measures the present against it line by line, the
  young who hid (29:8) now deriding (30:1), the light he walked by (29:3) now darkness (30:26), the
  comforter (29:25) now the mourner (30:31).
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:6–8; **forgetting as the beginning of bondage.** A generation dies and a king
  *"didn't know Joseph"*; Judges 2:10 repeats the seam. Israel's creed remembers the chapter in
  three verbs (Deuteronomy 26:6). See [[Another Generation Arose - Exodus 1.6-8 and Judges 2.8-10]]
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:24; ***"God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with
  Jacob"*** — **the remembering on which the whole exodus turns**, as Noah's rescue turned on 8:1 and
  Rachel's on 30:22, and the answer to a king who *"didn't know Joseph"* (1:8). See
  [[God Remembered - Noah, Abraham, Rachel, and Israel]]
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:15–16; ***zikrî***, ***"my memorial to all generations"*** — **the noun of *zākar*
  given to Israel.** God remembered (2:24); now he gives the people the name by which they will remember
  him. And the password for the elders, *pāqōd pāqadtî*, *"I have surely visited you"*, is Joseph's
  dying sentence kept. See [[God Remembered - Noah, Abraham, Rachel, and Israel]]
- [[I Am Yahweh]] — Exodus 6:5; ***"I have remembered my covenant"*** — **2:24 said again, by God in
  the first person.** The narrator's report becomes God's own statement to Moses after the failed
  audience — [[God Remembered - Noah, Abraham, Rachel, and Israel]]
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:2; **the first command to tell the story to the next generation.**
  *"That you may tell in the hearing of your son, and of your son's son, what things I have done to
  Egypt"* — *təsappēr*, the verb of 9:16's *"that my name may be declared"*. **It is given before the
  story is over**, and it becomes the Passover question (12:26–27; 13:8, 14) and Deuteronomy's *"When
  your son asks you in time to come … then you shall tell your son, 'We were Pharaoh's slaves in
  Egypt'"* (Deuteronomy 6:20–21, WEB). The first telling in Exodus is Moses' to Jethro (18:8). **The
  Philistines remember it too**: *"as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened their hearts"* (1 Samuel 6:6,
  WEB) — [[The Diseases of Egypt - The Plagues in Deuteronomy 28]]
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:14, 24–27; **the first *zikkārôn* in Scripture, and the first thing
  Israel is given to remember with.** *"This day shall be to you for a memorial"*; and the question built
  into the rite, *"What do you mean by this service?"*, answered with the story (12:26–27)
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:42; ***"It is a night to be much observed to Yahweh … to be much observed of all
  the children of Israel throughout their generations"*** — *lêl šimmurîm*, a night of watching (WLC), said
  first of Yahweh and then of Israel. **The keeping is mutual in the grammar**; whether it means God's vigil
  and Israel's in return is a reading the Hebrew allows and does not spell out
- [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] — Exodus 13:3, 8–9, 14–16; ***"Remember this day"*** — *zākôr*, the
  infinitive absolute of the Sabbath command (20:8), first used here (WLC lemma search). The feast is *"a
  memorial (zikkārôn) between your eyes"*, and the remembering is handed down as a script: *"You shall tell
  your son … It is because of that which Yahweh did for me when I came out of Egypt"* — **every later father
  says *I***. See [[When Your Son Asks - Telling the Exodus to the Next Generation]]
- [[Manna and Quail]] — Exodus 16:3, 32–34; **memory false and memory kept.** The congregation remembers Egypt
  as *"the meat pots"* and *"our fill of bread"* (16:3), against *"they made their lives bitter"* (1:14); and
  God commands an omer kept *"throughout your generations, that they may see the bread with which I fed
  you"* (16:32) — **the third memorial in three chapters, and the first that is an object rather than a
  rite**
- [[The Battle with Amalek]] — Exodus 17:14; **the first writing in Scripture is a memorial.** *"Write this for a
  memorial (zikkārôn) in a book, and rehearse it in the ears of Joshua: that I will utterly blot out the memory
  (zēker) of Amalek"* — the first *kātab* in Scripture (WLC lemma search, Genesis–Deuteronomy). **The paradox of a
  remembered erasure**, which Deuteronomy states in two clauses: *"you shall blot out the memory of Amalek … You
  shall not forget"* (25:19). ***Zēker*** **occurs once before, for God's own name**: *"this is my memorial
  (zikrî) to all generations"* (3:15; WLC) — **God's memory lasts *lədōr dōr*; Amalek's is erased by a war
  *middōr dōr*** (17:16)

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:8–11; remembering a day means keeping its prescribed rest
- [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]] — Exodus 20:24; *ʾazkîr*, God causing his name to be remembered
  where he will come and bless
- [[The Priestly Garments]] — Exodus 28:1–43; the twelve names worn *"for a memorial before Yahweh"*.
- [[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]] — Exodus 30:16; the atonement money *"a memorial for the children of Israel before Yahweh"*.
- [[The Priestly Garments Made]] — Exodus 39:1–31; Engraved shoulder stones explicitly made as memorials for Israel.
- [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]] — Proverbs 3:1; *"don't forget my teaching"*, against the woman who forgot (2:17)
- [[The Two Silver Trumpets]] — Numbers 10:9–10; the alarm brings Israel to mind, and the trumpets are a memorial.
- [[The Seventy Elders and the Quail]] — Numbers 11:5, 18, 20; Egypt remembered without its slavery.
- [[The War Against Midian]] — Numbers 31:1–54; the gold laid up *for a memorial*.
- [[Release the Servant with Gifts]] — Deuteronomy 15:12–18; *"You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt"* (15).
- [[Passover, Weeks, and Booths]] — Deuteronomy 16:1–17; *"that you may remember the day when you came out"* (3); *"remember that you were a slave"* (12).
- [[Care for the Neighbor and Ordered Distinctions]] — Deuteronomy 22:12; the fringes restated without Numbers' reminder clause.
- [[Pledges, Wages, Justice, and Gleaning]] — Deuteronomy 24:9, 18–22; Miriam remembered, Egypt remembered, the sheaf forgotten.
- [[Justice, Family Duty, Honest Weights, and Amalek]] — Deuteronomy 25:17–19; *"Remember … You shall not forget"*.
- [[First Fruits and the Tithe Declaration]] — Deuteronomy 26:5–13; the history recited over the basket in the first person plural, and *"neither have I forgotten them"*.

## Related themes

- [[Covenant]] — the usual object of the verb: what God remembers is what he swore
- [[Judgment]] — Genesis 8:1 is remembrance ending a judgment, Revelation 18:5 is remembrance
  beginning one
- [[Hope]] — what is left when the only ground for expecting anything is that someone has promised
  and does not forget
- [[Sabbath]] — the one commandment that opens with *remember*, and the one whose observance is
  itself an act of memory
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — the *ʾazkārâ*, the memorial portion burnt on the altar (Leviticus
  2:2), which is this verb turned into a rite
- [[Divine Grief and Relenting]] — the other family of verbs for God changing his posture toward the
  world
- [[Favor and Grace]] — why the remembering happens at all, given what 8:21 says about its object

## Related people

- [[Rachel]] — the last person God is said to remember in Genesis, and the only woman
