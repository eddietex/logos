# Remembrance

## Definition / overview

What God does when the story turns.

The Hebrew verb is **zākar**, and the first thing to say about it is that the English word
*remember* is a poor fit and quietly ruins the verses it appears in. In English, remembering is a
mental event that presupposes forgetting: I remembered because I had let it slip. Scripture never
says God forgot and later recovered, and it never treats his remembering as recollection. **Zākar
with God as its subject is a verb of action.** It means to turn toward, to take up again, to act on
a commitment already made. Its object is usually a person under pressure or a covenant already
sworn, and what follows it is never a thought — it is always an intervention.

Genesis 8:1 is the first occurrence with God as subject, and it is placed at the exact structural
centre of the flood narrative:

> **1** God remembered Noah, all the animals, and all the livestock that were with him in the ship;
> and God made a wind to pass over the earth. The waters subsided.

Nothing else in the account can turn the flood. The ark's construction cannot, Noah's righteousness
cannot, the passage of time cannot — the water is an un-creation, and only the one who withdrew the
world's order can restore it. What turns it is that God calls someone to mind, and the wind follows
in the same verse. See [[The Waters Recede]] and [[The Flood as Un-Creation]].

**The grammar of the pattern.** Once the verb is noticed it is visible everywhere, and it almost
always runs in the same three beats: a situation with no way out of itself, *God remembered X*, and
then an action that changes the situation immediately. *"God remembered Rachel… and opened her
womb"*. *"God remembered his covenant… and God saw the children of Israel"*, and the exodus starts.
*"Yahweh remembered her"*, and Hannah conceives. The verb is the hinge, and the sentence after it is
the plot.

**The human counterpart is a command.** What God does by remembering, Israel is repeatedly told to
do by remembering: *"Remember the Sabbath day"* (Exodus 20:8), *"you shall remember that you were a
slave in the land of Egypt"* (Deuteronomy 5:15, and six more times in Deuteronomy), *"remember
Yahweh your God, for it is he who gives you the power to get wealth"* (Deuteronomy 8:18). Here too
the word is not primarily cognitive. To remember the exodus is to keep the feast, free your slaves,
and leave the gleanings; to forget it is not amnesia but apostasy. The same verb governs both sides
of the covenant, and the whole sacrificial and festal calendar exists to make remembering something
a nation does with its body.

**And it is what the desperate ask for.** The shortest prayers in Scripture are requests to be
remembered. Samson: *"Lord Yahweh, remember me, please, and strengthen me, please, only this once"*
(Judges 16:28). Hezekiah on his sickbed: *"Remember now, Yahweh, I beg you, how I have walked before
you"* (Isaiah 38:3). Job, wanting to be hidden in Sheol until the wrath passes and then recalled:
*"that you would appoint me a set time and remember me"* (14:13) — a request that only makes sense
if remembering is what raises the dead. And the last of them, from a cross: *"Jesus, remember me
when you come into your kingdom"* (Luke 23:42). None of these are asking God to think about them.
They are asking him to do what he did in Genesis 8:1.

**The negative form is the deepest promise in the prophets.** If remembering is acting, then *not*
remembering is the deliberate refusal to act on what is remembered — which is what forgiveness is.
*"I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more"* (Jeremiah 31:34), quoted
twice in Hebrews as the heart of the new covenant. Isaiah 43:25 puts both halves in one sentence:
*"I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake; and I will not remember your sins."*
The one thing God undertakes to forget is the only thing anyone would want him to.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 8:1** — *"God remembered Noah"*: the first occurrence with God as subject, at the apex
  of the flood chiasm, and the turn on which the world's survival hangs. See [[The Waters Recede]].
- **Genesis 9:15–16** — *"I will remember my covenant"*: the rainbow is explicitly a memory aid, and
  the striking thing is whose. It is set in the sky so that *God* will see it and remember, not so
  that people will. Every purpose clause in 9:14–16 has him as its subject — *I bring a cloud*, *I
  will remember*, *I will look at it, that I may remember* — and Noah is never told to watch for it.
  Which makes the sign the visible, permanent form of the act 8:1 turned on: the verb that saved the
  world once is here attached to an object and given a trigger. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and
  [[The Bow in the Cloud]].
- **Genesis 19:29** — *"God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the middle of the overthrow"*:
  a man rescued because of somebody else's standing. The verse is the closest parallel in Genesis to
  8:1 — both use *wayyizkōr ʾĕlōhîm* to close a total judgment by naming the one person on the other
  side of it, and in both the remembering *is* the rescue rather than a feeling that precedes it.
  What is distinctive here is that the person remembered is not the person saved: Abraham is forty
  miles away, is never told, and is not the one in danger. See
  [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]].
- **Genesis 30:22** — *"God remembered Rachel… and opened her womb"*.
- **Exodus 2:24; 6:5** — *"God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob"*:
  four hundred years of silence end in this verb, and the exodus follows from it.
- **Exodus 20:8; Deuteronomy 5:15; 8:18; 15:15; 16:12** — the command in its human form: remember
  the Sabbath, remember that you were slaves, and let the memory determine how you treat other
  people.
- **Numbers 15:39–40** — tassels on the garment *"that you may see it, and remember all the
  commandments"*: memory built into clothing.
- **Judges 16:28; 1 Samuel 1:11, 19** — Samson's last prayer, and Hannah's request answered in the
  same words she used.
- **Job 14:13** — *"that you would… appoint me a set time and remember me"*: Job asking to be hidden
  in Sheol and then recalled, which is as close as the book comes to asking for resurrection. See
  [[Sheol]].
- **Psalm 8:4** — *"What is man, that you think of him?"*, the question the verb raises when a
  human being is on the receiving end of it.
- **Psalm 25:6–7; 79:8; 137:7** — remember your mercies, do not remember the sins of my youth, and
  remember what Edom did: the same verb turned to petition, to pardon, and to vengeance.
- **Psalm 105:8, 42; 106:45** — *"He has remembered his covenant forever"*, as the summary of the
  entire national history.
- **Isaiah 43:25; Jeremiah 31:34** — the promise not to remember, which is what forgiveness is.
- **Jeremiah 2:2** — *"I remember for you the kindness of your youth"*: God recalling Israel's first
  love as the ground of an appeal.
- **Malachi 3:16** — *"a book of memory was written before him"*, for those who feared Yahweh.
- **Luke 1:54, 72** — the Magnificat and the Benedictus both close on it: *"in remembrance of
  mercy"*, *"to remember his holy covenant"*. The incarnation is announced as an instance of the
  pattern.
- **Luke 22:19; 1 Corinthians 11:24–25** — *"Do this in memory of me"*: the human command, given
  again, and given a meal to carry it, as the exodus had one.
- **Luke 23:42** — *"Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom"*, and an answer given the
  same day.
- **Acts 10:4, 31** — Cornelius's prayers and alms *"gone up for a memorial before God"*.
- **Hebrews 8:12; 10:17** — Jeremiah 31:34 quoted twice, as the clause that makes the new covenant
  new.
- **Revelation 18:5** — *"God has remembered her iniquities"*: the pattern's other edge, and proof
  that the verb is about action rather than sentiment.

## Key passages

- [[The Waters Recede]] — *"God remembered Noah"* (8:1), the flood's hinge and the theme's first
  and defining instance
- [[The Covenant with Noah]] — the same verb attached to an object and given a trigger: *"I will look
  at it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant"* (9:16)
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — Job wanting his cry to have a permanent
  record and a witness on file, which is the same need approached from underneath
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — the theme at its darkest, as a sentence: *"His memory shall perish
  from the earth. He shall have no name in the street"* (18:17). In a world with no developed
  afterlife, a remembered name and a surviving line are what a man has instead of immortality, and
  Bildad's verdict takes both
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — the answer, one chapter later: *"Oh that my words were now
  written! … That with an iron pen and lead they were engraved in the rock forever!"* (19:23–24).
  Job wants a record rather than a memorial, because a record can still be entered as evidence — and
  the book the reader is holding is the outcome of that exchange
- [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]] — Genesis 21:1, the theme's sister verb. *Pāqad*,
  *visited*, enters Scripture here and does for Sarah what *zākar* did for Noah at 8:1 and for
  Abraham at 19:29: it names the moment God turns back toward something he had left alone for a long
  time. The two verbs run in parallel through the Old Testament and meet in the exodus, where God
  both *remembers* his covenant (Exodus 2:24) and *visits* his people (Exodus 3:16; 4:31). See
  [[Yahweh Visited Sarah - Paqad and the Visitations of God]]

- [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] — Genesis 25:8, a promise kept in its own
  vocabulary with nobody pointing at it. *Śêbâ ṭôbâ*, *a good old age*, occurs twice in Genesis: at
  15:15, where God promises it in the dark, and here, where it is paid. **The narrator does not say
  that it is the same phrase.** And the formula the verse ends on — *gathered to his people* — is
  itself a remembrance device, naming a man's dead as the thing he arrives at

## Related themes

- [[Judgment]] — Genesis 8:1 is remembrance ending a judgment, Revelation 18:5 is remembrance
  beginning one
- [[Hope]] — what is left when the only ground for expecting anything is that someone has promised
  and does not forget
- [[Sabbath]] — the one commandment that opens with *remember*, and the one whose observance is
  itself an act of memory
