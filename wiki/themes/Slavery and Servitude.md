# Slavery and Servitude

## Definition / overview

One person owned by another, and the Bible's long, uneven, unfinished reckoning with the fact that
its own world ran on it.

The vocabulary is worth having straight, because English flattens it. **ʿEbed** is the general word:
it covers a chattel slave, a debt-bondsman, a royal official, and a worshipper of God, and the same
noun is used of Pharaoh's courtiers, of Israel in Egypt, and of Moses, Job, David, and the Servant of
Isaiah 53. **ʾĀmâ** and **šipḥâ** are its feminine counterparts, *šipḥâ* being the household slave
attached to a mistress — which is Hagar's status, and what makes Genesis 16 legally possible. The
Bible never coins a separate word for *slave* as an abolitionist would want it to; the same word does
duty for the most degraded and the most exalted relationships in the book, and that ambiguity is a
theological fact about it, not a translation problem.

**Genesis introduces the institution without a rule attached to it.** Servants are simply there —
part of the wealth Abram acquires in Egypt (12:16), the three hundred eighteen *born in his house*
who fight the four kings (14:14), the steward who would have been his heir (15:2–3). Nothing is
legislated, nothing is condemned, and nothing is defended. The first sustained thing Genesis does
with slavery is not a law but a story: Genesis 16, in which a household solves a theological problem
with a woman it owns.

## Development across Scripture

**The curse that gets misused.** *"Canaan is cursed. He will be a servant of servants to his
brothers"* (9:25) is the first appearance of the vocabulary in Genesis, and it is a curse on a
Canaanite, spoken by a drunk man about a grandson who was not in the room. Its abuse as a warrant for
race slavery in the Atlantic world is a reading history the wiki names as false to the text: the
Canaanites are neighbours of Israel in the land, and the passage has nothing to do with Africa. See
[[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]].

**Genesis 16 is the theme's founding narrative.** Every element of the institution shows up in
sixteen verses:

- A woman is transferred between owners and reproductive uses without being asked (16:2–3).
- She is never called by her name by the people who own her — *my servant*, *your maid* — though the
  narrator names her seven times and God names her once.
- The formula *"Behold, your maid is in your hand. Do to her whatever is good in your eyes"* (16:6)
  states the legal reality baldly: unlimited discretion over another person.
- She is afflicted with **ʿānâ** — the verb Genesis 15:13 has just used of Egypt afflicting Israel,
  and Exodus 1:11–12 will use again.
- She runs, and is told by God to go back and submit (16:9).

The chapter passes no verdict. What it does instead is lend the episode the exodus's vocabulary, so
that the reader who reaches Exodus 1 finds the Hebrews suffering the thing a Hebrew household did.

**The Torah then regulates it, and the regulations pull in two directions at once.** Exodus 21:2–11
limits Hebrew debt-slavery to six years — and in the same passage treats a daughter sold as a
concubine differently. Leviticus 25:39–46 forbids Israelites to be held as slaves, calls them God's
own servants, and in the next breath permits permanent chattel slavery of foreigners. Deuteronomy
15:12–18 adds a generous severance and, uniquely in the ancient world, Deuteronomy 23:15–16 forbids
returning a runaway slave to their master — the exact opposite of what Genesis 16:9 tells Hagar to
do. Exodus 21:20–21 punishes a master who kills a slave under the rod and lets him go if the slave
survives a day or two. These are real mitigations of a real institution and they are not its
abolition, and the wiki says both.

**The exodus makes deliverance from slavery the defining act of God in the Old Testament.** *"I am
Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage"* (Exodus
20:2) is the preface to the Ten Commandments, and Israel's own memory of being slaves is the stated
ground of a whole class of laws (Deuteronomy 5:15; 15:15; 24:18, 22). The prophets use failure to
release slaves as a capital charge (Jeremiah 34:8–22 — the covenant of the cut calf, which is Genesis
15's own ceremony turned against Judah).

**The New Testament.** Slaves are addressed directly as members of the assemblies (Ephesians 6:5–9;
Colossians 3:22–4:1; 1 Peter 2:18–25), which is itself a status claim; Galatians 3:28 says *"there is
neither slave nor free"* in Christ; 1 Corinthians 7:21–23 tells slaves to take freedom if they can
get it, and calls the free *"bought with a price"*; and Philemon asks a master to receive a runaway
*"no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother"* without ever saying he must free
him. And the central Christian confession uses the word: *"he emptied himself, taking the form of a
servant"* (Philippians 2:7) — *doulos*, slave.

**Where this leaves a reader.** The Bible contains the fullest ancient critique of slavery available
in its world, the deliverance from slavery as its defining divine act, and laws that regulate rather
than abolish it. It does not hand down an abolitionist verdict. It hands down a trajectory, an
insistence that the slave is a person whom God hears, and a memory — *you were slaves* — which is the
lever every later abolitionist actually used. Genesis 16 is where the insistence starts, with God
addressing a slave by name and no one else in the story doing so.
**Job 31:13–15 is the furthest the Old Testament goes.** *"If I have despised the cause of my male
servant or of my female servant, when they contended with me"* — a slave with a lawsuit against his
master, heard — and the reason: *"Didn't he who made me in the womb make him? Didn't one fashion us in
the womb?"* **Master and slave share one maker and one manner of making.** It is the same ground as
Proverbs 14:31, 22:2 and Malachi 2:10, applied inside a household; it does not abolish the institution,
but it denies it any basis in the nature of the persons. See [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] and
[[You Have Fashioned Me as Clay - Job 10.8-12, Psalm 139, and the Potter]].
- **Exodus 1 — the bondage begins, and the book is built on its verb.** *ʿĀbad*, *serve*, is what
  Egypt makes Israel do five times over in 1:13–14; *"Let my son go, that he may serve me"* (4:23) is
  the demand that answers it; and *"the house of bondage"*, *bêt ʿăbādîm* (20:2), is what God brought
  them out of. **Exodus is the story of a change of masters.** The storage cities and the levy
  reappear under Solomon (1 Kings 9:19–21), and the law forbids an Israelite to do to another what
  Egypt did (Leviticus 25:43, 46). See [[The Oppression in Egypt]].

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:2, 10, 17

The same address recalls release from bondage (20:2), requires rest for male and female servants
(20:10), and lists the neighbor's servants among the household one must not covet (20:17).
**Protection and continuing ownership coexist.** The command limits a master's demand for work; it
does not announce emancipation for every servant. Deuteronomy 5:14–15 makes equal rest and
remembered slavery the explicit rationale. See
[[The Ten Commandments Retold - Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5]]. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### The first ordinance — Exodus 21:1–11

**The ordinances open with a bought Hebrew servant, before homicide, theft, or property.** He serves
six years and goes out *ḥopšî*, *free*, *ḥinnām*, *"without paying anything"* (21:2, WLC). That is
the word Laban used when he asked whether Jacob should *"serve me for nothing"* (Genesis 29:15). The
law also divides families: a wife the master gave stays with her children, and *"he shall go out by
himself"* (21:4). The man who will not leave because he loves his master, wife, and children has his
ear bored at the *məzûzâ*, the Passover doorpost, and serves *"for ever"* (21:5–6). A daughter sold
as a female servant *"shall not go out as the male servants do"* (21:7), but she may not be sold to
foreigners, must be allowed redemption, and keeps her food, clothing, and marital rights or goes
free (21:8–11).

**A correction of emphasis on this page:** its summary above calls Exodus 21:2–11 a limit on
*debt-slavery*. The passage itself names purchase (21:2) and a father's sale (21:7), not debt.
Debt is a plausible setting, and Leviticus 25:39 names poverty, but Exodus does not state it. The
tensions with Deuteronomy 15 (the woman's release) and Leviticus 25 (*for ever* against the
Jubilee) are set out on
[[The Hebrew Servant Goes Free - Exodus 21, Deuteronomy 15, and Jeremiah 34]]. See
[[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]].

### Violence, injury, and liability — Exodus 21:12–36

The same collection that punishes kidnapping with death (21:16) protects servants against some
acts of their masters: punishment when a servant dies under the rod (21:20), and release for a
destroyed eye or lost tooth (21:26–27). **Protection and ownership remain in tension:** 21:21
explains the master's exemption by calling the servant his property, and 21:32 pays the master
thirty shekels for a servant killed by a bull. This is unequal legal status, not abolition.
The WEB says *"gets up after a day or two"* in 21:21; the page does not substitute an unverified
claim about subsequent death. Nor does *"punished"* in 21:20 by itself specify execution.
See [[Kidnapping and Sale - Joseph and the Law of Exodus 21]].
See [[Laws of Violence and Injury]].
### Sold for his theft — Exodus 22:3

A thief who cannot make restitution *"shall be sold for his theft"* (22:3). Servitude here is a
way of paying a debt to the person robbed. The text does not say whether 21:2's six-year limit
applies to such a sale.
See [[Laws of Property and Restitution]].

### The servant’s son refreshed — Exodus 23:1–19

The seventh day is to refresh the son of the servant as well as the alien, while the ox and donkey
rest (23:12). It places a recurring limit on a household's extraction of labor. **The shared rest
does not cancel the servitude regulated in Exodus 21:1–11.** Protection and unequal status remain in
the same law; neither should be erased from the synthesis.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### Serve them, or serve Yahweh — Exodus 23:20–33

The book's master verb *ʿābad* appears three times in ten verses: *"nor serve them"* (23:24), *"You
shall serve Yahweh your God"* (23:25), and *"if you serve their gods"* (23:33; all MAM). At the edge
of the land, the danger is not a return to Egypt's service but a new master. Service to Yahweh is
paired with blessing; service to the peoples' gods with a snare. See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]].

## Key passages

- [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]] — Exodus 23:1–19; the servant’s son refreshed.

- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — Genesis 9:25–27, the vocabulary's first appearance, in a
  curse; and the history of its misuse
- [[Abram in Egypt]] — Genesis 12:16, where *"male servants, female servants"* are part of the payment
  taken for a wife, and the likeliest origin of the Egyptian slave in the next chapters
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — Genesis 14:14, the three hundred eighteen *born in his
  house*: a private army that is also property
- [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] — Genesis 15:2–3, a slave who would have inherited everything;
  and 15:13, four hundred years of servitude foretold as the shape of Israel's future
- [[Hagar and Ishmael]] — Genesis 16, the theme's founding narrative, and the only place in Scripture
  where God addresses a slave by name and sends her back
- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17:12–13, 23, 27, where the covenant sign is
  cut into the bodies of the people the household owns. Four times in one chapter the legislation
  names *"He who is born in your house, and he who is bought with your money"*, and the closing verse
  makes it the last thing said: *"All the men of his house, those born in the house, and those bought
  with money from a foreigner, were circumcised with him."* They are not promised anything by the
  covenant they are marked for, and they are not asked — and the same rite is what Exodus 12:44–48
  will make the door by which a purchased foreigner keeps the Passover. Genesis reports the whole of
  it without a word of comment, one chapter after a household solved a theological problem with a
  woman it owned
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:14, 17, where slaves are again both currency and casualties.
  *"Male servants and female servants"* are handed over as part of the settlement for a wife, in the
  same formula as 12:16 — and three verses later the household's *"female servants"* are named among
  those whose wombs God had shut and then healed. **They are counted in the compensation and counted
  in the damage, and nobody asks them anything in either place**
- [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]] — Genesis 21:9–21, the arrangement of Genesis 16
  ended by expulsion. Sarah says *"this servant"* and *"the son of this servant"* and never a name;
  the demand is that an acknowledged, circumcised firstborn be disinherited, which the second-
  millennium codes and Deuteronomy 21:15–17 would both have refused her; **God endorses it, and in
  the same breath puts the slave back into the sentence her owner and her master had both left her
  out of** — *"because of the boy, and because of your servant"* (21:12). She leaves with bread and a
  skin of water from a household Genesis has twice called rich, and ends the chapter free, with a
  wilderness of her own and a daughter-in-law she chose
- Genesis 37; 39–41 — Joseph sold, enslaved, imprisoned, and raised to rule the country that bought
  him *(not yet ingested)*
- Exodus 1–15 — the affliction, and the deliverance that becomes the Old Testament's defining act
- Exodus 21:1–11 ([[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]]), 20–21, 26–27; Leviticus 25:39–55; Deuteronomy
  15:12–18; 23:15–16 — the laws, and the tension between them
- Jeremiah 34:8–22 — Judah re-enslaving the freed, judged by the ceremony of Genesis 15
- Isaiah 42:1–9; 52:13–53:12 — *ʿebed* as the highest title in the book
- Galatians 3:28; 4:21–31; 1 Corinthians 7:21–23; Philemon; Philippians 2:7 — the New Testament's
  handling
- [[A Wife for Isaac]] — Genesis 24, whose protagonist is property. **He runs an estate, carries its
  wealth on ten camels, swears a binding oath and negotiates its terms first, prays the first unbidden
  petition in Scripture, and chooses the next matriarch of Israel — and Genesis never gives him a
  name.** He is *the servant* and *the man*, and *my master* is in his mouth thirteen times in one
  chapter. See [[Abraham's Servant]]

- [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] — Genesis 25:12, where the heading of Ishmael's
  genealogy still calls his mother *"Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's servant"* — **the identical
  two-part description she was given at 16:1**, applied thirty-eight years after the owner's death
  and decades after the expulsion that ended the arrangement. Genesis never stops identifying her by
  the woman who owned her, and never remarks on it

- [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]] — Genesis 29:24, 29; **two women given away in two matching verses**, as
  part of a wedding settlement: [[Zilpah]] to [[Leah]], [[Bilhah]] to [[Rachel]], each named and each
  the object of the sentence rather than its subject. ***Šipḥâ*** is the word used of [[Hagar]] at
  16:1, and within one chapter both will be given again — this time to Jacob, by their mistresses,
  on the Genesis 16 precedent — and **four of the twelve tribes will descend from them.** The same
  passage has a free man contract fourteen years of his own labour because he has nothing else to pay
  with, which is the theme's other half: **the household's slaves are property, and the son-in-law is
  wages**
- [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]] — Genesis 30:3–13, **the arrangement of Genesis 16 run twice more
  in one chapter, by a household that had watched it produce the wrong son.** [[Rachel]] gives
  [[Bilhah]] with the adoption formula *"that she may bear on my knees"* (30:3); [[Leah]] gives
  [[Zilpah]] four verses later — **and Leah is not barren.** She has borne four sons and has merely
  stopped, so **the second transfer is a move in a contest rather than a remedy for anything.** Both
  women are handed over ***ləʾiššâ***, *as wife*, and Genesis then calls them *concubine* at 35:22 and
  *wife* again at 37:2 without reconciling the terms. **Their mistresses name all four sons and neither
  slave is recorded speaking at any birth** — four of Israel's twelve tribes descend from two women the
  Bible never quotes. **And no later list distinguishes them**: 35:23–26 groups the twelve by mother and
  then says flatly, *"These are the sons of Jacob, who were born to him."* See
  [[The Twelve Sons and the Lists That Reorder Them]]
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:15, 33, 41; ***mākar***, *he sold us* — **two free
  daughters of the household use the verb for selling a slave about their own father**, and it is the
  verb Joseph's brothers will use of him at 37:28. Meanwhile [[Zilpah]] and [[Bilhah]] appear as
  *"the tent of the two female servants"*, unnamed, searched, and silent. **And Jacob's own twenty
  years are summed up in the noun Exodus reserves for Egypt**: ***ʿŏnî***, affliction (31:42)
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:27–28, 36; **the Bible's only priced brother, and the price is
  ordinary.** *"Sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver"* — **the sanctuary tariff's
  exact valuation for a male between five and twenty years old** (Leviticus 27:5), and Joseph is seventeen.
  **The law that supplies the figure also makes the act capital**: *"He who kidnaps a man and sells him …
  shall surely be put to death"* (Exodus 21:16; Deuteronomy 24:7). **Genesis passes no verdict at all**, and
  never says who took the money. He arrives in Egypt as the property of [[Potiphar]], *śar haṭṭabbāḥîm*,
  and the first Israelite in Egypt is a slave. See
  [[Twenty Pieces of Silver - The Price of a Brother]]
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:4, 14–15; **the slave waits on the men who wait on the
  king.** *Wayšāret*, *ministered* — **the verb of 39:4**, used a third time of Joseph in two chapters,
  now of a convicted man attending two of Pharaoh's household officers. **The chapter's plot turns
  entirely on whether a restored courtier will mention a slave's name to a king**, which is the exact
  measure of what a slave could and could not do for himself; and Joseph's own account of how he got
  here is *gunnōb gunnabtî*, *I was indeed stolen* — **the verb Exodus 21:16 makes a capital crime**
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:12, 40–44, **a slave is introduced to a king by his
  owner's title and is running the country forty verses later.** The cupbearer's *"a young man, a Hebrew,
  ***servant to the captain of the guard***"* (41:12) is **the last time Joseph's legal status is stated
  in Genesis**, and **the book never says he was manumitted** — it says he was promoted, which is not the
  same thing and which Genesis does not pause to reconcile. **The inversion is total**: the man who
  belonged to [[Potiphar]] is now the man *"without [whom] shall no man lift up his hand or his foot in
  all the land of Egypt"* (41:44). **And the chapter plants the instrument of a much larger servitude** —
  the fifth part of 41:34, which by 47:19–25 has the Egyptians saying *"buy us and our land for bread,
  and we and our land will be servants to Pharaoh."* **The book that opens Israel's bondage in Exodus 1
  has an Israelite design the machinery**
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:10–13; **the brothers call themselves *"your servants"* — *ʿăbādeyḵā* — five
  times over four verses, to the brother they sold into servitude.** It is ordinary deference to a
  foreign official and Genesis does not comment on it once. **The man receiving it was bought for
  twenty pieces of silver by the men offering it**
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:18, 28; **they fear being made what they made him.** *"That he may
  seek occasion against us, attack us, and **seize us as slaves**, along with our donkeys"* (43:18) — said
  by the ten men who sold a brother into servitude, **about the brother they sold**, in a chapter where
  they also call themselves *"your servants"* to his face (43:28). **Genesis does not point at it**, and
  44:16 will turn it into their own verdict: *"we are my lord's slaves"*

- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:9–33; ***ʿebed* saturates the chapter in both of
  its senses.** *Your servants* is court courtesy, said a dozen times; ***slave* is the actual
  sentence**, pronounced four (44:9, 10, 16, 17). **The men who sold a brother into Egyptian slavery
  offer themselves for it collectively** — *"we are my lord's slaves"* (44:16) — **and are refused**;
  [[Joseph's Steward]] has already narrowed the penalty to one man and declared the rest *blameless*
  (44:10), **which is the ruling that makes the chapter work.** And it ends with [[Judah]] asking to
  be that one man: *"let your servant stay instead of the boy, my lord's slave"* (44:33). **The
  vocabulary they feared at 43:18 is the vocabulary he volunteers in**
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:18–26; **the theme turns around and takes the Egyptians.**
  ***ʿĂbādîm*** **has run in one direction for ten chapters** — a boy sold for twenty pieces of
  silver (37:28), *"an Egyptian's slave"* (39:1), eleven brothers offering themselves as *"my lord's
  slaves"* and being refused (44:16). **At 47:19 a nation offers itself to Pharaoh and is accepted**:
  *"Buy us and our land for bread, and we and our land will be servants to Pharaoh."* **They propose
  it, they repeat it as gratitude** — *"You have saved our lives! … we will be Pharaoh's servants"*
  (47:25) — **and the man who was sold buys.** **Exodus 1:11–14 turns the same machinery on Israel**,
  and Deuteronomy 6:21 gives the phrase back to Israel as its own memory: *"We were Pharaoh's slaves
  in Egypt."* See [[Buy Us and Our Land - Egypt Sold into Servitude and Israel After It]]
- [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]] — Genesis 49:15; ***mas-ʿōbēd***, *a serving levy* — **the corvée, and
  the only time in Genesis a son of Jacob is told he will be put under one.** **[[Issachar]] sees that
  rest is good and the land is pleasant, and pays for both with his back**: *"He bows his shoulder to
  the burden, and becomes a servant doing forced labor."* **The noun is the Egyptian bondage's own** —
  Exodus 1:11 sets *taskmasters*, *śārê missîm*, over Israel — **and it is what Solomon puts the
  surviving Canaanites under** (1 Kings 9:21). **The word is spoken in Egypt, by a man whose
  descendants are four hundred years from hearing it applied to all of them**
- **Genesis 50:18 — *"behold, we are your servants"*, offered and refused for the second time**
  (compare 44:16–17): the dream of 37:7 completes in the one form [[Joseph]] will not accept —
  [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]]

- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:18–19; **the one place in Scripture where death is commended
  specifically for abolishing slavery.** *"There the prisoners are at ease together. They don't hear
  the voice of the taskmaster. The small and the great are there. The servant is free from his
  master."* The taskmaster (*nōgēś*) is the Exodus word for Israel's overseers in Egypt, and Job
  reaches for it to describe what the grave has none of. **It is said by a man who had *"a very
  great household"* of servants** (1:3) and had just lost most of them to raiders and fire in a
  single afternoon
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 24:9–11; *"There are those who pluck the
  fatherless from the breast, and take a pledge of the poor"* — a nursing child seized for a debt, as
  the creditor of 2 Kings 4:1 comes for the widow's two sons — and the labourers who make oil and
  tread winepresses *"within the walls of these men"*, unpaid and thirsty.
- [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] — Job 31:13–15; the slaves' lawsuit heard, because *"one"* fashioned master and slave in the
  womb; and 31:14, *"When he visits, what shall I answer him?"* — the master's treatment of his slave as
  a matter he will answer for to God.
- [[Behemoth and Leviathan]] — Job 41:4; ***ʿebed ʿôlām*, a servant forever.** *"Will he make a covenant with you,
  that you should take him for a servant forever?"* — the term of the slave who chooses lifelong service and
  has his ear pierced at the door (Exodus 21:6; Deuteronomy 15:17), applied to a creature no one can bind.
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:11–14; **the corvée.** *Śārê missîm*, *taskmasters* (officers of the levy), set over
  Israel *"to afflict them with their burdens"*; the storage cities [[Pithom]] and [[Rameses]]; and
  *ʿābad* five times in two verses, *bəpārek*, *ruthlessly*. **Genesis 15:13's *serve* and *afflict*
  kept word for word.** The adverb returns only in Leviticus 25's prohibition — *"You shall not rule
  over him with harshness, but shall fear your God"* — see
  [[With Harshness - Exodus 1.13-14 and Leviticus 25]]
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:11, 23; **the *burdens* of 1:11 seen, and the *ʿăbōdâ* named twice in one
  verse** — *"the children of Israel sighed because of the bondage … their cry came up to God because
  of the bondage"*. The king dies; the service does not end with him. **It is at this word that God
  turns** (2:24–25)
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:12; ***"you shall serve God on this mountain"*** — *taʿabdûn*, the book's governing
  root. **The first thing God says about Israel's future is that they will serve him**: the exodus is
  announced as a change of master before anything else. And the law of release takes up the bush's
  *rêqām*: a freed slave is not to be sent away *"empty"* (Deuteronomy 15:13), as Israel will not leave
  Egypt *"empty-handed"* (3:21). See [[You Shall Not Go Empty-Handed - The Plunder of Egypt]]
- [[Moses Returns to Egypt]] — Exodus 4:23; ***"Let my son go, that he may serve me"*** — *wəyaʿabdēnî* — **the
  book's thesis in one word.** Egypt made Israel serve; God claims the service, as a father claims a
  son. The demand is a change of master
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:9, 15–18; ***"Go … and work"*** — *ʿibdû*, *serve* — **Pharaoh's
  counter-command to 4:23.** Yahweh says *"that he may serve me"*; Pharaoh says *"serve"* and means the
  brickfields, makes the *service* heavy (*tikbad hāʿăbōdâ*, 5:9), and the Israelite officers call
  themselves *"your servants"* three times (5:15–16) — the posture of Genesis 47:19, 25. **The WEB's
  *work* is the Hebrew *serve***
- [[I Am Yahweh]] — Exodus 6:5–9; ***maʿăbidîm***, ***mēʿăbōdātām***, ***ʿăbōdâ qāšâ*** — **every
  *ʿābad* in God's speech is Egypt's.** The service God asked for (3:12; 4:23) is not mentioned, and
  the *"cruel bondage"* of 1:14 is what keeps Israel from listening (6:9)
- [[Water Turned to Blood]] — Exodus 7:16; ***"Let my people go, that they may serve me in the
  wilderness"*** — *wəyaʿabdunî*, the service God claims against the service Egypt imposes, now joined to
  the charge *"until now you haven't listened"*
- [[Frogs, Lice, and Flies]] — Exodus 8:1, 14, 20; ***"Let my people go, that they may serve
  me"***, twice more. The dead frogs are piled *ḥŏmārim ḥŏmārim*, *heaps* — the WLC tags it with the
  lemma of *ḥōmer*, the *"mortar"* of Israel's labour (1:14); the play is a reading, not the text's claim
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:3, 7–11, 24–26; **serve, three ways.** God's demand is unchanged,
  *"that they may serve me"* (10:3). **Pharaoh's *ʿăbādîm*, his servants, speak for the first time** —
  *"Let the men go, that they may serve Yahweh"* (10:7). Pharaoh's offers are each phrased *"Go, serve
  Yahweh"* with something held back (10:8, 11, 24). And Moses: *"of it we must take to serve Yahweh our
  God; and we don't know with what we must serve Yahweh"* (10:26). **The court that serves Pharaoh asks
  him to let Israel go to serve another master** — [[Pharaoh's Servants (of the Exodus)]]
- [[The Last Plague Announced]] — Exodus 11:5, 8; **the master's house bows to the slave's leader.**
  The firstborn of the *šipḥâ* behind the mill dies with Pharaoh's heir (11:5), and *"all these
  servants of yours"* will *"bow down themselves to me, saying, 'Get out'"* (11:8). **The release
  becomes an expulsion**: *gārēš yəgārēš*, *"he will surely thrust you out"* (11:1)
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:25–26; ***"What do you mean by this service?"*** **Israel trades one
  *ʿăbōdâ* for another in a single chapter.** The noun has meant the bondage of Egypt in every earlier use
  in Exodus (1:14; 2:23; 5:9, 11; 6:6, 9); here, twice, it is the Passover rite — the first service
  rendered to the God who said *"Let my son go, that he may serve me"* (4:23). The doorpost, *məzûzâ*,
  returns at 21:6 for the slave who chooses to stay
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:31, 44; ***"go, serve Yahweh"*** — **the king who made Israel serve tells it
  whom to serve**, in the verb of 4:23. And the Passover law's slave: *"every man's servant who is bought
  for money, when you have circumcised him, then shall he eat of it"* — **the freed people's first law about
  their own slaves admits them to the rite of freedom**, on Genesis 17's terms (*miqnat kesep*; WLC), while
  the hired servant is excluded
- [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] — Exodus 13:3, 5, 14; ***"out of the house of bondage"*** — *bêt
  ʿăbādîm*, a house of slaves, named for the first time in this sense (WLC) and from here a formula (20:2;
  Deuteronomy 5:6 and five more). **And the *ʿăbōdâ* changes masters again**: *"you shall keep this service
  (wəʿābadtā ʾet-hāʿăbōdâ)"* (13:5), the bondage-word now a festival
- [[The Crossing of the Sea]] — Exodus 14:5, 12, 31; **the root *ʿābad* three ways in one chapter.** Pharaoh:
  *"we have let Israel go from serving us"* (*mēʿobdēnû*); the people: *"Leave us alone, that we may serve
  the Egyptians … better for us to serve the Egyptians than to die in the wilderness"* — a word they say they
  spoke in Egypt and the text does not record; and the close, *"his servant Moses"* (*ʿabdô*), **the first
  time Moses is called Yahweh's servant** (WLC). **The book's root is used of Moses' relation to God at the
  moment Israel stops being Egypt's**
- [[Bitter Water at Marah]] — Exodus 15:23, 25; **Egypt's bitterness and Egypt's statute.** The water is
  *mārîm*, the root of the lives *"made bitter with hard service"* (1:14); and the *ḥōq* Yahweh sets at
  Marah has as its only Exodus precedent the brick *"quota"* of 5:14 (*ḥoqkem*; WLC). **The first statute
  after the sea replaces the quota of the house of bondage**
- [[Manna and Quail]] — Exodus 16:3, 29–30; **slavery remembered as plenty, and rest given as a day.** *"When
  we sat by the meat pots, when we ate our fill of bread"*; and the first time people *rest* (*šābat*) —
  the rest Pharaoh called idleness (5:5) — *"So the people rested on the seventh day"*

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:2, 10, 17; former slaves must give servants rest, while
  household servitude continues
- [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]] — Exodus 21:1–11; the first ordinance limits a Hebrew's service
  to six years, keeps a master-given wife and children, provides lifelong service at the doorpost,
  and protects a daughter sold as a female servant

- [[Laws of Violence and Injury]] — Exodus 21:12–36; distinctions and remedies for injury and loss
- [[Laws of Property and Restitution]] — Exodus 22:3; a thief who cannot pay is sold for his theft
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; *ʿābad* three times: the peoples' gods or Yahweh.
