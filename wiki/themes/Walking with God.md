# Walking with God

## Definition / overview

A phrase the Bible introduces without explaining, uses of exactly two men before the flood, and then
converts into its standard metaphor for a whole life lived toward God.

It appears first in [[From Adam to Noah]], in the middle of a register that says nothing about
anybody's character: *"After Methuselah's birth, Enoch walked with God for three hundred years"*
(5:22), and again two verses later, *"Enoch walked with God, and he was not found, for God took
him"* (5:24). Then it is said of one other person — *"Noah walked with God"* (6:9) — and then, in
that exact form, of no one else in Scripture.

**The Hebrew.** The verb is *hālak*, "to walk", in the *hithpael* stem, with the preposition *ʾet*,
"with". The *hithpael* here is iterative or habitual rather than strictly reflexive: *walked back
and forth*, *walked about*, *went on walking*. So the sense is not a single journey but a settled
manner of going, and the preposition makes it companionable rather than merely obedient. It is worth
noticing that the same stem of the same verb was used, two chapters earlier, of God himself: *"They
heard Yahweh God's voice walking in the garden in the cool of the day"* (3:8). Whatever the phrase
means, the vocabulary it uses is the vocabulary of Eden, applied now to a man rather than to God.

**What it does not do is define itself.** Genesis attaches no content — no law kept, no altar built,
no word received, no act performed. Enoch walks with God for three hundred years and the text
records nothing he did in them. Two consequences follow. The phrase has been enormously productive
in later theology and devotion precisely because it is empty of specifics; and any confident account
of what Enoch's righteousness consisted of is coming from somewhere other than Genesis. The
Septuagint already fills the gap, rendering both verses *euērestēsen tō theō*, *"he pleased God"* —
an interpretation, and the one Hebrews 11:5 builds its argument from when it says Enoch *"has had
testimony given to him that before his translation he had been well pleasing to God."* See
[[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]].

**Related but not identical constructions.** Abraham is told *"Walk before me, and be blameless"*
(17:1) — a different preposition (*lipnê*, before), and arguably a different relation: service in
God's sight rather than company at his side. Israel is repeatedly told to *walk in* God's ways
(Deuteronomy 8:6), which is obedience to a path. Micah 6:8 has *"walk humbly with your God"* and
uses the *ʾet* of Genesis 5. Sorting these is not pedantry: the Genesis 5 form is the intimate one,
it is used of two men, and the Bible does not hand it out again.

**Where it sits in the chapter.** This matters as much as the philology. Genesis 5 is a chapter about
dying — eight entries end *"then he died"* — and the one man of whom the walking is said is the one
man who does not. The phrase and the exception arrive in the same verse. Genesis does not say the
walking *caused* the taking, and the syntax of 5:24 sets them side by side rather than in sequence;
but the reader is plainly meant to connect them, and Hebrews 11:5 does exactly that. Then, one
chapter later, the same phrase is applied to the man who survives the flood. Both times, walking
with God goes with not being destroyed.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 5:22, 5:24** — [[Enoch (son of Jared)]] walks with God for three hundred years and is
  taken. The phrase's first appearance, with no antecedent and no explanation.
- **Genesis 6:9** — *"Noah was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time. Noah walked
  with God."* The second and last use of the exact idiom, of the second man to be exempted from a
  general death. It arrives third in a list of three descriptions, after *righteous* and
  *blameless*, and it is the only one of the three the narrative never illustrates: what Noah is
  then shown doing is obeying instructions, twice reported in the same four words. See
  [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 3:8** — the same verb and stem, of God walking in the garden: the vocabulary this idiom
  borrows.
- **Genesis 17:1** — *"Walk before me, and be blameless"*: both of Noah's words (6:9) given back to
  Abraham as an imperative, with the preposition changed from *ʾet*, *with*, to *lə-pānay*, *before*.
  It is the first thing ever asked of a party to a covenant in the Bible, and the switch is
  consistent afterwards — 24:40 and 48:15 keep *before*, and *walked with God* is never said of anyone
  again. *Walking with* is companionship, said of two men exempted from a general death; *walking
  before* is the posture of a servant in his lord's sight, the idiom of 1 Samuel 2:30 and 1 Kings
  8:25. Genesis changes the preposition at the exact point where the relationship acquires
  obligations. See [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].
- **Genesis 24:40** — *"Yahweh, before whom I walk, will send his angel with you"*: Abraham's own
  self-description, quoted back by his servant to a household in Mesopotamia, and the ground he gives
  for expecting the errand to succeed. See [[A Wife for Isaac]]. And **48:15**, Jacob on the God
  *"before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked"* *(not yet ingested)*.
- **Leviticus 26:12** — *"I will walk among you, and will be your God"*: the direction reversed,
  and made covenantal.
- **Micah 6:8** — *"to walk humbly with your God"*, using the *ʾet* of Genesis 5, and the closest
  the prophets come to defining the phrase as a requirement rather than a description.
- **Malachi 2:6** — of Levi, *"he walked with me in peace and uprightness"*.
- **Amos 3:3** — *"Do two walk together, unless they have agreed?"* — the metaphor turned into an
  argument about covenant.
- **Hebrews 11:5–6** — Enoch's walking read as faith, via the Septuagint's *"pleased God"*, and made
  the basis of the general claim that *"without faith it is impossible to be well pleasing to him."*
- Later strands: the New Testament's *peripateō* language for the Christian life — walking in
  newness of life (Romans 6:4), by faith and not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7), in the light (1 John
  1:7) — which is this Genesis metaphor naturalized into ordinary Christian vocabulary.

### The path you do not walk — Proverbs 1:15–16

Proverbs' first use of the road image is negative: *"My son, don't walk on the path with them. Keep your
foot from their path, for their feet run to evil"* (1:15–16). *Bəderek ʾittām*, *on the path with
them*, shares its two key words with Psalm 1:1's *derek ḥaṭṭāʾîm*, *"the path of sinners"*. Walking
*with God* has here its first counterpart in the book: walking *with them*. The two-ways teaching that
Proverbs 4:10–19 will make explicit starts as a question of company. Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*. See [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]].

### Ways and paths — Proverbs 2:8–20

The lecture uses *derek*, *ʾōraḥ*, and *maʿgāl* twelve times: God guards *"the paths of
justice"* and *"the way of his saints"* (2:8); the son will understand *"every good path"* (2:9); the
perverse walk *"the ways of darkness"* (2:13); the strange woman's paths go *"to the departed spirits"*
(2:18), and her guests never reach *"the paths of life"* (2:19); the lecture's purpose is *"that you
may walk in the way of good men"* (2:20). Psalm 1:6 and Matthew 7:13–14 give the two-ways picture its
classic forms (WEB, checked against bible-api.com). See [[If You Seek Her as Silver]]. Source: Proverbs 2 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Straight paths — Proverbs 3:6

*"In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight"* (3:6). *Make straight* is
*yəyaššēr*, from *yāšar*, the root of *the upright* (*yəšārîm*, 2:7, 21). The son walks; God straightens
the road. Proverbs 16:9 puts the same order as a saying — *"A man's heart plans his course, but Yahweh
directs his steps"*. See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]]. Source: Proverbs 3 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The way of wisdom and the path of the wicked — Proverbs 4:10–19

The father has already *"taught"* and *"led"* the son in *"the way of wisdom"* and *"straight
paths"* (4:11); the lecture is about staying on a road already begun. Six commands in two verses
keep him off the other one: *"Don't enter … Don't walk … Avoid it … don't pass by it. Turn from it,
and pass on"* (4:14–15). The two roads are then given their lighting — the righteous path
brightening like dawn, the wicked way so dark its walkers *"don't know what they stumble over"*
(4:18–19). See [[The Two Ways - Proverbs 4, Psalm 1, and Matthew 7]].

See [[The Two Paths]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Neither right nor left — Proverbs 4:26–27

*"Make the path of your feet level. Let all of your ways be established. Don't turn to the right
hand nor to the left. Remove your foot from evil"* (4:26–27). The idiom is Deuteronomy's — *"You
shall not turn away to the right hand or to the left"* (Deuteronomy 5:32) — and Joshua's
(Joshua 1:7; both WEB, verified at bible-api.com, not yet ingested); there the road is the law,
here the father's teaching. Job claimed to have walked it: *"I have kept his way, and not turned
away"* (Job 23:11). See [[Heart, Eyes, and Feet - Proverbs 4 and Job 31]].

See [[Keep Your Heart]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### The teaching that walks with you — Proverbs 6:22–23

*"When you walk, it will lead you"* (6:22): the teaching itself goes with the son, guiding
(*tanḥeh*, the verb of Exodus 15:13's *"You … have led the people"* and Psalm 23:3's *"He guides
me"*), keeping watch over his sleep, and talking with him when he wakes; *"Reproofs of instruction
are the way of life"* (6:23). The walk is lit by a lamp. See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]].
Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*,
2026-10-25.

In Proverbs 7 the young man *"went the way to her house"* (7:8, *yiṣʿāḏ*, *paced*), and the
father's verdict is a road warning: *"Don't let your heart turn to her ways. Don't go astray in her
paths"* (7:25). Her house is *"the way to Sheol"* (7:27). See [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 7; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Wisdom walks the paths of justice — Proverbs 8:2, 20

Wisdom stands *"where the paths meet"* (8:2, *bêṯ nəṯîḇôṯ*) and says *"I walk in the way of
righteousness, in the middle of the paths of justice"* (8:20, *nəṯîḇôṯ mišpāṭ*). The paths of justice
are the ones Yahweh guards at 2:8. Wisdom is not only a road the son takes (4:11) but a walker he can
follow. See [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

## Key passages

- [[Keep Your Heart]] — Proverbs 4:26–27; a level path, turning neither right nor left

- [[The Two Paths]] — Proverbs 4:10–19; the way of wisdom, the path of the wicked, and six commands to avoid it

- [[From Adam to Noah]] — where the phrase enters the Bible, and the man it exempts from the
  chapter's refrain
- [[Noah and the Flood]] — the idiom's second and final use, at 6:9, alongside the two words the
  passage does define
- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17:1, where the idiom is handed to Abraham
  with a different preposition and turned from a description into a command
- [[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]] — what Hebrews and Jude make of it, and
  the Septuagint's *"pleased God"*
- [[Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh]] — Genesis 48:15; ***"the God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac
  walked"*** — **the idiom's last appearance in Genesis, and [[Jacob]] assigns it to his fathers and does
  not claim it for himself.** **It is the *hithallēk* of [[Enoch (son of Jared)]] and [[Noah]] and of the
  command at 17:1**, *"I am God Almighty. Walk before me, and be blameless"* — **the one other verse where
  the verb and *El Shaddai* stand together, and 48:3 supplies the name.** **The man declining the verb is
  the one who has just called God his shepherd and his redeemer in the same sentence**
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:11; *"My foot has held fast to his steps. I have
  kept his way, and not turned away"* — the walk of Enoch and Noah told by the walker himself, in the
  chapter where he cannot find the one he walks after: God's *steps* are kept, and God is not in any
  direction (23:8–9). *"He knows the way that I take"* (23:10).
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:2–5; *"by his light I walked through darkness … when the friendship of
  God was in my tent"*. *Sôd ʾĕlôah*, God's intimate counsel, as a household presence — **the book's
  only description of Job's piety from inside**, and given in the past tense.
- [[The Offering for the Sanctuary]] — Exodus 25:8; ***"that I may dwell among them"*** — God proposing to live in
  the camp, not only to be walked with. Leviticus 26:11–12 joins the two: *"I will set my tent among you … I will
  walk among you"* (WEB; *not yet ingested*). See [[The Dwelling of God]]

- [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]] — Proverbs 1:15–16; the path walked *with them*, the book's first road
- [[If You Seek Her as Silver]] — Proverbs 2:8–20; the two roads, to the paths of life and to the shades
- [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]] — Proverbs 3:6; *"he will make your paths straight"*
- [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] — Proverbs 6:22–23; the teaching leads the son as he walks, and
  reproof is *"the way of life"*
- [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] — Proverbs 7:8, 25, 27; the way to her house, and her paths
- [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] — Proverbs 8:2, 20; Wisdom at the crossroads, walking the paths of justice
