# Walking with God

## Definition / overview

A phrase the Bible introduces without explaining, uses of exactly two men before the flood, and then
converts into its standard metaphor for a whole life lived toward God.

It appears first in [[From Adam to Noah]], in the middle of a register that says nothing about
anybody's character: *"After Methuselah's birth, Enoch walked with God for three hundred years"*
(5:22), and again two verses later, *"Enoch walked with God, and he was not found, for God took
him"* (5:24). Then it is said of one other person — *"Noah walked with God"* (6:9) — and then, in
that exact form, of no one else in Scripture.

**The Hebrew.** The verb is *hālak*, "to walk", in the *hithpael* stem, with the preposition *ʾet*,
"with". The *hithpael* here is iterative or habitual rather than strictly reflexive: *walked back
and forth*, *walked about*, *went on walking*. So the sense is not a single journey but a settled
manner of going, and the preposition makes it companionable rather than merely obedient. It is worth
noticing that the same stem of the same verb was used, two chapters earlier, of God himself: *"They
heard Yahweh God's voice walking in the garden in the cool of the day"* (3:8). Whatever the phrase
means, the vocabulary it uses is the vocabulary of Eden, applied now to a man rather than to God.

**What it does not do is define itself.** Genesis attaches no content — no law kept, no altar built,
no word received, no act performed. Enoch walks with God for three hundred years and the text
records nothing he did in them. Two consequences follow. The phrase has been enormously productive
in later theology and devotion precisely because it is empty of specifics; and any confident account
of what Enoch's righteousness consisted of is coming from somewhere other than Genesis. The
Septuagint already fills the gap, rendering both verses *euērestēsen tō theō*, *"he pleased God"* —
an interpretation, and the one Hebrews 11:5 builds its argument from when it says Enoch *"has had
testimony given to him that before his translation he had been well pleasing to God."* See
[[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]].

**Related but not identical constructions.** Abraham is told *"walk before me, and be blameless"*
(17:1) — a different preposition (*lipnê*, before), and arguably a different relation: service in
God's sight rather than company at his side. Israel is repeatedly told to *walk in* God's ways
(Deuteronomy 8:6), which is obedience to a path. Micah 6:8 has *"walk humbly with your God"* and
uses the *ʾet* of Genesis 5. Sorting these is not pedantry: the Genesis 5 form is the intimate one,
it is used of two men, and the Bible does not hand it out again.

**Where it sits in the chapter.** This matters as much as the philology. Genesis 5 is a chapter about
dying — eight entries end *"then he died"* — and the one man of whom the walking is said is the one
man who does not. The phrase and the exception arrive in the same verse. Genesis does not say the
walking *caused* the taking, and the syntax of 5:24 sets them side by side rather than in sequence;
but the reader is plainly meant to connect them, and Hebrews 11:5 does exactly that. Then, one
chapter later, the same phrase is applied to the man who survives the flood. Both times, walking
with God goes with not being destroyed.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 5:22, 5:24** — [[Enoch (son of Jared)]] walks with God for three hundred years and is
  taken. The phrase's first appearance, with no antecedent and no explanation.
- **Genesis 6:9** — *"Noah was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time. Noah walked
  with God."* The second and last use of the exact idiom, of the second man to be exempted from a
  general death. It arrives third in a list of three descriptions, after *righteous* and
  *blameless*, and it is the only one of the three the narrative never illustrates: what Noah is
  then shown doing is obeying instructions, twice reported in the same four words. See
  [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 3:8** — the same verb and stem, of God walking in the garden: the vocabulary this idiom
  borrows.
- **Genesis 17:1; 24:40; 48:15** *(not yet ingested)* — the patriarchs *walk before* God, and Jacob
  speaks of the God *"before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked"*. A related but distinct
  construction.
- **Leviticus 26:12** — *"I will walk among you, and will be your God"*: the direction reversed,
  and made covenantal.
- **Micah 6:8** — *"to walk humbly with your God"*, using the *ʾet* of Genesis 5, and the closest
  the prophets come to defining the phrase as a requirement rather than a description.
- **Malachi 2:6** — of Levi, *"he walked with me in peace and uprightness"*.
- **Amos 3:3** — *"Do two walk together, unless they have agreed?"* — the metaphor turned into an
  argument about covenant.
- **Hebrews 11:5–6** — Enoch's walking read as faith, via the Septuagint's *"pleased God"*, and made
  the basis of the general claim that *"without faith it is impossible to be well pleasing to him."*
- Later strands: the New Testament's *peripateō* language for the Christian life — walking in
  newness of life (Romans 6:4), by faith and not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7), in the light (1 John
  1:7) — which is this Genesis metaphor naturalized into ordinary Christian vocabulary.
