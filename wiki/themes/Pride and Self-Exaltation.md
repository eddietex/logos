# Pride and Self-Exaltation

## Definition / overview

Human beings lifting themselves up — making a name, ascending, securing a position that is not
theirs — and God bringing them down. It is one of the most consistent moral patterns in Scripture,
and its founding text does not use the word.

**Genesis 11:4 states the theme in the first person plural and never labels it.** *"Let's make a
name for ourselves"* — *naʿăśeh-lānû šēm* — is the whole of it: a project undertaken so that the
builders will be someone, and undertaken against a scattering they were commanded to accept. The
narrator of [[The Tower of Babel]] supplies no *gāʾôn* (pride), no *rûm* (be high), no accusation of
any kind. God's stated reason for acting is that *"nothing will be withheld from them"*, which is an
observation about capability. The theme is entirely in the quoted human speech, and every reader who
has called Babel a story about pride has done so by hearing verse 4 rather than by being told.

**That reticence does not survive into the prophets.** When Isaiah and Ezekiel come to write about
the empires, they put a first-person boast in the mouth of the king and the boast is the indictment:

- **Isaiah 14:13–14** — the king of Babylon says in his heart that he will ascend to heaven, exalt his
  throne above the stars of God, and be like the Most High; and is brought down to Sheol.
- **Ezekiel 28:2, 6–9** — the prince of Tyre says *"I am a god, I sit in the seat of God"*, and is
  told he is a man and will die the death of the slain.
- **Daniel 4:30** — Nebuchadnezzar, on the roof of his palace: *"Is not this great Babylon, which I
  have built for the royal dwelling place, by the might of my power and for the glory of my
  majesty?"* Seven verses later he is eating grass. It is the closest any later text comes to
  restating Genesis 11:4 as a single sentence, and it is set in the same city.

**Three things distinguish the biblical treatment from a generic moral about arrogance.**

**1. It is usually collective and political before it is personal.** Babel is a people, not a man.
The prophetic oracles are addressed to Babylon, Tyre, Assyria, Egypt, Moab, and Edom — powers, with
their kings as the voice. The individual proverbs of the wisdom books (Proverbs 16:18) come later and
sit inside that frame rather than replacing it.

**2. The remedy is reversal, not merely rebuke.** The pattern is always that the height is taken
away: the builders are scattered, the king of Babylon goes down to Sheol, Nebuchadnezzar goes out to
the field, Herod is struck (Acts 12:21–23). Mary's song and Jesus' sayings state the rule as a
symmetry — the mighty put down from their thrones and the lowly exalted (Luke 1:52); everyone who
exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted (Luke 14:11; 18:14;
Matthew 23:12).

**3. The counter-image is a name that is given.** Genesis answers 11:4 three verses later with 12:2,
where God tells Abram *"I will bless you and make your name great"* — the same noun, a different
subject. The New Testament closes the arc at Philippians 2:6–11, where one who *"didn't consider
equality with God a thing to be grasped"* is given *"the name which is above every name"*. Grasping
and receiving are the two halves of the theme. See
[[A Name for Ourselves and a Great Name]].

**What the theme is not.** Genesis is not against building, cities, technology, or ambition as such —
[[City and Civilization]] sets out how carefully the book declines to condemn any of them, and
[[The Tower of Babel]] goes out of its way to explain the brick-making process. The fault, on every
reading that the text supports, is located in what the building was *for*.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 3:5–6** — *"you will be like God, knowing good and evil"*: the first offer of exaltation,
  accepted. See [[The Fall]].
- **Genesis 6:4** — *ʾanšê haššēm*, **men of the name**, the pre-flood *gibbōrîm*, immediately before
  God sees that human wickedness is great. See
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- **Genesis 11:4** — *"let's make a name for ourselves"*, and a tower with its head in the heavens.
  The founding text, and it names no sin. See [[The Tower of Babel]].
- **Genesis 12:2** — *"I will bless you and make your name great"*: the answer, with the subject
  changed.
- **Deuteronomy 8:17–18** — *"You shall say in your heart, 'My power and the might of my hand has
  gotten me this wealth'"*: the temptation given as a warning to a nation about to succeed.
- **1 Samuel 2:3–8; Luke 1:51–53** — Hannah's song and Mary's, the same reversal sung twice a thousand
  years apart.
- **Proverbs 16:18; 11:2; 29:23** — pride before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall: the
  theme compressed to proverb.
- **Isaiah 2:11–17** — a day of Yahweh *"on every lofty tower"* and everything lifted up, and the
  loftiness of man bowed down.
- **Isaiah 14:12–15** — the taunt over the king of Babylon: five *"I will"* clauses, and Sheol.
- **Ezekiel 28:1–19** — the prince of Tyre, whose heart is lifted up because of his beauty and his
  wisdom in trade.
- **Obadiah 3–4** — Edom in the clefts of the rock: *"though you make your nest among the stars, I
  will bring you down from there."*
- **Daniel 4:28–37; 5:20–23** — Nebuchadnezzar humbled and restored, and Belshazzar who knew it and
  did not learn.
- **Matthew 23:12; Luke 14:11; 18:9–14** — the rule stated three times, and the Pharisee and the tax
  collector.
- **Acts 12:21–23** — Herod accepting the acclamation of a god.
- **Romans 12:3, 16; 1 Corinthians 4:7** — *"what do you have that you didn't receive?"*
- **Philippians 2:5–11** — the one who did not grasp, and the name that is given.
- **James 4:6, 13–16; 1 Peter 5:5–6** — *"God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble"*, and
  the merchant planning next year's profit.
- **Revelation 18:7** — Babylon saying in her heart *"I sit a queen… and will in no way see
  mourning"*: verse 4 of Genesis 11 still being spoken in the last book. See
  [[Babel and Babylon the Great]].

### Wise in your own eyes — Proverbs 3:7

*"Don't be wise in your own eyes. Fear Yahweh"* (3:7, *ʾal-təhî ḥāḵām bəʿêneḵā*). The book's form of
pride is intellectual: self-assessed wisdom, which it later ranks below folly — *"There is more hope
for a fool than for him"* (26:12). Isaiah makes it a woe (5:21, *ḥăḵāmîm bəʿênêhem*), and Paul an
exhortation, *"Don't be wise in your own conceits"* (Romans 12:16). The remedy given is not a lower
opinion of oneself but the fear of Yahweh. See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]]. Source: Proverbs 3 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Mockery answered, humility favored — Proverbs 3:21–35

Proverbs 3:34 opposes mockers and the humble; 3:35 contrasts glory inherited by the wise
with the shame of fools. The WEB quotations in James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5 speak of God
resisting the proud and giving grace to the humble. Both contexts call for humility
before God; 1 Peter also applies it to relationships in the community. See
[[Grace to the Humble - Proverbs 3.34, James 4.6, and 1 Peter 5.5]].

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### Haughty eyes — Proverbs 6:17

First of the seven things Yahweh hates is *"haughty eyes"* (6:17, *ʿênayim rāmôṯ*, *raised
eyes*). Proverbs keeps pride in the eyes — compare *"Don't be wise in your own eyes"* (3:7) — and
will call *"Everyone who is proud in heart"* an abomination (16:5). See
[[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### What Wisdom hates — Proverbs 8:13

*"I hate pride, arrogance, the evil way, and the perverse mouth"* (8:13). *Gēʾâ wəgāʾôn*, *pride and
arrogance*, are two nouns from one root, *gāʾâ*, *to rise*. They head Wisdom's list as *"haughty
eyes"* headed the seven things Yahweh hates (6:17), and both lists are joined to the fear of Yahweh.
See [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:2

Pride brings shame, whereas humility accompanies wisdom. This pairs a social outcome with a
disposition toward learning; it does not say that every humiliated person must have been proud.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:9, 15

Self-honor without bread is inferior to obscure provision, and a fool’s own verdict on his way is
insufficient. Wisdom listens to another person’s counsel. Reputation and self-confidence are thus
tested against material reality and a willingness to be corrected.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Pride and quarrels — Proverbs 13:10

*"Pride only breeds quarrels, but with ones who take advice is wisdom"* (10): pride's opposite here is not lowliness in general but willingness to be advised.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### Before honor is humility — Proverbs 15:25, 33

Yahweh *"will uproot the house of the proud"* (25), whose estate is the threat to the widow's borders; and *"Before honor is humility"* (33), repeated in the WEB at 18:12 against *"Before destruction the heart of man is proud"*. Humility is set beside the fear of Yahweh again at 22:4.

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### Pride goes before destruction — Proverbs 16:5, 18–19

*"Everyone who is proud in heart is an abomination to Yahweh"* (5), and the book's best-known line on the subject: *"Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall"* (18). Verse 19 ranks *"a lowly spirit with the poor"* above dividing plunder *"with the proud"*. With 11:2, 15:33, and 18:12, pride and humility become a sequence: what comes before a fall, and what comes before honour.

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### The high heart — Proverbs 18:11–12

The rich man's wall is high *"in his own imagination"* (11), and the next verse says what such a
heart does: *"Before destruction the heart of man is proud, but before honor is humility"* (12). The
first half rewrites 16:18; the second repeats 15:33 word for word in Hebrew (Sefaria). See
[[The Plans of the Heart]] and [[A Gentle Answer]].

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Right in his own eyes — Proverbs 21:2, 4, 24

Every way is right in a man's own eyes (2); the high look and proud heart are *"the lamp of the
wicked"* and sin (4); the proud man's name is *scoffer* (24). Pride appears as self-approval that
Yahweh's weighing overrules.

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### Humility and the mocker — Proverbs 22:4, 10

Humility is joined to the fear of Yahweh and rewarded with wealth, honor, and life (22:4). The
mocker — named in 21:24 as *"the proud and haughty man"* — is to be driven out, and strife,
quarrels, and insults go with him (22:10).

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### Do not exalt yourself before the king — Proverbs 25:6–7, 14, 27

*"Don't exalt yourself in the presence of the king, or claim a place among great men"* (25:6). The
reason is public: better to be told *"Come up here"* than to be put lower *"in the presence of the
prince"* (25:7). Jesus' parable of the wedding guests tells the same scene and adds the rule
*"everyone who exalts himself will be humbled"* (Luke 14:11); see
[[Come Up Here - Proverbs 25.6-7 and Luke 14.7-11]]. The chapter adds the boaster of gifts never
given, *"clouds and wind without rain"* (25:14), and closes on seeking one's own honour (25:27).

See [[The Glory of Kings]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25.

### Wise in his own eyes — Proverbs 26:5, 12, 16

Chapter 26 uses *wise in his own eyes* (*ḥāḵām bəʿênāyw*) three times. The fool must be answered
*"lest he be wise in his own eyes"* (5). The man who already is has less hope than a fool (12). And the
sluggard is *"wiser in his own eyes than seven men who answer with discretion"* (16). Self-assurance is
the end point of both the fool's portrait and the sluggard's, and the one fault the chapter ranks
below folly itself. Proverbs 3:7 had set it against the fear of Yahweh. See
[[Wise in His Own Eyes - Proverbs 3.7, 26.12, Isaiah 5.21, and Romans 12.16]].

See [[Answer a Fool]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 26.

### Do not praise yourself — Proverbs 27:1–2, 21

Chapter 27 opens and turns on the root *hll*, *praise*. *"Don't boast about tomorrow"* is
*ʾal-tiṯhallēl* (1): the boast claims a future no one can see, *"for you don't know what a day may
bring"*. *"Let another man praise you, and not your own mouth"* (2) moves the praise to a stranger's
lips, the courtier's rule of 25:6–7 applied to speech. And *"man is refined by his praise"* (21) puts
praise received in the furnace of 17:3: how a man takes his praise tests him. See
[[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow - Proverbs 27.1, Luke 12.16-21, and James 4.13-16]].

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 27; Hebrew from Sefaria.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:11, 26

The rich man is wise in his own eyes, while a poor person with understanding sees through him (11).
Self-trust is called folly in verse 26. The sayings expose the assumption that success makes someone
the final judge of his own wisdom, without saying that all wealthy people lack understanding.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

Pride brings a person low; a lowly spirit gains honor. This repeats the reversal of 15:33 and
18:12, without naming an agent or a date for the reversal. It is moral instruction, not evidence
that every socially humiliated person was proud.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:23; the passage page records the
cross-reference sources and interpretive limits.

### Agur’s words — Proverbs 30:2–4, 9, 12–13

Confessed ignorance and unanswered questions oppose the generation whose eyes are lifted up and
whose self-estimate of purity is false. Fullness may also breed the denial Agur fears. Humility
concerns knowledge, moral judgment, and dependence on God.

See [[The Words of Agur]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:1–14 and the comparisons documented there.

### Lifting up yourself — Proverbs 30:32

Agur's words end with pride: *"If you have done foolishly in lifting up yourself, or if you have
thought evil, put your hand over your mouth"* (32). The generation of *"lofty"* eyes (13) is answered
by the gesture of silence Job made before God. See
[[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.
