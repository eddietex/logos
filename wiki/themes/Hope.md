# Hope

## Definition / overview

*Tiqvâ* — from *qāwâ*, to wait, and related to a noun meaning *cord*. Hope in the Hebrew Bible is
not optimism about probabilities; it is a line thrown out and held onto, expectation directed at
someone. That is why it can be described spatially, as something that has a place and can be cut
off, and why Job can ask where his is and whether it will be coming down to the grave with him.

The theme enters this wiki through Job 17, and it enters as a question rather than a possession.
*"My days are past, my plans are broken off, as are the thoughts of my heart. … where then is my
hope? as for my hope, who shall see it? Shall it go down with me to the gates of Sheol, or descend
together into the dust?"* (17:11, 15–16). Two features of that are worth marking.

**Job does not say he has no hope.** He asks where it is and who will see it. The grammar is of
something mislaid rather than something abandoned, and the difference matters, because forty verses
earlier the same speech has said *"my witness is in heaven"* (16:19). Both are in the same breath
of the same man, and the book does not choose between them. See
[[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]].

**And the horizon is short, for a reason.** Job's hope has to be realized before he dies, because
the Sheol he expects is not a place where accounts are settled — it is dark, silent, and
undifferentiated. See [[Sheol]]. The resources later readers bring to his despair (resurrection, a
final assize) are not yet available in the canon at this point, and the wiki's practice is to let
his question stand at its own date rather than to answer it out of a later book.

The friends supply the counterfeit, and Job names it: *"They change the night into day, saying 'The
light is near' in the presence of darkness"* (17:12). That is a precise description of premature
comfort — not lying about the facts, but relabelling them, and telling a man in the dark that dawn
is close because you cannot bear to sit in the dark with him.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 5:29** — Lamech naming Noah for the comfort he hopes is coming from the cursed ground:
  the wiki's first instance of hope, and one that receives at best a partial answer. See
  [[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]] and [[From Adam to Noah]].
- **Genesis 8:6–12** *(not yet ingested)* — the raven and the dove: hope reduced to sending a bird
  out and watching whether it comes back.
- **Job 6:11; 7:6; 14:7–12, 19** — the earlier statements. 14:7 is the sharpest: *"there is hope for
  a tree … that it will sprout again"* — and then the flat comparison, *"but man
  dies, and is laid low."*
- **Job 13:15** — *"Though he slay me, yet will I wait for him"*, the most famous statement of hope
  in the book and one of the most textually uncertain verses in it; the marginal Hebrew reading
  gives the opposite sense, *"I have no hope"*. Treated when Job 13 is ingested.
- **Job 17:15–16** — [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]], the question asked and left
  open.
- **Job 19:25–27** — *"I know that my Redeemer lives"*, the counter-statement, in Hebrew almost as
  broken as 13:15.
- **Job 42:10–17** — an answer given in events rather than in argument.
- **Psalm 39:7; 42:5, 11; 62:5; 130:5–7** — hope as waiting, and the Psalter's habit of addressing
  the hoping self in the second person: *"Why are you in despair, my soul?"*
- **Psalm 88** — the psalm with no turn, which the canon keeps.
- **Proverbs 13:12** — *"Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a
  tree of life."*
- **Lamentations 3:18–24** — *"my strength and my hope has perished from Yahweh"*, and then, six
  verses later, *"This I recall to my mind; therefore I have hope."* The same reversal Job performs
  in Job 16–17, in the opposite order.
- **Jeremiah 29:11** — *"to give you hope and a future"*, spoken to exiles with seventy years to
  wait, which is the context the verse is usually quoted without.
- **Isaiah 40:31** — those who *wait* for Yahweh renewing strength: the same verb.
- **Daniel 12:2; Isaiah 26:19** — resurrection, appearing late, and changing what hope can be
  addressed to.
- **Romans 4:18; 5:2–5; 8:24–25** — hoping against hope, hope that does not disappoint, and *"hope
  that is seen is not hope"* — which is Job 17:15's *"who shall see it?"* answered by redefining the
  question.
- **1 Corinthians 15:19; 1 Thessalonians 4:13** — hope located explicitly beyond this life, and
  grief distinguished from the grief of those *"who have no hope"*.
- **Hebrews 6:19** — *"an anchor of the soul, a hope both sure and steadfast"* — the cord image of
  *tiqvâ*, arrived at independently in Greek.
