# Hope

## Definition / overview

*Tiqvâ* — from *qāwâ*, to wait, and related to a noun meaning *cord*. Hope in the Hebrew Bible is
not optimism about probabilities; it is a line thrown out and held onto, expectation directed at
someone. That is why it can be described spatially, as something that has a place and can be cut
off, and why Job can ask where his is and whether it will be coming down to the grave with him.

The theme enters this wiki through Job 17, and it enters as a question rather than a possession.
*"My days are past, my plans are broken off, as are the thoughts of my heart. … where then is my
hope? as for my hope, who shall see it? Shall it go down with me to the gates of Sheol, or descend
together into the dust?"* (17:11, 15–16). Two features of that are worth marking.

**Job does not say he has no hope.** He asks where it is and who will see it. The grammar is of
something mislaid rather than something abandoned, and the difference matters, because forty verses
earlier the same speech has said *"my witness is in heaven"* (16:19). Both are in the same breath
of the same man, and the book does not choose between them. See
[[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]].

**And the horizon is short, for a reason.** Job's hope has to be realized before he dies, because
the Sheol he expects is not a place where accounts are settled — it is dark, silent, and
undifferentiated. See [[Sheol]]. The resources later readers bring to his despair (resurrection, a
final assize) are not yet available in the canon at this point, and the wiki's practice is to let
his question stand at its own date rather than to answer it out of a later book.

The friends supply the counterfeit, and Job names it: *"They change the night into day, saying 'The
light is near' in the presence of darkness"* (17:12). That is a precise description of premature
comfort — not lying about the facts, but relabelling them, and telling a man in the dark that dawn
is close because you cannot bear to sit in the dark with him.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 5:29** — Lamech naming Noah for the comfort he hopes is coming from the cursed ground:
  the wiki's first instance of hope, and one that receives at best a partial answer. See
  [[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]] and [[From Adam to Noah]].
- **Genesis 8:6–12** — the raven and the dove: hope reduced to sending a bird out and watching
  whether it comes back, three times at seven-day intervals, until the day she does not. See
  [[The Waters Recede]]. The chapter's larger point about hope is that Noah's did not turn anything;
  8:1 did, before he sent a single bird.
- **Job 6:11; 7:6; 14:7–12, 19** — the earlier statements; for 6:11 and 7:6 see
  [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]. 14:7 is the sharpest: *"there is hope for
  a tree … that it will sprout again"* — and then the flat comparison, *"but man
  dies, and is laid low."*
- **Job 13:15** — *"Though he slay me, yet will I wait for him"*, the most famous statement of hope
  in the book and one of the most textually uncertain verses in it. **The written Hebrew text (Ketiv)
  reads *lōʾ*, giving *"Behold, he will kill me. I have no hope"* (WEB); the marginal reading (Qere)
  reads *lô*, giving *"for him I will wait"* (KJV).** *This page earlier had the two the wrong way
  round — corrected when Job 13 was ingested.* See [[Though He Slay Me]] and
  [[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]].
- **Job 17:15–16** — [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]], the question asked and left
  open.
- **Job 19:25–27** — *"I know that my Redeemer lives"*, the counter-statement, in Hebrew almost as
  broken as 13:15.
- **Job 42:10–17** — an answer given in events rather than in argument — [[The Restoration of Job]].
- **Psalm 39:7; 42:5, 11; 62:5; 130:5–7** — hope as waiting, and the Psalter's habit of addressing
  the hoping self in the second person: *"Why are you in despair, my soul?"*
- **Psalm 88** — the psalm with no turn, which the canon keeps.
- **Proverbs 13:12** — *"Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a
  tree of life."* See [[Hope Deferred]].
- **Lamentations 3:18–24** — *"my strength and my hope has perished from Yahweh"*, and then, six
  verses later, *"This I recall to my mind; therefore I have hope."* The same reversal Job performs
  in Job 16–17, in the opposite order.
- **Jeremiah 29:11** — *"to give you hope and a future"*, spoken to exiles with seventy years to
  wait, which is the context the verse is usually quoted without.
- **Isaiah 40:31** — those who *wait* for Yahweh renewing strength: the same verb.
- **Daniel 12:2; Isaiah 26:19** — resurrection, appearing late, and changing what hope can be
  addressed to.
- **Romans 4:18; 5:2–5; 8:24–25** — hoping against hope, hope that does not disappoint, and *"hope
  that is seen is not hope"* — which is Job 17:15's *"who shall see it?"* answered by redefining the
  question.
- **1 Corinthians 15:19; 1 Thessalonians 4:13** — hope located explicitly beyond this life, and
  grief distinguished from the grief of those *"who have no hope"*.
- **Hebrews 6:19** — *"an anchor of the soul, a hope both sure and steadfast"* — the cord image of
  *tiqvâ*, arrived at independently in Greek.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:24, 28

The righteous anticipate joy and receive their desire, whereas the wicked meet feared disaster and
lose their hope. Job 3:25–26 places realized dread and unrest in the life of the upright man of Job
1:1. Hope’s moral direction and a sufferer’s current feelings cannot therefore be treated as
interchangeable evidence.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:7, 23, 28

The wicked person’s death ends hope and the expectation of power; righteous desire and wicked
expectation lead in opposite directions. Trust in riches ends in a fall. The sayings describe the
failure of a particular hope, without supplying a developed account of the afterlife.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Hope deferred — Proverbs 13:12, 19

*"Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a tree of life"* (12) makes no moral contrast: the sick heart belongs to anyone who waits. Fulfilled longing is *"sweet to the soul"* (19). Job's dialogue is the extended case of the first line (Job 6:8, 11; 7:6; 14:19; 17:15; 19:10). See [[Hope Deferred and the Tree of Life - Proverbs 13.12 and the Hope of Job]].

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

## Key passages

- [[Hope Deferred]] — Proverbs 13:12, 19.

- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:7, 23, 28.

- [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]] — Proverbs 10:24, 28; The righteous anticipate joy and receive
  their desire, whereas the wicked meet feared disaster and lose their hope.

- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — hope asked after and not found, four verses
  from the highest confidence in the book
- [[From Adam to Noah]] — Lamech's hope at Genesis 5:29, the wiki's first
- [[The Waters Recede]] — hope as a bird sent out over water, and a partial answer to 5:29 arriving
  in a sentence nobody in the story hears
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — the theme's sharpest pair of verses. *"My hope he has plucked
  up like a tree"* (19:10) and *"I know that my Redeemer lives"* (19:25), fifteen verses apart and
  never reconciled: the confidence is not the recovery of the hope but something asserted in its
  place
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — the counter-image. Job had said at 14:7 that a felled tree has hope
  of sprouting; 18:16 dries the roots and cuts the branch, and 19:10 concedes the picture and names
  the gardener
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:14, 23; **a man makes the only request he ever makes,
  is right about everything, and waits two more years.** The chapter gives the request and its failure
  nine verses apart and **passes no comment on the interval**; Psalm 105:19's *"until the time that his
  word happened, and Yahweh's word proved him true"* is the only line in Scripture that reads the delay
  as anything. **Genesis simply ends the chapter on the forgetting** and lets 41:1's *"two full years"*
  stand unexplained
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:1, **two full years, measured to the day, between being right about
  everything and anything happening about it.** ***Miqqēṣ šənātayim yāmîm*** — *two years of days*, the
  idiom for two complete years — **is the whole content of the interval**, and **Genesis offers no
  account of what it was for.** No divine speech, no narratorial comment, no suggestion that Joseph
  prayed or despaired or waited well. **Psalm 105:19 remains the only text in Scripture that says
  anything about it** — *"until the time that his word happened, and Yahweh's word proved him true"* —
  and it is a verdict imposed from outside, centuries later. **The relief, when it comes, comes through a
  man's insomnia and a courtier's self-interest**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:27–28; ***"It is enough"*** — ***rab*** — **a word about
  quantity, and the whole of [[Jacob]]'s recovery.** He is told the truth and does not believe it
  (45:26); he sees the wagons and his spirit revives (45:27); **and what he says is not that he
  believes but that the evidence has become sufficient.** *"Joseph my son is still alive. I will go and
  see him before I die."* **The man who said three times that he was going down to [[Sheol]] mourning
  names a different journey in the same grammar of descent** — and what he wants at the end of it is
  not an explanation but a sight
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:4; ***"I will also surely bring you up again."*** **The sentence the
  rest of the Pentateuch is the working-out of**, spoken to a man who will die in Egypt (49:33) and be
  carried back as a body (50:13). **The promise is made to both the individual and the nation in the
  same pronoun**, and only one of them collects it. At 46:30 he asks for nothing further: *"Now let me
  die, since I have seen your face"*
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:29–30; **the oath is an act of it.** **A
  hundred-and-forty-seven-year-old in the richest province of the world's greatest power, holding the
  only freehold his family has ever been given** (47:11), **makes his son swear to carry him out of
  it** — **which is only sane if 46:4 is true.** *"I will also surely bring you up again"* was spoken
  to him at [[Beersheba]]; **47:29 is the first act by anyone in the family that treats the descent
  as temporary**, and it comes seventeen years after the settling and two verses after the report
  that they were multiplying exceedingly
- [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]] — Genesis 49:18; ***"I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh"*** — **the
  first occurrence in the Bible of the noun *yəšûʿâ*, salvation**, and **the only prayer in a
  thirty-three-verse chapter.** ***Qiwwîtî*** **is the waiting that strains toward**, the verb of
  Psalm 130:5 and Isaiah 40:31, **and the tense is perfect**: he is reporting a lifetime of it, not
  beginning one. **Genesis supplies no object** — no *from what* and no *for whom* — **and the man
  saying it is five verses from his death, in a foreign country, having buried one wife by a road and
  mourned a living son for twenty years.** See
  [[I Have Waited for Your Salvation - The First Yeshuah in Scripture]]
- **Genesis 50:24–25 — *pāqōd yipqōd*, *God will surely visit you***, said twice by the one patriarch
  God never speaks to, about a departure he is given no date for and will not see —
  [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]]

- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26; **the floor.** There is no petition in the chapter, no
  expectation, and no addressee: Job speaks of God in the third person throughout and asks nothing
  of him. **Everything the book later reaches for — the umpire of 9:33, the witness of 16:19, the
  Redeemer of 19:25 — is built on ground this chapter cleared completely**, which is why the wiki
  reads Job's trajectory as ascent rather than collapse. Set against 17:15's *"where then is my
  hope?"*, which is at least a question addressed to someone
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 4:6 and 5:16, **the noun *tiqwâ*'s first two occurrences in the
  book**: *"Isn't the integrity of your ways your hope?"* and *"So the poor has hope."* Hope offered
  to Job as the yield of his integrity, and then as a future (5:19–26). Job's reply is immediate —
  *"What is my strength, that I should wait?"* (6:11) — and by 17:15 he is asking where his hope is
  at all
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:11 and 7:6, **hope refused and then declared spent.**
  *"What is my strength, that I should wait?"* answers Eliphaz's 4:6 directly, and 7:6 — *"My days
  are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, and are spent without hope"* — turns on the noun's other
  sense: *tiqwâ* is also a *cord* (Joshua 2:18), and the shuttle has run to the end of the thread.
  Job's consolation in the same speech is not hope but integrity (6:10)
- [[Bildad's First Speech]] — Job 8:13, *"The hope of the godless man shall perish"*; **the noun Job
  had just declared spent (7:6), made a mark of the godless.** The papyrus without water is its image.
  Job's answer comes at 14:7 — *"there is hope for a tree"* — and grants the plant a second life Bildad
  did not give it. See [[The Papyrus Without Water - Bildad's Plants, Psalm 1, and Jeremiah 17]].
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:18, 20; ***"You shall be secure, because there is hope"*** — and three verses
  later, *"Their hope shall be the giving up of the spirit."* **Zophar is the friend who sets the two
  hopes side by side**, the penitent's and the wicked's, and the second is death. *Tiqwâ* has now
  been used by all three friends (4:6; 8:13; 11:18)
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 13:15; 14:7–19; **the speech in which hope is granted to a tree and
  worn away from a man.** *"There is hope (tiqwâ) for a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout
  again"* (14:7) — and *"so you destroy the hope of man"* (14:19), the same noun at either end of a
  passage that imagines, in between, God hiding Job in Sheol and remembering him (14:13–15). 13:15 is
  either *"I have no hope"* (Ketiv, WEB) or *"for him I will wait"* (Qere, KJV); the verb is *yāḥal*,
  *wait*, the verb Job had refused at 6:11. See
  [[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]] and
  [[There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts]]
- [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]] — Job 27:8–10; ***"For what is the hope of the godless, when
  he is cut off?"*** Hope defined by its object: *"Will God hear his cry … Will he delight himself in
  the Almighty, and call on God at all times?"* The test Job has been passing all through the dialogue,
  calling on God continually, and the one the friends have never sat — none of them addresses God once
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:18–20; ***"I shall die in my own house … My root is spread out to
  the waters. The dew lies all night on my branch"***: the hope of a full life, Eliphaz's 5:26 and the
  tree of Psalm 1:3, quoted as something Job *said* and no longer can. See
  [[There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts]].
- [[Now They Mock Me]] — Job 30:26; ***"When I looked for good, then evil came; When I waited for light,
  there came darkness."*** *Qiwwîtî* and *ʾăyaḥălâ*, the two verbs of waiting hope, each met by its
  opposite. The tree-hope of 29:18–20 has its answer in the next chapter: not a root by the water but
  *"the house appointed for all living"* (30:23).
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:10–17; **the tree sprouts.** *"There is hope for a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout
  again"* (14:7) — Job denied it of a man; the epilogue gives him a new household, four generations, and a death *"old
  and full of days"*. What he hoped for at 19:25–27 was vindication before death, and that is what arrives. See
  [[There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts]].
