# Judgment

## Definition / overview

God's response to wrongdoing: coming, investigating, sentencing, and — in Genesis, without
exception — providing for the sentenced. [[The Fall]] runs the sequence for the first time, and
the shape it takes there governs almost every judgment scene that follows.

**The pattern in Genesis 3:**

1. **God comes.** He is heard *"walking in the garden in the cool of the day"* (3:8) — the same
   presence that was a gift the day before.
2. **He seeks and asks.** *"Where are you?"* (3:9) is a question to which he needs no answer;
   what it does is require the man to speak first. Then *"Who told you that you were naked?"*,
   *"Have you eaten…?"*, *"What have you done?"* — an interrogation that lets each party say for
   themselves what happened.
3. **Answers are heard, including the evasions.** The man blames the woman and, past her, God
   himself; the woman blames the serpent. Neither is corrected and neither is excused.
4. **Sentence is pronounced**, in reverse order of the questioning — serpent, woman, man — and
   with the curse formula reserved for the serpent and the ground (see [[Curse]]).
5. **Provision follows sentence.** God clothes them (3:21), and the expulsion itself is given a
   protective reason: *"lest he reach out his hand, and also take of the tree of life"* (3:22).

That last step is what distinguishes biblical judgment from mere punishment, and Genesis keeps
repeating it: Cain is sentenced and given a protective sign (4:15), the flood is decreed and an
ark is specified (6:14), Babel is scattered and immediately followed by the call of Abram (12:1).
Judgment in Genesis is never the end of the story it is told inside.

**The second run, in [[Cain and Abel]]**, follows the template so closely that the correspondence
is clearly deliberate — and each step is heavier.

| Genesis 3 | Genesis 4 |
|---|---|
| *"Where are you?"* (3:9) | *"Where is Abel, your brother?"* (4:9) |
| *"What have you done?"* to the woman (3:13) | *"What have you done?"* to Cain (4:10) |
| Evasion: blame passed along (3:12–13) | Denial: an outright lie, plus a counter-question (4:9) |
| The **ground** cursed (3:17) | The **man** cursed (4:11) |
| Ground resists: thorns and sweat (3:18–19) | Ground refuses: *"it won't yield its strength"* (4:12) |
| Driven out of the garden (3:23–24) | Driven from the ground and from God's presence (4:14, 16) |
| Provision: coats of skins (3:21) | Provision: a sign that keeps him alive (4:15) |

Three things are new. God **warns before the act** (4:6–7), which no one in Genesis 3 received.
The **victim is heard** — *"the voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the ground"* (4:10)
is the first time judgment answers a cry rather than an act observed. And the provision is granted
in answer to the condemned man's own protest (4:13–14), which makes the mercy explicitly
responsive: Cain says the sentence is more than he can bear, and something is done about it
without the sentence being lifted.

Two features of the Genesis 3 scene are worth keeping in view. The **serpent is never asked a
question** — the process of inquiry is extended to the humans and not to it. And the sentences
are **descriptive of a world**, not merely retributive: thorns, pain, mortality, and distorted
rule are the conditions the rest of Scripture is set in.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 3:8–24** — the first judgment scene: God comes, seeks, questions, sentences, clothes,
  exiles.
- **Genesis 4:6–16** — the same sequence over Cain, question for question (*"Where is Abel your
  brother?"* answering *"Where are you?"*), preceded this time by a warning and closed by a sign
  that protects the condemned.
- **Genesis 4:17–24** — the theme by its absence. A city built in defiance of a sentence, the first
  polygamy, and a boast of disproportionate killing all pass without God coming, questioning, or
  saying anything at all. It is the first stretch of the Bible in which he does not appear, and
  what makes it legible as a *withholding* rather than an oversight is that the same chapter has
  just run the full sequence twice. Genesis is establishing that judgment is not automatic and not
  immediate — which is the premise the flood account will need, and the premise Abraham argues from
  at 18:25.
- **Genesis 6:5–8** — the template with the questioning removed. God *sees* (6:5) and *resolves*
  (6:7), and between them he grieves; nobody is summoned, nobody is asked, nobody answers, nobody
  protests. Genesis 3 and 4 both gave the accused a hearing, and Genesis 4:17–24 withheld the whole
  sequence; this is the first judgment in Scripture that is announced rather than conducted, and the
  reason is stated in the verdict itself — the fault is not an act to be examined but the
  *imagination of the heart*, and there is nothing to establish. What survives of the pattern is the
  provision, and it comes as a single name in the last four words: *"But Noah found favor in
  Yahweh's eyes."* The ark is specified nine verses later, in the same speech that announces the end
  of all flesh. See [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]] and [[Favor and Grace]].
- **Genesis 6:9–7:24** — the sentence executed, and the first judgment in Scripture larger than a
  household. Three things are new. The **stated ground shifts from the heart to the hands**: 6:5 had
  the *imagination* of the heart, 6:11 and 6:13 have *ḥāmās*, violence, and 6:12 has *"all flesh had
  corrupted their way"*. The **punishment is the crime's own verb** — the earth *corrupted* itself
  (*šāḥat*) and God says *"I will destroy them"* (*šāḥat*), so the judgment is described as a
  withdrawal rather than an importation: he lets go of a world that was already ruining itself. And
  the **provision is specified inside the sentence**, as it always is here — the ark's dimensions
  (6:14–16) are given between the announcement of the end of all flesh (6:13) and the announcement
  of the flood (6:17), with the Bible's first covenant (6:18) between them. What is missing is any
  hearing: nobody is summoned, and unlike Sodom nobody stands in front of God to argue. See
  [[Noah and the Flood]] and [[The Flood as Un-Creation]].
- **Genesis 8:1–22** — the judgment lifted, and formally limited. It turns on *"God remembered
  Noah"* (see [[Remembrance]] and [[The Waters Recede]]), and it ends with the first time God binds
  his own future conduct in Scripture: *"I will never again strike every living thing, as I have
  done."*
- **Genesis 9:1–17** — the limit made public and given a sign, and two further moves the theme has not
  made before. **God gives up an instrument.** *"There will never again be a flood to destroy the
  earth"* (9:11) is not a mood but a covenant, and it removes one option from him permanently. Note
  what it does not remove: the undertaking is specifically about a *flood*, not about judgment as such,
  which is exactly how 2 Peter 3:5–7 reads it. And **he delegates.** The requiring of a murdered man's
  blood was his own act in Genesis 4, done personally and without a rule; 9:6 says the shedding will be
  *by man*. Judgment in the ordinary case now runs through human hands, and the Torah spends four
  chapters building the safeguards Genesis omits. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and
  [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]].
- **Genesis 9:25** — and then the delegation goes further than 9:6 delegated it, twenty verses later
  and without authorization. 9:6 handed human hands a rule with a stated offence, a stated penalty, and
  a stated ground (*"for God made man in his own image"*). At 9:25 a man passes sentence on his own
  authority: no offence is stated against the person sentenced, no penalty scale is invoked, God does
  not speak or appear anywhere in the pericope, and no later biblical writer ever cites the verdict.
  The judgment template of Genesis 3 and 4 — God comes, questions, sentences, provides — is not
  withheld here as it was at 4:17–24; it is **replaced**, by a human being doing all four badly. See
  [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the mildest judgment in the primeval history, and the one that names no
  offence. God comes down to see, as he did at 3:8–9 and 4:9–10, but there is no interrogation and no
  sentence: he states a capability (*"nothing will be withheld from them"*), confuses the language,
  and scatters. Nothing is destroyed, nobody is cursed, nobody dies, and the words *sin*, *evil*, and
  *wickedness* do not occur. Whether it is retribution, the enforcement of the command of 9:1, or a
  protective limit of the kind set at 3:22 is genuinely open; see [[The Tower of Babel]], which sets
  out the readings without choosing.
- **Genesis 18:16–33** — the theme's most explicit chapter, and it adds three things the template
  never had. **The charge is a cry**: *"Because the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great"* (18:20) —
  *zaʿăqâ*, the noun of Abel's blood at 4:10 and of Israel under Egypt at Exodus 2:23, which means
  the case is brought by victims rather than observed from above. **The sentence is explicitly
  deferred**: *"I will go down now, and see whether their deeds are as bad as the reports which have
  come to me. If not, I will know"* (18:21) — the descent of 11:5, but this time stated as an
  inquiry, so that the verdict is still open when the arguing starts. And **the standard is stated
  in advance by a human being**: *"Shouldn't the Judge of all the earth do right?"* (18:25), the
  first time anyone in Scripture holds God to a criterion, using the word-pair God had given him six
  verses earlier. God then concedes six times without objecting. See
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] and
  [[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].
- **Genesis 19:1–29** — the sentence carried out, and the answer to the bargain that is not in its
  terms: ten are not found, and *"God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out"* (19:29). **The chapter
  is the Old Testament's template for judgment, and every part of the template is visible in it.**
  The charge is investigated before it is executed (18:21) and the agents report the investigation
  as done (19:13). The evidence is a crowd — *"both young and old, all the people from every
  quarter"* (19:4) — so that the reader learns the verdict by counting rather than by being told it.
  The righteous are removed rather than left in, which is not what Abraham asked for. And the
  execution is held up by one man's exposure: *"I can't do anything until you get there"* (19:22),
  which is 18:25's principle stated as an operating limit on the executioner rather than as a
  question. The vocabulary it leaves behind — *sulfur and fire*, *the overthrow* — becomes the
  standing language of judgment for the rest of the canon. See
  [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] and [[Fire and Sulfur from the Sky]].
- Note that Genesis 8:21 gives the verdict of 6:5
  again — *"the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth"* — as the reason for **never**
  doing this again, so the same fact grounds both the flood and the promise against it.
- **Genesis 12:17** — the shortest judgment scene in the book, and the one with none of the
  template's parts. *"Yahweh afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai,
  Abram's wife."* No coming down, no question, no charge, no sentence pronounced, and no word spoken
  to anybody — an affliction and a stated reason, in one verse. Two features are worth marking.
  It falls on a **household** rather than a person, which is the pattern at 20:18 as well (*"Yahweh
  had closed up tight all the wombs of the house of Abimelech"*). And the party struck is the one
  who was **deceived**: Pharaoh acted on what he was told, and Genesis offers no theory reconciling
  that with justice. The verse is doing something other than adjudicating — it is protecting a
  promise — and the book lets the difference stand unexplained. See [[Abram in Egypt]].
- **Genesis 15:13–16** — judgment put on a clock, in both directions. *"I will also judge that
  nation, whom they will serve"* (15:14) is the Bible's first announced judgment on a **specific
  named-by-implication people** — Egypt — and it is announced four hundred years in advance, to a man
  who will be dead before it starts. And *"the iniquity of the Amorite is not yet full"* (15:16)
  supplies the reason the promise waits: the people currently on the land keep it until their guilt
  is **complete** (*šālēm*). This is the Torah's clearest statement that God defers judgment rather
  than declining it, and it is a hard doctrine in both directions. It is remarkable restraint — four
  centuries in which a people is left alone, at the cost of Israel's own slavery in the meantime.
  It also presumes a measurable threshold past which dispossession is just. The one thing that keeps
  it from being ethnic is that the Torah immediately turns it on Israel: Leviticus 18:28's *"that the
  land not vomit you out also"*, which 2 Kings 17 and 25 then report happening. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] and [[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: the plagues and the exodus as judgment on Egypt's
  gods; the covenant lawsuit form in the prophets (Micah 6:1–8, Isaiah 1:2–20); the day of Yahweh
  (Amos 5:18–20, Joel 2, Zephaniah 1); exile as judgment and return as its limit; Jesus on
  judgment deferred (Matthew 13:24–30) and enacted (Matthew 25:31–46); Romans 2:1–11; the great
  white throne, Revelation 20:11–15.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:5–7

The commands contain sanctions as well as prohibitions: iniquity reaches the third and fourth
generation of those who hate God, and misuse of the name is not held guiltless (20:5, 7). Loving
kindness to thousands stands beside these warnings (20:6). **The generational claim remains in
tension with Ezekiel 18:19–20's refusal to charge a righteous son with his father's iniquity and
with Job 21:19–21's objection.** Neither suffering nor ancestry by itself supplies a verdict on an
individual. See [[Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers - Job 21.19, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 18]].
See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### Violence, injury, and liability — Exodus 21:12–36

A planned killer cannot use God's altar to escape liability (21:14), while the unintentional
killer receives a place of flight (21:13). An owner's prior warning and failure to restrain a bull
change his liability (21:28–30). Judges also constrain the husband's claim after injury to a
pregnant woman (21:22). The ordinances require distinctions between cases, rather than treating
the mere occurrence of harm as proof of the same guilt in everyone involved.
See [[Laws of Violence and Injury]].
### Before God — Exodus 22:8–11

A disputed claim with no witness goes *"before God"*, and *"He whom God condemns shall pay double"*
(22:9). The KJV reads *"the judges"* here, and the Hebrew verb for *condemns* is plural (MAM). The
wiki records both readings and does not choose between a sanctuary procedure and a human court.
A keeper's case without a witness is settled by *"the oath of Yahweh"* (22:11).
See [[Laws of Property and Restitution]].

### Truth without partiality — Exodus 23:1–19

The commands prohibit malicious witnesses, a verdict bent by a crowd, favoritism toward a poor
litigant, denial of the poor person's rights, and bribes (23:1–8). Verses 3 and 6 are complementary
restraints: poverty is neither a warrant to distort judgment nor a reason to withhold justice. God's
refusal to justify the wicked (23:7) grounds the prohibition of a lethal false charge.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The priestly garments — Exodus 28:1–43

The breastplate is *"the breastplate of judgment"*, holding the Urim and the Thummim, and Aaron
bears *"the judgment of the children of Israel on his heart before Yahweh continually"*
(Exodus 28:15, 29–30). Exodus does not say how a judgment was obtained; later texts show the
Urim used for inquiry. See [[The Priestly Garments]] and [[Urim and Thummim - The Breastplate of Judgment and Later Inquiry]].

### A sanction stated before a case is narrated — Exodus 31

Exodus 31:14–15 attaches death to Sabbath profanation and work, alongside the cutting-off formula.
The passage names no defendant, hearing, or sentence carried out. It defines the covenant sanction;
it does not tell a judgment story or specify how the penalty is administered.

See [[Bezalel, Oholiab, and the Sabbath Sign]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Total destruction is announced and averted through intercession (32:10–14). About three thousand
then die at the Levites’ hands, and Yahweh strikes the people at the close (32:28, 35). The latter
plague has no stated death toll. The narrative holds corporate preservation and ongoing
accountability together; it does not identify every victim or explain the selection. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

God’s refusal to go among Israel is explained as protection against consuming a stiff-necked people
(33:3–5). Threatened absence is therefore both loss and restraint of judgment. Moses secures a
promise of presence (33:17); the chapter does not narrate a new execution or revoke every
consequence retained in 32:34–35. See [[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Exodus 34:7 places forgiveness and refusal to clear the guilty in one self-description, then
extends the visitation of iniquity to children and grandchildren. Unlike 20:5, the WEB here does
not repeat the qualification about those who hate God. **The tension with Job 21:19–21 and
Ezekiel 18:19–23 remains:** Job demands recompense to the wrongdoer, and Ezekiel refuses to
charge a righteous son with his father's iniquity. Shared consequences and personal guilt are
possible distinctions, not an explicit resolution supplied by Exodus 34. See [[Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers - Job 21.19, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 18]]. See
[[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

The death requirement for Sabbath work in 35:2 remains in force in the narrative after covenant
renewal. No trial or punishment is enacted in this unit. The freewill character of the offerings
in 35:29 therefore cannot be generalized into an absence of covenant obligations or sanctions.

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The priestly garments made — Exodus 39:1–31

The breastplate is made and fastened in 39:8–21, but the stored WEB does not repeat
the designation “breastplate of judgment” or mention the Urim and Thummim here. Chapter 28:30
still prescribes putting them in it. Silence about their manufacture is not evidence that they
were omitted in disobedience, nor does this unit explain how inquiry worked.

See [[The Priestly Garments Made]]. Source: cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Wisdom's oracle — Proverbs 1:20–33

Wisdom's first speech has the form of a prophetic judgment oracle — appeal (*"How long?"*, 1:22),
indictment introduced by *yaʿan*, *because* (1:24; compare Isaiah 65:12), sentence (1:26–31), and a
closing alternative (1:33) — but no judge acts in it. The *calamity* comes *"like a storm"* and *"like a
whirlwind"* (1:27, *šôʾâ*, *sûp̄â*), and the refusers *"eat of the fruit of their own way"* (1:31).
Wisdom's part is to laugh, in Psalm 2:4's verbs (*ʾeśḥāq … ʾelʿaḡ*), and not to answer (1:28). See
[[Wisdom Calls in the Street]] and
[[When I Called, You Didn't Answer - Proverbs 1.24, Isaiah 65.12, and Jeremiah 7.13]]. Source: Proverbs 1 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The evaluations that forbid envy — Proverbs 3:21–35

Proverbs 3:32–35 gives the reason not to imitate violence: Yahweh rejects the perverse,
curses the wicked household, and mocks mockers, alongside friendship, blessing, and
grace for their opposites. It reports no investigation or named defendant. **Tension:**
Job 21:7–9's safe wicked households prevent this general pronouncement from being used
as a transparent description of all present outcomes.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:3, 9, 24–31

The wicked face frustrated desire, exposure, disaster, loss of hope, and removal; Yahweh’s way
brings destruction to evildoers. There is no named defendant or court scene. Job 21:7–13’s
prosperous wicked remain counter-evidence to treating these judgments as an immediate and
exceptionless account of experience.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:1, 4, 20–21, 23, 31

Yahweh rejects dishonest weights and perverse hearts while delighting in accurate weights and
blameless ways. Riches cannot profit in the day of wrath, and verse 31 reasons from the righteous
being repaid to the greater exposure of the wicked. The chapter does not date that day or narrate a
courtroom scene.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:2, 7, 19, 21–22

Yahweh condemns wicked plans and rejects lying lips while delighting in truthful conduct. The
sayings state evaluation and outcome rather than narrating a hearing. Job’s upright suffering and
the secure households of the wicked remain explicit limits on inferring divine condemnation from
present circumstances.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Mišpāṭ: king, scales, and lot — Proverbs 16:2, 10–11, 33

Yahweh weighs the motives (2); the king's lips give judgment (10); the *"scales of justice"* are Yahweh's (11); and the lot's every *mišpāṭ* is from him (33) — the word of Exodus 28:30's *"breastplate of judgment"* (Hebrew from Sefaria). See [[The Lot Is Cast into the Lap - Proverbs 16.33 and the Casting of Lots]].

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Perverted justice — Proverbs 17:8, 15, 23, 26

Five sayings on the corrupted court: the bribe that works for its giver (8); *"He who justifies the wicked, and he who condemns the righteous, both of them alike are an abomination to Yahweh"* (15); the bribe taken *"to pervert the ways of justice"* (23), in the vocabulary of Exodus 23:6–8 and Deuteronomy 16:19; and the righteous punished and officials flogged *"for their integrity"* (26). See [[A Bribe Blinds - Proverbs 17.23, Exodus 23.8, and Deuteronomy 16.19]].

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

  and the vocabulary it leaves behind — *sulfur and fire*, *the overthrow* — becomes the canon's
  standing language for the thing

- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:30–38: the aftershock, and the one pericope in the chapter
  where God does not appear at all. Nine verses with no divine name, no speech, and no act,
  immediately after twenty-nine that had Yahweh in almost every one. What the rescued household does
  in the country the fire left behind is reported and not judged — which is its own statement about
  what judgment does and does not accomplish
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:3–7, the shortest judgment scene in Genesis and the only one
  the defendant argues his way out of. The sentence is passed in the first sentence spoken —
  *"Behold, you are a dead man"* — a plea of innocence is entered, conceded, and the sentence
  converted into a condition (*"restore the man's wife … and you will live"*). **The chapter after
  the fire falls on a city that never argued shows a king who does, and it takes him one exchange.**
  The sanction meanwhile is neither fire nor plague but withheld fertility, disclosed only in the
  last verse, and lifted by prayer- [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:24–26; **a capital sentence pronounced on hearsay and reversed by
  the defendant's evidence in two verses.** *"Bring her out, and let her be burned"* — **no hearing, no
  witnesses, no interval, and no jurisdiction**: [[Tamar]] is living in her father's house, not
  Judah's, and Judah is a resident alien at [[Adullam]] with no office at all. **He pronounces, and it
  is carried out** — she is brought out. **The penalty is also the severest the Torah knows**, reserved
  there for a priest's daughter (Leviticus 21:9) and for a man who marries a woman and her mother
  (Leviticus 20:14); **the ordinary penalty for adultery is stoning** (Deuteronomy 22:23–24), and Judah
  reaches past it. **And the charge presupposes the tie he had declined to honour**: she can only be
  guilty if she is bound to his house. **The same man reverses himself in one verse**, on evidence he
  is handed privately — which is the wiki's clearest instance of judgment in Genesis being a person
  rather than a procedure
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:1–3, 19–22; **two sentences delivered in identical
  grammar, and the offence that earned either is never stated.** A king is angry, two officers are
  imprisoned without a hearing the text reports, and at a birthday feast one is restored and one hanged.
  **The chapter's interest is entirely in whether the verdicts were correctly foretold, not in whether
  they were just** — *"as Joseph had interpreted to them"* is the whole of its comment. **Genesis gives
  the reader no basis for thinking either man deserved what he got**
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 9–10; **the trial without a venue.** Job's first full use of the courtroom: *rîb*,
  *mišpāṭ*, *"who will summon me?"* (9:19), *"that we should come together in judgment"* (9:32),
  *"show me why you contend with me"* (10:2), *"you renew your witnesses against me"* (10:17). God
  is plaintiff, judge, and stronger party at once, and no umpire stands over both (9:33).
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:10; *"If he… convenes a court (yaqhîl), then who can oppose him?"* — **the court
  Job said could not be convened (9:32–33), described as already in session**, in a verse built out
  of Job's own 9:11–12
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 13:7–28; 14:16–17; **the trial prepared.** *"I have set my cause in
  order"* (*ʿāraktî mišpāṭ*, 13:18); the procedure offered (*"call, and I will answer; or let me
  speak, and you answer me"*, 13:22), which the whirlwind adopts (38:3; 40:7); the charge sheet
  requested (13:23), the written indictment (13:26), the stocks (13:27), and the record sealed in a
  bag (14:17). And the friends warned that God judges partiality *for* himself (13:7–10) — which he
  does, at 42:7
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:17, 19–22, 30; **judgment that does not show.** *"How often
  is it … that God distributes sorrows in his anger?"* (21:17) asks about frequency, not existence.
  Deferred judgment on the children is refused (21:19–21); *"Shall any teach God knowledge, since he
  judges those who are high?"* (21:22) turns the friends' charge of presumption back on them. And
  21:30 is the crux: the WEB's *"the evil man is reserved to the day of calamity"* makes the
  travellers testify to a postponed judgment, while most modern translations read *"spared in the day
  of calamity"*. The passage page reads *spared*, in context, and records both.
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:4, 15–16; *"that he enters with you into judgment"* — the
  courtroom Job has demanded since chapter 9, offered back as a sentence already passed — and the
  wicked of *"the old way"*, *"snatched away before their time"*, most likely the Flood generation
  used as an exemplum.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 24:1; ***"Why aren't times laid up by the Almighty? Why don't
  those who know him see his days?"*** **The court with no sitting days.** The *ʿittîm* are appointed
  times of judgment and *his days* the days of reckoning; Job's complaint widens from his own unheard
  case (23:3–7) to a universe whose docket is never called, and 24:2–17 is the list of cases waiting.
  At 23:7 Job expects to be *"delivered forever from my judge"* — acquitted by God from God.
- [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]] — Job 27:2; ***"who has taken away my right (mišpāṭî)"***.
  *Mišpāṭ* as Job's case, his due in court, removed by the judge himself; the lawsuit of chapters 9,
  13 and 23 reduced to one clause, inside an oath that appeals to the same God for the truth
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 32:2–5; *wayyiḥar ʾap*, the wrath *"kindled"* four times, against Job and against the
  three friends — the anger God will express at 42:7 in the same words, against the three friends only,
  and not at Elihu.
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:10–30; **judgment without a trial.** *"Far be it from God, that he
  should do wickedness"* (34:10) — Abraham's *ḥālilâ* of Genesis 18:25, used to close the question
  Abraham opened. *"He doesn't need to consider a man further, that he should go before God in
  judgment"* (34:23): God sees, so the hearing Job has demanded since chapter 9 is unnecessary. He
  strikes the mighty *"in a moment … even at midnight"* (34:20) and *"in the open sight of others"*
  (34:26), because they *"caused the cry of the poor to come to him"* (34:28).
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:6, 17, 31; 37:23; **judgment in the rain.** God *"gives to the
  afflicted their right"* (*mišpāṭ*, 36:6); Job is *"full of the judgment of the wicked"* (36:17); the storm
  is how *"he judges the people"* and also how *"He gives food in abundance"* (36:31); and *"In justice and
  great righteousness, he will not oppress"* (37:23).
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:12–15; 40:2; **judgment by daybreak, and the lawsuit turned.** The
  morning that shakes the wicked out of the earth (38:13); and *"Shall he who argues contend (rîb) with
  the Almighty?"* (40:2), the case Job brought now called to answer.
- [[Behemoth and Leviathan]] — Job 40:8–14; **the bench offered to Job.** *"Will you even annul my judgment
  (mišpāṭî)? Will you condemn me, that you may be justified?"* (40:8) — then the robe and the task: bring every
  proud man low, *"crush the wicked in their place"*, and *"your own right hand can save you"* (40:10–14). The
  judgment Job said God neglected (21; 24) is handed to him as a challenge.
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:7–9; **the trial's verdict, given to the other side.** Job asked for a hearing; the verdict,
  when it comes, is on the friends: *"My wrath is kindled against you"*. It is turned aside by sacrifice and by the
  prayer of the man they judged, *"that I not deal with you according to your folly"* (42:8).
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:19–20; ***"I will reach out my hand and strike Egypt with all my wonders."***
  *Wəhikkêtî* is *nākâ*, **the verb Moses used on one Egyptian in secret** (2:12): what the man did by
  his own hand to one, God will do by his to the country. **The judgment is announced before the
  request it answers is made**, with the refusal foreknown
- [[Moses Returns to Egypt]] — Exodus 4:21–23; **the last plague announced before the first.** *"I will kill your son,
  your firstborn"*, measure for measure for a king whose house killed Israel's sons — and *"I will
  harden his heart"*, the first hardening statement
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:21; ***"May Yahweh look at you, and judge"*** — *yēreʾ … wəyišpōṭ*
  — **the right God invoked for the right acts against the wrong man.** God has *seen* (2:25; 3:7) and
  will judge — but Egypt, *"with great judgments"* (6:6, *šəpāṭîm*). Compare Sarai's *"Yahweh judge
  between me and you"* (Genesis 16:5)
- [[I Am Yahweh]] — Exodus 6:6; ***šəpāṭîm gədōlîm***, ***"great judgments"*** — **the officers
  asked Yahweh to judge Moses (5:21); Yahweh promises to judge Egypt.** *Šəpāṭîm* occurs three times
  in Exodus: 6:6, 7:4, and 12:12, *"Against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments"*
- [[Aaron's Staff Before Pharaoh]] — Exodus 7:4; ***bišpāṭîm gədōlîm***, *"by great judgments"*,
  repeating 6:6 — the second of Exodus's three *šəpāṭîm*. **The first sign that follows judges no
  one**; it is proof, and the judgments are still ahead
- [[Water Turned to Blood]] — Exodus 7:14–25; **the first of the *"great judgments"* falls on the
  river.** God strikes (*nākâ*, the verb he promised at 3:20) the water Egypt lives by; the narrator
  names the striker, *"after Yahweh had struck the river"* (7:25). Unlike the rod-sign, this one damages
  the land
- [[Frogs, Lice, and Flies]] — Exodus 8:1–32; **the second, third, and fourth plagues.** *"I will plague all
  your borders"* uses *nāgap*, the verb of the Passover's striking (12:23, 27); the land is
  *"corrupted"* with the flood's verb; and judgment begins to discriminate — the swarms fall on Egypt
  and not on Goshen (8:22)
- [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]] — Exodus 9:1–35; **the fifth, sixth, and seventh plagues.** *"All my
  plagues against your heart"* (*maggēpōt*, the noun of *nāgap*); *"you would have been cut off from
  the earth"*; and a hail *"such as has not been in Egypt since the day it was founded"*, rained with
  fire by the verb of Sodom's judgment (*māṭar*, Genesis 19:24). **Judgment now comes with a warning
  and a way of escape**, and those who heed it are spared (9:19–21) — [[Fire and Sulfur from the Sky]]
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:1–29; **the judgment measured in generations.** The locusts are such *"as
  neither your fathers nor your fathers' fathers have seen"* (10:6), and *"Before them there were no
  such locusts as they, nor will there ever be again"* (10:14), words Joel reuses for a plague on
  Judah (Joel 2:2). Egypt's court says *"Egypt is destroyed"* (10:7). The darkness is the *ʾăpēlâ* of
  Deuteronomy's curse (28:29) — [[The Day of the Locust - Exodus 10 and Joel 1-2]] —
  [[The Diseases of Egypt - The Plagues in Deuteronomy 28]]
- [[The Last Plague Announced]] — Exodus 11:1–10; **the last judgment on Egypt announced, top to
  bottom.** *"Yet one plague more"* — *negaʿ*, the word of the *"great plagues"* on Abram's Pharaoh
  (Genesis 12:17, WLC); God himself will *"go out into the middle of Egypt"* (11:4); the dead run
  *"from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne, even to the firstborn of the female
  servant who is behind the mill"* (11:5). **The slave girl's son is the chapter's hardest line**;
  the page records readers' answers and adopts none —
  [[A Great Cry - The Outcry of the Oppressed and the Cry of Egypt]]
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:12–13, 23; ***"Against all the gods of Egypt I will execute
  judgments"***. *Šəpāṭîm*, in the Torah only at 6:6; 7:4; 12:12; and Numbers 33:4, where it is fulfilled
  (WLC). And ***"the destroyer"***, named at 12:23 and not identified — Yahweh, an angel, or a power he
  restrains; the wiki adopts none. See
  [[The Door and the Destroyer - Lot's House and the Passover Night]]
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:29–36; **the judgment of Genesis 15:14 executed.** *"I will also judge that
  nation"* — the firstborn struck at midnight, *"Yahweh struck"*, with no destroyer named (12:29); and
  *"Afterward they will come out with great wealth"* — *"They plundered the Egyptians"* (12:36). The
  narrator gives the act to Yahweh alone; who the destroyer of 12:23 was is still not said
- [[The Crossing of the Sea]] — Exodus 14:17–18, 23–28; **the last judgment on Egypt's army, and the
  knowledge it was for.** God hardens *"the heart of Egypt"* so that *"they shall go in after them"*, and
  *"The Egyptians shall know that I am Yahweh"*; the army is panicked, clogged, and *"overthrown"* —
  *nāʿar*, *shaken off*, only here in the Torah (WLC lemma search) — and *"There remained not so much as one
  of them"*, the locusts' epitaph (10:19). **The knowledge promised arrives as the army's last words**:
  *"Yahweh fights for them against the Egyptians!"* (14:25)
- [[The Song of the Sea]] — Exodus 15:4–12; **judgment sung.** *"You send out (šālaḥ) your wrath. It consumes them as
  stubble"* (15:7) — the verb of *"Let my people go"*, and the stubble Israel gathered for Egypt's bricks
  (5:12; WLC). **The Song gives the drowning to God's own right hand** (15:6, 12), where the prose gave the
  stretched-out hand to Moses (14:21, 27).
- [[Bitter Water at Marah]] — Exodus 15:26; **the plagues remembered as diseases God put.** *"I will put
  none of the diseases on you, which I have put (śamtî) on the Egyptians"* (WLC). **Judgment on Egypt
  becomes the measure of a promise to Israel**, and Deuteronomy 28:60 makes it a threat: *"He will bring on
  you again all the diseases of Egypt"* (WEB; *not yet ingested*)
- [[Manna and Quail]] — Exodus 16; **a complaint with a charge of murder in it, and no judgment.** God *"heard"*
  and fed; the hoarded bread rots and the Sabbath-breakers find nothing. **Numbers 11 tells a second quail story
  that ends in plague** (11:31–34; *not yet ingested*), and Numbers 15 the stoning of a man gathering sticks
  on the Sabbath (15:32–36; *not yet ingested*): **the first instances are met with provision, the later ones
  with judgment**
- [[The Battle with Amalek]] — Exodus 17:14, 16; **a sentence without a stated cause.** *"I will utterly blot out
  the memory of Amalek from under the sky"* — *māḥâ*, the verb of the flood (Genesis 6:7; WLC) — and war
  *"from generation to generation"*. **Exodus gives no reason**; Deuteronomy gives one forty years on, the attack on
  the stragglers and *"he didn't fear God"* (25:18). Recorded, not harmonized
- [[Jethro's Counsel]] — Exodus 18:13–26; **judging as daily work.** *"Moses sat to judge the people"* from
  morning to evening — *šāpaṭ*, the verb of 2:14's *"Who made you … a judge?"*, four times in the chapter (WLC) —
  until Jethro divides it: *"every great matter they shall bring to you, but every small matter they shall judge
  themselves"*. **Israel's first courts, before its first law code** —
  [[Too Heavy for You Alone - Exodus 18, Numbers 11, and Deuteronomy 1]]

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:5–7; generational warning, loving kindness, and guilt for
  misuse of the name

- [[Laws of Violence and Injury]] — Exodus 21:12–36; distinctions and remedies for injury and loss
- [[Laws of Property and Restitution]] — Exodus 22:8–11; disputed claims *"before God"*, and the oath where no one saw
- [[The Priestly Garments]] — Exodus 28:1–43; the breastplate of judgment and the Urim and Thummim.
- [[The Priestly Garments Made]] — Exodus 39:1–31; Breastplate made; Urim and Thummim and inquiry not narrated.
- [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]] — Proverbs 1:20–33; a judgment oracle in Wisdom's voice, with the laughter of Psalm 2:4 and no judge acting
