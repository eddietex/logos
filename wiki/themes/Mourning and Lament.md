# Mourning and Lament

## Definition / overview

**Grief in Scripture is a performance with a vocabulary, a wardrobe, a posture, and a clock**, and
the Bible describes it far more often than it describes the emotion underneath. The rite is
remarkably stable from Genesis to Job: the garment is torn, sackcloth is put on, dust or ashes go on
the head, the voice is raised, the mourner sits or lies on the ground, food is refused, and the whole
thing runs for a stated term — most often seven days.

**Lament is the speech half of it**, and it is a distinct act. Mourning is what the body does;
lament is what the mouth does, and the Bible's laments run from a single cry to the forty-two verses
of Job 3 and the five acrostic poems of Lamentations. **The wiki keeps the two together on one page
because Job holds them apart so deliberately**: chapter 2 is mourning with no words in it at all, and
chapter 3 is lament with nothing else.

Three things are worth stating at the outset because they are counter-intuitive to a modern reader.

**It is public by design.** Sitting in ashes is done to be seen sitting in them; Mordecai does it
*"in the middle of the city"* (Esther 4:1). Grief that nobody witnesses is not a lesser form of the
rite, it is a failure of it.

**It is bounded.** Seven days for Jacob at Atad (Genesis 50:10), seven for Saul at Jabesh Gilead
(1 Samuel 31:13), thirty for Aaron and for Moses, seventy in Egypt for Jacob (Genesis 50:3). A
mourning that will not end is a recognised pathology and Genesis 37:35 is its case study.

**It is not the same as repentance, and it is indistinguishable from it.** The posture, the ashes and
the sackcloth are identical whether a man has lost a son or turned from a sin, and **that ambiguity
is what destroys Job's friends**: they arrive, find a man in the exact attitude of a penitent, and
conclude there is something to be penitent about. See [[Sitting in Dust and Ashes]].

## Development across Scripture

**Genesis establishes the rite and immediately shows it going wrong.**

**Jacob is the wiki's first full mourner and its first bad one.** Shown the bloodied coat, he *"tore
his clothes, and put sackcloth on his waist, and mourned for his son many days"*, then refuses every
attempt at consolation: *"I will go down to Sheol to my son mourning"* (Genesis 37:34–35). **The rite
is performed correctly and the grief is unbounded and the premise is false** — Joseph is alive. See
[[Joseph Sold into Slavery]]. Two more torn garments follow from the same family: Reuben at the empty
pit (37:29) and the brothers at the discovery of the cup (44:13).

**Genesis 50 gives the Bible's first stated mourning term**, and gives two at once. Jacob's body is
embalmed and Egypt mourns seventy days (50:3); then the cortege stops at the threshing floor of Atad
and keeps seven days of *"very great and severe lamentation"* (50:10), which the watching Canaanites
name the place for. **An Egyptian term and a Hebrew term in a single funeral**, in the one Genesis
chapter where the two countries bury the same man. See [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]] and
[[Abel Mizraim]].

**Job takes the rite over completely, and uses it three times in two chapters.**

At **1:20**, told that his children are dead, Job *"arose, and tore his robe, and shaved his head,
and fell down on the ground"* — and then does the thing no rite requires: *"and worshiped."* See
[[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]].

At **2:8** he is sitting among the ashes with a potsherd, and the material has shifted from a
gesture he performs to a place he lives. See [[The Second Test]].

At **2:12–13** three friends arrive and perform the rite *for* him — raised voices, torn robes, dust
thrown over their heads — and then keep **seven days and seven nights** on the ground beside him
without speaking. **They hold a funeral for a man who is alive**, and the book offers it as an
accurate estimate of the situation. See [[Seven Days of Silence]] and
[[Seven Days and Seven Nights - The Week of Mourning]].

**Then chapter 3 turns mourning into lament**, and the book's second half begins. *"Let the day
perish in which I was born"* is the rite's speech act pushed past every boundary the rite has: not a
lament for the dead but a lament that a living man was ever born, and its closest relative in
Scripture is Jeremiah 20:14–18, which says almost the same thing in almost the same order.

**The rest of the Hebrew Bible fills the pattern out.** Ashes and sackcloth in Esther 4, Jonah 3,
Daniel 9; dust on the head at Joshua 7:6, 1 Samuel 4:12, 2 Samuel 1:2, Lamentations 2:10; the
professional mourning women of Jeremiah 9:17–20; David's lament over Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel
1:19–27) and over Absalom (2 Samuel 18:33); and the book of Lamentations, which is the rite written
out at length over a city. **Isaiah 61:3's *"a garland for ashes"* is the reversal promised**, and
the terms are the rite's own.

**The New Testament keeps the practice and reframes the term.** Jesus weeps at a grave (John 11:35)
at a funeral with professional mourners present, and Paul asks not that Christians stop grieving but
that they not grieve *"like the rest, who have no hope"* (1 Thessalonians 4:13) — **a clause that
concedes the grief and disputes only its horizon.** Revelation 21:4 ends the rite rather than
regulating it.

## Key passages

- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:29, 34–35: the first torn garment of the wiki, the first
  sackcloth, and the refusal of comfort.
- [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]] — Genesis 50:1–14: Joseph falling on his father's face, seventy
  days in Egypt, seven at [[Abel Mizraim]].
- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — Job 1:20: the full rite, performed by a man who
  then worships.
- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:8: the ashes as a residence.
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:12–13: the rite performed for a living man, and the wiki's
  anchor text for the whole pattern.
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — Job 16–17, the lament from inside, and Job's
  own sackcloth at 16:15.
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — Job 19:13–20, the catalogue of everyone who has gone.
- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:13, the brothers tearing their clothes.
- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:2, weeping loud enough that the house of Pharaoh hears.
