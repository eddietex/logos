# Mourning and Lament

## Definition / overview

**Grief in Scripture is a performance with a vocabulary, a wardrobe, a posture, and a clock**, and
the Bible describes it far more often than it describes the emotion underneath. The rite is
remarkably stable from Genesis to Job: the garment is torn, sackcloth is put on, dust or ashes go on
the head, the voice is raised, the mourner sits or lies on the ground, food is refused, and the whole
thing runs for a stated term — most often seven days.

**Lament is the speech half of it**, and it is a distinct act. Mourning is what the body does;
lament is what the mouth does, and the Bible's laments run from a single cry to the twenty-six verses
of Job 3 and the five acrostic poems of Lamentations. **The wiki keeps the two together on one page
because Job holds them apart so deliberately**: chapter 2 is mourning with no words in it at all, and
chapter 3 is lament with nothing else.

Three things are worth stating at the outset because they are counter-intuitive to a modern reader.

**It is public by design.** Sitting in ashes is done to be seen sitting in them; Mordecai does it
*"in the middle of the city"* (Esther 4:1). Grief that nobody witnesses is not a lesser form of the
rite, it is a failure of it.

**It is bounded.** Seven days for Jacob at Atad (Genesis 50:10), seven for Saul at Jabesh Gilead
(1 Samuel 31:13), thirty for Aaron and for Moses, seventy in Egypt for Jacob (Genesis 50:3). A
mourning that will not end is a recognised pathology and Genesis 37:35 is its case study.

**It is not the same as repentance, and it is indistinguishable from it.** The posture, the ashes and
the sackcloth are identical whether a man has lost a son or turned from a sin, and **that ambiguity
is what destroys Job's friends**: they arrive, find a man in the exact attitude of a penitent, and
conclude there is something to be penitent about. See [[Sitting in Dust and Ashes]].

## Development across Scripture

**Genesis establishes the rite and immediately shows it going wrong.**

**Jacob is the wiki's first full mourner and its first bad one.** Shown the bloodied coat, he *"tore
his clothes, and put sackcloth on his waist, and mourned for his son many days"*, then refuses every
attempt at consolation: *"I will go down to Sheol to my son mourning"* (Genesis 37:34–35). **The rite
is performed correctly and the grief is unbounded and the premise is false** — Joseph is alive. See
[[Joseph Sold into Slavery]]. Two more torn garments follow from the same family: Reuben at the empty
pit (37:29) and the brothers at the discovery of the cup (44:13).

**Genesis 50 gives the Bible's first stated mourning term**, and gives two at once. Jacob's body is
embalmed and Egypt mourns seventy days (50:3); then the cortege stops at the threshing floor of Atad
and keeps seven days of *"very great and severe lamentation"* (50:10), which the watching Canaanites
name the place for. **An Egyptian term and a Hebrew term in a single funeral**, in the one Genesis
chapter where the two countries bury the same man. See [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]] and
[[Abel Mizraim]].

**Job takes the rite over completely, and uses it three times in two chapters.**

At **1:20**, told that his children are dead, Job *"arose, and tore his robe, and shaved his head,
and fell down on the ground"* — and then does the thing no rite requires: *"and worshiped."* See
[[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]].

At **2:8** he is sitting among the ashes with a potsherd, and the material has shifted from a
gesture he performs to a place he lives. See [[The Second Test]].

At **2:12–13** three friends arrive and perform the rite *for* him — raised voices, torn robes, dust
thrown over their heads — and then keep **seven days and seven nights** on the ground beside him
without speaking. **They hold a funeral for a man who is alive**, and the book offers it as an
accurate estimate of the situation. See [[Seven Days of Silence]] and
[[Seven Days and Seven Nights - The Week of Mourning]].

**Then chapter 3 turns mourning into lament**, and the book's second half begins. *"Let the day
perish in which I was born"* is the rite's speech act pushed past every boundary the rite has: not a
lament for the dead but a lament that a living man was ever born, and its closest relative in
Scripture is Jeremiah 20:14–18, which says almost the same thing in almost the same order. See
[[Let the Day Perish]] and [[Cursing the Day of Birth - Job 3 and Jeremiah 20]].

**And the two chapters together are the wiki's clearest statement of the distinction this page is
built on.** Chapter 2 is the rite with no words in it — torn robes, dust, the ground, a seven-day
term, and *"no one spoke a word to him."* **Chapter 3 is the words with no rite**: Job tears
nothing, puts nothing on his head, and performs no gesture at all. **The body has finished and the
mouth has started**, and the book arranges the seam so the difference cannot be missed. **Note also
what the lament is not about.** Ten of Job's children died in chapter 1, and chapter 3 does not
mention them. **He does not mourn the dead here; he mourns that he was ever born to lose them**,
which is what separates a lament of this kind from the funeral rite it grows out of.

**The rest of the Hebrew Bible fills the pattern out.** Ashes and sackcloth in Esther 4, Jonah 3,
Daniel 9; dust on the head at Joshua 7:6, 1 Samuel 4:12, 2 Samuel 1:2, Lamentations 2:10; the
professional mourning women of Jeremiah 9:17–20; David's lament over Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel
1:19–27) and over Absalom (2 Samuel 18:33); and the book of Lamentations, which is the rite written
out at length over a city. **Isaiah 61:3's *"a garland for ashes"* is the reversal promised**, and
the terms are the rite's own.

**The New Testament keeps the practice and reframes the term.** Jesus weeps at a grave (John 11:35)
at a funeral with professional mourners present, and Paul asks not that Christians stop grieving but
that they not grieve *"like the rest, who have no hope"* (1 Thessalonians 4:13) — **a clause that
concedes the grief and disputes only its horizon.** Revelation 21:4 ends the rite rather than
regulating it.

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

Israel mourns on hearing that Yahweh will not go among them and ceases to wear jewelry (33:3–4). God
then commands the removal, and the narrator reports compliance from Horeb onward (33:5–6). This is
grief over threatened divine absence, not a funeral. The outward act is explicit; a comprehensive
account of each person’s inward repentance is not. See [[Show Me Your Glory]].

### Hidden bitterness — Proverbs 14:10, 13

*"The heart knows its own bitterness and joy; he will not share these with a stranger"* (10), and *"Even in laughter the heart may be sorrowful"* (13). Grief is partly hidden from onlookers — the limit Job's friends did not respect (Job 7:11; 10:1). See [[The Heart Knows Its Own Bitterness - Proverbs 14.10 and Job's Complaint]].

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### The aching heart — Proverbs 15:13, 15

*"A glad heart makes a cheerful face; but an aching heart breaks the spirit"* (13), and *"All the days of the afflicted are wretched"* (15). With 13:12 and 14:10, these describe grief from the inside without passing a verdict on the griever.

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### A time for every purpose — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

Weeping stands beside laughter and mourning beside dancing (4). Grief has a place in the
poem's account of life; the later commendation of rejoicing (12) does not erase that place.
Tearing and sewing occur in verse 7, but the verse names no garment or funeral. A mourning
association is possible, not established by the wording. No prescribed duration of mourning
is supplied here.

See [[A Time for Every Purpose]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

### The tears of the oppressed — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

The tears in 4:1 belong to oppressed people, not to a described funeral. No torn garments,
seven-day term, or bereavement is specified. The Preacher's preference for death and never
having been (2–3) responds to others' tears, whereas Job 3 speaks from the sufferer's own
condition. Lament here exceeds the page's account of formal mourning rites. The repeated
absence of comfort is preserved at [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].

See [[The Tears of the Oppressed]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–4:3; the passage page
records the additional comparison sources.

### The house of mourning — Ecclesiastes 7:1–14

The house of mourning is preferred to the house of feasting, because death is the end of all
and the living should take it to heart (7:2); the wise heart is there, the fool's in the house
of mirth (4). Sorrow is ranked above laughter (3). This is a judgment about what the living
learn from mourning, not a description of a rite: no funeral, garments, or period of days is
specified. **Tension:** the same book commends eating, drinking, and enjoyment as God's gift
(2:24; 5:18) and commands joy in prosperity (7:14).

See [[The House of Mourning]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:1–14; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

## Key passages

- [[The Tears of the Oppressed]] — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3; Tears of living victims; no funeral or bounded mourning rite.

- [[A Time for Every Purpose]] — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15; Weeping and mourning have their time beside
  joy.

- [[A Gentle Answer]] — Proverbs 15:13, 15.

- [[Show Me Your Glory]] — Exodus 33:1–23; Jewelry removed in mourning over threatened absence.

- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:29, 34–35: the first torn garment of the wiki, the first
  sackcloth, and the refusal of comfort.
- [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]] — Genesis 50:1–14: Joseph falling on his father's face, seventy
  days in Egypt, seven at [[Abel Mizraim]].
- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — Job 1:20: the full rite, performed by a man who
  then worships.
- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:8: the ashes as a residence.
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:12–13: the rite performed for a living man, and the wiki's
  anchor text for the whole pattern.
- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26: **the wiki's anchor text for lament as distinct from
  mourning**, and Scripture's most extreme instance of it.
- [[Cursing the Day of Birth - Job 3 and Jeremiah 20]] — the birth-curse as a form with conventions,
  performed twice in the Hebrew Bible and nowhere else.
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — Job 16–17, the lament from inside, and Job's
  own sackcloth at 16:15.
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — Job 19:13–20, the catalogue of everyone who has gone.
- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:13, the brothers tearing their clothes.
- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:2, weeping loud enough that the house of Pharaoh hears.
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 7:11, ***"Therefore I will not keep silent. I will speak in
  the anguish of my spirit. I will complain in the bitterness of my soul."*** Lament claimed as a
  right, and the seven days of silence (2:13) formally ended; the same chapter is where the lament
  first turns into address to God (7:7)
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:12, 32–33; **music and a funeral, both given to the
  wicked.** *"They sing to the tambourine and harp, and rejoice at the sound of the pipe"* — the harp
  and pipe that Job will say at 30:31 have *"turned to mourning"*. And the wicked man's own death is
  properly mourned: carried to the grave, watched, followed by *"all men"*. Job's mourning was held
  over a living man (2:12–13); the wicked man's is held at the right time and by a crowd.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:2; 24:12; *"Even today my complaint (śîḥî) is
  rebellious. His hand is heavy in spite of my groaning"* — and, in chapter 24, the lament given to
  other people: *"From out of the populous city, men groan. The soul of the wounded cries out, yet God
  doesn't regard the folly."*
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:25; ***"as one who comforts the mourners"*** — *ʾăbēlîm yənaḥēm*, the
  verb of the friends' errand (2:11). Job remembers being the comforter; in the next chapter his harp
  *"has turned to mourning"* (30:31). See [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].
- [[Now They Mock Me]] — Job 30:28–31; ***"I go mourning without the sun"*** — *qōdēr hillaktî*, the
  psalmist's *"I go mourning all day long"* (Psalm 38:6) — ***"I am a brother to jackals, and a companion
  to ostriches"***, the pair Micah 1:8 howls and moans like, and ***"my harp has turned to mourning, and
  my pipe into the voice of those who weep"***: Jubal's *kinnôr* and *ʿûgāb* (Genesis 4:21), the music of
  21:12, turned. The comforter of 29:25 ends as the mourner, and no comforter is named. See
  [[A Brother to Jackals - Job 30.29 and the Creatures of the Ruins]].
- [[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]] — Job 42:6; **the ash-heap at the end.** *"Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and
  ashes"* — said still sitting where 2:8 put him, in Abraham's pair (Genesis 18:27) and his own of 30:19, with
  the comforters' verb, *nḥm*. Whether it is repentance or consolation, the mourner's place is where Job's last
  words are spoken. See [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:11; **the comfort of 2:11, given at last.** *"They comforted him, and consoled
  him"* — the two verbs (*nûd*, *nḥm*) the three friends came with in chapter 2, now from the brothers, sisters and
  acquaintances who had been estranged (19:13–14), at a meal in his house. See [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:22–23; ***"Why … why"*** — **lament as accusation, in the form the
  Psalms will use** (*"Why do you sleep, Lord? … Why do you hide your face …"*, Psalm 44:23–24). Moses
  laments not the suffering alone but that his own obedience caused it
- [[The Last Plague Announced]] — Exodus 11:6; ***"There shall be a great cry throughout all the
  land of Egypt, such as there has not been, nor shall be any more."*** **The cry changes sides**:
  *ṣəʿāqâ* was Israel's under the taskmasters (3:7, 9), and now it is Egypt's over its firstborn
  (11:6; 12:30). The only other *ṣəʿāqâ gədōlâ* in the Torah is Esau's (Genesis 27:34, WLC) —
  [[A Great Cry - The Outcry of the Oppressed and the Cry of Egypt]]
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:30; ***"there was a great cry in Egypt, for there was not a house where there was
  not one dead"*** — the cry of 11:6 kept, *ṣəʿāqâ gədōlâ* (WLC). **The text records the cry and no rite of
  mourning**; the next Egyptian act is to urge Israel out. Numbers 33:4 places the departure *"while the
  Egyptians were burying all their firstborn"* (WEB). See
  [[A Great Cry - The Outcry of the Oppressed and the Cry of Egypt]]
- [[The House of Mourning]] — Ecclesiastes 7:2–4; the house of mourning preferred to the house of feasting.
