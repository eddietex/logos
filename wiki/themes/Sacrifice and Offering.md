# Sacrifice and Offering

## Definition / overview

Something given to God, and the question of whether it is received. The Bible's first instance is
in [[Cain and Abel]], and what is most striking about it is how much is missing: there is no
command to bring anything, no altar, no priest, no ritual, and no stated rule by which one gift is
accepted and the other is not. Two brothers bring what their trades produce, *"as time passed"*,
apparently on their own initiative.

> **4:3–5** As time passed, Cain brought an offering to Yahweh from the fruit of the ground. Abel
> also brought some of the firstborn of his flock and of its fat. Yahweh respected Abel and his
> offering, but he didn't respect Cain and his offering.

The word used of **both** gifts is *minḥâ* — a general word for tribute or gift, and later the
technical Levitical term for the **grain** offering (Leviticus 2). That matters for the most
common explanation of the difference: Genesis does not distinguish the two offerings by medium,
and the sacrificial system it anticipates prescribes both animal and grain offerings as
acceptable. What the verse does distinguish is quality — Abel brings *firstborn* animals and their
*fat*, the parts Leviticus will reserve for God (Leviticus 3:16), while Cain's gift carries no
qualifier at all — and order: in both halves of the sentence the **person** is named before the
offering. *Yahweh respected Abel, and his offering.*

That order is the one the New Testament follows. Hebrews 11:4 attributes the difference to faith
and says Abel *"had testimony given to him that he was righteous"*; 1 John 3:12 explains Cain's
act by the character of his works. Neither is stated in Genesis. A fourth reading declines to
supply a reason at all, on the grounds that Genesis is about to make unexplained choice a habit —
Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh — and that Cain's real test is whether
he can live with a verdict he did not get to audit. Genesis 4:7 says he could have.

Two features of this first scene outlast it. Offering is **response**, not transaction: nothing in
the text suggests either brother is buying anything, and nothing suggests God needed it. And an
offering is **inseparable from the one who brings it**, which is the point every prophet will
later make against sacrifice offered by people who are doing evil (Isaiah 1:11–17, Amos 5:21–24,
Micah 6:6–8) and which Jesus makes practical: leave the gift at the altar and go be reconciled to
your brother first (Matthew 5:23–24). The brother is the same word this passage's murder is
committed under.

Genesis 3:21, where God clothes the man and woman in coats of skins, is often read as the true
first sacrifice. The text says nothing of the kind — no altar, no offering, no ritual language —
but something did die so that they could be covered, and the reading has a long history. It is
recorded on [[The Fall]] as a reading rather than as what the verse states.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 3:21** — the coats of skins: not called a sacrifice, but a death that clothes.
- **Genesis 4:3–5** — the first offerings, uncommanded and unequally received; *minḥâ* used of
  both.
- **Genesis 8:20–21** — the first *altar* in Scripture, the first *burnt offering* (*ʿōlâ*, wholly
  consumed), and the first *rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ*, the **pleasant aroma**, which becomes the standing
  Levitical description of an accepted sacrifice. Four things set the terms here for everything
  after: nobody commanded it, it costs a seventh of every clean species alive, God's response is
  addressed to no one (*"Yahweh said in his heart"*), and the resolve it produces is grounded in
  human evil rather than human merit. See [[The Waters Recede]] and [[A Pleasing Aroma]].
- **Genesis 9:4** — the premise the whole sacrificial system will rest on, stated first as a dietary
  rule. *"Flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat"*: blood is life, and therefore
  not food. Leviticus 17:11 completes the thought with the other half — *"I have given it to you on
  the altar to make atonement for your souls"* — so the one thing that may never be eaten is the one
  thing that may be offered. Genesis 9 states the withholding centuries before the altar law explains
  what the withheld thing is for. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and [[Blood]].
- **Job 1:5** — Job sanctifies his children himself and offers a burnt offering for each, after
  every round of their feasting, on the hypothesis *"It may be that my sons have sinned, and
  renounced God in their hearts."* No altar is mentioned, no priest, no command, and no offence
  known to have occurred: the first offering in Scripture made for a **possible** sin, and one
  located in the heart, where no ritual system can reach it. The pattern closes at 42:8, where God
  sends the three friends to Job with their bulls and rams and accepts his prayer for them. See
  [[Job of Uz]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- **Genesis 12:7–8** — the first patriarchal altars, and the pattern they set. Abram builds at
  [[Shechem (city)]] *"to Yahweh, who had appeared to him"* — the altar answers a theophany — and again on
  the ridge by [[Bethel]], where he *"called on Yahweh's name"*. **Neither verse mentions a victim, a
  burning, or an aroma.** That silence is consistent across the patriarchal altars (12:7, 12:8,
  13:18, 26:25, 33:20) and is a sharp break from Noah at 8:20–21, where the whole point was what went
  up from it. Abram's altars mark ground and declare a name: they are claims staked, in a land the
  narrator has just said belongs to Canaanites, by a man who owns none of it. See
  [[The Call of Abram]] and [[The Altar at Shechem and the Covenant at Shechem]].
- **Genesis 15:9–17** — the theme's most instructive near-miss: everything sacrificial is present and
  no sacrifice occurs. **The five species are exactly the Levitical set** — *"a heifer three years
  old, a female goat three years old, a ram three years old, a turtledove, and a young pigeon"* —
  herd, flock, and the two birds that let a poor worshipper bring a pair where a rich one brings a
  bull (Leviticus 1; 5:7; 12:8). **The bird is not divided** (15:10), matching Leviticus 1:17's
  *"shall not divide it apart"* precisely. Fire arrives, at evening, and terror falls on the man.
  And yet: no altar is built, nothing is burned, no blood is applied, no aroma goes up, nobody eats,
  and no word from the sacrificial vocabulary appears. The animals are the **terms of an oath**, not
  gifts — halves laid opposite each other to make a corridor, and the self-curse of Jeremiah
  34:18–20 is the meaning of walking it. Genesis has the whole apparatus of Israelite sacrifice
  four centuries early and puts it to a completely different use. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] and
  [[Passing Between the Pieces]].
- **Genesis 22:1–19 — the Abraham cycle's only completed sacrifice, and the theme's hinge.** After
  three altars with nothing said to be offered on them, and a covenant ceremony with the whole
  apparatus and no offering, **this is the first burnt offering in Genesis since Noah's** — and it is
  the first one the book narrates step by step. Verse 9 runs the procedure Leviticus 1 will
  prescribe: the altar built, *"the wood in order"*, the victim bound and laid on it. Three things
  enter Scripture here. **The *ʿōlâ* asked of a human being** — the whole-offering demanded of a son,
  and then refused. **Substitution**: the ram offered *"instead of his son"*, *taḥat*, the first time
  one life is said to stand in the place of another. **And provision as the sacrifice's ground**:
  *"God will provide himself the lamb for a burnt offering"* (22:8), which the chapter answers with a
  ram rather than a lamb and turns into the name of a mountain. **The order matters** — the knife is
  stopped before the ram is seen, so the substitute does not buy the reprieve; it follows it. See
  [[The Binding of Isaac]] and [[The Ram Instead of the Son - Substitution at Moriah]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: Passover and the blood on the doorposts (Exodus 12);
  the five offerings and the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 1–7, 16); *"the life of the flesh is in
  the blood"* (Leviticus 17:11); *"to obey is better than sacrifice"* (1 Samuel 15:22) and the
  prophetic critique; Psalm 51:16–17 on a broken spirit; Isaiah 53 and the servant as offering;
  Hebrews 9–10 on a sacrifice that is not repeated; Romans 12:1 on bodies as living sacrifices.

### The altar law — Exodus 20:24–26

**The first law of sacrifice in Exodus concerns the altar, not the victim.** It is to be of earth,
or of stones on which no tool has been lifted, since a tool *"polluted"* it (*ḥālal*, WLC), and
there are to be no steps. It receives burnt offerings and peace offerings, sheep and cattle
(20:24–26). Deuteronomy 27:5–7 and Joshua 8:30–31 apply the law at Mount Ebal; see
[[An Altar of Uncut Stones - Exodus 20, Deuteronomy 27, and Joshua 8]]. **A tension is left in
place.** Exodus 27:1–8 prescribes a crafted altar of acacia wood and bronze for the tabernacle, and
the book does not say how the two provisions relate. See [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]].

**Exodus 22:29 forbids delay.** *"You shall not delay to offer from your harvest and from the
outflow of your presses"* — Hebrew *məlēʾātəkā wədimʿăkā*, literally *your fullness and your tear*,
which the WEB and the Septuagint both interpret as produce of field and press. The same verses give
the firstborn of sons and of animals to God. See [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

### Feasts, first fruits, and limits — Exodus 23:1–19

Three annual feasts require appearance before Yahweh; nobody is to appear empty (23:14–17). Blood is
not offered with leavened bread, festival fat does not remain overnight, and the first of the first
fruits is brought to God's house (23:18–19). The goat-and-milk prohibition follows with no
explanation. See [[Festivals and First Fruits]] and
[[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]].

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The covenant sacrifices — Exodus 24:4–8

Moses builds an altar *"under the mountain"* with twelve pillars, and *"young men of the children of
Israel"* offer burnt offerings and peace offerings of cattle (24:4–5). **No priest officiates**: the
Aaronic priesthood is not yet ordained (28–29), and Moses, not the young men, handles the blood. The
peace offerings (*zəbāḥîm šəlāmîm*, MAM) are the sacrifice whose meat is eaten (Leviticus 7:15), and the
leaders eat and drink before God at 24:11. See [[The Blood of the Covenant]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

### The altar, court, and maintained light — Exodus 27:1–21

The altar of Exodus 27:1–8 is acacia wood overlaid with brass, with horns, utensils, a grating,
and poles for carrying. Exodus 38:1 identifies the matching object as the altar of burnt
offering. No offering is performed in chapter 27. The difference from the earth or uncut-stone
altar of 20:24–26 remains explicit: Exodus gives both commands without explaining their
relationship. Filling the hollow frame with earth is a proposed reconciliation, not an
instruction stated in 27:8. See [[The Altar and the Court]] and
[[An Altar of Uncut Stones - Exodus 20, Deuteronomy 27, and Joshua 8]].

### The ordination of the priests — Exodus 29:1–46

One chapter prescribes the sin offering (29:14), the burnt offering (29:18), the ram of consecration
with its wave offering (29:22–26), the priests' portion from peace offerings (29:28), and the
twice-daily lamb with flour, oil, and wine (29:38–41). The sin offering's remains are burned
*"outside of the camp"*, the first such rule in Exodus. In the stored WEB, 29:14 is also the first
*"sin offering"* in Genesis and Exodus. The *"continual burnt offering"* (*ʿōlat tāmîd*, MAM) becomes
the base of Israel's worship. See [[The Ordination of the Priests]],
[[The Continual Burnt Offering - Exodus 29, Numbers 28, and Daniel 8]], and
[[Outside the Camp - The Sin Offering and Hebrews 13]].

### The incense altar, the ransom, and the holy oil — Exodus 30:1–38

The incense altar is an altar on which sacrifice is forbidden: no burnt offering, meal offering, or
drink offering, and no *"strange incense"* (30:9). Only the prescribed incense is burned, morning and
evening, and the blood of the sin offering touches its horns once a year (30:10). The census
half shekel is called *"an offering to Yahweh"*, *tərûmâ* (30:13, MAM), the word of the sanctuary
offering of 25:2, but unlike that offering it has a fixed amount. See
[[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

The feast at the calf includes burnt offerings and peace offerings (32:6), categories already used
at the covenant ratification (24:5). Familiar rites do not by themselves make the worship
faithful. Neither the offerings nor the feast prevent the indictment of 32:7–8. See [[They Sat Down to Eat and Drink - Exodus 32 in First Corinthians 10]]. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Sacrificial meals can draw the people into other worship (34:15), while appearance before Yahweh
must not be empty (34:20). Blood must not be offered with leavened bread; the Passover sacrifice
must not remain until morning; first fruits go to Yahweh's house (34:25–26). The chapter
prescribes these practices without narrating a sacrifice at the renewed ascent. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

Exodus 35:5, 21–29 carries out the willing contribution requested in 25:2–7. The gifts include
materials and the products of skilled hands; no sacrificial animal is killed here. No fixed amount
is assigned to each donor. This differs from the equal, required half-shekel payment of 30:11–16,
even though both support the Tent of Meeting. See
[[Willing Hearts - The Sanctuary, the Temple, and the Collection]].

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The makers and the surplus — Exodus 35:30–36:7

The offering continues every morning after the workers receive the accumulated materials
(36:3). Their report of excess prompts Moses to stop further contributions (36:4–6), and the
narrator confirms sufficiency and surplus (36:7). This is the end of a particular collection,
not a prohibition on future offerings. No sacrifice or disposal of excess material is narrated.
See [[Willing Hearts - The Sanctuary, the Temple, and the Collection]] and
[[Enough for the Work - Manna and the Sanctuary Offering]].

See [[Bezalel's Workmen and the Surplus]].

### The furnishings made — Exodus 37:1–29

The table's gold vessels for pouring (37:16), the incense altar (37:25–28), and the incense
(37:29) prepare for prescribed service. No bread is set out or incense burned here; those acts
appear in 40:23, 27. The gold incense altar remains distinct from the altar of burnt offering
whose making starts at 38:1. The report does not transfer the offerings prohibited in 30:9
to the incense altar.

See [[The Furnishings Made]]. Source: the cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-24.

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

The altar of burnt offering is made with horns, utensils, grating, and carrying poles (38:1–7). No
offering is burned on it here. Gold and brass are counted as contributed metal, while the silver is
explicitly tied to the half-shekel census levy (38:24–29). The altar law’s earth-or-stone provision
(20:24–25) and this crafted altar remain distinct; no earth filling is stated.

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The work completed — Exodus 39:32–43

The golden altar, sweet incense, bronze altar, and associated vessels are brought with
the completed work (39:38–39). This realizes the material preparation for worship without
narrating a sacrifice or incense offering. The willing gifts of 35:20–29 have become equipment;
Moses’ blessing of the people does not say those gifts purchased pardon for the calf.

See [[The Work Completed]]. Source: cited passages checked in the stored WEB Genesis and Exodus
texts, 2026-10-25.

- [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] — Genesis 28:18: **a pillar and oil instead of an altar and a victim,
  and nothing is killed.** Abraham built four altars and Isaac one; **Jacob raises a standing stone**
  — the first of four — and pours ***šemen*** on it, which is **the first oil in the Bible** and the
  first anointing in it, performed on a rock by a man with no office. Genesis 31:13 has God call it
  exactly that: *"where you anointed a pillar."* The law will later hate standing stones outright
  (Deuteronomy 16:22), which is a tension this wiki records without resolving; see
  [[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]]
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:54; ***wayyizbaḥ yaʿăqōb zebaḥ*** — **the first *zebaḥ*
  in Scripture**, noun and verb, and **the first covenant meal in the Bible.** Every offering Genesis
  has recorded until now goes up whole — Noah's *ʿōlâ* (8:20), the ram at Moriah (22:13) — **and this
  one is eaten**: *"called his relatives to eat bread. They ate bread, and stayed all night in the
  mountain."* **There is no altar named, no priest, and no stated recipient**; what the rite ratifies
  is an agreement between two men. **His grandfather's covenant was cut with halved animals and
  fire** (15:9–17); **this one is dinner**
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:1–3, 14; **an altar commanded rather
  than volunteered, and the Bible's first drink offering.** Every patriarchal altar before this one is
  built on the builder's own initiative after a theophany; **here God orders it in advance** —
  *"Make there an altar to God, who appeared to you when you fled"* — which is the pattern the
  Levitical system will run on. Then ***nesek***: *"He poured out a drink offering on it"* (35:14),
  **the word's first occurrence in Scripture**, poured by a herdsman onto a standing stone with no
  priest, no tabernacle, and nothing said about what was in it. The law will make it a fixed adjunct
  to the burnt offering, always wine, always priestly (Exodus 29:40; Numbers 15:5–10). See
  [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]]
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:1; ***"came to Beersheba, and offered sacrifices
  to the God of his father, Isaac."*** **The last sacrifice in Genesis, and it functions as a
  question.** **[[Jacob]] has already decided to go** (45:28); **he stops at the border town and
  sacrifices to the God who forbade this journey to [[Isaac]]** (26:2), **and the answer comes that
  night.** **No altar is named, no reason is given, and the text does not call it an enquiry** — **but
  it is the only sacrifice in the book immediately followed by a theophany**
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:8–9; **seven bulls and seven rams.** A burnt offering on the scale of Numbers 23:1
  and 1 Chronicles 15:26, brought by the friends to Job, who stands as priest for them as he stood for his children
  (1:5) — then on a hypothesis of sin, now on God's word. No altar, priesthood, or sanctuary is named. See
  [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:18; ***"let us go three days' journey into the wilderness, that we may sacrifice to
  Yahweh, our God."*** **The exodus is first requested as a sacrifice.** Whether the request is a test, a
  ruse, or a literal festival at the mountain of 3:12 is recorded on the passage page with three
  readings; none adopted
- [[Frogs, Lice, and Flies]] — Exodus 8:8, 25–28; **the sacrifice that cannot be offered in
  Egypt.** Pharaoh concedes sacrifice first *"in the land"*, then in the wilderness *"not very far
  away"*. Moses refuses the first because what Israel sacrifices is *"the abomination of the Egyptians"*
  and would get them stoned, and will not say what it is: *"as he shall command us"* (8:27) —

- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:9, 25–26; **the feast and the animals for it.** *"We must hold a feast to
  Yahweh"* — *ḥag*, the first use of the noun in the Bible; the next is the Passover (12:14) (WLC). Moses
  asks Pharaoh for *"sacrifices and burnt offerings"*, *zəbāḥîm wəʿōlôt*, and refuses to leave *"a
  hoof"*: *"we don't know with what we must serve Yahweh, until we come there"*. **Israel does not yet
  know how it will worship; the law of offerings is given at the mountain.** The first burnt offering
  actually offered in Exodus is Jethro's (18:12)
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:1–28; **the first sacrifice Exodus prescribes, and it is offered in
