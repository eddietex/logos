# Sacrifice and Offering

## Definition / overview

### Acceptable victims — Leviticus 22:17–33

Vow and freewill burnt offerings must be unblemished males (18–19), as at Leviticus 1:3; peace
offerings must be "perfect" with no sex stated (21), consistent with Leviticus 3:1, 6. A deformed or
incomplete bull or lamb may be a freewill offering but not a vow (23). Newborns are acceptable from
the eighth day (27), a mother and young are not killed the same day (28), and thanksgiving is eaten
the same day (29–30). An animal with a defect "shall not be acceptable for you" (20).

See [[Acceptable Victims and the Holy Name]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 22:17–33.



### Who eats the holy things — Leviticus 22:1–16

Israel's holy things and heave offering become the priest's food (2, 12), but eating them is
governed by the eater's condition and household membership (4–13). Unwitting eating is repaid
with a fifth added (14).

See [[Eating the Holy Things]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 22:1–16.


### Priestly holiness — Leviticus 21

Priests offer the bread of God and offerings made by fire (6, 8, 21). A descendant with a defect
cannot offer but may eat both holy and most holy food (22). This distinguishes serving at the
altar from receiving the priestly portion.

See [[The Holiness of the Priests]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 21.


Something given to God, and the question of whether it is received. The Bible's first instance is
in [[Cain and Abel]], and what is most striking about it is how much is missing: there is no
command to bring anything, no altar, no priest, no ritual, and no stated rule by which one gift is
accepted and the other is not. Two brothers bring what their trades produce, *"as time passed"*,
apparently on their own initiative.

> **4:3–5** As time passed, Cain brought an offering to Yahweh from the fruit of the ground. Abel
> also brought some of the firstborn of his flock and of its fat. Yahweh respected Abel and his
> offering, but he didn't respect Cain and his offering.

The word used of **both** gifts is *minḥâ* — a general word for tribute or gift, and later the
technical Levitical term for the **grain** offering (Leviticus 2). That matters for the most
common explanation of the difference: Genesis does not distinguish the two offerings by medium,
and the sacrificial system it anticipates prescribes both animal and grain offerings as
acceptable. What the verse does distinguish is quality — Abel brings *firstborn* animals and their
*fat*, the parts Leviticus will reserve for God (Leviticus 3:16), while Cain's gift carries no
qualifier at all — and order: in both halves of the sentence the **person** is named before the
offering. *Yahweh respected Abel, and his offering.*

That order is the one the New Testament follows. Hebrews 11:4 attributes the difference to faith
and says Abel *"had testimony given to him that he was righteous"*; 1 John 3:12 explains Cain's
act by the character of his works. Neither is stated in Genesis. A fourth reading declines to
supply a reason at all, on the grounds that Genesis is about to make unexplained choice a habit —
Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh — and that Cain's real test is whether
he can live with a verdict he did not get to audit. Genesis 4:7 says he could have.

Two features of this first scene outlast it. Offering is **response**, not transaction: nothing in
the text suggests either brother is buying anything, and nothing suggests God needed it. And an
offering is **inseparable from the one who brings it**, which is the point every prophet will
later make against sacrifice offered by people who are doing evil (Isaiah 1:11–17, Amos 5:21–24,
Micah 6:6–8) and which Jesus makes practical: leave the gift at the altar and go be reconciled to
your brother first (Matthew 5:23–24). The brother is the same word this passage's murder is
committed under.

Genesis 3:21, where God clothes the man and woman in coats of skins, is often read as the true
first sacrifice. The text says nothing of the kind — no altar, no offering, no ritual language —
but something did die so that they could be covered, and the reading has a long history. It is
recorded on [[The Fall]] as a reading rather than as what the verse states.

## Development across Scripture

### Covenant warnings and remembered promises — Leviticus 26:14–46

*I will not take delight in the sweet fragrance of your offerings* (31): the Hebrew is *lōʾ ʾārîaḥ*,
*I will not smell*. The law itself allows for refused sacrifice. See [[A Pleasing Aroma]].

See [[Covenant Warnings and Remembered Promises]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Lamps and weekly bread — Leviticus 24:1–9

Frankincense serves as the bread’s memorial fire offering (7), while the bread itself
is assigned for priestly eating (9). The fire-offering classification therefore does
not mean that all twelve cakes are burned.

See [[The Lamps and the Bread Before Yahweh]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 24:1–9.

### Booths and remembered deliverance — Leviticus 23:33–44

Seven days and the eighth-day assembly require fire offerings (36). The calendar
summary lists burnt, meal, sacrifice, and drink offerings for their appointed days,
besides Sabbaths and personal gifts, vows, and freewill offerings (37–38). No animal
inventory for this feast is supplied here.

See [[The Feast of Booths]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:33–44; comparisons as cited.

### Trumpets and atonement — Leviticus 23:23–32

Both the first and tenth days require an offering made by fire (25, 27). This unit
does not list animals or quantities. The Day of Atonement’s detailed priestly rite
remains in chapter 16, alongside the calendar’s emphasis on communal duties.

See [[Trumpets and the Day of Atonement]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:23–32; contextual references as cited.

### First fruits and harvest provision — Leviticus 23:9–22

A lamb with flour, oil, and wine accompanies the sheaf (12–13). The later bread comes
with seven lambs, a bull, and two rams as burnt offerings, a goat as sin offering, and
two lambs as peace offerings (18–20). The leavened loaves are waved and assigned to the
priest, not commanded to burn. The apparent tension with 2:11 is qualified by 2:12's
first-fruits exception; neither passage supports a ban on all holy leavened bread.

See [[First Fruits and the Feast of Weeks]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:9–22; contextual references as cited.


### Sabbath and the opening feasts — Leviticus 23:1–8

Offerings made by fire are commanded for all seven days of Unleavened Bread (8).
This calendar notice does not identify their animals, quantities, or officiants. It
should not be expanded into a detailed ritual by importing information silently.

See [[Sabbath, Passover, and Unleavened Bread]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:1–8; Exodus 12:16 where cited.


### Field and community — Leviticus 19:19–37

The man in the slave-woman case brings a ram as a trespass offering to the Tent of Meeting
(21–22). Fourth-year fruit is holy for praise (24), but the passage gives no detailed
fruit-offering rite.

See [[Holy Living in Field and Community]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19.


### Holiness and the neighbor — Leviticus 19:1–18

Peace offerings may be eaten on the offering day and the next; third-day remains are burned (5–8).
A tension in scope remains: this general wording gives the window of the vow/freewill case in
7:16–18, while thanksgiving in 7:15 has a same-day deadline. Chapter 19 does not explicitly repeal
that distinction.

See [[Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19:1–18.
See also [[Peace Offerings and Paid Vows - Proverbs 7.14 and Leviticus 7.16]].


### Sacrifice and blood — Leviticus 17:1–16

Livestock slaughter is brought to the Tent of Meeting as peace offerings (3–6);
this redirects field sacrifices to Yahweh and excludes goat idols (7). Hunted edible
animals have a distinct blood-disposal rule (13), not a command to bring them as offerings.

See [[The Place of Sacrifice and the Life in the Blood]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 17; comparisons as cited.


### Two goats and two burnt offerings — Leviticus 16:1–34

Aaron's bull and the people's two goats are sin offerings, with a ram each for burnt offerings
(3, 5, 24). One goat is killed; the other is presented alive and sent away (9–10, 20–22),
though verse 5 counts the pair as one sin offering. The carcasses whose blood entered the
Holy Place are burned outside the camp, as in Leviticus 4:11–12, 21 (27). The text does not
explain why the goats' roles are divided.

See [[The Day of Atonement]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 16; comparisons as cited.


### Bodily discharges — Leviticus 15:1–33

The male discharge and prolonged female discharge conclude with the same paired
bird offerings on the eighth day (14–15, 29–30). These are the standard provision here,
not an explicitly means-tested alternative as in Leviticus 12:8. Ordinary seminal
emission and menstruation receive no offering requirement in their respective sections.

See [[Bodily Discharges and Cleansing]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 15; comparisons as cited.

### Houses in the promised land — Leviticus 14:33–57

One bird is killed over running water and the living bird is released after the
cleansing rite (49–53). The house procedure names no sin, trespass, burnt, or meal
offering and no altar service. Those offerings in 14:10–32 belong to the restored
person’s distinct eighth-day procedure and must not be imported here.

See [[Mildew in a House]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 14:33–57; contextual references as cited.

### Cleansing after healing — Leviticus 14:1–32

The ordinary eighth-day provision has two male lambs, a year-old ewe lamb, three tenths of an
ephah of flour with oil, and a log of oil (10). The poor bring one male lamb, one tenth of flour
with oil, a log of oil, and two birds (21–22). The retained lamb is the trespass offering; birds
supply sin and burnt offerings.

See [[The Cleansing of the Leper]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 14:1–32; comparisons as cited.


### Purification after childbirth — Leviticus 12:1–8

After the purification days, the mother brings a year-old lamb for a burnt offering and
a pigeon or turtledove for a sin offering. If she cannot afford the lamb, two birds replace
the usual pair of offerings. Unlike 5:11–13, this chapter gives no flour alternative.

See [[Purification After Childbirth]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 12.


- **Genesis 3:21** — the coats of skins: not called a sacrifice, but a death that clothes.
- **Genesis 4:3–5** — the first offerings, uncommanded and unequally received; *minḥâ* used of
  both.
- **Genesis 8:20–21** — the first *altar* in Scripture, the first *burnt offering* (*ʿōlâ*, wholly
  consumed), and the first *rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ*, the **pleasant aroma**, which becomes the standing
  Levitical description of an accepted sacrifice. Four things set the terms here for everything
  after: nobody commanded it, it costs a seventh of every clean species alive, God's response is
  addressed to no one (*"Yahweh said in his heart"*), and the resolve it produces is grounded in
  human evil rather than human merit. See [[The Waters Recede]] and [[A Pleasing Aroma]].
- **Genesis 9:4** — the premise the whole sacrificial system will rest on, stated first as a dietary
  rule. *"Flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat"*: blood is life, and therefore
  not food. Leviticus 17:11 completes the thought with the other half — *"I have given it to you on
  the altar to make atonement for your souls"* — so the one thing that may never be eaten is the one
  thing that may be offered. Genesis 9 states the withholding centuries before the altar law explains
  what the withheld thing is for. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and [[Blood]].
- **Job 1:5** — Job sanctifies his children himself and offers a burnt offering for each, after
  every round of their feasting, on the hypothesis *"It may be that my sons have sinned, and
  renounced God in their hearts."* No altar is mentioned, no priest, no command, and no offence
  known to have occurred: the first offering in Scripture made for a **possible** sin, and one
  located in the heart, where no ritual system can reach it. The pattern closes at 42:8, where God
  sends the three friends to Job with their bulls and rams and accepts his prayer for them. See
  [[Job of Uz]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- **Genesis 12:7–8** — the first patriarchal altars, and the pattern they set. Abram builds at
  [[Shechem (city)]] *"to Yahweh, who had appeared to him"* — the altar answers a theophany — and again on
  the ridge by [[Bethel]], where he *"called on Yahweh's name"*. **Neither verse mentions a victim, a
  burning, or an aroma.** That silence is consistent across the patriarchal altars (12:7, 12:8,
  13:18, 26:25, 33:20) and is a sharp break from Noah at 8:20–21, where the whole point was what went
  up from it. Abram's altars mark ground and declare a name: they are claims staked, in a land the
  narrator has just said belongs to Canaanites, by a man who owns none of it. See
  [[The Call of Abram]] and [[The Altar at Shechem and the Covenant at Shechem]].
- **Genesis 15:9–17** — the theme's most instructive near-miss: everything sacrificial is present and
  no sacrifice occurs. **The five species are exactly the Levitical set** — *"a heifer three years
  old, a female goat three years old, a ram three years old, a turtledove, and a young pigeon"* —
  herd, flock, and the two birds that let a poor worshipper bring a pair where a rich one brings a
  bull (Leviticus 1; 5:7; 12:8). **The bird is not divided** (15:10), matching Leviticus 1:17's
  *"shall not divide it apart"* precisely. Fire arrives, at evening, and terror falls on the man.
  And yet: no altar is built, nothing is burned, no blood is applied, no aroma goes up, nobody eats,
  and no word from the sacrificial vocabulary appears. The animals are the **terms of an oath**, not
  gifts — halves laid opposite each other to make a corridor, and the self-curse of Jeremiah
  34:18–20 is the meaning of walking it. Genesis has the whole apparatus of Israelite sacrifice
  four centuries early and puts it to a completely different use. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] and
  [[Passing Between the Pieces]].
- **Genesis 22:1–19 — the Abraham cycle's only completed sacrifice, and the theme's hinge.** After
  three altars with nothing said to be offered on them, and a covenant ceremony with the whole
  apparatus and no offering, **this is the first burnt offering in Genesis since Noah's** — and it is
  the first one the book narrates step by step. Verse 9 runs the procedure Leviticus 1 will
  prescribe: the altar built, *"the wood in order"*, the victim bound and laid on it. Three things
  enter Scripture here. **The *ʿōlâ* asked of a human being** — the whole-offering demanded of a son,
  and then refused. **Substitution**: the ram offered *"instead of his son"*, *taḥat*, the first time
  one life is said to stand in the place of another. **And provision as the sacrifice's ground**:
  *"God will provide himself the lamb for a burnt offering"* (22:8), which the chapter answers with a
  ram rather than a lamb and turns into the name of a mountain. **The order matters** — the knife is
  stopped before the ram is seen, so the substitute does not buy the reprieve; it follows it. See
  [[The Binding of Isaac]] and [[The Ram Instead of the Son - Substitution at Moriah]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: Passover and the blood on the doorposts (Exodus 12);
  the five offerings and the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 1–7, 16); *"the life of the flesh is in
  the blood"* (Leviticus 17:11); *"to obey is better than sacrifice"* (1 Samuel 15:22) and the
  prophetic critique; Psalm 51:16–17 on a broken spirit; Isaiah 53 and the servant as offering;
  Hebrews 9–10 on a sacrifice that is not repeated; Romans 12:1 on bodies as living sacrifices.

### The altar law — Exodus 20:24–26

**The first law of sacrifice in Exodus concerns the altar, not the victim.** It is to be of earth,
or of stones on which no tool has been lifted, since a tool *"polluted"* it (*ḥālal*, WLC), and
there are to be no steps. It receives burnt offerings and peace offerings, sheep and cattle
(20:24–26). Deuteronomy 27:5–7 and Joshua 8:30–31 apply the law at Mount Ebal; see
[[An Altar of Uncut Stones - Exodus 20, Deuteronomy 27, and Joshua 8]]. **A tension is left in
place.** Exodus 27:1–8 prescribes a crafted altar of acacia wood and bronze for the tabernacle, and
the book does not say how the two provisions relate. See [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]].

**Exodus 22:29 forbids delay.** *"You shall not delay to offer from your harvest and from the
outflow of your presses"* — Hebrew *məlēʾātəkā wədimʿăkā*, literally *your fullness and your tear*,
which the WEB and the Septuagint both interpret as produce of field and press. The same verses give
the firstborn of sons and of animals to God. See [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

### Feasts, first fruits, and limits — Exodus 23:1–19

Three annual feasts require appearance before Yahweh; nobody is to appear empty (23:14–17). Blood is
not offered with leavened bread, festival fat does not remain overnight, and the first of the first
fruits is brought to God's house (23:18–19). The goat-and-milk prohibition follows with no
explanation. See [[Festivals and First Fruits]] and
[[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]].

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The covenant sacrifices — Exodus 24:4–8

Moses builds an altar *"under the mountain"* with twelve pillars, and *"young men of the children of
Israel"* offer burnt offerings and peace offerings of cattle (24:4–5). **No priest officiates**: the
Aaronic priesthood is not yet ordained (28–29), and Moses, not the young men, handles the blood. The
peace offerings (*zəbāḥîm šəlāmîm*, MAM) are the sacrifice whose meat is eaten (Leviticus 7:15), and the
leaders eat and drink before God at 24:11. See [[The Blood of the Covenant]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

### The altar, court, and maintained light — Exodus 27:1–21

The altar of Exodus 27:1–8 is acacia wood overlaid with brass, with horns, utensils, a grating,
and poles for carrying. Exodus 38:1 identifies the matching object as the altar of burnt
offering. No offering is performed in chapter 27. The difference from the earth or uncut-stone
altar of 20:24–26 remains explicit: Exodus gives both commands without explaining their
relationship. Filling the hollow frame with earth is a proposed reconciliation, not an
instruction stated in 27:8. See [[The Altar and the Court]] and
[[An Altar of Uncut Stones - Exodus 20, Deuteronomy 27, and Joshua 8]].

### The ordination of the priests — Exodus 29:1–46

One chapter prescribes the sin offering (29:14), the burnt offering (29:18), the ram of consecration
with its wave offering (29:22–26), the priests' portion from peace offerings (29:28), and the
twice-daily lamb with flour, oil, and wine (29:38–41). The sin offering's remains are burned
*"outside of the camp"*, the first such rule in Exodus. In the stored WEB, 29:14 is also the first
*"sin offering"* in Genesis and Exodus. The *"continual burnt offering"* (*ʿōlat tāmîd*, MAM) becomes
the base of Israel's worship. See [[The Ordination of the Priests]],
[[The Continual Burnt Offering - Exodus 29, Numbers 28, and Daniel 8]], and
[[Outside the Camp - The Sin Offering and Hebrews 13]].

### The incense altar, the ransom, and the holy oil — Exodus 30:1–38

The incense altar is an altar on which sacrifice is forbidden: no burnt offering, meal offering, or
drink offering, and no *"strange incense"* (30:9). Only the prescribed incense is burned, morning and
evening, and the blood of the sin offering touches its horns once a year (30:10). The census
half shekel is called *"an offering to Yahweh"*, *tərûmâ* (30:13, MAM), the word of the sanctuary
offering of 25:2, but unlike that offering it has a fixed amount. See
[[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

The feast at the calf includes burnt offerings and peace offerings (32:6), categories already used
at the covenant ratification (24:5). Familiar rites do not by themselves make the worship
faithful. Neither the offerings nor the feast prevent the indictment of 32:7–8. See [[They Sat Down to Eat and Drink - Exodus 32 in First Corinthians 10]]. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Sacrificial meals can draw the people into other worship (34:15), while appearance before Yahweh
must not be empty (34:20). Blood must not be offered with leavened bread; the Passover sacrifice
must not remain until morning; first fruits go to Yahweh's house (34:25–26). The chapter
prescribes these practices without narrating a sacrifice at the renewed ascent. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

Exodus 35:5, 21–29 carries out the willing contribution requested in 25:2–7. The gifts include
materials and the products of skilled hands; no sacrificial animal is killed here. No fixed amount
is assigned to each donor. This differs from the equal, required half-shekel payment of 30:11–16,
even though both support the Tent of Meeting. See
[[Willing Hearts - The Sanctuary, the Temple, and the Collection]].

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The makers and the surplus — Exodus 35:30–36:7

The offering continues every morning after the workers receive the accumulated materials
(36:3). Their report of excess prompts Moses to stop further contributions (36:4–6), and the
narrator confirms sufficiency and surplus (36:7). This is the end of a particular collection,
not a prohibition on future offerings. No sacrifice or disposal of excess material is narrated.
See [[Willing Hearts - The Sanctuary, the Temple, and the Collection]] and
[[Enough for the Work - Manna and the Sanctuary Offering]].

See [[Bezalel's Workmen and the Surplus]].

### The furnishings made — Exodus 37:1–29

The table's gold vessels for pouring (37:16), the incense altar (37:25–28), and the incense
(37:29) prepare for prescribed service. No bread is set out or incense burned here; those acts
appear in 40:23, 27. The gold incense altar remains distinct from the altar of burnt offering
whose making starts at 38:1. The report does not transfer the offerings prohibited in 30:9
to the incense altar.

See [[The Furnishings Made]]. Source: the cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-24.

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

The altar of burnt offering is made with horns, utensils, grating, and carrying poles (38:1–7). No
offering is burned on it here. Gold and brass are counted as contributed metal, while the silver is
explicitly tied to the half-shekel census levy (38:24–29). The altar law’s earth-or-stone provision
(20:24–25) and this crafted altar remain distinct; no earth filling is stated.

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The work completed — Exodus 39:32–43

The golden altar, sweet incense, bronze altar, and associated vessels are brought with
the completed work (39:38–39). This realizes the material preparation for worship without
narrating a sacrifice or incense offering. The willing gifts of 35:20–29 have become equipment;
Moses’ blessing of the people does not say those gifts purchased pardon for the calf.

See [[The Work Completed]]. Source: cited passages checked in the stored WEB Genesis and Exodus
texts, 2026-10-25.

### The tabernacle raised and filled — Exodus 40:1–38

The furniture moves into use: bread is arranged (40:23), incense burned at the golden
altar (40:26–27), and burnt and meal offerings offered at the outer altar (40:29).
The narrator attributes these acts to Moses. The chapter does not give animals, amounts,
or a separate atonement rite for these offerings, and the incense altar is not confused
with the altar of burnt offering.

See [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. Source: Exodus 40 checked in `raw/bible/Exodus.md` (WEB), 2026-10-25.

**The strange woman opens with a sacrifice.** *"Sacrifices of peace offerings are with me. Today I
have paid my vows"* (Proverbs 7:14). The peace offering was the sacrifice the worshipper ate, and
Leviticus 7:16 limits a vow offering's meat to the day of sacrifice and the next, so her invitation
is to a feast that cannot wait. The worship is real or a pretext — the text does not say — and in
either case it is the first line of a seduction. See [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] and
[[Peace Offerings and Paid Vows - Proverbs 7.14 and Leviticus 7.16]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 7;

### The wicked man's sacrifice — Proverbs 15:8

*"The sacrifice made by the wicked is an abomination to Yahweh, but the prayer of the upright is his delight"* (8). The verdict falls on the worshipper, not the form — verse 9 names the wicked man's *way* as the abomination — and Proverbs sharpens it at 21:3, 27. Job 42:8–9 is a narrated case: the friends' burnt offering accepted through Job's prayer. See [[The Prayer of the Upright - Proverbs 15.8, 29 and Job 42.8]].

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### Atonement without an offering — Proverbs 16:6

Proverbs 16:6 names the means of atonement as *"mercy and truth"*, not an offering, and 21:3 will say *"To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to Yahweh than sacrifice."* Neither verse abolishes sacrifice; both, with 15:8, measure it by the worshipper.

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Sacrifices of strife — Proverbs 17:1

The WEB's *"a house full of feasting with strife"* translates *zibḥê-rîb*, *sacrifices of strife* (Hebrew from Sefaria): the *zebaḥ* meal of 7:14's *"Sacrifices of peace offerings"*, eaten in a quarrelling house. A dry morsel in quiet is better. As at 15:8 and 16:6, the sacrifice is not what makes the house good.

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### More acceptable than sacrifice — Proverbs 21:3, 27

Righteousness and justice are more acceptable than sacrifice (3), and the wicked man's sacrifice is
an abomination, more so when brought with wicked intent (27), intensifying 15:8. The chapter ranks
conduct over offering without abolishing offering. See
[[Justice More Than Sacrifice - Proverbs 21.3, 1 Samuel 15.22, and Hosea 6.6]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### The sacrifice of fools — Ecclesiastes 5:1

*"To draw near to listen is better than to give the sacrifice of fools, for they don’t know that
they do evil"* (1). Like Proverbs 21:3, 27 and 1 Samuel 15:22, the verse ranks rather than
abolishes: sacrifice is assumed, and the fault lies in the offerer who brings it without
listening. See [[Justice More Than Sacrifice - Proverbs 21.3, 1 Samuel 15.22, and Hosea 6.6]].

See [[Let Your Words Be Few]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:1–7; the passage page records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

The person who sacrifices and the person who does not share the same event (2). No
specific offering or sanctuary is named. Mortality is the point of comparison, not a
new ruling that all offerings are worthless; the obligation to listen in 5:1 remains.

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### No blemish in you — Song of Solomon 4:7

*"You are all beautiful, my love. There is no spot in you"* (4:7). The word for *spot* is *mûm*
(Sefaria, *Miqra according to the Masorah*), the *defect* that bars a priest from the altar and an
animal from being offered in Leviticus 21:17–18 and 22:20–21 (WEB checked at bible-api.com;
Leviticus not fetched). The Song uses it as praise of a body, as 2 Samuel 14:25 does of Absalom. It
does not mention an altar or an offering, and the wiki does not read a sacrificial meaning into the
verse. See [[No Blemish in You - Song of Solomon 4.7, Leviticus 22.21, and 2 Samuel 14.25]].

See [[You Are All Beautiful]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 4:1–7; comparison sources are
identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The burnt offering — Leviticus 1:1–17

The burnt offering has three forms: cattle, sheep or goats, and turtledoves or young pigeons. Each
ends in the same pleasant-aroma description (1:9, 13, 17). Burning the whole offering is not a claim
that every removed part reaches the fire: the cattle are flayed (6), and the bird’s crop with its
filth is discarded (16). No meal for the worshipper is prescribed.

See [[The Burnt Offering]] . Source: stored WEB Leviticus 1; comparisons identified on the passage
page.

### The meal offering — Leviticus 2

Grain, oil, and prescribed preparations are accepted offerings (2:1–10). A memorial is burned and the remainder belongs to the priests. This prevents treating all acceptable offerings as animal victims or wholly burned gifts. See [[The Meal Offering]].

### The peace offering — Leviticus 3

The peace offering permits male or female cattle and flock animals without defect (3:1, 6). Specified fat and organs, including the lamb's tail fat, are burned; this differs from the whole burnt offering and the grain memorial. Chapter 7 supplies the meat-sharing regulations, not chapter 3.

See [[The Peace Offering]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 3; contextual comparisons documented on the passage page.

### Priest and congregation — Leviticus 4:1–21

The priest and congregation each bring a bull; the elders represent the assembly in hand-laying
(15). Fat is burned on the altar as in the peace offering (8–10), but the carcass is burned
outside the camp (11–12, 21). These are distinct fires and destinations, not a whole burnt
offering on the altar.

See [[The Sin Offering for Priest and Congregation]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 4:1–21;
contextual comparisons documented on the passage page.

### Ruler and individual — Leviticus 4:22–35

A male goat is specified for the ruler, a female goat or lamb for the ordinary person
(23, 28, 32). Fat follows the peace-offering pattern, but these are sin offerings with
explicit forgiveness, not peace offerings or whole burnt offerings.

See [[The Sin Offering for Ruler and Individual]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 4:22–35; contextual
comparisons documented on the passage page.

### Confession and means — Leviticus 5:1–13

A female flock animal, two birds with different functions, or fine flour serve the stated cases according to means (6–13).

See [[Confession and Offerings According to Means]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:1–13.

### Holy things and unknown guilt — Leviticus 5:14–19

Both cases prescribe a ram without defect as a trespass offering. Unlike 5:7–13, this unit supplies no bird or flour alternative; it does not repeat the slaughter or blood-handling directions.

See [[The Trespass Offering for Holy Things and Unknown Guilt]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:14–19.

### Fraud, restitution, and forgiveness — Leviticus 6:1–7

The offender brings a ram without defect, according to valuation, as a trespass offering (6). No
cheaper alternative, slaughter procedure, or blood rite is specified in this unit; the offering
does not replace repayment.

See [[Restitution for Fraud and False Oaths]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6:1–7; contextual comparisons documented there.

### Altar fire and priestly portions — Leviticus 6:8–23

The burnt offering remains on the hearth overnight, with fresh wood each morning. The meal offering has an eaten remainder, but every priest’s meal offering is wholly burned (23). The earlier remainder rule must not be generalized beyond this explicit exception.

See [[The Altar Fire and the Priests’ Meal Offerings]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6:8–23; comparisons documented there.

### Holy flesh and priestly portions — Leviticus 6:24–7:10

Sin and trespass offerings provide holy food, except that sin offerings whose blood enters the
tent for atonement must be burned (6:30). Burnt-offering skins belong to the priest (7:8). Grain
allocations in 7:9–10 retain the wholly burned priestly-offering exception of 6:23.

See [[The Priests’ Portions of Sin and Trespass Offerings]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6:24–7:10.

### Thanksgiving and the peace-offering meal — Leviticus 7:11–21

Thanksgiving meat must be eaten the same day; vow and freewill meat may also be eaten
the next day. Third-day eating invalidates acceptance and brings iniquity (7:15–18).
The shared meal remains an offering belonging to Yahweh, not unrestricted leftovers.

See [[Thanksgiving, Vows, and the Peace Offering]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6–7.

### Fat, blood, and priestly shares — Leviticus 7:22–38

The donor personally brings fat and breast; the fat burns, the breast goes to Aaron and his
sons, and the right thigh to the officiant (30–34). The summary of offering laws includes
consecration and locates the commands at Sinai (37–38).

See [[Fat, Blood, and the Priests’ Share]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 7:22–38.

### Aaron and his sons consecrated — Leviticus 8:1–36

The bull sin offering, whole burnt ram, and consecration ram have distinct treatments.
The ordination thigh burns and Moses receives the breast (25–29), unlike the ongoing
peace-offering portions in 7:31–34. The different rites explain the apparent portion conflict.

See [[Aaron and His Sons Consecrated]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 8; Exodus 28:1; 29; Leviticus 7:38.

### The eighth-day service — Leviticus 9

The inaugural service coordinates sin, burnt, meal, and peace offerings, besides the morning burnt
offering (9:8–21). Fire from before Yahweh consumes the burnt offering and fat after the narrated
burning actions (24). See [[The Eighth Day and the Appearing Glory]] .

### The eighth-day offerings completed — Leviticus 10:12–20

The meal offering (9:17), the peace-offering breast and thigh (9:21), and the people's goat (9:15) are disposed of after the deaths. Moses' complaint applies the eat-or-burn rule of 6:26–30 to the goat. See [[Eaten or Burned - The Sin Offering in Leviticus 6, 9, and 10]].

See [[The Uneaten Sin Offering]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 10:12–20.

  exactly that: *"where you anointed a pillar."* The law will later hate standing stones outright
  (Deuteronomy 16:22), which is a tension this wiki records without resolving; see
  [[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]]
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:54; ***wayyizbaḥ yaʿăqōb zebaḥ*** — **the first *zebaḥ*
  in Scripture**, noun and verb, and **the first covenant meal in the Bible.** Every offering Genesis
  has recorded until now goes up whole — Noah's *ʿōlâ* (8:20), the ram at Moriah (22:13) — **and this
  one is eaten**: *"called his relatives to eat bread. They ate bread, and stayed all night in the
  mountain."* **There is no altar named, no priest, and no stated recipient**; what the rite ratifies
  is an agreement between two men. **His grandfather's covenant was cut with halved animals and
  fire** (15:9–17); **this one is dinner**
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:1–3, 14; **an altar commanded rather
  than volunteered, and the Bible's first drink offering.** Every patriarchal altar before this one is
  built on the builder's own initiative after a theophany; **here God orders it in advance** —
  *"Make there an altar to God, who appeared to you when you fled"* — which is the pattern the
  Levitical system will run on. Then ***nesek***: *"He poured out a drink offering on it"* (35:14),
  **the word's first occurrence in Scripture**, poured by a herdsman onto a standing stone with no
  priest, no tabernacle, and nothing said about what was in it. The law will make it a fixed adjunct
  to the burnt offering, always wine, always priestly (Exodus 29:40; Numbers 15:5–10). See
  [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]]
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:1; ***"came to Beersheba, and offered sacrifices

  question.** **[[Jacob]] has already decided to go** (45:28); **he stops at the border town and
  sacrifices to the God who forbade this journey to [[Isaac]]** (26:2), **and the answer comes that

  it is the only sacrifice in the book immediately followed by a theophany**

- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:1–28; **the first sacrifice Exodus prescribes, and it is offered in
