# Oaths and Vows

## Definition / overview

Binding yourself with words — and in the Bible, two different acts that English blurs together.

An **oath** (*šebûʿâ*, from the verb *nišbaʿ*) calls God to witness that a statement is true or that
a commitment will be kept; it is sworn to a person and invokes a sanction. A **vow** (*neder*) is a
promise made to God, usually conditional: *if you do this, I will do that*. The first oath in
Scripture is Abram's, and the first vow is Jacob's, and both are made by men with nothing in hand.

*"Abram said to the king of Sodom, 'I have lifted up my hand to Yahweh, God Most High, possessor of
heaven and earth, that I will not take a thread nor a sandal strap nor anything that is yours'"*
(Genesis 14:22–23). See [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].

**Three things about that first oath are worth keeping.**

- **The gesture is literal.** *I have lifted up my hand* is the physical form of swearing, not a
  metaphor for it; the same idiom is used of God himself in Deuteronomy 32:40 (*"I lift up my hand
  to heaven and declare, as I live forever"*) and Ezekiel 20:5.
- **The tense means he had already decided.** The perfect — *I have lifted up my hand* — puts the
  oath before the conversation. He did not weigh the king of Sodom's offer and decline it; he had
  ruled it out in advance, and the speech is a report of a decision, not the making of one.
- **He swears in order to refuse.** The Bible's first oath is not a promise to give something but a
  binding of himself against receiving. And he immediately declines to extend it to anyone who did
  not take it: *"the portion of the men who went with me: Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre. Let them take
  their portion"* (14:24).

## Development across Scripture

- **God's first oath is sworn without words, at Genesis 15:17.** Before the *"I have sworn by
  myself"* formula there is a ceremony: five animals halved, a corridor between them, and *"a
  smoking furnace, and a flaming torch"* passing down it while Abram sleeps. Jeremiah 34:18–20
  explains what walking that corridor means — *may I be made like these if I break my word* — so
  Genesis 15 is a **self-maledictory oath performed rather than spoken**, and the party who swears
  it is the one for whom the sanction cannot apply. It is the strongest form of oath the ancient
  world had, and the human party takes none of it. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] and
  [[Passing Between the Pieces]].
- **Then he swears by himself.** *"I have sworn by myself, says Yahweh"* (Genesis 22:16), the
  formula Hebrews 6:13–18 builds on: *"since he could swear by none greater, he swore by himself."*
  Divine oaths in Scripture are what make a promise unconditional.
- **The patriarchs swear constantly, and the gestures are physical.** Abraham and Abimelech at
  Beersheba, where the well and the town are named for the oath (Genesis 21:22–34); the servant with
  his hand under Abraham's thigh (24:2–9), which Jacob repeats with Joseph (47:29–31); Jacob and
  Laban at the heap of stones (31:44–54); Joseph's brothers swearing to carry his bones (50:25).
- **Jacob's vow is the model conditional one.** *"If God will be with me … and Yahweh will be my
  God … Of all that you will give me I will surely give a tenth to you"* (Genesis 28:20–22). It is
  a bargain, and Scripture does not rebuke it. See [[Tithing]].
- **The law regulates rather than discourages.** A vow is optional, but once made it binds:
  *"When you vow a vow to God, don't defer to pay it … It is better that you should not vow, than
  that you should vow and not pay"* (Ecclesiastes 5:4–5; compare Deuteronomy 23:21–23). Numbers 30
  governs women's vows within a household; Leviticus 27 sets redemption values; Numbers 6 defines
  the Nazirite vow. And the third commandment guards the misuse of the name an oath invokes
  (Exodus 20:7).
- **Two vows in the histories go badly.** Jephthah's, which he keeps at the cost of his daughter
  (Judges 11:30–40), and Saul's battlefield curse, which nearly kills Jonathan over a taste of honey
  (1 Samuel 14:24–45). Both are narrated as disasters produced by a rash word, and Proverbs 20:25
  states the moral: *"It is a snare to a man to make a rash dedication."*
- **Jesus cuts underneath the whole institution.** *"Don't swear at all … But let your 'Yes' be
  'Yes' and your 'No' be 'No'"* (Matthew 5:33–37; James 5:12), with Matthew 23:16–22 attacking the
  scribal grading of oaths by what they swore on. The traditions divide on whether this abolishes
  oaths (the Anabaptist and Quaker reading) or forbids the evasive use of them (the majority
  reading, which notes that Paul calls God as witness at Romans 1:9 and 2 Corinthians 1:23, and that
  Hebrews treats God's own oath as exemplary). The wiki records both.

## Key passages

- Genesis 14:22–23 — the first human oath, [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]]
- Genesis 15:9–18 — the first divine oath, enacted rather than spoken,
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]]
- Jeremiah 34:18–20 — the only passage that explains the rite, and the sanction being executed
- Genesis 22:16; Hebrews 6:13–18 — God swearing by himself
- Genesis 21:22–34; 24:2–9; 31:44–54; 47:29–31 — the patriarchal oaths and their gestures
- Genesis 28:20–22 — Jacob's vow
- Exodus 20:7; Leviticus 19:12 — the name, and false swearing by it
- Numbers 6:1–21; 30:1–16; Leviticus 27 — Nazirite vows, household vows, redemption values
- Deuteronomy 23:21–23; Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 — pay what you vow
- Judges 11:30–40; 1 Samuel 14:24–45 — the rash vow
- Matthew 5:33–37; 23:16–22; James 5:12 — let your yes be yes
