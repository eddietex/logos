# The Nations

## Definition / overview

### Crossing the Jordan — Joshua 3:1–17

Canaanites, Hittites, Hivites, Perizzites, Girgashites, Amorites, and Jebusites are named in the promised expulsion (10). The passage gives no individual encounter with them, and no battle takes place here.

See [[Crossing the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 3:1–17.


### Rahab and the spies — Joshua 2:1–24

A woman within threatened Jericho confesses Yahweh’s rule in heaven and earth and seeks protection under an Israelite oath (9–14). The story distinguishes her response from the king’s pursuit rather than treating all inhabitants as a single moral actor.

See [[Rahab and the Spies]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 2:1–24.

### The Song of Moses — Deuteronomy 31:30–32:47

The nations receive bounds (8), a foolish nation provokes Israel (21), enemies might claim divine victory as their own (27), and nations are finally invited to rejoice with his people (43). No particular later empire is named.

See [[The Song of Moses]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:30–32:47.


### Return and the circumcised heart — Deuteronomy 30:1–10

Israel is driven *among all the nations* (1) and gathered *from all the peoples* (3). The curses pass to Israel's enemies (7); the nations here are places of exile and sources of persecution, not observers as in 29:24.

See [[Return Restoration and the Circumcised Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 30:1–10.

### The covenant in Moab — Deuteronomy 29:1–29

Israel has encountered other nations’ idols (16–18); later nations ask why Yahweh devastated Israel’s land (24–28). Their question receives a covenant explanation rather than an account of Yahweh’s defeat.

See [[The Covenant in Moab and the Hidden Things]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 29:1–29.

### Covenant sanctions — Deuteronomy 28:15–68

An unnamed distant nation invades, the king is deported, and Israel becomes a byword among peoples (36–37, 49–50, 64). The text does not identify a particular later empire, so no identification is imposed here.

See [[Curses for Disobedience]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 28:15–68.


### Blessings in the land — Deuteronomy 28:1–14

Israel is promised elevation, recognition as bearing Yahweh’s name, and freedom from dependence on foreign lenders (1, 10, 12–13). The fear of other peoples is a promised consequence, not an instruction to exploit them.

See [[Blessings for Obedience (Deuteronomy)]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 28:1–14.

### Belonging and obedience — Deuteronomy 26:16–19

Israel is promised elevation in praise, name, and honor above the nations Yahweh has made
(19). The comparison asserts a distinctive status while recognizing his creation of the other
peoples. Read with 10:14–17, election and judicial impartiality remain alongside one another;
this verse offers no permission to treat other peoples unjustly.

See [[A People for Yahweh and a God for Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 26:16–19.

### The nokrî and interest — Deuteronomy 23:20

*"You may lend on interest to a foreigner"*. The word is *nokrî* (WLC 23:21, morphhb), the foreigner
of 14:21, 15:3, and 17:15, not the resident *gēr* whom Deuteronomy protects. The verse gives no reason
for the permission beyond the contrast with the brother. See [[Fugitives, Interest, Vows, and a Neighbor's Produce]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 23:15–25.

### Who may enter the assembly — Deuteronomy 23:3–8

Four nations, sorted by their history with Israel. The Ammonite and the Moabite are excluded from
Yahweh's assembly *"even to the tenth generation … forever"*, for not meeting Israel with bread and
water and for hiring Balaam (3–4), and Israel is not to *"seek their peace nor their prosperity"* (6).
The Edomite is not to be abhorred, *"for he is your brother"*, and the Egyptian, *"because you lived as
a foreigner in his land"*; their children enter in the third generation (7–8). The same book protects
Ammon's and Moab's land (2:9, 19). Isaiah 56:3–7 later promises the foreigner who holds fast to the
covenant a place in Yahweh's house. See
[[The Eunuch and the Foreigner - Deuteronomy 23.1-8 and Isaiah 56.3-7]].

See [[The Assembly and the Holy Camp]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 23:1–14; 2:9, 19; Isaiah 56:3–7
checked at bible-api.com (WEB).

### Possession without merit — Deuteronomy 9:1–6

The nations are stronger than Israel, with fortified cities and the tall Anakim (1–2). Their wickedness explains expulsion (4–5); the unit names no new nation and supplies no list of specific offenses.

See [[Not Because of Your Righteousness]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 9:1–6.


### Wilderness and wealth — Deuteronomy 8:1–20

The destruction of the nations becomes a warning to Israel itself (20). The comparison concerns
shared liability to judgment, not a new list of peoples or a newly narrated conquest.

See [[Remember the Wilderness and the Giver of Wealth]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 8:1–20.


### Heard and lived — Deuteronomy 4:32–40

No nation has heard God and lived, and no god has taken a nation out of another nation (33–34). Nations *greater and mightier than you* are driven out so that Israel can have their land (38).

See [[Has Any People Heard God and Lived]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:32–40.

### No form and no image — Deuteronomy 4:15–31

The sun, moon, and stars are what Yahweh *has allotted to all the peoples under the whole sky* (19), set against Israel as his *people of inheritance* (20); compare Deuteronomy 32:8–9 (stored WEB, not yet ingested). Whether the allotment means the lights as lights or a worship permitted to the nations is not settled by the verse. Exile will scatter Israel *among the peoples* and leave it *few in number among the nations* (27).

See [[No Form Seen and No Image Made]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:15–31.

### Statutes and Horeb — Deuteronomy 4:1–14

The peoples are imagined as an audience that hears Israel's statutes and admires them (6). No great nation has a god so near, or a law so righteous (7–8). The nations' admiration is anticipated, not narrated.

See [[Hear the Statutes and Remember Horeb]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:1–14.

### Og’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 3:1–11

The two kings are identified as Amorite rulers (8), while Sidonians and Amorites supply different
names for Hermon (9). Og’s Rephaim identity (11) accompanies his political identity as king of
Bashan; the text does not explain a genealogy relating these designations.

See [[The Defeat of Og Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:1–11.

### Sihon’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 2:24–37

The promised dread extends to peoples under the whole sky (25), while the concrete battle concerns
Sihon. The passage does not narrate the conquest of every nation; Moab, Edom, and Ammon retain
distinct roles and limits.

See [[The Defeat of Sihon Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:24–37.

### Protected neighbors and the wilderness generation — Deuteronomy 2:1–23

Esau’s and Lot’s descendants hold protected grants (5, 9, 19). The recollections of earlier
populations and their displacement extend the account beyond Israel; Israel’s own grant is not
permission to seize every neighbor’s land.

See [[Around Seir and Past Moab and Ammon]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:1–23.

### Drive out the inhabitants — Numbers 33:50–56

The inhabitants of Canaan are collectively marked for removal (52), without a list of named
peoples. Israel is not exempt from the fate threatened against them (56). This is the speech’s
theological framing, not independent evidence about each inhabitant’s conduct.

See [[Drive Out the Inhabitants]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 33:50–56.

### Foreigners and acceptable offerings — Leviticus 22:17–33

Foreigners in Israel may bring burnt offerings under the same no-defect rule (18), as at Leviticus
17:8. Yet Israel must not offer "the bread of your God from the hand of a foreigner as any of these",
because "there is a defect in them" (25). In the WEB context the stated reason is the animals'
defect; the two verses are kept together rather than reduced to inclusion or exclusion alone.

See [[Acceptable Victims and the Holy Name]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 22:17–33.



### Penalties and separation — Leviticus 20

Israel must not imitate the nation being expelled and is set apart to belong to Yahweh (23–26).
This is the law’s theological account of the inhabitants’ conduct, not independent evidence about
every person in that land.

See [[Penalties and a People Set Apart]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 20; comparisons as cited.


*Gôyim* — the peoples of the earth, taken together as a category. Everything the Bible says about
them assumes the list in Genesis 10.

The theme begins with a claim that is easy to skate over because it is stated as a genealogy rather
than as a doctrine: **there is one human family and every people belongs to it.** Seventy nations,
one ancestor, no ranking, and no separate creation for anybody. [[The Table of Nations]] puts Israel's
own line in the list as one entry among seventy, gives the largest and most detailed branch to
Israel's enemies, and draws borders for exactly one of them — the land Israel does not yet have. Paul
states the same claim as doctrine in Athens: *"He made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on
all the surface of the earth, having determined appointed seasons and the boundaries of their
dwellings"* (Acts 17:26).

**Seventy is the number, and it is used deliberately.** Fourteen from Japheth, thirty from Ham,
twenty-six from Shem. Scripture reaches for seventy whenever it wants to say *all of them, in an
order*: seventy of Jacob's house go down into Egypt (Genesis 46:27; Exodus 1:5; Deuteronomy 10:22),
seventy elders eat before God on the mountain (Exodus 24:1, 9) and receive the spirit (Numbers
11:16–25), seventy bulls are offered across the seven days of Sukkot (Numbers 29:12–34), and seventy
are sent out two by two in Luke 10:1. Deuteronomy 32:8 puts the first two of those in a single
sentence. See [[Seventy Nations - Genesis 10 in Deuteronomy 32 and Luke 10]].

**The tension the theme carries.** Genesis 10 is a peaceful, orderly account of the earth filling up,
and Genesis 11 immediately reframes the same fact as a judgment: the nations are scattered because
they tried not to be. So *the nations* are, from the start, both the fulfilment of a blessing
(*"Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the earth"*, 9:1, reported done at 10:32) and the residue
of a punishment. The Bible never picks one. It resolves the tension instead by making the nations the
object of the promise: three verses after Babel's aftermath, Abram is called, and the reason given is
*"All the families of the earth will be blessed through you"* — using Genesis 10's own word,
*mišpəḥōt*, families. The election of one family exists for the seventy.

**Exodus 19:5–6 places Israel among peoples whose earth already belongs to Yahweh.** Particular
possession does not deny divine ownership of other peoples. Israel is addressed as a holy
nation and priestly kingdom, giving its distinction a vocation rather than a claim that it
alone exists under God's rule. The scope of that priestly role is not spelled out here. See
[[Israel at Mount Sinai]].

## Development across Scripture

### Lender to many nations — Deuteronomy 15:1–11

*"You will lend to many nations, but you will not borrow. You will rule over many nations, but they will not rule over you"* (15:6). Within Israel the foreigner (*nokrî*) may be required to repay (3; WLC, morphhb), and the release is for the brother. The promise recurs at 28:12 and is reversed at 28:44.

See [[The Year of Release and the Open Hand]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 15:1–11.

### Above all peoples — Deuteronomy 14:2, 21

Israel is chosen *"above all peoples who are on the face of the earth"* (14:2), and the food law marks the difference: what dies of itself may be sold to a foreigner (21). Acts 10:28 later reads Peter's vision of unclean animals as being about people.

See [[A Holy People and Its Food]] and [[All Foods Clean - Leviticus 11, Deuteronomy 14, Mark 7, and Acts 10]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 14:2, 21; Acts 10:28 verified from bible-api.com (WEB).

### Do not ask how they served their gods — Deuteronomy 12:29–13:18

After Yahweh cuts off the nations, Israel must not be *"ensnared to follow them"* or ask how they served their gods (12:29–30), because they did every abomination Yahweh hates (12:31). The gods of *"the peoples who are around you, near to you, or far off"* are the enticer's offer (13:7).

See [[Do Not Follow Other Gods]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 12:29–13:18.

### Nations greater and mightier — Deuteronomy 11:18–32

Yahweh will drive out *"nations greater and mightier than yourselves"* (23) and lay the fear and dread of Israel on the land (25), on condition of Israel's obedience (22). The curse is going after *"other gods, which you have not known"* (28).

See [[Set These Words Before You]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 11:18–32.

### Blessing and removal — Deuteronomy 7:12–26

The nations Israel fears are *more than I* (17), but Yahweh will do to them what he did to Egypt
(19). They are removed gradually (22), their kings delivered and their name blotted out (24). The
diseases of Egypt fall on *all those who hate you* (15).

See [[Blessing and the Removal of the Nations]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:12–26.

### Chosen in love — Deuteronomy 7:1–11

Hittite, Girgashite, Amorite, Canaanite, Perizzite, Hivite, and Jebusite form the seven-nation
roster (1). They are described as greater and mightier than Israel. The collective list defines
the targets of the commands; no individual representative is named.

See [[A People Chosen in Love]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:1–11.

### Balaam’s last oracles — Numbers 23:27–24:25

Moab, Edom, Amalek, the Kenite, Asshur, and Eber enter the oracle’s horizon, with ships from Kittim
(24:17–24). Their exact historical referents are not all supplied, and an unqualified map of
fulfillment would exceed this text.

See [[Balaam's Last Oracles]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 23:27–24:25.


### The first two oracles — Numbers 22:36–23:26

Israel is depicted as dwelling alone and not being listed among the nations (23:9). The oracle
asserts a distinct status; it does not claim that Israel lacks relations or conflicts with
neighboring peoples.

See [[Balaam's First Two Oracles]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 22:36–23:26.


### Balak’s embassies — Numbers 22:1–21

Moab and Midian cooperate against Israel through an embassy (4–7). God speaks to Balaam outside
Israel’s camp and protects Israel’s blessing there; the people do not participate in the
negotiations.

See [[Balak Sends for Balaam]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 22:1–21.

### Covenant warnings and remembered promises — Leviticus 26:14–46

The nations are both exile and audience: Israel is scattered among them and perishes among them (33,
38), and the exodus happened *in the sight of the nations* (45).

See [[Covenant Warnings and Remembered Promises]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Poor brother and service — Leviticus 25:35–55

*The nations that are around you* are a permitted source of slaves (44), with the families of
resident aliens (45). The status is permanent and heritable (46). Israelites may be sold to an
alien living in the land, but only on hired-servant terms (47–54).

See [[The Poor Brother and Redemption from Service]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 25.

### Forbidden unions and the land — Leviticus 18:1–30

Egypt and Canaan supply practices Israel must not imitate (3). The nations’ conduct explains expulsion, but Israel receives the same warning (24–28). This is the text’s theological portrayal, not independent ethnographic evidence about every inhabitant.

See [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18.

- **Genesis 9:1, 9:7; 10:32** — the command to fill the earth, and the report that it was done. The
  only command of God in the primeval history that is simply carried out.
- **Genesis 10:1–32** — the seventy: one ancestry for all of them, organized by families, languages,
  lands, and nations, and never by rank. See [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the same dispersal narrated as an event: one people on one plain, building so
  as to avoid being *"scattered abroad on the surface of the whole earth"*, and scattered anyway. The
  nations become something God did rather than something that happened. See [[The Tower of Babel]].
- **Genesis 12:1–3** *(not yet ingested)* — *"All the families of the earth will be blessed through
  you"*: the answer to both chapters, and the hinge of the book.
- **Deuteronomy 32:8** — the bounds of the peoples set at the division of humanity, *"according to the
  number of the children of Israel"* in the Masoretic Text and *"according to the number of the sons
  of God"* in the Septuagint and at Qumran.
- **1 Kings 8:41–43; Isaiah 56:6–8** — the foreigner who comes to the temple, and a house *"called a
  house of prayer for all peoples"*.
- **Isaiah 2:2–4; Micah 4:1–3** — all nations flowing to the mountain of Yahweh's house.
- **Isaiah 19:23–25** — the hardest verse on the theme in the Old Testament: Egypt called *"my
  people"*, Assyria *"the work of my hands"*, alongside Israel.
- **Jonah** — a prophet sent to a nation, furious that it is spared.
- **Psalm 87** — Rahab, Babylon, Philistia, Tyre, and Cush registered as born in Zion.
- **Acts 2:5–11** — *"devout men, from every nation under the sky"*, each hearing in his own language:
  the languages of Babel not undone but made transparent. See
  [[The Table of Nations at Pentecost]].
- **Genesis 11:10–32** — the narrowing. Having spread seventy peoples across the map and scattered
  them, Genesis pulls one thread out of it and follows a single household from Shem to Abram. The
  seventy are not mentioned again for a chapter, and when they return at 12:3 they are the object of
  a blessing: *"in you all the families of the earth will be blessed."* The particular line exists
  for the sake of the general map, and this register is where the book turns from one to the other.
  See [[From Shem to Abram]].
- **Genesis 12:1–3** — and the narrowing turns out to be aimed outward. One man is called out of the
  seventy peoples, and the last clause of the promise names them as the point: *wənibrəkû bəkā kōl
  mišpəḥōt hāʾădāmâ*, *"All the families of the earth will be blessed through you"* — using
  *mišpāḥâ*, the Table of Nations' own organising word (10:5, 18, 20, 31, 32). Two things follow.
  Election in Genesis is instrumental from its first sentence: the choice of one household is stated
  as a mechanism for everybody else, not as a preference over them. And the promise is aimed at the
  primeval history's two casualties at once — the scattered *families*, and the *ʾădāmâ*, the ground
  cursed at 3:17. Whether the nations *are blessed* or *bless themselves* by Abram is a live
  grammatical question and the New Testament settles it in one direction; see
  [[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]] and [[The Call of Abram]].
- **Acts 17:26; Romans 15:8–12; Galatians 3:8** — the theme argued as doctrine, and Genesis 12:3 read
  as *"the Good News beforehand to Abraham"*.
- **Revelation 5:9; 7:9; 14:6; 21:24–26** — *"every tribe, language, people, and nation"*, the
  fourfold formula that is Genesis 10:5, 20, and 31 turned into worship, and the kings of the earth
  bringing their glory into the city.

### Six peoples, and their gods — Exodus 23:20–33

The Amorite, Hittite, Perizzite, Canaanite, Hivite, and Jebusite (23:23) — the six of the bush
(3:8) in another order — are to be *"cut off"*, driven out little by little, and refused covenant or
residence (23:23, 29–33). The reason given each time is their gods (23:24, 33). The commands are
specific to these peoples and this land. The wiki does not read them as a general stance toward
other nations, which Genesis's Table of Nations and blessing for *"all the families of the earth"*
(12:3) would not support. See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

The Canaanite, Amorite, Hittite, Perizzite, Hivite, and Jebusite are again named for removal (33:2;
compare 23:23). They are peoples, not six individual actors. Moses then asks how Israel will be
distinguished from all other peoples and answers with divine accompaniment (33:16), not merely the
possession of their land. See [[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

The Amorite, Canaanite, Hittite, Perizzite, Hivite, and Jebusite are named for removal (34:11).
The following treaty and marriage warnings specify the risk of being drawn into their sacrifices
and gods (34:12–16). These commands address Israel's entry into this land; they should not be
turned without argument into a timeless prohibition of contact with every foreign people. The
promise is future, not a report of conquest completed. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### Righteousness exalts a nation — Proverbs 14:34

*"Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is a disgrace to any people"* (34) — the collection's first saying about peoples, applying its moral order to every nation and not to Israel alone.

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### One statute for the stranger — Numbers 9:14

The stranger who keeps the Passover does so by the same statute as the native, *ḥuqqâ ʾaḥat* (WLC). See [[One Law for the Native and the Stranger]].

See [[The Passover in the Second Year]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 9.

### An invitation to a Midianite — Numbers 10:29–32

Moses offers an outsider the good Yahweh has promised Israel, without any stated condition. Whether Hobab accepts is not recorded. See [[Hobab and the Kenites - Numbers 10, Judges 1 and 4, and 1 Samuel 15]].

See [[The March from Sinai]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 10.

### The Cushite wife — Numbers 12:1

Two chapters after Hobab the Midianite is invited to stay, a foreign wife is an occasion of complaint. Yahweh neither rebukes nor defends the marriage. The objection is not stated, and it may have nothing to do with where she came from. See [[Cushite Woman (of Moses' time)]].

See [[Miriam and Aaron Against Moses]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 12. Word locations from WLC (morphhb) tags; Greek from Rahlfs 1935.

### The spies — Numbers 13

Verse 29 is a map: Amalek in the South, the Hittite, Jebusite, and Amorite in the hills, and the
Canaanite by the sea and the Jordan. Rahlfs adds the Hivite. The peoples named at Exodus 3:8 as a
list here have regions. See [[The Anakim]] and [[Amalek]].

See [[The Twelve Spies]].

### The audience — Numbers 14

Moses argues that the Egyptians and the people of the land know what Yahweh has done, and will draw
conclusions (13–16). Yahweh's name stands in their hearing. See
[[What the Egyptians Will Hear - Moses' Argument in Exodus 32, Numbers 14, and Deuteronomy 9]].

### Seventy bulls — Numbers 29:12–34

The bulls of the seven days of tents total seventy. Numbers does not comment on the number. In the
Talmud, Sukkah 55b, Rabbi Elazar says they correspond to the seventy nations of the world and that the
one bull of the eighth day corresponds to Israel (William Davidson translation via Sefaria, checked
2026-11-05). See [[Seventy Nations - Genesis 10 in Deuteronomy 32 and Luke 10]].

See [[The Offerings of the Appointed Feasts]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 28:16–29:40.

### To be like the nations — Deuteronomy 17:14–20

Israel's request is foreseen: *"I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me"* (17:14). It is granted, but the king may not be a *nokrî*, a foreigner (15), and the law at once makes him unlike other kings. 1 Samuel 8:5, 20 repeats the phrase (WEB and WLC checked; not stored). See [[Like All the Nations - Deuteronomy 17.14 and 1 Samuel 8.5, 20]].

See [[The Law for the King]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 17:14–20.

### What the nations listen to — Deuteronomy 18:9–14

*"These nations that you shall dispossess listen to those who practice sorcery and to diviners; but as for
you, Yahweh your God has not allowed you so to do"* (14). As with the king *"like all the nations"*
(17:14), the law defines Israel against them; their practices are why they are driven out (12).

See [[A Prophet Like Moses]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 18:9–22.

### Near and far — Deuteronomy 20:10–18

The law of war divides the nations in two. Cities *"very far off"* are offered peace, and their people
become forced laborers or, if they fight, lose their males (10–15). The cities of the six peoples of the
land — Hittite, Amorite, Canaanite, Perizzite, Hivite, Jebusite — are to be utterly destroyed, *"that
they not teach you to follow all their abominations"* (16–18). The Girgashite of 7:1 is absent from this
list, without explanation.

See [[Laws for Going to War]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 20:1–20.

- [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] — Genesis 15:19–21: ten peoples listed as the contents of a
  land grant, the longest such list in the Bible, in the book whose founding promise was that all
  the families of the earth would be blessed through the man receiving it (12:3). Genesis holds the
  two without comment — and two of the ten, the Kenites and the Kenizzites, turn out to be on
  Israel's side when the land is actually taken
- [[Hagar and Ishmael]] — Genesis 16:10–12, where a nation is promised **inside** the family rather
  than around it. Ishmael is the first of the peoples Genesis derives from Abraham outside the
  covenant line (with Esau's Edom at 36, and Keturah's sons at 25:1–4), and the only one to receive
  the offspring promise in the covenant's own formula. His mother is Egyptian, his wife will be
  Egyptian (21:21), his twelve princes answer Israel's twelve tribes (25:12–16), and 16:12's *"He
  will live opposite all of his brothers"* comes back at 25:18 as a statement about his descendants —
  the same four-word phrase, *ʿal-pənê kol-ʾeḥāyw*, with a different verb (*yiškōn*, *dwell*, becomes
  *nāpāl*, *fall* or *settle*), which is where the reading of the clause is actually decided.
  Genesis blesses him, makes him a nation, and gives the covenant to someone else, without ever
  explaining how the two sit together
- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17, where *gôyim* enters the promise itself.
  Twelve chapters of *a great nation*, singular, become *"the father of a multitude of nations"*
  (17:4–5) and *"a mother of nations"* (17:16), with the new name built on the plural. In the same
  chapter the covenant sign is put on *"any foreigner"* bought into the household (17:12–13, 27) —
  the boundary and the door through it, legislated in the same paragraph, which is what Exodus 12:48
  will make explicit
- [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] — Genesis 18:18, where the promise of 12:3 is
  restated with the vocabulary raised: *"all the **nations** of the earth will be blessed in him"*,
  where 12:3 had *"all the families of the earth"*. It is quoted by God to himself, as the reason for
  telling Abraham what he intends — and what Abraham does with the information is argue for the
  survival of a Canaanite city. The promise's beneficiaries make their first appearance as a case,
  not a category
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — the first chapter in which Abram actually deals with the
  nations rather than being promised them: an Elamite overlord, a king of *Goiim* (*nations*, with no
  country attached), three Amorite covenant-partners, a Canaanite priest of God Most High whose
  blessing he takes, and a Sodomite king whose money he refuses. It is also where an outsider first
  needs a word for him, and uses *the Hebrew* (14:13)

- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:37–38: two peoples given an origin, an etymology, and a
  *"to this day"*. Where the Table produces a nation as a name in a list, this produces one with a
  scene attached, and the scene is the ugliest in the Abraham cycle. What the canon then does with
  the two is not what a slur would do: Deuteronomy 2:9 and 2:19 grant Moab and Ammon their
  territory in the formula used of Israel's own inheritance and call them *"the children of Lot"*,
  and Ruth the Moabitess is David's great-grandmother. See
  [[Moab and Ammon - The Two Nations Born in the Cave]]
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:4–7, where a nation outside the line speaks for itself. The
  king of Gerar calls his own people *a righteous nation* to God's face and is conceded; he receives
  the first dream in Scripture; and he is healed by the prayer of the man who wronged him — **the
  first concrete instance of 12:3's *"in you all the families of the earth will be blessed"*, and it
  takes the form of a foreign household's wombs being opened**. The pattern of [[Abram in Egypt]] is
  confirmed rather than reversed: the outsider is again the one with the moral vocabulary, and again
  the one God speaks to
- [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]] — Genesis 21:13, 18, 20–21, **a nation founded in
  a desert by a slave**. Ishmael is promised the *gôy gādôl* of 12:2, God is *with* him before he is
  said to be with anyone in the covenant line, and his line begins with an Egyptian mother and an
  Egyptian wife his mother fetches for him. Genesis gives the non-covenant nations more than the
  arguments later built on them do, and this is the clearest case
- [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] — Genesis 21:22–34, where a foreign king reads God's favour off a
  foreigner's prosperity and acts on it: *"God is with you in all that you do."* He is right, and he
  is reasoning politically rather than piously — a man who is backed is a man you sign with. The
  pattern holds for the fourth consecutive pericope: **the outsider supplies the moral and
  theological vocabulary**, and the covenant family supplies the behaviour that needs excusing.
  Abimelech asks Abraham to swear he will not *deal falsely*, one chapter after he did
- [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] — Genesis 22:20–24 and 23:3–20, the nations on both sides
  of the family. The genealogy of Nahor's twelve is an Aramean and north Arabian **map** written as a
  household — Aram, Chesed (behind the *Chaldees* of Ur), Uz, Buz, Maacah — which makes the peoples
  north-east of Canaan Abraham's brother's descendants. And the chapter that follows is the
  friendliest encounter in Genesis between a patriarch and the inhabitants of the land: the children
  of Heth call him *"a prince of God"*, offer him the best of their tombs for nothing, and conduct a
  land sale for him at their own gate. **They are also named, in the Torah's conquest lists, among
  the peoples his descendants are to dispossess**, and Genesis does not remark on it. See
  [[The Children of Heth]] and [[Ephron the Hittite]]
- [[A Wife for Isaac]] — Genesis 24: the covenant line marries out of Canaan and into Aram, and will
  do it again for Jacob. The Arameans in this chapter are courteous, generous, quick to credit
  Yahweh, and blessed with the covenant's own words at 24:60 — and Genesis 31:53 and Joshua 24:2 are
  blunt about what the branch that stayed behind actually worshipped. See
  [[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]] and [[Aram Naharaim]]

- [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] — Genesis 25:1–18, where the covenant family is
  shown to be the demographic source of half the Near East. **Twenty-eight names in eight verses**:
  sixteen Arabian descendants by [[Keturah]] — Midian and Sheba and Dedan among them — and Ishmael's
  twelve princes *"according to their nations"*, with villages and encampments from Havilah to Shur.
  Sheba and Dedan are already filed under Ham at 10:7, which is what a genealogy of peoples does. And
  Isaiah 60:6–7 brings five of these names back to Zion with gold and incense; see
  [[Abraham's Other Sons in Isaiah 60]]
- [[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]] — Genesis 25:23, where a nation is forecast from inside a
  womb. ***Šənê gôyîm*** — *two nations* — and *ləʾōm miləʾōm*, *people against people*: **the
  oracle's own vocabulary is national, not personal**, and what it predicts is a political relation
  that 2 Samuel 8:13–14 records and 2 Kings 8:20–22 records ending. Then 25:30 names the nation from
  a bowl of stew. **Edom is the one neighbour the Hebrew Bible consistently calls *brother***, which
  is what makes Deuteronomy 23:7 (*"You shall not abhor an Edomite"*) and Obadiah's charge of
  fratricide two halves of the same fact. See [[Edom]] and
  [[The Elder Will Serve the Younger - Genesis 25.23 in Malachi and Romans]]
- [[Isaac in Gerar]] — Genesis 26:4, 10–16, 28–29; **the promise that *"all the nations of the
  earth"* will be blessed in his offspring, restated to a man living among them and being pushed
  around by them.** The Philistine king protects the household by capital edict, expels it for
  outgrowing his kingdom, and then comes to sign with it because *"we saw plainly that Yahweh was
  with you"*. **The chapter's foreigners are, in order, better behaved than the patriarch, frightened
  of him, and finally dependent on his goodwill** — the three postures Genesis gives the nations
  around this family, all inside thirty-three verses. And the last two verses run it the other way:
  Esau marries into the nations, and his parents grieve. See [[Envy]] and
  [[Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt]]
- [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]] — Genesis 28:3: ***qəhal ʿammîm***, *a company of peoples*,
  and ***qāhāl*** here is **its first occurrence in the Hebrew Bible** — the assembly word the
  Septuagint renders *ekklēsia*, appearing first as a father's wish over a fugitive. God grants it in
  the same terms at 35:11, *"a nation and a company of nations."*
- [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] — Genesis 28:14: ***"In you and in your offspring will all the families
  of the earth be blessed"* — the fifth and last statement of the promise to the nations in Genesis**
  (12:3, 18:18, 22:18, 26:4, 28:14), and **the only one after the first to use the first one's
  noun.** 12:3 and 28:14 both have ***mišpəḥōt hāʾădāmâ***, *the families of the earth*; 22:18 and
  26:4 have *the nations of the earth*. The promise ends, verbally, where it began — in the mouth of
  the grandson. And the same verse promises Jacob ***ûpāraṣtā***, bursting out in four directions:
  **the scattering Babel organised a civilisation to prevent, given as a blessing.** See
  [[Babel and the Gate of Heaven]].
- [[Jacob's Flocks]] — Genesis 30:27: **an Aramean says *Yahweh*, reads providence correctly, and
  arrives at it by divination.** *"I have divined that Yahweh has blessed me for your sake"* is
  Genesis 12:3 stated by a beneficiary rather than by God, **and it is used as a reason to detain the
  man the blessing runs through.** [[Laban (person)]] joins [[Melchizedek]],
  [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]], and [[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]] in the book's standing habit
  of handing accurate theology to outsiders and never explaining where they got it — **and he is the
  only one of them who acts on it by refusing to let the carrier go**, which is what [[Egypt]] will
  do at national scale. See [[Blessed for His Sake - Laban's House and Potiphar's]] and
  [[Divination in Genesis - Laban's Nahash and Joseph's Cup]]
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:20, 24, 47, 53; **the branch that stayed behind is
  finally shown to be another people.** Genesis calls Laban ***the Aramean*** twice more here, and
  then **lets him speak Aramaic on the page** — *Jegar Sahadutha* against Jacob's *Galeed*, **the only
  Aramaic in the Torah** — and lets him swear by *"the God of Nahor"* beside the God of Abraham under
  a plural verb. **Language, gods, and a border, in twelve verses.** Deuteronomy 26:5 keeps the whole
  of it in one line of Israel's creed: *"My father was a Syrian ready to perish"*
- [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] — Genesis 34:1–31; **a Canaanite town given speech, motive, a
  ruler, an assembly, and a unanimous decision — and then destroyed.** The [[Shechem (city)]] Hivites
  are the most fully drawn foreign community in Genesis outside Egypt: [[Hamor]] argues a policy of
  full integration (*"we will become one people"*, 34:16, 22), the gate assembly deliberates and
  votes, and **the one frankly acquisitive line in the chapter is theirs** — *"Won't their livestock
  and their possessions and all their animals be ours?"* (34:23). **Both peoples in the chapter act in
  bad faith and only the Israelite side is labelled** *bĕmirmâ* **by the narrator.** The episode is
  also the theme's hard case in the other direction: **the covenant sign, which Genesis 17 explicitly
  opens to outsiders and Exodus 12:48 will make the door into the people, is here used to shut a whole
  town out of existence.** See [[Circumcision]] and [[Deception]]
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:11; ***gôy ûqəhal gôyim***, *"a nation
  and a company of nations will be from you"* — **granting the exact request [[Isaac]] made at 28:3**,
  *qəhal ʿammîm*, in the same divine name he made it in. The covenant line is told for the first time
  that it will be **plural**: not one people but an assembly of them, with kings, in a book whose
  seventy nations at chapter 10 were the problem the call of Abram answered
- [[The Descendants of Esau]] — Genesis 36:1–43: **the fullest national register Genesis gives
  anyone outside the covenant line**, and the last of the four times the book deals with the
  passed-over branch first. Edom receives a ruling family, fourteen clan-chiefs by mother, eleven
  districts, and eight kings *"before any king reigned over the children of Israel"* — and the
  chapter grants it all without a word of disparagement, in Edom's own political vocabulary
  (***ʾallûp̄***, *clan-chief*). **It also registers the people Edom displaced**: eleven verses for
  the Horites, whom the rest of the Torah mentions only to say they were destroyed. See
  [[Seir the Horite]] and [[Edom]]
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:5–8; **Egyptians receive true revelation in their
  sleep and cannot read it.** The pattern is Genesis's most consistent and least explained habit — a
  dream to [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] at 20:3, a dream to [[Laban (person)]] at 31:24, an accurate omen at
  30:27 — **and here it reaches two palace servants in a jail.** What is new is that **the outsiders
  need an Israelite to read it**, and that the Israelite tells them where the reading comes from without
  ever saying the covenant name to them: he says ***ʾĕlōhîm***, as he did at 39:9 and will at 41:16
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:38, 45, 57, **the theme's high-water mark in Genesis.** **A pagan
  king makes the strongest theological confession in the book** — *"Can we find such a one as this, a man
  in whom is the Spirit of God?"* (41:38) — **and he makes it about a foreign convict, as a question to
  his own courtiers, on empirical grounds**; see [[A Man in Whom Is the Spirit of God]]. **An Egyptian
  court receives a true revelation in its sleep**, as [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] did at 20:3 and
  [[Laban (person)]] did at 31:24, **and needs an Israelite to read it.** **Joseph marries into the priesthood of
  the Egyptian sun cult at [[On]] and Genesis does not comment.** And at 41:57 ***"all countries came
  into Egypt, to Joseph, to buy grain"*** — **the promise of 12:3 operating before anyone in the chapter
  knows it is a promise**, and the only time in Genesis that the whole earth is fed through a son of
  Abraham
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:10, 12, 20; **three foreign mothers in one register, handled
  three different ways.** **[[Shaul (son of Simeon)]] is flagged as *"the son of a Canaanite woman"***;
  **[[Asenath]] the Egyptian is given her father's name and his priestly office**; **and
  [[Shua's Daughter]], the Canaanite mother of five of the seventy, is not mentioned.** **Genesis
  supplies no rule that accounts for all three.** The household then takes up formal residence inside
  an empire, with a grant of land from its king
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:13–26; **an entire foreign nation, addressed collectively,
  given three speeches and not one name.** *"Give us bread, for why should we die in your presence?"*
  (47:15); *"Buy us and our land for bread"* (47:19); *"You have saved our lives!"* (47:25). **It is
  more direct speech than most named characters in Genesis get**, and **the largest non-Israelite
  population the book has put on the page since chapter 10** — **on it in order to be bought.**
  **The one Egyptian group Genesis distinguishes is the priesthood**, exempted at 47:22, **and the
  one Egyptian it has named is [[Potiphera]], whose daughter is the mother of two tribes of Israel**
- [[Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh]] — Genesis 48:4, 5, 19; **two of Israel's twelve tribes are
  constituted out of foreigners, by declaration.** *"Your two sons, who were born to you **in the land of Egypt** … are
  mine"* (48:5) — **their mother is [[Asenath]], daughter of a priest of [[On]]** (41:45) — **and the
  household that would not marry Canaanite women adopts her children without mentioning her.**
  ***Məlōʾ haggôyim***, *a multitude of nations* (48:19), **occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible and is
  promised to the younger of the two**; **Romans 11:25's *"fullness of the Gentiles"* is the same two
  words in Greek, and the wiki records the echo without claiming a citation.** *"A company of peoples"*
  (48:4) is the promise to Jacob himself. See [[Adoption]] and
  [[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]]
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:8, 17; ***"the Canaanite, the Hittite, the Amorite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and
  the Jebusite"*** — **five of them from the ten of Genesis 15:19–21, plus the Hivite**, the list of six
  that becomes the standard. The land is promised by naming who holds it, and the promise does not
  explain what will become of them
- [[The Heads of the Fathers' Houses]] — Exodus 6:15, 25; ***"Shaul the son of a Canaanite woman"*** copied word for word
  from Genesis 46:10, and ***Pînəḥās***, Aaron's grandson, with a name usually derived from Egyptian
  *the Nubian* (not certain) — the priestly line carrying an Egyptian-sounding name, as Moses does
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:19; **the stranger under the same rule as the native.** Leaven cuts off
  *"whether he is a foreigner, or one who is born in the land"*; the first *ʾezraḥ* in Scripture. The
  circumcised stranger may keep the Passover itself under *"one law"* (12:48–49, the next pericope)
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:38, 43–49; **a mixed multitude goes up with Israel, and the law sorts
  outsiders.** *"A mixed multitude"* (*ʿēreb rab*, only here in the Torah; WLC) leaves with them,
  unidentified; then the Passover excludes the *ben-nēkār*, the *tôšāb*, and the *śākîr*, and admits the
  circumcised *gēr*, *"as one who is born in the land"*, under ***"One law"***. See
  [[One Law for the Native and the Stranger]]
- [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] — Exodus 13:5; ***"the Canaanite, and the Hittite, and the Amorite, and
  the Hivite, and the Jebusite"*** — five peoples, where the bush listed six (3:8, 17) and Genesis 15:19–21
  ten; the Perizzite is missing (WLC). No reason is given, and the wiki reads none in
- [[The Song of the Sea]] — Exodus 15:14–16; **the nations hear.** Philistia, Edom, Moab, Canaan — **the first naming of
  *Philistia* as a land**, and the *"chiefs of Edom"* of Genesis 36 (WLC). They tremble, melt, and stand
  *"as still as a stone"* until Israel passes. Rahab repeats the Song's words (Joshua 2:9). See
  [[Terror Has Fallen on Us - The Song of the Sea in Rahab's Mouth]].
- [[The Battle with Amalek]] — Exodus 17:8–16; **the first nation to attack Israel**, and the first after the sea
  to meet them at all. *"Then Amalek came"*. **The next foreigner to come to the camp is Jethro of Midian, with
  blessing and a sacrifice** (18:1–12; [[Jethro's Counsel]]): one nation comes with a sword and one with an offering
- [[Jethro's Counsel]] — Exodus 18:1–27; **the nation that comes in peace.** One chapter after Amalek's
  sword, the priest of Midian hears, comes, blesses Yahweh, confesses *"Now I know that Yahweh is greater than all
  gods"*, offers the first burnt offering in Exodus, and gives Israel its judiciary — then goes home. All three
  *šālôm* in Exodus are his (4:18; 18:7, 23; WLC) —
  [[Blessed Be God Who Delivered - Melchizedek and Jethro]]
- [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] — Exodus 19:5–6; a particular nation within the God-owned earth
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; six peoples to be driven out, for their gods' sake.
- [[The Passover in the Second Year]] — Numbers 9:14; the stranger at the Passover.
- [[The March from Sinai]] — Numbers 10:29–32; a Midianite offered a share of Israel's good.
- [[Miriam and Aaron Against Moses]] — Numbers 12:1; a wife from Cush, or from Midian.
- [[The Twelve Spies]] — Numbers 13:28–29, 33; the peoples of the land by region
- [[The People Refuse the Land]] — Numbers 14:13–16; the nations as the audience of Israel's fate
- [[Offerings in the Land and Sins of Ignorance]] — Numbers 15:14–16, 26, 29; *as you are, so shall the foreigner be*
- [[The Offerings of the Appointed Feasts]] — Numbers 29:12–34; seventy bulls, read in Sukkah 55b as offered for the seventy nations
- [[Do Not Follow Other Gods]] — Deuteronomy 12:29–13:18; their rites not to be imitated; their gods near and far.
- [[A Holy People and Its Food]] — Deuteronomy 14:2, 21; a people set apart from all peoples by its food.
- [[The Year of Release and the Open Hand]] — Deuteronomy 15:1–11; Israel to lend to many nations and not borrow (6); the *nokrî* not released (3).
- [[The Law for the King]] — Deuteronomy 17:14–20; *"like all the nations"* (14); no foreigner as king (15).
- [[A Prophet Like Moses]] — Deuteronomy 18:9–14; the nations' diviners, and why they are driven out.
- [[Laws for Going to War]] — Deuteronomy 20:10–18; distant cities and the cities of the six peoples.
- [[The Assembly and the Holy Camp]] — Deuteronomy 23:3–8; Ammon and Moab excluded, Edom and Egypt
  admitted in the third generation.
- [[Fugitives, Interest, Vows, and a Neighbor's Produce]] — Deuteronomy 23:20; interest permitted from the *nokrî*.
- [[Justice, Family Duty, Honest Weights, and Amalek]] — Deuteronomy 25:17–19; Amalek's memory to be blotted out from under the sky.
