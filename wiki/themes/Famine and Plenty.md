# Famine and Plenty

## Definition / overview

**Food running out, and what people do about it.**

**Famine is the engine of the patriarchal narratives more often than war, and far more often than
persecution.** It is what **moves the family**: it sends [[Abraham]] to Egypt at 12:10, it nearly sends
[[Isaac]] there at 26:1, and it brings all seventy of [[Jacob]]'s household there permanently at
46:1–7. **Every one of Genesis's three famines drives the covenant line toward Egypt**, and **the third
one does not let it come back.**

**Genesis treats famine as a fact of the world and not as a punishment.** **No famine in the book is
attributed to anyone's sin**, none is announced as a judgment, and **the one time a famine is explained
at all, the explanation is that God is telling Pharaoh in advance so that food can be stored**
(41:25–36). **This is worth stating plainly because the rest of the Old Testament often does otherwise** —
Deuteronomy 28:23–24, 1 Kings 17:1, Amos 4:6 and Haggai 1:10–11 all make a famine a verdict. **Genesis
does not.**

**The theme's other half is plenty, and Genesis is more suspicious of that.** **The book's recurring
difficulty is not scarcity but success** — Abram and Lot part because *"the land was not able to bear
them"* (13:6), Jacob and Laban fall out over flocks, Esau and Jacob separate for the same reason as their
grandfathers — **and the one time abundance is managed rather than fought over, it is managed by a
foreign state.**

## Development across Scripture

### Genesis's three famines

| where | who | what happens |
|---|---|---|
| **12:10** | [[Abraham]] | *"There was a famine in the land"* — he goes down to Egypt, lies about his wife, and is expelled with more than he came with. **The first descent, and the template.** See [[Abram in Egypt]] |
| **26:1** | [[Isaac]] | *"besides the first famine that was in the days of Abraham"* — and **God forbids the descent**: *"Don't go down into Egypt."* He stays, sows in the famine year, and reaps a hundredfold. See [[Isaac in Gerar]] |
| **41:27–57** | [[Joseph]] | **seven years, announced seven years in advance**, over *"all the surface of the earth"* — and it is the one that relocates Israel. See [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] |

**The three are a deliberate series and Genesis marks it**: 26:1 explicitly cross-references 12:10. **The
pattern is descent, danger, and enrichment**, and
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]] traces how closely the first rehearses
the Exodus.

**The middle one is the exception that proves the theme's shape.** **Isaac is the only patriarch told to
stay**, and he is the only one who prospers agriculturally rather than by the gift of a foreign king.

### Genesis 41 — the first managed famine in Scripture

**Nothing before this chapter shows anyone planning for a shortage.** What Genesis 41 supplies is **a
complete programme**, and it is proposed by a prisoner:

1. **Forecast** — seven years of plenty, then seven of famine, *"established by God"* and imminent
   (41:29–32).
2. **An officer** — *"a discreet and wise man … set over the land"* (41:33).
3. **A bureaucracy** — *pəqidim*, overseers (41:34).
4. **A levy** — ***the fifth part***, twenty per cent of the harvest of the good years (41:34).
5. **Storage** — city by city, *"laid up grain under the hand of Pharaoh for food in the cities"*
   (41:35, 48).
6. **Distribution** — the storehouses opened and grain **sold**, first to Egyptians and then to *"all
   countries"* (41:56–57).

**The programme works**, and Genesis reports it as competence rather than as a miracle: *"the seven years
of famine began to come, just as Joseph had said"* (41:54). **The food was not multiplied; it was
stored.** See [[Work]] and [[Wisdom]].

**The dream's own image of famine is the harshest in the Bible.** **The lean cattle eat the fat ones and
look exactly as they did before** — *"it couldn't be known that they had eaten them"* (41:21). **Seven
years of surplus disappear into seven years of need without leaving a mark**, which is a precise
description of subsistence: **abundance does not accumulate, it is consumed.**

### The bill, and Genesis's silence about it

**The programme has a second half and the book does not moralise about it either.** By 47:13–26 the
Egyptians have paid for grain with **their money, then their livestock, then their land, then
themselves** — *"buy us and our land for bread, and we and our land will be servants to Pharaoh"* — and
**the fifth part becomes the permanent law of Egypt.** **The priests alone are exempt** (47:22).

**A famine that began as a warning ends with a population owning nothing and a crown owning everything**,
administered by the man whose descendants will be enslaved by that same crown in Exodus 1. **Genesis
records the whole sequence as good government and passes no comment**, which is the same posture it takes
on Joseph's Egyptian marriage. See [[Wealth and Possessions]], [[Kingship and Empire]], and
[[Slavery and Servitude]].

### Beyond Genesis

- **Ruth 1:1** — *"there was a famine in the land"*, and a family goes to Moab. **The book that opens the
  same way Genesis 12 does, and ends with a great-grandson named David.**
- **2 Samuel 21:1; 24:13** — famine as a stated judgment, which Genesis never does.
- **1 Kings 17–18** — Elijah's drought, a widow's jar that does not run out, and **the one famine
  Scripture answers by multiplying food instead of storing it.**
- **2 Kings 4:38–44; 6:25** — famine in Elisha's ministry, down to the price of a donkey's head.
- **Amos 8:11** — *"not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing Yahweh's words"* — **the
  metaphor's sharpest use.**
- **Acts 11:28** — a famine *"over all the world"* prophesied by Agabus, **answered by a collection sent
  from Antioch to Judea** — the New Testament's version of storing against a forecast.
- **Revelation 6:5–6** — the third horseman, **a pair of scales and a day's wage for a day's bread.**

### The counter-image

**Scripture's picture of the end is agricultural surplus**: *"the plowman shall overtake the reaper"*
(Amos 9:13), *"every man under his vine and under his fig tree"* (Micah 4:4), and a tree that
*"yielded its fruit every month"* (Revelation 22:2). **The condition Genesis 41 manages for seven years,
the prophets make permanent.** See [[The Tree of Life - Eden and the New Jerusalem]].

### Exodus 16 — the storehouse reversed

**The first hunger after Egypt makes Israel remember Egypt as plenty**: *"when we sat by the meat pots, when
we ate our fill of bread"* (Exodus 16:3). **God's answer reverses Joseph's famine policy point by point.**
Joseph's programme stored the surplus of good years and centralized it — *"Joseph gathered up all the
money"* into Pharaoh's house (Genesis 47:14), with the verb *lāqaṭ* (WLC). **The manna is gathered with the
same verb, by every household, for one day; it is measured so that *"he who gathered much had nothing over,
and he who gathered little had no lack"* (16:18); and what is stored rots** (16:20) — except on the sixth day,
when storing is commanded and the bread keeps (16:24). **Plenty is not managed by a state; it is given daily
and cannot be accumulated.** See [[Manna and Quail]].

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:24–26

Withheld grain draws a public curse; selling it draws blessing. No famine is stated here, so the
chapter adds an ethical judgment on access to food rather than a new famine episode. Making
provisions available can serve the community even when the transaction is a sale.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:9, 11, 27

Hunger beneath self-honor contrasts with bread from cultivation; neglected game contrasts with
prized possessions. No regional famine is described. The promise of bread through labor stands
beside Job 24:10–11’s hungry and thirsty workers, who show that productive effort does not always
secure access to its fruit.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Appetite and the swept field — Proverbs 13:4, 23, 25

The sluggard's soul desires and has nothing (4); the poor man's productive field is swept away by injustice (23); the righteous eats to satisfaction while *"the belly of the wicked goes hungry"* (25). Plenty and hunger are tied to conduct in 4 and 25 and to another's wrong in 23.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

## Key passages

- [[Hope Deferred]] — Proverbs 13:4, 23, 25.

- [[Whoever Loves Correction]] — Proverbs 12:9, 11, 27.

- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:24–26.

- [[Abram in Egypt]] — Genesis 12:10; **the Bible's first famine**, and the first descent to Egypt
- [[Isaac in Gerar]] — Genesis 26:1–12; **the second famine, the one descent God forbids**, and a
  hundredfold harvest reaped in the famine year on land Isaac does not own
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:1–57; **seven years forecast, a fifth part levied,
  grain stored city by city, and the whole earth arriving to buy.** *"There was famine in all lands, but
  in all the land of Egypt there was bread"*
- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — Genesis 13:5–12; **the opposite problem** — *"the land was not able to
  bear them"*
- [[Jacob's Flocks]] — Genesis 30:25–43; abundance as the thing two men fight over
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:1–5; **41:57's *"all countries came into Egypt"* becomes ten specific men with
  donkeys.** The famine is stated of [[Canaan]] by name (42:5), it is what puts the brothers in front
  of Joseph, **and it is what will overrule Jacob's refusal two verses into the next chapter.** *"Go
  down there, and buy for us… so that we may live, and not die"* (42:2) states the stakes plainly
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:1–2, 16, 31–34; **the famine wins the argument that ten sons could
  not.** *"The famine was severe in the land"* (43:1) is four words, and it reverses [[Jacob]]'s
  *"My son shall not go down with you"* (42:38) without anyone persuading him of anything — *"When they
  had eaten up the grain … 'Go again.'"* **Then the chapter ends in a banquet**: an animal butchered, bread
  at noon, portions sent from the governor's table, **and men who came to beg for food drinking until they
  are merry**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:6, 11, 18, 23; **the chapter's engine, and the verse that
  puts a clock on it.** *"For these two years the famine has been in the land, and there are yet five
  years, in which there will be no plowing and no harvest"* (45:6) — **the seven of 41:30 with two
  spent** — and every imperative in the chapter is urgent because of it. **The economics of the cycle
  reverse here**: the brothers came down with silver to buy grain (42:3) and go home with *"the fat of
  the land"* (45:18), twenty loaded donkeys (45:23) and a promise of maintenance — ***wəkilkaltî***,
  *"there I will nourish you"* (45:11). **The famine that emptied Canaan is what moves Israel into
  Egypt**
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 47:4, 12; **the famine is still the engine and it surfaces
  in the brothers' own mouths**: *"there is no pasture for your servants' flocks. For the famine is
  severe in the land of Canaan"* (47:4). **The pericope ends on a ration** — *"Joseph nourished his
  father, his brothers, and all of his father's household, with bread, according to their families"*
  (47:12), ***wayəkalkēl***, **the verb of his promise at 45:11**
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:13–26; **the theme's terminal statement, and the only place
  in Scripture that shows what a famine administration actually does to a country.** *"There was no
  bread in all the land; for the famine was very severe"* (47:13) **is the last time Genesis says
  it.** The seven years announced at 41:30 are collected in four instalments — **the money** (47:14),
  **the livestock** (47:16–17), **the land and the people** (47:18–21), **and a fifth of every
  harvest for ever** (47:23–26) — **and what the famine finally produces is not starvation but a
  change in the ownership of Egypt.** **Nobody dies**; *"You have saved our lives!"* (47:25) is the
  people's own verdict, **spoken as they become Pharaoh's servants.** See
  [[A Fifth to Pharaoh - Joseph's Tax and the Rights of the King]]
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:27–31; 37:13; **the rain that judges and feeds.** *"For by these he
  judges the people. He gives food in abundance"* (36:31): the same storm as flood and as harvest. The
  rain comes *"for correction, or for his land, or for loving kindness"* (37:13), and the hymn does not say
  which is which.
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:27, 39–41; **food for creatures no one feeds.** Grass made to grow on
  *"the waste and desolate ground"* (38:27); the lioness's prey; and the raven's young who *"wander for
  lack of food"* until God provides (38:41).
- [[Manna and Quail]] — Exodus 16:3, 16–21; **hunger out of Egypt, and an economy of enough.** *"To kill this
  whole assembly with hunger (bārāʿāb)"* — Genesis's famine word, its only use in Exodus (WLC lemma search,
  Genesis–Exodus) — **and a reversal of Joseph's system**: where *"Joseph gathered up (lāqaṭ) all the money …
  into Pharaoh's house"* (Genesis 47:14) and Egypt *"laid up grain"* in cities, the manna is gathered a day at
  a time, cannot be stored, and comes out to *"no lack"* and *"nothing over"* (16:18). Kept, it *"bred
  worms"*. See [[He Who Gathered Much - Exodus 16.18 and 2 Corinthians 8.15]]
