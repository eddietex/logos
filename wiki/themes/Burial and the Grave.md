# Burial and the Grave

## Definition / overview

What the living do with the dead, and why Scripture treats it as a claim rather than a disposal.

**The theme starts late.** Genesis reports a great many deaths before it reports a single burial.
Abel is killed and his blood cries from the ground, and nothing is said about a grave (4:10). Eight
men *die* in the register of chapter 5 and none of them is buried on the page. An entire world drowns
in chapter 7. Terah dies at Haran (11:32), Haran dies before his father in Ur (11:28), Lot's wife
becomes a pillar of salt (19:26) — no funerals anywhere. **The first burial Genesis narrates is
Sarah's, and it takes eighteen verses of legal negotiation to get to it** (23:1–20). See
[[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]].

That disproportion is the theme's first fact. The book spends more words on acquiring the ground than
on the interment: the burial itself is one verse (23:19), and the chapter's last verse goes back to
the deed. **Genesis is not interested in the rite. It is interested in the title.**

### What a grave is for in the Hebrew Bible

Three things, and they are not the same three a modern reader expects.

1. **A holding.** *ʾĂḥuzzat qeber*, *a possession for a burial place* — the phrase the deed uses
   twice (23:20; and Abraham's own request at 23:4, 9). It is a property term. The tomb is the one
   piece of the land promise that is executed inside Genesis, and the book keeps reciting the
   purchase every time it returns to it. See [[Land and Sojourning]] and [[Machpelah]].
2. **A gathering.** The recurring idiom is *"gathered to his people"* (25:8, 17; 35:29; 49:29, 33) —
   used **in addition to** dying and **in addition to** being buried, and used of Abraham before any
   of his relatives lies in that tomb, which means it is not simply a description of a family plot.
   What it describes is not stated. See [[Life and Death]] and [[Sheol]].
3. **A claim on the future.** Jacob makes Joseph swear to carry him out of Egypt (47:29–31); Joseph
   makes his brothers swear to carry his bones (50:24–25), and Exodus 13:19 and Joshua 24:32 record
   the oath being kept four centuries and a conquest later. **A grave in the land is how a family in
   exile keeps a country addressed.**

### Not to be buried

The counter-image is as strong as the image. To be left unburied is, throughout Scripture, the
sharpest curse available: Jezebel eaten by dogs (2 Kings 9:10, 34–37), Jehoiakim *"buried with the
burial of a donkey, drawn and cast forth beyond the gates of Jerusalem"* (Jeremiah 22:19), the dead
of Jerusalem *"food for the birds of the sky"* (Jeremiah 7:33; Psalm 79:2–3), and the curse of
Deuteronomy 28:26. Conversely, burying the dead — including a stranger's dead — becomes a mark of
piety in Tobit and a work of mercy in later Jewish and Christian practice. **The consistent
assumption is that a body left out is a person left out.**

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 23:1–20** — the first burial and the first purchase: [[Sarah]] at Machpelah, a field, a
  cave, and every tree in it, bought from [[Ephron the Hittite]] for four hundred shekels and
  witnessed at the gate. Abraham refuses a free tomb twice.
- **Genesis 25:8–10** — Abraham *"was gathered to his people"*, and Isaac and Ishmael bury him
  together in the same cave. *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 35:8** — Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, buried under an oak below Bethel, and **the tree is
  named** *Allon Bacuth*, the oak of weeping — a grave marked and not owned. *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 35:19–20** — Rachel buried on the road to Ephrath with a pillar over the grave, the one
  matriarch not at Machpelah; Jacob is still explaining it at 48:7. *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 47:29–31; 49:29–32; 50:1–14** — Jacob embalmed in Egypt, mourned seventy days, and
  carried back to Hebron with chariots and horsemen: **the largest procession in Genesis, mounted to
  execute a deed made three generations earlier.** *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 50:24–26; Exodus 13:19; Joshua 24:32** — Joseph's bones: an oath at the end of Genesis,
  kept at the exodus, discharged at Shechem after the conquest.
- **Deuteronomy 34:5–6** — Moses buried by God in a valley in Moab, *"but no man knows where his tomb
  is"*: the one grave in the Torah deliberately unlocated, at the edge of a land he does not enter.
- **1 Samuel 31:11–13; 2 Samuel 21:12–14** — the men of Jabesh Gilead recover Saul's body, and David
  later buries his bones in his father's tomb; a burial as the closing of an account.
- **2 Kings 13:20–21; 23:16–18** — a corpse revived on touching Elisha's bones, and a prophet's tomb
  spared at Bethel: graves as places where something remains.
- **Isaiah 22:16; 53:9** — a tomb cut in the rock as vanity, and *"they made his grave with the
  wicked, and with a rich man in his death"*.
- **Ezekiel 37:12–13** — *"I will open your graves, and cause you to come up out of your graves"*:
  the grave as the thing God undoes.
- **The Gospels** — Joseph of Arimathea's new tomb, borrowed rather than owned; the burial recorded
  as a fact in the creedal summary of 1 Corinthians 15:4 (*"that he was buried"*), because an empty
  tomb requires a full one first.
