# Forgiveness

## Definition / overview

**Genesis waits until its last chapter to use the word.**

**The Hebrew idiom is *nāśāʾ* — *to lift*, *to carry*** — **and what gets lifted is the weight of
what was done.** Its first appearance as a request between human beings is Genesis 50:17, in
[[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]]: ***śāʾ nāʾ pešaʿ ʾaḥeykā wəḥaṭṭāʾtām***, *"now please forgive
the disobedience of your brothers, and their sin."* **Nobody in the Bible asks anybody for
forgiveness before this verse.**

**That is a striking thing for a book with this much wrongdoing in it.** **Genesis has forty-nine
chapters of fratricide, deception, rape, massacre, betrayal and sale, and the word does not come
up once.** **What it has instead is a series of settlements without it** — a mark on [[Cain]]
instead of a pardon (4:15), a treaty with [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] instead of an apology
(21:22–32), [[Esau]] running and embracing and never once being asked (33:4), a heap of stones and
a boundary between [[Jacob]] and [[Laban]] (31:44–53). **Reconciliation in Genesis is normally
achieved by ritual, distance, or silence.** **Chapter 50 is where somebody finally says the word,
and it is said by men who cannot think of another way out.**

## Development across Scripture

### In Genesis

**The theme arrives late and arrives twice, and the two occurrences are not the same.**

**At 45:5–8, in [[Joseph Reveals Himself]], [[Joseph]] does not forgive his brothers — he
reinterprets them.** *"Don't be grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that you sold me here, for God
sent me before you to preserve life."* **The clause *you sold me* survives, but the sentence is
built so that the sending is what carries the weight.** **Nobody asks for anything and nobody is
pardoned; the act is relocated.** See
[[God Sent Me Before You - The Verb That Replaces the Sale]].

**At 50:15–21 the brothers ask, and Joseph answers differently.** *"As for you, **you meant evil
against me**, but God meant it for good"* (50:20). **Seventeen years later, with their father dead
and no one left to protect, he calls the thing by its name for the first time and forgives it
anyway.** **The second version is harder and more complete than the first, because it does not need
the crime to have been something else.** Filed as
[[You Meant Evil Against Me - The Two Intentions at Genesis 50.20]].

**Two things in the scene are worth keeping distinct.** **Joseph does not say he has the standing to
forgive** — *"am I in the place of God?"* (50:19) **is a refusal of the judge's seat, not a claim to
the pardoner's**, and it is the same idiom his father used to refuse [[Rachel]] at 30:2
([[Am I in God's Place - Jacob at 30.2 and Joseph at 50.19]]). **And what he actually gives them is
not a verdict but food and reassurance**: *"I will nourish you and your little ones"*, and *"he
comforted them, and spoke kindly to them"* (50:21). **Genesis's one act of forgiveness is
practical.**

### Afterwards

- **The Torah makes *nāśāʾ* God's own word.** Exodus 34:6–7 — *"forgiving iniquity, disobedience,
  and sin"* — **uses the same verb and two of the same three nouns as Genesis 50:17.** **The brothers
  ask Joseph for forgiveness that the Sinai formula also attributes to God; Exodus 34:7 does not
  say that humans cannot forgive.**
- **The request for pardon is already explicit in Exodus 34:9**, before Leviticus. The earlier
  wording here placed the discussion only in Leviticus and later; that chronology was misleading.
  The present comparison concerns the WEB's pardon language, not an independently verified
  concordance claim about every Hebrew occurrence or subject.
- **The Day of Atonement makes the lifting literal**: the goat *"shall bear on himself all their
  iniquities"* into the wilderness (Leviticus 16:22), *nāśāʾ* again.
- **[[Lamech (son of Methushael)]]'s boast of seventy-sevenfold vengeance** (4:24) **is the sentence
  Jesus inverts at Matthew 18:21–22** when Peter asks how many times to forgive. Filed as
  [[Seventy-Sevenfold - Lamech's Boast and Jesus on Forgiveness]].
- **The Lord's Prayer ties the two directions together** — *"forgive us our debts, as we also forgive
  our debtors"* (Matthew 6:12) — **and Stephen and Jesus both die asking for the forgiveness of
  people who did not ask** (Luke 23:34; Acts 7:60), **which is one step past anything in Genesis.**

### An angel who will not pardon — Exodus 23:20–33

*"He will not pardon your disobedience, for my name is in him"* (23:21). The verb is *nāśāʾ*, to
*lift* or *bear* a transgression (*lōʾ yiśśāʾ ləpišʿăkem*; MAM). The warning is not that forgiveness
is impossible, but that this messenger is not to be treated lightly because he will not overlook
rebellion. After the calf, Moses asks God himself to *"pardon our iniquity"* with a different verb,
*sālaḥ* (34:9; MAM; [[The Covenant Renewed]]). The wiki records the difference in wording and draws no
further conclusion from it. See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Moses explicitly asks God to forgive the people after confessing their great sin (32:31–32). The
answer assigns erasure to the sinner and orders continued travel with punishment still pending
(32:33–34). The relenting of 32:14 is real, but it is not an explicit declaration that every
offense has been pardoned. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

The assurance of presence in 33:14–17 advances the recovery after the calf, but is not phrased as a
blanket declaration of pardon. God’s free mercy in 33:19 and the warning about consuming the people
in 33:3–5 remain together. The renewed plea for pardon follows in [[The Covenant Renewed]] (34:9). See
[[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

God's self-description includes forgiveness of iniquity, disobedience, and sin, immediately
alongside refusal to clear the guilty (34:7). Moses then asks for pardon (34:9), and God
announces a covenant (34:10). This gives the request a setting of renewed relationship without
supplying the explicit pardon declaration later found in Numbers 14:20 (WEB verified at
bible-api.com, 2026-10-24). The statements about forgiveness and accountability remain a tension
to interpret, not evidence that one sentence is unreal. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:12, 18

Love’s covering of wrongs offers an image of relational forbearance, not an explicit declaration of
divine pardon. Hiding hatred with lying lips (18) is its counterfeit. Proverbs 28:13 separately
calls for confessing and renouncing one’s own sins. See
[[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]].

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Covering an offense — Proverbs 17:9

*"He who covers an offense promotes love; but he who repeats a matter separates best friends."* Covering is the refusal to retell; 28:13 keeps the limit on the other side, *"He who conceals his sins doesn't prosper."* See [[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]].

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:11

Discretion delays anger and makes overlooking an offense honorable (11). Like 17:9's covering, this
describes a response to another person's wrong, not a denial that it happened. The same chapter
condemns false witnesses (5, 9, 28) and praises instruction (20, 25, 27), so forbearance does not
erase truth or correction. See [[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]]
.

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

## Key passages

- [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] — Proverbs 19:11; Overlooking an offense is called
  glory, alongside continued correction and truthful judgment.

- [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]] — Proverbs 17:9.

- [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]] — Proverbs 10:12, 18; Love’s covering of wrongs offers an image
  of relational forbearance, not an explicit declaration of divine pardon.

- [[The Covenant Renewed]] — Exodus 34:1–28; Pardon sought after God declares forgiveness without erasing
  judgment.

- [[Show Me Your Glory]] — Exodus 33:1–23; Restored presence is not a blanket cancellation of
  consequences.

- [[The Golden Calf]] — Exodus 32:1–35; Moses explicitly asks God to forgive the people after confessing their great sin (32:31–32).

- [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]] — Genesis 50:15–21; **the first request for forgiveness between
  human beings in Scripture, and the granting of it**
- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:1–15; **the same reconciliation without the word**, and with
  the crime reinterpreted rather than named
- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:18–34; [[Judah]] offering himself in
  [[Benjamin]]'s place, which is what makes the disclosure possible
- [[The Reconciliation with Esau]] — Genesis 33:1–20; **an embrace with no confession, no request,
  and no word for what happened**
- [[Cain and Abel]] — Genesis 4:1–16; **the alternative to forgiveness: a mark, and a country east of
  Eden**
- [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] — Genesis 4:23–24; Lamech's seventy-sevenfold, the
  counter-text
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 7:21, ***"Why do you not pardon (tiśśāʾ) my disobedience, and
  take away (taʿăbîr) my iniquity?"*** — the two verbs of Micah 7:18 and the creed of Exodus 34:7,
  asked of God as a question by a man who has confessed no specific sin. The verb *ʿābar* returns at
  Zechariah 3:4, in the accuser's other courtroom scene, as God's own act: *"I have caused your
  iniquity to pass from you."* See
  [[Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives]]
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:6, on one reading of *yaššeh*: ***"God has forgotten some of your iniquity."***
  **The only mention of God's forgetting of sin in the friends' speeches, and it is an accusation** —
  the forgetting proves the debt is larger than what has been paid. Set against Job's own plea for a
  pardon he was not getting (7:21)
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 33:24–28; the sinner on the edge of the pit, a mediator beside him, and God saying
  *"Deliver him from going down to the pit, I have found a ransom"* (33:24); the man then sings, *"I have
  sinned … He has redeemed my soul from going into the pit"* (33:27–28). Grace before the confession, and
  the confession sung in public.
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:17; **forgiveness asked of a man.** Pharaoh says to Moses and Aaron,
  ***"please forgive my sin"***, *śāʾ nāʾ ḥaṭṭāʾtî*, *lift, please, my sin* — **the words of Joseph's
  brothers to Joseph**, *śāʾ nāʾ pešaʿ ʾaḥêkā wəḥaṭṭāʾtām* (Genesis 50:17, WLC). Both requests go to the
  injured man, not to God; Pharaoh adds *"and pray to Yahweh your God"*. **Joseph wept and forgave;
  Moses prays and the locusts go, but the text says nothing of forgiveness, and the heart is hardened
  again** (10:20)
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; *"he will not pardon your disobedience"*.
