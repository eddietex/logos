# War

## Definition / overview

Organized violence between peoples — which the Bible introduces late, reports without praising, and
then never stops narrating.

The word *milḥāmâ* first occurs at Genesis 14:2: *"they made war with Bera, king of Sodom."* Ten
chapters of Genesis have passed with a murder (4:8), a boast about killing (4:23–24), a world
*"filled with violence"* (6:11), and a *gibbōr* who founds kingdoms (10:8–10) — but no armies until
here. When war arrives it is between nine foreign kings over twelve years of unpaid tribute, and the
man the book is about is not in it. He joins only when it takes a member of his household. See
[[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].

**Genesis is conspicuously restrained about it.** The battle of the four kings against the five gets
one verse (14:10). Abram's night attack gets one clause: *"He divided himself against them by night,
he and his servants, and struck them"* (14:15). The chapter then spends eight verses on what he does
with the proceeds. Nothing is said about whether he was right to go, whether God approved, or how
many died — and God does not speak in the chapter at all. The evaluation the reader wants is exactly
what is withheld, which is Genesis's habit with its patriarchs.

## Development across Scripture

- **The one war in Genesis Abram fights is a rescue, and he keeps nothing from it.** No territory,
  no plunder, no captives, no vassals. Set against the imperial reprisal that opens the chapter —
  tribute, rebellion, punitive campaign — the contrast is the chapter's political argument: he beats
  four kings and refuses to become one. See [[Kingship and Empire]] and
  [[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].
- **The other Genesis violence is a massacre, and it is condemned.** Simeon and Levi at Shechem
  (34:25–30), which Jacob calls a thing that has *"troubled me, to make me odious to the inhabitants
  of the land"* and curses on his deathbed: *"Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce"* (49:5–7).
  Genesis has one war and one atrocity, and grades only the second.
- **At the Exodus the fighting is God's and Israel stands still.** *"Yahweh will fight for you, and
  you shall be still"* (Exodus 14:14); *"Yahweh is a man of war"* (15:3). The conquest then makes
  Israel the instrument, under the *ḥērem* — the devoting of a city to destruction (Deuteronomy
  7:1–5; 20:16–18; Joshua 6–11) — which is the hardest material in the Old Testament and which
  Deuteronomy itself justifies not by Israel's righteousness but by the Amorites' iniquity
  (Deuteronomy 9:4–5; compare Genesis 15:16). The wiki records that this is contested ground and
  does not resolve it.
- **Even the conquest laws are bounded.** Deuteronomy 20 requires an offer of terms first, exempts
  the newly married, the new householder, the planter of a vineyard, and the afraid, and forbids
  cutting down fruit trees for siege works — *"for is the tree of the field man, that it should be
  besieged by you?"* (20:19).
- **The kings are told not to trust it.** *"A king is not saved by the multitude of an army"*
  (Psalm 33:16); *"Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of Yahweh our
  God"* (Psalm 20:7); the law's king may not multiply horses (Deuteronomy 17:16). David, the Bible's
  most successful soldier, is refused the temple for exactly this: *"You have shed blood abundantly,
  and have made great wars"* (1 Chronicles 22:8).
- **The prophets make the end of war the shape of the future.** *"They shall beat their swords into
  plowshares … neither shall they learn war any more"* (Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3); the bow cut off and
  peace spoken to the nations (Zechariah 9:10); *"Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit"*
  (Zechariah 4:6).
- **And the New Testament reassigns the weapons.** *"All those who take the sword will die by the
  sword"* (Matthew 26:52); *"our wrestling is not against flesh and blood"* (Ephesians 6:12); *"the
  weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh"* (2 Corinthians 10:4). Revelation keeps the imagery of
  battle and gives the rider a sword that comes out of his mouth (19:15, 21).

## Key passages

- Genesis 14:1–17 — the first war, [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]]
- [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] — Genesis 21:22, 32, the theme's negative case. **Phicol, *the captain
  of his army*, is named twice and does nothing**; he is the standing reminder of what the king could
  do instead, and the entire function of his rank in the scene is that it is not used. A seized well,
  a formal complaint, and a general in the room — settled with sheep, seven lambs, and an oath
- Genesis 34:25–30; 49:5–7 — Shechem, and the curse on it
- Exodus 14:14; 15:3 — Yahweh fights
- Deuteronomy 20:1–20 — the laws of war, with their exemptions
- Deuteronomy 7:1–5; Joshua 6:17–21 — the *ḥērem*
- 1 Samuel 30:1–25 — David's rescue raid and his rule about the spoil
- Psalm 20:7; 33:16–17; 1 Chronicles 22:8 — the distrust of arms
- Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3; Zechariah 9:10 — the swords beaten down
- Matthew 26:52; 2 Corinthians 10:3–5; Ephesians 6:10–17 — the weapons reassigned
