# War

## Definition / overview

### Possession without merit — Deuteronomy 9:1–6

Divine advance and human action belong together: Yahweh brings the nations down and Israel drives them out (3). Explicit tension: 9:3 says they perish quickly, while 7:22 says removal is little by little. Decisive encounters within gradual occupation are a possible reading, not an explanation supplied by either verse.

See [[Not Because of Your Righteousness]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 9:1–6.


### Fullness and remembrance — Deuteronomy 6:10–25

Thrusting out enemies remains part of possessing the good land (18–19). The text presents this as
Yahweh’s promise; it gives no battle procedure here and does not erase the displacement behind the
inherited cities.

See [[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 6:10–25.

### Setting of the instruction — Deuteronomy 4:44–49

The narrator recalls the defeat of Sihon and possession of the two Amorite kingdoms (46–47). This summary does not repeat the civilian deaths explicitly reported in 2:34 and 3:6; its brevity does not contradict or erase them.

See [[The Setting of the Covenant Instruction]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:44–49.

### Eastern inheritance — Deuteronomy 3:12–22

Armed men cross while families and livestock stay in the granted cities (18–20). Yahweh’s fighting
for Israel (22) accompanies commanded human participation rather than replacing it.

See [[The Eastern Inheritance and the Charge to Joshua]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:12–22.

### Og’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 3:1–11

Sixty fortified cities and many unwalled villages fall (4–5). The account explicitly includes the
destruction of women and little ones while livestock and goods are retained as plunder (6–7); it
cannot be reduced to combat against soldiers.

See [[The Defeat of Og Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:1–11.

### Sihon’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 2:24–37

Battle at Jahaz leads to the destruction of every inhabited city, expressly including women and
little ones, with no survivors reported (32–34). Livestock and goods become spoil (35). This is not
a bloodless transfer or a claim limited to combatants; Ammon remains outside the permitted conquest
(37).

See [[The Defeat of Sihon Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:24–37.

### Protected neighbors and the wilderness generation — Deuteronomy 2:1–23

Israel must not contend with the descendants of Esau or Lot (5, 9, 19). The asides recall other
peoples’ violent displacement, so this is a restriction on these campaigns rather than a universal
ban on warfare.

See [[Around Seir and Past Moab and Ammon]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:1–23.

### The sentence and the ascent — Deuteronomy 1:34–46

Weapons and willingness to fight cannot substitute for Yahweh’s presence (41–44). The Amorites
pursue Israel like bees; no casualty count is supplied.

See [[The Sentence and the Presumptuous Ascent]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:34–46; Numbers 14 and 20 for comparisons.

### The spies and the refusal — Deuteronomy 1:19–33

The people fear taller peoples, cities *fortified up to the sky*, and the Anakim (28). Moses' answer is *Yahweh your God who goes before you, he will fight for you* (30), recalling the word at the sea (Exodus 14:14). The war is refused before it begins.

See [[The Spies and the Refusal to Go Up]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:19–33.

### Moses’ address in Moab — Deuteronomy 1:1–5

The defeats of Sihon and Og are recalled as completed events that precede the teaching (4). This
notice compresses the campaigns narrated in Numbers 21:21–35.

See [[The Words of Moses Beyond the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:1–5.

### Drive out the inhabitants — Numbers 33:50–56

Israel is commanded to drive out all the inhabitants (52). The text envisages coercive
dispossession and warns of continuing harassment (55), but narrates no battle and supplies
no casualty list or command here to kill each inhabitant. Those distinctions should not
soften the expulsion it actually commands.

See [[Drive Out the Inhabitants]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 33:50–56.

### The second census — Numbers 26:1–65

The 601,730 are those eligible for war from twenty upward (2, 51), not the entire population. Levi
uses another age threshold and another total.

See [[The Second Census]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 26.


### Baal Peor — Numbers 25:1–18

The closing command to harass and strike Midian grounds subsequent hostility in the Peor deception
(16–18). No military campaign is narrated in this unit.

See [[Baal Peor and the Zeal of Phinehas]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 25:1–18.


Organized violence between peoples — which the Bible introduces late, reports without praising, and
then never stops narrating.

The word *milḥāmâ* first occurs at Genesis 14:2: *"they made war with Bera, king of Sodom."* Ten
chapters of Genesis have passed with a murder (4:8), a boast about killing (4:23–24), a world
*"filled with violence"* (6:11), and a *gibbōr* who founds kingdoms (10:8–10) — but no armies until
here. When war arrives it is between nine foreign kings over twelve years of unpaid tribute, and the
man the book is about is not in it. He joins only when it takes a member of his household. See
[[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].

**Genesis is conspicuously restrained about it.** The battle of the four kings against the five gets
one verse (14:10). Abram's night attack gets one clause: *"He divided himself against them by night,
he and his servants, and struck them"* (14:15). The chapter then spends eight verses on what he does
with the proceeds. Nothing is said about whether he was right to go, whether God approved, or how
many died — and God does not speak in the chapter at all. The evaluation the reader wants is exactly
what is withheld, which is Genesis's habit with its patriarchs.

## Development across Scripture

### Blessing and removal — Deuteronomy 7:12–26

Yahweh fights through the hornet (20), confusion (23), and the delivery of kings (24). Israel
consumes the peoples delivered to it (16), but only *little by little*, lest the wild animals
increase (22). The destruction is complete in extent but gradual in pace (22–24).

See [[Blessing and the Removal of the Nations]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:12–26.

### Chosen in love — Deuteronomy 7:1–11

The seven nations are to be utterly destroyed after Yahweh delivers them to Israel; neither
covenant nor mercy is allowed (1–2). Destruction of worship objects follows separately (5), so the
command cannot be reduced to removing idols. This is prospective instruction, not a completed
battle report.

See [[A People Chosen in Love]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:1–11.

### Balaam’s last oracles — Numbers 23:27–24:25

Blessing includes violent victory over adversaries (24:8, 17–20), not merely fertility and peace.
The later sayings widen the horizon to captivity and the destruction of conquering powers.

See [[Balaam's Last Oracles]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 23:27–24:25.


### The first two oracles — Numbers 22:36–23:26

The second oracle ends with Israel as a lion consuming prey and the blood of the slain (23:24). This
is martial imagery promising power, not a report of a new battle in this unit.

See [[Balaam's First Two Oracles]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 22:36–23:26.


### Sihon and Og — Numbers 21:21–35

The request for transit becomes battle when Sihon attacks (21–24). Og also comes out to fight (33);
Yahweh promises victory and the account ends with no survivors and possession of the land (34–35).
These are lethal conquests, not merely diplomatic successes.

See [[Sihon and Og]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:21–35.


### Arad and Hormah — Numbers 21:1–3

An enemy attack takes captives; Israel’s vow and Yahweh’s answer lead to destruction
of both the Canaanites and their cities. Neither tactics nor the captives’ fate is
reported. Divine deliverance and lethal destruction stand together in the account.

See [[Arad and Hormah]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:1–3.

### Edom refuses passage — Numbers 20:14–21

Edom threatens the sword and comes out in force; Israel withdraws (18–21). Military
coercion succeeds without a battle being narrated.

See [[Edom Refuses Passage]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 20:14–21.


### Vows, valuations, and devoted things — Leviticus 27:1–34

Leviticus never mentions war here, but *ḥērem* (28–29) is the word of the conquest texts. See
[[Devoted to Destruction - Leviticus 27.28-29, Deuteronomy 7, Joshua 6-7, and 1 Samuel 15]].

See [[Vows, Valuations, and Devoted Things]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 27.

### Covenant warnings and remembered promises — Leviticus 26:14–46

War reverses: Israel is struck before enemies, flees when no one pursues, is besieged, and at last
falls to a driven leaf (17, 25, 36–37). The sword is *brought* and *drawn* by Yahweh himself (25, 33).

See [[Covenant Warnings and Remembered Promises]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Blessings for obedience — Leviticus 26:1–13

Peace and the sword stand side by side: no sword through the land (6), enemies falling by the sword
(7–8). Five chasing a hundred gives no credit to skill. See
[[Five Shall Chase a Hundred - Leviticus 26.8, Deuteronomy 32.30, and Joshua 23.10]].

See [[Blessings for Obedience]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

- **The one war in Genesis Abram fights is a rescue, and he keeps nothing from it.** No territory,
  no plunder, no captives, no vassals. Set against the imperial reprisal that opens the chapter —
  tribute, rebellion, punitive campaign — the contrast is the chapter's political argument: he beats
  four kings and refuses to become one. See [[Kingship and Empire]] and
  [[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].
- **The other Genesis violence is a massacre, and it is condemned.** Simeon and Levi at Shechem
  (34:25–30), which Jacob calls a thing that has *"troubled me, to make me odious to the inhabitants
  of the land"* and curses on his deathbed: *"Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce"* (49:5–7).
  Genesis has one war and one atrocity, and grades only the second.
- **At the Exodus the fighting is God's and Israel stands still.** *"Yahweh will fight for you, and
  you shall be still"* (Exodus 14:14); *"Yahweh is a man of war"* (15:3). The conquest then makes
  Israel the instrument, under the *ḥērem* — the devoting of a city to destruction (Deuteronomy
  7:1–5; 20:16–18; Joshua 6–11) — which is the hardest material in the Old Testament and which
  Deuteronomy itself justifies not by Israel's righteousness but by the Amorites' iniquity
  (Deuteronomy 9:4–5; compare Genesis 15:16). The wiki records that this is contested ground and
  does not resolve it.
- **Even the conquest laws are bounded.** Deuteronomy 20 requires an offer of terms first, exempts
  the newly married, the new householder, the planter of a vineyard, and the afraid, and forbids
  cutting down fruit trees for siege works — *"for is the tree of the field man, that it should be
  besieged by you?"* (20:19).
- **The kings are told not to trust it.** *"A king is not saved by the multitude of an army"*
  (Psalm 33:16); *"Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of Yahweh our
  God"* (Psalm 20:7); the law's king may not multiply horses (Deuteronomy 17:16). David, the Bible's
  most successful soldier, is refused the temple for exactly this: *"You have shed blood abundantly,
  and have made great wars"* (1 Chronicles 22:8).
- **The prophets make the end of war the shape of the future.** *"They shall beat their swords into
  plowshares … neither shall they learn war any more"* (Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3); the bow cut off and
  peace spoken to the nations (Zechariah 9:10); *"Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit"*
  (Zechariah 4:6).
- **And the New Testament reassigns the weapons.** *"All those who take the sword will die by the
  sword"* (Matthew 26:52); *"our wrestling is not against flesh and blood"* (Ephesians 6:12); *"the
  weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh"* (2 Corinthians 10:4). Revelation keeps the imagery of
  battle and gives the rider a sword that comes out of his mouth (19:15, 21).
- **In Exodus Israel's first war comes before its law, and it is fought with a raised rod.** At the sea God
  fought and Israel was *"still"* (14:14); at Rephidim Israel fights and the rod decides it (17:8–13). The war is
  then made permanent and written down — *"from generation to generation"* (17:16) — and the next time *milḥāmâ*
  is heard in Exodus it is Joshua mistaking the calf's festival for *"the noise of war in the camp"* (32:17; WLC;
  *not yet ingested*). See [[The Battle with Amalek]].

### God fights ahead of Israel — Exodus 23:20–33

The conquest is promised almost entirely as God's work: *"I will be an enemy to your enemies"*
(23:22), his terror sent ahead, the peoples confused (*wəhammōtî*, the panic verb of 14:24; MAM),
their backs turned, and the hornet driving them out (23:27–28). Israel's own part is named at the end
of 23:31: *"you shall drive them out before you."* The pace is set for the land's sake — *"little by
little"*, lest it become desolate (23:29–30). Joshua 24:12 later looks back on the hornet *"not with
your sword, nor with your bow"* (WEB; *not yet ingested*). See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] and
[[Little by Little - The Hornet, the Snare, and the Land Not Taken]].

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:18, 24

Verse 18 applies counsel to war: plans are established by advice and war requires wise
guidance. Verse 24's question about understanding one's divinely directed way limits the
planner's confidence. The saying names neither a particular campaign nor a just-war criterion,
and does not guarantee victory for every advised undertaking.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### The prepared horse — Proverbs 21:22, 31

The wise man scales the city of the mighty (22), and the horse is prepared for battle while victory
is with Yahweh (31). Preparation is assumed; its outcome is not owned. See
[[Victory Is with Yahweh - Proverbs 21.31, Exodus 15, and Psalm 20.7]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### War by wise guidance — Proverbs 24:5–6

*"A wise man has great power … for by wise guidance you wage your war; and victory is in many
advisors."* Its second line repeats 11:14b word for word in Hebrew, and its first reworks 20:18. The
argument is that wisdom outweighs strength because even war is won by counsel. See
[[A Multitude of Counselors - Proverbs 11.14 and Exodus 18]].

See [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:3–22.

### A time for every purpose — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

The final pair is a time for war and a time for peace (8). War belongs to the human world
the poem describes; no army, enemy, campaign, or command to fight is named. The claim that
God makes everything beautiful in its time (11) must not be turned into approval of any
particular war: the passage also denies humans a complete view of God's work.

See [[A Time for Every Purpose]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

### No discharge in war — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

Verse 8 places no discharge in war beside limits on containing the spirit and controlling
the day of death. No campaign, army, legal exemption, or command to fight is specified.
Reading the war clause as an illustration of mortality's inescapability is possible,
but the verse does not explicitly call death a war. Job 14:14 imagines waiting through
warfare for release; the connection compares verified WEB language without claiming an
identical Hebrew term. See [[No Power over the Day of Death - Job 14 and Ecclesiastes 8.8]].

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

Battle supplies one of five cases in which ability does not secure the outcome (11).
No war, army, or combatant is named. Proverbs 21:31 attributes victory to Yahweh despite
the prepared horse; Ecclesiastes names time and chance. The related limits on strength
are retained without making the two explanations identical. See
[[Time and Chance and the Prepared Horse]].

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

A great king besieges a little city with few men and builds great bulwarks against it (9:14);
a poor wise man delivers it (15). *"Wisdom is better than weapons of war"* (18). The wisdom
tradition's preference for counsel over force (Proverbs 21:22; 24:5–6) is affirmed, with the
added observation that the deliverer is forgotten. See
[[Wisdom Better Than Strength - Proverbs 21.22, 24.5-6, and Ecclesiastes 9.13-18]].

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Swords at a wedding — Song of Solomon 3:7–8

Sixty mighty men escort Solomon's carriage. *"They all handle the sword, and are expert in war. Every
man has his sword on his thigh, because of fear in the night"* (3:8). The Hebrew *mǝlummǝḏê
milḥāmâ*, *trained in war*, and *gibbōrîm*, *mighty men* (Sefaria), are the vocabulary of soldiers.
Here they guard a wedding procession; the danger, *paḥaḏ ballêlôṯ*, *fear in the nights*, is not
named. Psalm 45:3 gives the bridegroom king himself a sword on his thigh. See
[[A Royal Wedding Song - Psalm 45 and Song of Solomon 3.6-11]].

See [[Solomon's Wedding Procession]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 3:6–11; Hebrew read from
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### A tower hung with shields — Song of Solomon 4:4

*"Your neck is like David's tower built for an armory, whereon a thousand shields hang, all the shields
of the mighty men"* (4:4). Weapons become an image of beauty: height, straightness, perhaps
ornaments like a row of shields. *Gibbōrîm*, the *mighty men* of Solomon's escort (3:7), return as
the owners of the shields. Ezekiel 27:11 says of Tyre that soldiers *"hung their shields on your walls
all around. They have perfected your beauty"* (WEB checked at bible-api.com; Hebrew *šilṭêhem*,
Sefaria), the same picture of shields as a city's adornment.

See [[You Are All Beautiful]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 4:1–7; comparison sources are
identified on the passage page.

### Awesome as an army with banners — Song of Solomon 6:4–10

The army comparison occurs at both ends of the unit (6:4, 10). Between them the woman's
eyes overcome the man (6:5). Martial force becomes a way of praising her formidable
presence, following the tower and shields of 4:4. No attack, military command, or battle
is narrated; the image does not make the beloved an enemy.

See [[My Dove Is Unique]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 6:4–10; comparisons use the stored WEB
Song 1:5–6; 4:1–9 and Proverbs 31:28–31 where noted.

### The census — Numbers 1:1–54

Numbers opens with a muster. *All who are able to go out to war* (3) translates *kol-yōṣēʾ ṣābāʾ*, and *ṣābāʾ* occurs sixteen times in the chapter (WLC). Exodus 12:41 already called Israel *Yahweh's armies*. This is the first time the armies are counted by tribe, and the one tribe left out guards the sanctuary.

See [[The Census in the Wilderness of Sinai]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 1.

### The camp — Numbers 2:1–34

The muster becomes a formation. Four camps of three stand *according to their divisions*, each under a standard, each with a set turn on the march, and *ṣābāʾ* occurs twenty times (WLC). The whole of it is drawn up around a tent, and nobody fights in the chapter. See [[By His Own Standard - Degel in Numbers and the Song of Solomon]].

See [[The Camp Around the Tent]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 2.

### Blown for battle — Numbers 10:9

The alarm of the march is also the alarm of war *in your land*. It is not a tactic in the verse. Its result is that Israel is *remembered before Yahweh* and saved. Numbers 31:6 and 2 Chronicles 13:12–15 show priests with the trumpets at a battle.

See [[The Two Silver Trumpets]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 10.

### Armies on the road — Numbers 10:14–28, 35

Each tribe goes *according to their armies*, under a commander, and Moses prays for Yahweh's enemies to scatter (35). No battle comes in the unit. See [[Rise Up, Yahweh - The Ark's Cry in Numbers 10, Psalm 68, and Psalm 132]].

See [[The March from Sinai]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 10.

### The spies — Numbers 13

Moses' questions are partly a commander's: strong or weak, few or many, camps or strongholds
(18–19). *Be courageous* (20) is the verb *ḥāzaq*. The answer in verse 28 is about fortified cities.
Caleb's *possess it* and the others' *stronger than we* are the first open disagreement over whether
to fight for the land.

See [[The Twelve Spies]].

### Going up alone — Numbers 14

The first battle Israel loses is one it chose for itself, with the ark and Moses left behind. See [[The Defeat at Hormah]].

### The war against Midian — Numbers 31

A war commanded as vengeance for Peor: a thousand from each tribe, a priest with the trumpets, and a
battle told in two verses (1–8). Most of the chapter is the aftermath: Moses' anger that the women were
spared and his order to kill the boys and the women who had lain with a man (14–18), purification of
soldiers and spoil (19–24), the plunder halved between fighters and congregation (25–47), and the
officers' gold brought *to make atonement for our souls* after a count in which *there lacks not one man
of us* (49–50). The text reports Moses' order without comment; the wiki does not soften it. See
[[Half to the Fighters - Numbers 31 and David's Statute at Ziklag]].

See [[The War Against Midian]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 31.

### Reuben, Gad, and the Land Beyond the Jordan — Numbers 32:1–42

The two eastern tribes are bound to go *armed before Yahweh to the war* (20) until the land is
*subdued before Yahweh* (22). Moses' repeated *before Yahweh* makes the conquest Yahweh's war; the tribes take
up the phrase in their second answer (27, 32). The chapter ends with three Manassite conquests in Gilead
(39–42).

See [[Reuben, Gad, and the Land Beyond the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 32.

  under forced labour** (49:15). **Genesis narrates none of it**: there is no battle of Jacob's, no
  archer in [[Joseph]]'s story, and the one massacre the book does narrate (34:25–29) is the thing
  49:5–7 curses. **The chapter's vocabulary is the conquest's, spoken in Egypt before any of it**
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:22–23; 39:19–25; **weapons that belong to God.** The hail kept
  *"against the day of battle and war"* (38:23), as at Joshua 10:11; and the war-horse that *"mocks at
  fear"* and smells the battle afar off (39:22, 25) — the army's animal, whose courage God asks whether
  Job gave.
- [[Behemoth and Leviathan]] — Job 41:8, 25–29; **the whole armoury useless.** *"Remember the battle, and do so
  no more"* (41:8); sword, spear, dart, pointed shaft, arrow, sling-stone, club and javelin listed and
  dismissed — *"He counts iron as straw; and brass as rotten wood"* (41:27), *"He laughs at the rushing of the
  javelin"* (41:29).
- [[Aaron's Staff Before Pharaoh]] — Exodus 7:4; ***ṣibʾōtay ʾet-ʿammî***, *"my armies, my
  people"* — **the first time God calls Israel *my armies***, before a single Israelite has borne arms.
  In Exodus *ṣābāʾ* occurs only at 6:26; 7:4; 12:17, 41, 51 (WLC)
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:37, 41, 51; **a slave column counted and named as an army.** *"About six
  hundred thousand on foot"* — *raglî*, the foot-soldier's word, in the Torah only here and Numbers 11:21
  (WLC) — and *"all of Yahweh's armies went out"*, *ṣibʾôt YHWH*: the first time the narrator calls Israel
  Yahweh's armies (7:4 was God's *"my armies"*). **Israel carries plunder and dough, and fights no one**
- [[The Crossing of the Sea]] — Exodus 13:17; 14:14, 24–25; **the first war Yahweh fights.** God avoids the
  Philistine road *"Lest perhaps the people change their minds when they see war"* (13:17) — *milḥāmâ*,
  Pharaoh's word at 1:10 — and Moses says ***"Yahweh will fight for you, and you shall be still"*** (14:14),
  the first time Yahweh is the subject of *lāḥam* (WLC lemma search, Torah). **Israel does not fight**; the
  Egyptians confess *"Yahweh fights for them"* (14:25). The panic sent on the army, *hāmam* (14:24), is the
  verb of 23:27's promise, *"I will … confuse all the people to whom you come"* (WLC). Deuteronomy builds on
  14:14: *"he will fight for you, according to all that he did for you in Egypt"* (1:30, WEB; *not yet
  ingested*)
- [[The Song of the Sea]] — Exodus 15:3, 9; **the warrior named.** *"Yahweh is a man of war. Yahweh is his name"* — the
  *milḥāmâ* of Pharaoh's fear (1:10) and of the road God avoided (13:17) becomes a title. The enemy's six
  first-person verbs — *"I will pursue. I will overtake. I will divide the plunder … I will draw my sword"*
  (15:9) — are answered by one breath (15:10).
- [[The Battle with Amalek]] — Exodus 17:8–16; **the first war Israel fights.** *"Then Amalek came and fought
  with Israel"* — the first human enemy to *lāḥam* against Israel (WLC). Where the sea had *"Yahweh will fight for
  you, and you shall be still"* (14:14), Rephidim has *"Choose men for us, and go out, fight"* (17:9); the outcome
  follows *"God's rod"* in Moses' raised hand, and Joshua wins *"with the edge of the sword"*, the phrase of the
  massacre at Shechem (Genesis 34:26; WLC). **The war is not ended but declared permanent**: *"Yahweh will have
  war (milḥāmâ) with Amalek from generation to generation"* (17:16). The road God chose so Israel would not
  *"see war"* (13:17) did not avoid this one
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; God's terror, confusion, and hornet; a conquest *"little by little"*.
- [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]] — Proverbs 24:5–6; War waged by wise guidance; victory in many advisors.
- [[My Dove Is Unique]] — Song of Solomon 6:4–10; awesome as an army with banners.
- [[The Census in the Wilderness of Sinai]] — Numbers 1:1–54; the muster of those able to go out to war.
- [[The Camp Around the Tent]] — Numbers 2:1–34; the four camps in formation.
- [[The Two Silver Trumpets]] — Numbers 10:5–9; march alarms, and the alarm of war in the land.
- [[The March from Sinai]] — Numbers 10:14–28, 35; the armies march, and the ark goes out against Yahweh's enemies.
- [[The Twelve Spies]] — Numbers 13:17–20, 28–31; a reconnaissance, and a dispute about fighting
- [[The Defeat at Hormah]] — Numbers 14:42–45; battle without the ark or Moses
- [[The War Against Midian]] — Numbers 31:1–54; the war of vengeance and its aftermath.
- [[Reuben, Gad, and the Land Beyond the Jordan]] — Numbers 32:1–42; armed service *before Yahweh* as the price of land.
