# Sin

## Definition / overview

Human action against God's word, and the condition that follows from it. Genesis narrates the
first instance in [[The Fall]] without ever using the word: Hebrew *ḥaṭṭāʾt* does not appear until
4:7, and even the term *fall* is later theology rather than the text's own. What Genesis 3 gives
instead is an anatomy, in order:

1. **A question about God's word.** *"Has God really said…?"* (3:1) — not a denial but a
   destabilizing, and the command is misquoted on both sides before it is broken. The serpent
   makes it harsher than it was; the woman makes it vaguer, drops its generosity, adds a fence,
   and softens its penalty (3:2–3).
2. **A suspicion about God's motive.** *"For God knows that in the day you eat it… you will be
   like God"* (3:5). The prohibition is recast as protectionism. Everything the man and woman have
   been given is left unmentioned, and the one withheld thing becomes the whole picture.
3. **Appetite, licensed by the suspicion.** *"Good for food… a delight to the eyes… to be desired
   to make one wise"* (3:6). The desire is not for something evil; the tree really is all three
   things.
4. **The act, and its immediate sharing.** She takes, eats, and gives to her husband *"with
   her"*, and he eats. The first sin is not private in the same verse it is committed.
5. **Concealment, then blame.** Fig leaves, then trees, then *"the woman whom you gave to be with
   me"* (3:12) and *"the serpent deceived me"* (3:13). Hiding from each other, from God, and from
   responsibility, in that sequence.

The content of the sin is not appetite or curiosity but **the claim to decide good and evil for
oneself** — which is what *"you will be like God, knowing good and evil"* offers, and what 3:22
confirms was in fact obtained. Autonomy is the prize, and it is a real one; the deception is only
about its cost.

**The word arrives in [[Cain and Abel]]**, one chapter after the act it is usually attached to.
*Ḥaṭṭāʾt* — whose root sense is *missing* a mark — appears first at 4:7, and Genesis introduces it
not as a definition but as an animal:

> **4:7** If you do well, won’t it be lifted up? If you don’t do well, sin crouches at the door.
> Its desire is for you, but you are to rule over it.

Four things about that first occurrence set the theme's terms.

- **It is personified, and external.** The participle *rōbēṣ*, "crouching", is what a beast does
  lying in wait, and it is masculine where *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is feminine — a grammatical mismatch that
  leaves the crouching thing feeling like a separate creature at the threshold. Genesis 3 gave sin
  an anatomy; Genesis 4 gives it a posture.
- **It is spoken before the act, to the person about to commit it.** Cain is warned. Nobody in
  Genesis 3 was.
- **It is stated to be resistible.** *"You are to rule over it"* is the one clause in a
  syntactically difficult verse that is not difficult at all. Whatever Genesis later says about the
  human heart, its first use of the word *sin* comes attached to a command that assumes it can be
  mastered.
- **It reuses the vocabulary of 3:16.** *Tešûqâ* ("desire") and *māšal* ("rule"), the rarest word
  pair in the Hebrew Bible, describing here an appetite to dominate — see
  [[Desire and Rule - Genesis 3.16 and 4.7]].

What follows in the same passage is the theme's first escalation: warned, Cain does it anyway, and
the second recorded sin in Scripture is murder. Genesis then shows the thing spreading before it
defines it; by 6:5 *"every imagination of the thoughts of [man's] heart was continually only
evil"*, and the flood follows. The doctrine of an inherited condition
is drawn from these narratives, chiefly by Paul; it is not stated in them. See
[[The First Adam and the Last Adam]] for where the universal claim actually comes from and how it
has been argued.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 3:1–7** — the first disobedience, told without the vocabulary of sin.
- **Genesis 3:8–13** — hiding and blame as the first consequences, ahead of any sentence.
- **Genesis 4:7** — the word itself, personified: *"sin crouches at the door. Its desire is for
  you, but you are to rule over it"*, reusing the *tešûqâ* of 3:16, and addressed to a man who has
  not yet acted.
- **Genesis 4:8–16** — the first murder, three verses after the first warning; a lie added to the
  act, and the first curse pronounced on a person.
- **Genesis 4:19–24** — the theme's first stretch with no divine word in it at all. Nothing in
  Lamech's five verses is called sin, no one is warned, and no sentence follows; the reader is left
  to notice that the thing 4:7 said could be ruled is now being sung about. What has changed since
  4:8 is not the act but the attitude — Cain killed and hid, and his descendant kills and makes a
  poem of it. Genesis shows the condition spreading before it has any vocabulary for it. See
  [[Vengeance]].
- **Genesis 6:5** — the verdict, and the theme's turn from acts to the interior. *"Yahweh saw that
  the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of man's
  heart was continually only evil."* Three universalizing words in one clause — **every**,
  **continually**, **only** — and the noun is *yēṣer*, from *yāṣar*, the potter's verb used of God
  forming the man from dust at 2:7: the *shaping* or bent of the mind. Two large traditions start
  here. In rabbinic thought the *yēṣer hārāʿ*, the evil inclination, becomes a full anthropology,
  paired with a good inclination and understood as an impulse to be mastered — which is 4:7's
  *"you are to rule over it"* systematized. In Christian theology the verse is a principal support
  for original sin and total depravity. What it asserts is the universality, the interiority, and
  the constancy of evil in that generation; what it does not assert is a mechanism of transmission,
  which is Romans 5:12's contribution and not this verse's. See
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- **Genesis 6:11–13** — the exterior, at last. Having diagnosed the heart, Genesis names a deed
  word: *ḥāmās*, **violence**, twice (6:11, 13), and adds that *"all flesh had corrupted their way
  on the earth"* (6:12). This is the first time the narrative charges a specific social evil rather
  than a condition, and it closes the circuit the primeval history has been building — the *yēṣer*
  of 6:5 produces the *ḥāmās* of 6:11, which is Lamech's boast in
  [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] gone universal. Note the verb the charge is made with:
  the earth *corrupted* itself (*šāḥat*, 6:11–12), and God's sentence uses the same word — *"I will
  destroy them"* (*šāḥat*, 6:13, 17). Sin and its punishment are, at this one point in Scripture,
  the same act described twice. See [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 8:21** — the same verdict after the flood, almost word for word, and drawn on for the
  opposite conclusion: because the human heart is evil from youth, God will *not* strike the earth
  again. The fact does not change; what is done about it does — which is also the Bible's first
  statement that a judgment did not fix what it was sent against. See [[The Waters Recede]].
- **Genesis 9:20–25** — 8:21's verdict demonstrated on the best man alive, eight verses after it is
  used as a reason for mercy. And demonstrated without a word of comment: Genesis does not call the
  drunkenness a sin, does not call Ham's seeing a sin, and gives no divine reaction to either. The
  moral machinery has changed shape. In Genesis 3 and 4 an offence is followed by God arriving,
  questioning, and sentencing; here nobody arrives, and what supplies the sentence instead is an
  offended human being pronouncing a curse on a third party. The narrator's silence is his standing
  practice — he keeps it over Lamech's boast at 4:23–24 too — but it is worth naming, because a reader
  looking for the passage's verdict will not find one. See [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].
- **Exodus 9:27, 34 — the confession that relapses.** Pharaoh is the first person in Exodus to say
  *"I have sinned"* (*ḥāṭāʾtî*), and he says it with Abraham's categories of righteous and wicked.
  When the hail stops, *"he sinned yet more"*, with the same verb. **The confession is accurate and
  lasts exactly as long as the danger.** God's hardening (9:12) and Pharaoh's sinning (9:34) stand in
  the same chapter, and the text does not let either cancel the other. See
  [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: the sacrificial system as sin's remedy (Leviticus
  4–5, 16); *"Behold, I was born in iniquity"* (Psalm 51:5); the prophets on sin as covenant
  breach rather than ritual failure; Romans 3:23, 5:12–21, 7; James 1:14–15 on desire conceiving
  and giving birth; 1 John 1:8–9, 3:4.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:17, 20

The prohibition of coveting addresses desire before it becomes acquisition (20:17), and Moses gives
the encounter's aim as preventing sin (20:20). Romans 7:7–12 later distinguishes that good command
from the sin that exploits it to produce coveting and death. The tension between intended restraint
and sin's contrary use belongs in [[Coveting and Love - The Commandments in Romans 7 and 13]], not
in a claim that the law itself is sinful. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### "Lest they make you sin against me" — Exodus 23:20–33

The peoples are not to live in the land *"lest they make you sin against me"* (23:33). The sin named
is serving their gods, and its image is a *snare*, *môqēš* (MAM). The angel, meanwhile, *"will not
pardon your disobedience"* (*pišʿăkem*, 23:21; MAM). See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]].

### The priestly garments — Exodus 28:1–43

Aaron *"shall bear the iniquity of the holy things"* — iniquity attached to Israel's holy gifts,
not only to wrongdoing — *"that they may be accepted"* (Exodus 28:38). The priests themselves
can *"bear iniquity, and die"* if they approach uncovered (28:43). See [[The Priestly Garments]].

### The ordination of the priests — Exodus 29:1–46

The priests' service begins with a sin offering for themselves (29:10–14). They eat *"those things
with which atonement was made"* (29:33). Then the altar too is atoned for and cleansed each day for
seven days (29:36–37). In MAM the verb *wəḥiṭṭēʾtā* (*cleanse*) is written with the consonants of
*ḥaṭṭāʾt* (*sin offering*) in the same verse. In the stored WEB text, 29:33 is the first
*"atonement"* in Genesis and Exodus. See [[The Ordination of the Priests]] and [[Outside the Camp - The Sin Offering and Hebrews 13]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

The people rapidly turn from the commanded way (32:8). Moses calls the act a great sin to Aaron,
to the people, and to God (32:21, 30–31). Aaron’s excuse does not remove his agency, and the final
verse attributes the calf both to the people and to Aaron (32:35). See [[The Golden Calf]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

The people’s stiff-necked character is repeated as the reason divine accompaniment would consume
them (33:3, 5). The problem left by the calf is thus more than the existence of an image: the calf
has been destroyed, but a resistant people still needs mercy and mediation. See
[[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Moses asks pardon for our iniquity and sin while acknowledging the people's stiff-neckedness
(34:9). This confession follows the proclamation that God forgives iniquity, disobedience, and
sin (34:7). The sequence distinguishes an offered basis for appeal from a claim that the calf
was harmless or that the people now need no further obedience. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The priestly garments made — Exodus 39:1–31

The crown and linen breeches are made (39:28, 30–31), but the explanation about bearing
the iniquity of holy things and the danger of bearing iniquity oneself remains in 28:38, 43.
Chapter 39 does not repeat that language or report an act of atonement. Aaron’s continuing place
after the calf is apparent; the clothing account does not itself explain his guilt’s removal.

See [[The Priestly Garments Made]]. Source: cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Sinners who recruit — Proverbs 1:8–19

Proverbs' first use of *ḥaṭṭāʾîm*, *sinners*, defines them by what they invite others into: *"if
sinners entice you, don't consent"* (1:10). Their sin is social before it is anything else — a gang
offering a young man a place, a share, and *"one purse"* (1:14) — and its content is ambush and
bloodshed *"without cause"* (1:11). Psalm 1:1's *derek ḥaṭṭāʾîm*, *"path of sinners"*, uses the same
two words as the father's *"don't walk on the path with them"* (1:15). Isaiah 59:7 and Romans 3:15
later carry the line *"their feet run to evil"* (1:16) from a gang to a nation to everyone — see
[[Their Feet Run to Evil - Proverbs 1.16, Isaiah 59.7, and Romans 3.15]]. Hebrew checked against
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*; Psalm, Isaiah, and Romans in WEB checked against
bible-api.com. See [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]].

### Evil that takes away sleep — Proverbs 4:16–17

The wicked *"don't sleep, unless they do evil. Their sleep is taken away, unless they make
someone fall"* (4:16), and they *"eat the bread of wickedness, and drink the wine of violence"*
(4:17). Sin is drawn as appetite and compulsion: something its doers need before they can rest,
against the sweet sleep promised to the wise in 3:24.

See [[The Two Paths]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

**Proverbs 8:36 — sin as missing Wisdom.** *"But he who sins against me wrongs his own soul"*:
*ḥōṭəʾî*, from *ḥāṭāʾ*, usually glossed *to miss* (a lexical sense not checked here), set against
*mōṣʾî*, *whoever finds me* (8:35). The one who misses Wisdom *ḥōmēs nap̄šô* — does violence to his
own soul, the root of Genesis 6:11's *ḥāmās*. Sin against her is described first as self-harm. See
[[Wisdom at the Creation]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:12, 16, 18–19, 23

Sin appears in the use of gain, lying and slander, excessive speech, and the fool’s pleasure in
wrongdoing. Love responds to wrongs without stirring strife (12), but verse 18 rejects hiding
hatred. These sayings attend to both harmful action and the appetite or intention behind it.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:1, 3, 6, 9, 20–21, 27, 31

The chapter locates wrongdoing in fraud, treachery, harmful speech, perverse intention, and the
pursuit of evil. Wickedness repeatedly becomes the agent of its own downfall. That claim is ethical
instruction, not evidence that every sufferer has committed a corresponding offense.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:2, 5–6, 12–13, 17, 20, 22

Wicked plans, deceitful advice, desire for plunder, false witness, and lying lips make wrongdoing
both inward and social. Verse 13 pictures evil speech trapping its own speaker. The warning
identifies real moral hazards; it does not establish that every person in trouble has been caught by
personal sin.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Sin pursued — Proverbs 13:6, 19, 21–22

Wickedness *"overthrows the sinner"* (6), fools detest turning from evil (19), misfortune *pursues* sinners (21), and the sinner's wealth is stored for the righteous (22). Sin is pictured as something that acts on its doer.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### Mocking atonement, despising a neighbour, disgracing a people — Proverbs 14:9, 21, 34

Fools *"mock at making atonement for sins"* (9, WEB's rendering); despising a neighbour *"sins"* (21); and sin is *"a disgrace to any people"* (34).

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:7, 9, 27, 30

Verse 9 questions who can claim to have purified the heart and to be without sin. It sits
beside recognizable integrity (7), inward searching (27), and the cleansing image of punishment
(30). This challenges self-exoneration without making moral distinctions meaningless. Job 2:3
prevents turning the general question into proof that a particular sufferer deserves
affliction. See [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]].

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:4, 13–14, 24

Concealing sins is contrasted with confessing and renouncing them (13). Verse 24 gives a concrete
false defense: the person who robs parents denies that it is wrong. Acknowledgment must therefore
include abandoning the wrong, rather than only finding words for it. The hardened heart (14)
supplies the opposing posture.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

Sin is a snare for its practitioner and grows under wicked ascendancy. Anger multiplies it, and
complicity in theft makes silence self-destructive; wrongdoing damages both public life and the
wrongdoer.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:6, 16, 22, 24; the passage page records the
cross-reference sources and interpretive limits.

### Agur’s words — Proverbs 30:6, 8–14

The unit names or depicts falsehood, denial of God, theft, slander, parental dishonor,
self-deception, pride, and oppression. Agur’s prayer anticipates his own vulnerability to wrongdoing
before the sayings describe others. It does not assign every poor or rich person those acts.

See [[The Words of Agur]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:1–14 and the comparisons documented there.

### From dust to dust — Ecclesiastes 3:16–22

Wickedness appears where justice and righteousness should be found (16). The observation
concerns a public failure without identifying a particular offender or mechanism. Verse 17
answers with divine judgment; the text does not say that animals die for personal wrongdoing
when it later compares their end with humanity's (19–20).

See [[From Dust to Dust]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–22; comparisons as identified above.

### The tears of the oppressed — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

The evil work seen under the sun (3) follows the tears of the oppressed and the power
of their oppressors (1). The unit describes harm without naming a particular statute,
crime, or offender. Its moral evaluation must not be transferred to the victims as an
explanation of their tears. Nor does the common fate discussed in 3:19 make oppressor and
oppressed morally indistinguishable.

See [[The Tears of the Oppressed]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–4:3; the passage page
records the additional comparison sources.

### The mouth that leads into sin — Ecclesiastes 5:6

*"Don’t allow your mouth to lead you into sin. Don’t protest before the messenger that this was
a mistake."* An unpaid vow is sin by the speaker's own mouth, and calling it *a mistake* —
*šəḡāḡâ*, the term for an unintentional sin, in the Mechon-Mamre Masoretic text read
2026-10-27 — is a second fault. Verse 1 adds that fools *"don’t know that they do evil"* even
while sacrificing.

See [[Let Your Words Be Few]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:1–7; the passage page records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### None who does good without sin — Ecclesiastes 7:15–29

*"Surely there is not a righteous man on earth, who does good and doesn’t sin"* (7:20) — the
book's general statement of human sinfulness, beside Proverbs 20:9 and 1 Kings 8:46 (see
[[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]). The sinner is ensnared by the woman of 7:26, and 7:29
traces the many schemes to humanity after an upright making (see
[[God Made Man Upright - Ecclesiastes 7.29 and Genesis 1-3]]). As in Job, the general
statement does not diagnose a particular sufferer; the righteous man of 7:15 perishes without
any charge being laid.

See [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29; comparison sources and Hebrew checks identified on the passage page.

### Wickedness cannot deliver — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

The hearer must not persist in an evil thing before the king (3), and wickedness cannot
deliver those who practice it (8). Neither a particular conspiracy nor a timetable of
punishment is supplied. The harmful dominion of verse 9 does not by itself identify its
victim as wicked. Verse 8 denies wickedness saving power without saying that the wicked
never enjoy temporary power or a long life; 7:15's observation remains visible.

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Delay encourages evil — Ecclesiastes 8:10–17

The failure to execute sentence speedily is given as a reason people become intent
on evil (11). A sinner repeats crimes and lives long (12); longevity is therefore no
proof of innocence. Verse 14 explicitly distinguishes conduct from outcomes rather
than allowing observed misfortune to identify someone as wicked.

See [[The Wicked Buried and the Work Beyond Finding]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:10–17;
comparisons are identified on the passage page.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

Human hearts are full of evil and madness while people live, before they go to the dead
(3). The common event is itself called an evil, while the wicked and righteous remain
distinct (2). The verse does not say that each sudden disaster in 12 is a targeted
punishment or that God approves the evil of 3 when he accepts works in 7.

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

*"One sinner destroys much good"* (9:18). The saying is about disproportion — a single person's
wrongdoing undoing the gains of wisdom — and is restated as an image in 10:1, where a little folly
outweighs wisdom and honor. No particular sin or sinner is identified.

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Priest and congregation — Leviticus 4:1–21

Unintentional violations of Yahweh’s prohibitions incur guilt (2, 13). The priest’s offense can
implicate the people (3), while the assembly’s offense can be hidden before it becomes known
(13–14). The prescribed response is a sin offering, not an assertion that lack of intention
makes the act nonexistent.

See [[The Sin Offering for Priest and Congregation]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 4:1–21;
contextual comparisons documented on the passage page.

### Ruler and individual — Leviticus 4:22–35

The ruler and common person can violate a command unwittingly and still be guilty
(22, 27). Recognition leads to the prescribed offering; ignorance is not treated as innocence.

See [[The Sin Offering for Ruler and Individual]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 4:22–35; contextual
comparisons documented on the passage page.

### Confession and means — Leviticus 5:1–13

Withheld testimony, impurity contact, and rash swearing are addressed together, without making every offense identical or every case inadvertent.

See [[Confession and Offerings According to Means]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:1–13.

### Holy things and unknown guilt — Leviticus 5:14–19

Unwitting wrongdoing against holy things (15) and prohibited acts done without knowledge (17–18) still incur liability. Lack of intent is not here a declaration that no wrong occurred.

See [[The Trespass Offering for Holy Things and Unknown Guilt]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:14–19.

  mercy rather than for judgment
- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — that diagnosis borne out eight verses later, reported
  without a verdict and with God absent from the scene
- [[The Tower of Babel]] — the vocabulary absent altogether: no *sin*, no *evil*, no *wickedness*, no
  *violence* in nine verses that end in a divine act against a human project. Genesis leaves the
  reader to supply the category, and the history of interpretation is the history of readers supplying
  different ones
- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — 13:13, the charge with no content: *rāʿîm wəḥaṭṭāʾîm lə-Yhwh*,
  **wicked and sinners against Yahweh**, both of the Bible's blunt words at once and the specification
  that makes it more than social wrongdoing — and not one particular given. What Sodom actually did is
  supplied later and from several directions at once (Genesis 18:20; 19:4–11; Ezekiel 16:49–50;
  Isaiah 1:10–17; Jude 7); see [[Sodom]]
- [[The First Adam and the Last Adam]] — the New Testament's universalizing of it
- [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] — Job 15:14–16, the friends' anthropology at full strength (*"What is
  man, that he should be clean?"*), and the wiki's standing case of a largely true doctrine used as
  a false diagnosis
- [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]] — the three Job speeches on human impurity, and what
  separates them from Genesis 6:5 and Romans 3
- [[Job of Uz]] — Job 1:5, sin as a hypothesis about someone else's heart (*"It may be that my sons
  have sinned, and renounced God in their hearts"*), covered by sacrifice before any offence is
  known — and named with *bārak*, *to bless*, the euphemism that carries the book's four hinge
  moments
- [[Zophar's Second Speech]] — the friends' most interesting account of it: sin as something eaten
  for the taste of it, held under the tongue, and then acting as cobra venom inside the body that
  swallowed it (20:12–15). The closest any of the three comes to saying sin is intrinsically
  destructive rather than merely penalised
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — sin assumed everywhere and specified nowhere: twenty-one verses of
  punishment with no offence named, from which the offence is to be inferred

- [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] — Genesis 19:4–13: the charge stated as a *cry* by the
  agents who carry out the sentence (*"the outcry against them has grown so great before Yahweh"*),
  so that what is punished is grounded in what was done to people. What the text disputes among its
  own readers is *which* sin; the range of scriptural answers is set out at [[Sodom]]

- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:30–38: the hardest ungraded scene in the book. Incest,
  intoxication administered twice to a man the text says did not know, and two national etymologies
  hung on the result — and not one word of the theme's vocabulary anywhere in the nine verses. No
  character is condemned, none is excused, and God is not named. The verdict readers supply here
  says more about them than the passage does, which is exactly the position [[The Tower of Babel]]
  leaves them in
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:6, 9, where the vocabulary finally arrives and lands on the
  wrong man. **The word is used twice in the chapter and never of Abraham**: God says he *"withheld
  you from sinning against me"*, and Abimelech asks *"How have I sinned against you, that you have
  brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin?"* — *ḥăṭāʾâ gədōlâ*, the phrase Exodus 32:21, 30, 31
  will use of the golden calf. Two things follow. **The offence would have been against God rather
  than against the injured husband**, and it would have been incurred without intent — which is the
  situation Leviticus 4 and Numbers 15:22–31 build the category of the *šəgāgâ* around. See
  [[Sinning Unwittingly - Integrity, Ignorance, and Guilt]]
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:22; **an offence reported in one
  clause with no verdict, no motive, and no sentence finished.** *"Reuben went and lay with Bilhah,
  his father's concubine, and Israel heard of it"* — and the Masoretic Text breaks the verse in the
  middle rather than say what Jacob thought. **No moral term appears anywhere in it**; the Septuagint
  supplies *"and it appeared evil before him"*, which the wiki records as a reading rather than a
  recovered clause. Genesis's verdict arrives fourteen chapters later, at 49:4, and calls the act
  ***defilement*** of a bed rather than a wrong done to a woman who is not consulted
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:18–33; **attempted fratricide, a sale, and a sustained lie, and
  the narration passes no verdict on any of it.** No divine name occurs in the chapter; nobody is rebuked;
  **the only judgement anybody makes is Jacob's and it is factually wrong.** The wiki notes that **the
  verdict has to come from elsewhere in the canon**: Exodus 21:16 and Deuteronomy 24:7 make the sale
  capital, and Acts 7:9 names the motive — *"the patriarchs, moved with jealousy against Joseph, sold him
  into Egypt."* **Genesis's own retrospect is put in Joseph's mouth rather than the narrator's** —
  *"you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good"* (50:20)
- [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:7, 10, 26; **three wrongs named in one chapter, ranked by nobody.**
  [[Er]] is *raʿ bəʿênê YHWH* with **no offence specified**; [[Onan]]'s is specified in the same
  sentence as his act — *"lest he should give offspring to his brother"* — and [[Judah]]'s is specified
  by himself: *"because I didn't give her to Shelah, my son."* **The deceptions [[Tamar]] commits are
  never named as wrong at all**, by the narrator or by God, and the only verdict in the chapter goes
  the other way. **On Onan, the wiki states plainly what the verse contains**: the act described is
  coitus interruptus and **the reason given is the defrauded brother**, not the spilled seed; the later
  moral tradition built on it is real, old, and outside what Genesis reports. **The wiki records the
  chapter's refusal to rank any of this** rather than supplying the ranking
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:1; 41:9; ***ḥāṭāʾ* at both ends of the episode, and
  Genesis never says what either offence was.** Two officers *"offended their lord"* (40:1) — the plain
  verb *sinned* — and the text supplies no act, no trial, and no proportion between the two sentences
  handed down. **Then the restored officer uses the same root of himself**, about what he did to a
  prisoner: *"I remember my faults today"* (41:9). **The chapter that opens with men sinning against a
  king closes with a man sinning against a slave**, and only the second is ever named as such
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 4:17–19, the first of the book's three *what is man* passages,
  and the only one given the authority of a vision: *"Shall a man be more pure than his Maker?"*
  Mortals live *"in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust"*. And 4:8's *"those who plow
  iniquity, and sow trouble, reap the same"* — sin as a crop, the image Hosea 8:7 and Galatians 6:7
  will use. See [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:24 and 7:20–21; **sin invited and sin hypothesised, never
  confessed and never denied in principle.** *"Cause me to understand wherein I have erred"* uses
  *šāgâ*, the inadvertent fault of Leviticus 4 — Job is open to an error he does not know of, as at
  1:5. And *"If I have sinned, what do I do to you?… Why do you not pardon my disobedience?"* is the
  first time Job uses the vocabulary of sin of himself, and it is conditional. See
  [[Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives]]
- [[Bildad's First Speech]] — Job 8:4; ***"he has delivered them into the hand of their
  disobedience (pišʿām)."*** **Sin as the agent of a death, inferred from the death.** No act of the
  children's is named, here or anywhere; their transgression is deduced from a collapsed house, and
  the reader knows the house fell for another reason.
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 10:6, 14; **sin searched for and marked, never named.** *"You inquire after my
  iniquity, and search after my sin, although you know that I am not wicked"*; *"if I sin, then
  you mark me."* Job demands the charge (10:2) and does not receive it.
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:6, 14; **sin as debt**, on which God the creditor has not yet collected in full,
  and as a lodger — *"Don't let unrighteousness dwell in your tents"* — to be evicted before the
  hands can be raised in prayer
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 13:23, 26; 14:4, 16–17; **sin asked for by count.** *"How many are my
  iniquities and sins? Make me know"* (13:23); *"you… make me inherit the iniquities of my youth"*
  (13:26); and the record *"sealed up in a bag"* (14:17). 14:4, *"Who can bring a clean thing out of an
  unclean? Not one"*, later read as a text on original sin, is in context a plea for leniency
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:19–21; **whether sin is inherited as a debt.** *"You say,
  'God lays up his iniquity for his children.' Let him recompense it to himself, that he may know
  it."* The friends' last retreat, and Job's refusal: sin should be paid by the sinner, and a dead
  father will not feel what his sons suffer. The objection Jeremiah 31:29–30 and Ezekiel 18 later
  make in God's name. See
  [[Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers - Job 21.19, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 18]].
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:5–9; **the first specific sins anyone attributes to Job, and
  all invented.** *"Isn't your wickedness great? Neither is there any end to your iniquities"* —
  then pledges, the naked stripped, bread and water withheld, widows, the fatherless. The book's
  verdict on the charges is given by the narrator (1:1) and by God (1:8; 2:3) before they are made.
- [[Bildad's Third Speech]] — Job 25:4–6; **the friends' anthropology at its lowest.** Not *every man
  sins* but *"man, who is a worm, the son of man, who is a worm!"* (25:6), set beneath a moon with no
  brightness and stars that are not pure. No sin is named and no sinner; being human is the charge.
  See [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]] and
  [[A Worm and No Man - Job 25.6, Psalm 22.6, and Isaiah 41.14]].
- [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] — Job 28:28; ***"To depart from evil is understanding."*** *Sûr mērāʿ*, the negative half
  of the definition of wisdom, and the second half of Job's description at 1:1, 8 and 2:3. **Turning
  from evil is made a form of knowledge** — *bînâ*, understanding — rather than only a moral act
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 33:9, 27; Job's claim summarised as *"I am clean, without disobedience"* (33:9) —
  Zophar's version (11:4), not Job's words — set against the rescued man's *"I have sinned, and perverted
  that which was right, and it didn't profit me"* (33:27): the confession the whole dialogue has asked
  Job to make, given a better reason.
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:8, 37; 35:6, 8; **the verdict hardened.** Job goes *"in company with
  the workers of iniquity"* (34:8) and *"adds rebellion to his sin"* (34:37) — *pešaʿ* on top of *ḥaṭṭāʾt*,
  sin before the suffering and rebellion since. And sin cannot touch God: *"If you have sinned, what
  effect do you have against him?"* (35:6), Job's 7:20 returned; it hurts *"a man as you are"* (35:8).
- [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]] — Exodus 9:27, 34; **the first confession by Pharaoh, and the
  relapse.** ***"I have sinned this time. Yahweh is righteous, and I and my people are wicked"*** —
  *ḥāṭāʾtî happaʿam*. When the hail stops, *"he sinned yet more"* — *wayyōsep laḥăṭōʾ*, the verb of his
  own confession — *"and hardened his heart"*. **The word for the confession becomes the word for the
  relapse.** He will confess again at 10:16
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:16–17; **the second confession, and the more specific.** ***"I have sinned
  against Yahweh your God, and against you. Now therefore please forgive my sin again"*** —
  *ḥāṭāʾtî laYHWH ʾĕlōhêkem wəlākem … śāʾ nāʾ ḥaṭṭāʾtî ʾak happaʿam*. **It names both parties sinned
  against**, as the first (*"I have sinned this time"*, 9:27) did not, and asks forgiveness *"only this
  time"*. It is followed, like the first, by a hardening (10:20), now Yahweh's

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:17, 20; the commands reach desire; the encounter is intended
  to prevent sin
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; the peoples' gods as a cause of sin; the angel who will not pardon transgression.
- [[The Priestly Garments]] — Exodus 28:1–43; iniquity of the holy things borne by Aaron; the priests' own risk.
- [[The Ordination of the Priests]] — Exodus 29:10–14, 33, 36–37; the priests' own sin offering, and atonement for the altar.
- [[The Priestly Garments Made]] — Exodus 39:1–31; Manufacture does not enact the earlier provision for bearing iniquity.

- [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]] — Proverbs 1:8–19; *ḥaṭṭāʾîm* who entice, and the *"path of sinners"* of Psalm 1:1
- [[Wisdom at the Creation]] — Proverbs 8:36; *"he who sins against me wrongs his own soul"* — missing Wisdom as violence to oneself
