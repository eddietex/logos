# Sexual Sin

## Definition / overview

### Priestly holiness — Leviticus 21

The priest’s daughter who prostitutes herself is said to profane her father and is sentenced to
burning (9). Marriage eligibility is separately regulated in 7 and 13–15; widowhood or
disability is not thereby identified as a sexual offense.

See [[The Holiness of the Priests]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 21.


### Penalties and separation — Leviticus 20

Adultery and several prohibited unions carry death (10–16), while other cases name cutting off,
bearing iniquity, or childlessness (17–21). Verse 13 addresses male same-sex intercourse and
assigns death to both participants; it does not describe modern categories of orientation.

See [[Penalties and a People Set Apart]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 20; comparisons as cited.


**Genesis narrates a great deal of illicit sex and legislates none of it.** There is no commandment in
the book, no prohibition issued to anyone, and — with two exceptions — **no verdict passed by the
narrator on any of it.** What the book gives instead is a sequence of episodes in which the wrongness is
**assumed by the characters**, usually by foreigners, and **stated out loud for the first time by a
Hebrew slave who is refusing** (39:9).

**That is why this page exists and why it is opened where it is.** The material has been accumulating
since Genesis 19 with nowhere to put it: **[[Marriage]] covers the ordinance and not its violation**,
and **[[Testing]] explicitly excludes temptation** — its own summary says so. **Genesis 39 is the first
outright refusal in the sequence and the first time the category is named from inside the story**, which
makes it the natural place to open the page rather than Sodom, where the wiki would have had to supply
the vocabulary itself.

**The page covers the violation of sexual boundaries**: adultery, incest, coercion, and the household
arrangements Genesis reports without comment. **It does not cover polygamy or concubinage as such**,
which Genesis treats as ordinary and which belong to [[Marriage]], nor the specific dynamics of
[[Slavery and Servitude]], under which several of these episodes actually occur.

## Development across Scripture

### Field and community — Leviticus 19:19–37

Intercourse with the pledged slave woman incurs punishment and requires the man’s ram and priestly
atonement (20–22). Making a daughter a prostitute is forbidden (29). The first case does not
specify consent or coercion, so neither should be supplied as a narrative fact.

See [[Holy Living in Field and Community]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19.


### Forbidden unions and the land — Leviticus 18:1–30

The chapter prohibits specified kinship unions (6–18), intercourse during menstrual impurity (19), adultery (20), male same-sex intercourse (22), and intercourse with animals (23). It describes acts, not modern categories of sexual orientation.

See [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18.

### In Genesis: assumed, never commanded

**The striking thing is how confident everyone is about a rule nobody has been given.**

- **Pharaoh, 12:18** — *"What is this that you have done to me? Why didn't you tell me that she was your
  wife?"* **An Egyptian king rebukes the patriarch**, and the rebuke assumes that taking another man's
  wife is wrong and that Abram knew it.
- **Abimelech, 20:9** — *"you have brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin."* **A Philistine king
  uses the word *sin*, ***ḥăṭāʾâ***, **of adultery, four hundred years before Sinai** — and God has
  already told him *"I withheld you from sinning against me"* (20:6). **The theology of 39:9 is first
  spoken to a foreigner, by God.**
- **Joseph, 39:9** — *"How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?"* **The first time
  the person facing the act names the category**, and he names God as the party wronged rather than the
  husband. See [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]].

**The two places the narrator does pass judgment are both about something else.** 34:7 calls the rape of
Dinah ***nəbālâ***, *folly in Israel* — **and the phrase is the brothers' as much as the narrator's**, in
a chapter whose verdict Jacob reverses on his deathbed. **38:7 and 38:10 report that Yahweh killed Er
and then Onan**, and 38:10 says only that *"the thing which he did was evil in Yahweh's sight"* — the
evil named is **the refusal of levirate duty**, not the act itself. **Genesis's only two divine
executions in the Joseph cycle are in this territory and neither is explained.**

### The Genesis roster

| passage | what happens | the book's verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **12:10–20; 20:1–18; 26:6–11** | a patriarch passes his wife off as his sister, three times | **none on the patriarch**; the foreign kings object |
| **19:4–11** | the men of Sodom demand the visitors | the only episode Genesis treats as self-evidently monstrous |
| **19:30–38** | Lot's daughters and their father | **no verdict**; two nations descend from it |
| **34:1–31** | Shechem and Dinah | ***nəbālâ***, *folly in Israel* — and a massacre Jacob condemns |
| **35:22** | Reuben and Bilhah | *"Israel heard of it"*, and the sentence stops there |
| **38:1–30** | Er, Onan, Judah and Tamar | **two men killed**; Judah's own verdict is *"she is more righteous than I"* |
| **39:7–20** | Potiphar's wife and Joseph | **the refusal**, and the first naming of the category |

**Six of the seven involve the covenant family, and in five of those the family is the offender.**
**Genesis does not use any of it to argue a moral point**, which is the single most consistent feature of
the book's handling of the subject.

### After Genesis

**The law arrives and names everything at once.** Adultery becomes the seventh commandment (Exodus
20:14); Leviticus 18 and 20 give the incest lists, several of which **retroactively prohibit marriages
Genesis reports without comment** — Abraham and Sarah as half-siblings (20:12), Jacob married to two
sisters (Leviticus 18:18). See
[[Two Sisters - The Marriage Genesis Records and Leviticus Forbids]].

**Deuteronomy 22:23–27 supplies the procedure** that makes Potiphar's wife's *"I cried with a loud
voice"* the decisive claim it is. **Proverbs 6–7 builds the adulteress into a set piece** whose furniture
— the absent husband, the seized garment, the empty house — is Genesis 39's. **The prophets turn the
whole vocabulary into a figure for idolatry**, which is where most of the Bible's actual usage of the
language ends up: Hosea's marriage, Jeremiah 3, Ezekiel 16 and 23.

**The New Testament keeps the category and relocates it inward.** *"Everyone who gazes at a woman to
lust after her has committed adultery with her already in his heart"* (Matthew 5:28) — **which is
Genesis 39:7's *set her eyes on* read as the offence rather than the prelude to it.** Paul's imperative
is the verb of 39:12: *"Flee sexual immorality"* (1 Corinthians 6:18), *"flee from youthful lusts"*
(2 Timothy 2:22). **Joseph running out of a house without his clothes is the paradigm case the epistles
never cite by name and clearly have in view.**

### What the theme is not

**It is not a doctrine of purity, and Genesis gives no grounds for building one.** The book never
praises anyone for continence except by narrating it, never shames anyone for its absence, and **places
[[Judah and Tamar]] immediately before [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]] without a word of comparison** —
one man going down and taking what he should not, the next taken down and refusing. **The juxtaposition
is the argument, and Genesis leaves it entirely to the reader.**
### Job: the look under oath

**Job 31 moves the sin from the deed to the eye.** Its oath opens, before any act, with *"I made a
covenant with my eyes, how then should I look lustfully at a young woman?"* (31:1), sets *"if my heart
walked after my eyes"* (31:7) before the neighbour's door (31:9), and calls adultery *"a heinous crime"*,
*"an iniquity to be punished by the judges"*, and *"a fire that consumes to destruction"* (31:11–12).
**It is the Old Testament's most explicit claim that the look itself falls under judgment**, the
ground on which Matthew 5:28 builds. See [[A Covenant with My Eyes - Job 31.1 and Matthew 5.28]].

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:14, 17

What the Joseph narrative treated as betrayal of a master and sin against God becomes an explicit
prohibition of adultery (20:14). The restriction on coveting a neighbor's wife also addresses desire
(20:17). Matthew 5:27–30 takes up the adultery command and the lustful gaze; it does not introduce
inward accountability into a Torah concerned only with outward acts. See
[[Murder, Adultery, and the Heart - Exodus 20 and Matthew 5]]. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

**The Book of the Covenant has two sexual cases, side by side and very unlike.** The man who
*entices* an unbetrothed virgin pays the bride-price and marries her, or pays it anyway if her
father refuses (Exodus 22:16–17); the man who lies with an animal is put to death (22:19). The first
is treated as a wrong to be made good, the second as a capital offence, and the text gives no reason
for either. **The seduction case is the remedy Shechem offered in Genesis 34:12.** See
[[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

### The strange woman — Proverbs 2:16–19

Proverbs' recurring figure enters here: *"the strange woman, even … the foreigner who flatters with her
words; who forsakes the friend of her youth, and forgets the covenant of her God"* (2:16–17, *ʾiššâ
zārâ … nokriyyâ*). The wiki reads her as another man's wife — *strange* because she belongs to
another household — on the strength of *"the friend of her youth"* and *"the covenant"*, the terms
Malachi 2:14 uses for marriage; the alternative, a literally foreign woman, is kept open on the
passage page. **Adultery is defined as forgetting a covenant**, and its end is a house that *"leads
down to death"*, from which *"None who go to her return again"* (2:18–19). Her danger is her speech,
*her words* made smooth (2:16), set against *my words*, the father's (2:1). The couplet returns at
7:5, and the figure is developed in chapters 5–7. She has no person page; this theme is her page. See
[[If You Seek Her as Silver]] and
[[The Wife of Your Youth - Proverbs 2.17, Malachi 2.14, and Jeremiah 3.4]]. Source: Proverbs 2 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The strange woman's lecture — Proverbs 5

Chapter 5 gives the strange woman a whole lecture. Her lips *"drip honey"*, but her end is
wormwood and her steps lead to [[Sheol]] (5:3–5); the son is to keep away from *"the door of her
house"* or see honour, years, wealth, and labour pass to strangers (5:8–10). The WEB now renders
*zārâ* as *"adulteress"* (5:3, 20), the word it rendered *"the strange woman"* at 2:16. **The
remedy is not only distance but a wife**: *"Drink water out of your own cistern"* (5:15). The
lecture's last argument is that nothing is hidden — *"the ways of man are before Yahweh's eyes"*
(5:21) — which answers Job 24:15's adulterer who says *"No eye shall see me"*. Job 31:9–12 swears
to the same door and invokes the same loss on himself. See [[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]] and
[[The Neighbor's Door - Proverbs 5 and Job 31.9-12]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 5; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### Fire in the lap — Proverbs 6:20–35

The strange woman's second lecture names her outright as *ʾēšeṯ ʾîš*, *a man's wife*, and *ʾēšeṯ
rēʿēhû*, *his neighbour's wife* (6:26, 29). Against her the son is told *"Don't lust after her
beauty in your heart"* (6:25), with the verb of the tenth commandment (*taḥmōḏ*, Exodus 20:17). The
argument is the nature of the act: *"Can a man scoop fire into his lap, and his clothes not be
burned?"* (6:27); *"Whoever touches her will not be unpunished"* (6:29), the verb of Genesis 20:6
and 26:11. A hungry thief can repay, even sevenfold; the adulterer *"destroys his own soul"*, and
the husband's jealousy takes no ransom (6:30–35). Proverbs names no court, where Leviticus 20:10 and
Deuteronomy 22:22 prescribe death; the wiki records the two pictures without merging them. See
[[The Lamp of the Commandment]],
[[Fire in the Lap and the Garment Left Behind - Proverbs 6.27-29 and Genesis 39]], and
[[No Ransom for the Adulterer - Proverbs 6.29-35, Leviticus 20.10, and Deuteronomy 22.22]]. Source:
stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

**Proverbs 7 tells the strange woman's seduction as a scene.** The father watches from his window
as a young man *"void of understanding"* walks toward her corner at nightfall (7:6–9). She is not a
prostitute but dresses as one (*šîṯ zônâ*, 7:10); she has a husband, away *"on a long journey"*
(7:19). She seizes him, offers a feast of peace-offering meat and a perfumed bed, and he follows
*"as an ox goes to the slaughter"* (7:22). The lecture ends where 2:18 and 5:5 ended: *"Her house is
the way to Sheol"* (7:27). Genesis 38 supplies the wiki's other woman dressed as a prostitute, and
the opposite verdict. See [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] and
[[The Attire of a Prostitute - Genesis 38 and Proverbs 7.10]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 7; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

**Proverbs 9 turns the strange woman into Folly.** The foolish woman of 9:13–18 is *loud* as the
strange woman was (*hōmiyyâ*, 7:11; 9:13), sits *"at the door of her house"* (9:14; 5:8), and sends
her guests to Sheol (9:18; 2:18; 5:5; 7:27). Her menu is *"Stolen water is sweet. Food eaten in secret
is pleasant"* (9:17) — water that belongs to another man's well, against *"Drink water out of your own
cistern"* (5:15). Whether she is the strange woman under a wider name or a figure of all folly that
includes her, the text does not say. See [[The Two Feasts]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 5, 7, 9;
Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The deep pit — Proverbs 22:14

*"The mouth of an adulteress is a deep pit. He who is under Yahweh's wrath will fall into it"*
(22:14). The strange woman of chapters 2, 5, 7, and 9 is compressed into one image of her speech,
repeated at 23:27. The saying places Yahweh's wrath before the fall without saying what caused it.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### The deep pit — Proverbs 23:26–28

*"For a prostitute is a deep pit; and a wayward wife is a narrow well. Yes, she lies in wait like a
robber, and increases the unfaithful among men."* The prologue's strange woman compressed to three
lines. In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read 2026-10-26), *deep pit* is the phrase of
22:14, *wayward wife* is *noḵriyyāh* (*foreign woman*, as at 2:16), and *lies in wait* is the verb of
7:12 and of the gang at 1:11. See
[[Two Voices in the Street - The Strange Woman and Wisdom in Proverbs 7-9]].

See [[Do Not Envy Sinners]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 23:12–24:2.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

The companion of prostitutes squanders wealth, contrasted with the son whose love of wisdom
delights his father. The saying names neither a particular woman nor an adulterous marriage; its
stated consequence is household and financial loss.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:3; the passage page records the cross-reference
sources and interpretive limits.

### The adulterous woman's way — Proverbs 30:19–20

After four ways that leave no trace, ending with *"the way of a man with a maiden"*, Agur adds the
adulterous woman's: she eats, wipes her mouth, and says, *"I have done nothing wrong"* (20). The
image recalls Folly's *"Food eaten in secret is pleasant"* (9:17). What the verse exposes is the
denial: an act that leaves no visible trace is claimed to be no wrong at all. Whether verse 19's
fourth way is wonder at love or already the shadow of verse 20 is disputed; the text calls it
amazing and connects it with *"so"*. See
[[Two Voices in the Street - The Strange Woman and Wisdom in Proverbs 7-9]].

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

### Lemuel’s mother — Proverbs 31:3

Lemuel is warned not to give his strength to women or his ways to what destroys kings. The verse
can be read with Proverbs’ warnings about destructive sexual entanglement, but it does not name
adultery, prostitution, a particular woman, or a particular event in Lemuel’s life. It must not
be converted into a claim that women as a class are destructive: the authoritative teacher in
this very passage is a woman. The mother’s instruction qualifies that generalization within
the unit itself.

See [[The Words of King Lemuel]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9 and the comparisons documented
there.

### The woman more bitter than death — Ecclesiastes 7:26

The Preacher finds *"more bitter than death the woman whose heart is snares and traps, whose
hands are chains"*; the one who pleases God escapes her, the sinner is caught (7:26). The
imagery matches Proverbs' adulteress — bitter in the end, going down to death, a deep pit for
the one under Yahweh's wrath (Proverbs 5:4–5; 22:14) — though the verse does not use the word
*adulteress*. The relative clause defines which woman is meant; the verse is not a statement
about women in general. 7:28's count is harder and is discussed on the passage page. See
[[More Bitter Than Death - Ecclesiastes 7.26, Proverbs 5.4, and 22.14]].

See [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29; comparison sources and Hebrew checks identified on the passage page.

### Hidden from everyone — Numbers 5:11–31

Here the act is defined by what cannot be shown: hidden, concealed, no witness, not caught (13). Only the woman is examined, and the other man never appears again. Leviticus 20:10 and Deuteronomy 22:22 put both to death, and the difference is left standing on the passage page.

See [[The Suspected Wife]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 5.

## Related themes

- [[Marriage]] — the ordinance this theme is the violation of; Genesis's polygamy and concubinage belong
  there rather than here
- [[Testing]] — **explicitly not this.** *Nissâ* is God's initiative and is disclosed to the reader;
  Genesis 39 has no *nissâ*, no divine initiative, and no statement that Joseph is being tested
- [[Sin]] — the general category, whose vocabulary (*ḥăṭāʾâ*, *rāʿâ*) this material supplies some of the
  book's sharpest instances of
- [[Marriage]], [[Deception]] — the two that most of these episodes actually run on
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — *ʿerwâ*, the legal term Leviticus 18 uses throughout, and the garment
  taken from Joseph
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — the condition under which Hagar, Bilhah, Zilpah and Joseph are all
  involved, and which Genesis never separates from the sexual arrangements it reports
- [[Judgment]] — the two executions of Genesis 38, and the absence of judgment everywhere else
- [[Idolatry]] — where the prophets take this whole vocabulary
