# Land and Sojourning

## Definition / overview

### Benjamin’s inheritance — Joshua 18:11–28

Inheritance is defined by family, borders, cities, and villages (11–28). The list does not establish occupation. Beth Arabah’s inclusion in both Judah’s and Benjamin’s lists (15:61; 18:22) remains an explicit unresolved overlap.

See [[The Inheritance of Benjamin]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 18:11–28; comparisons as cited.

### The survey at Shiloh — Joshua 18:1–10

The gift of the land requires action: seven tribes have not divided their inheritance, and Joshua
rebukes their delay in possessing it (2–3). A written city survey precedes allocation by lot (9–10);
the already allotted shares remain in place (5, 7).

See [[The Land Surveyed at Shiloh]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 18:1–10; comparisons as cited.

### Joseph asks for more — Joshua 17:14–18

The claim of insufficient land is answered with forest clearance and future expulsion, not a newly narrated lot ceremony (14–18). Allotment, usable land, and occupation are distinct.

See [[The Children of Joseph Ask for More]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 17:14–18.

### Manasseh’s inheritance — Joshua 17:1–13

Daughters receive the commanded inheritance; ten parts are counted besides the eastern land (3–6).
Towns within other tribal territories and inhabitants not removed distinguish legal allocation from
actual occupation (8–13).

See [[The Inheritance of Manasseh]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 17:1–13.

### Ephraim’s inheritance — Joshua 16:1–10

Family-based boundaries and Ephraimite cities within Manasseh’s territory distinguish allotment from a simple block of exclusive occupation (5–9). Gezer’s remaining Canaanites further distinguish inheritance from complete possession (10).

See [[The Inheritance of Ephraim]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 16:1–10.

### Judah’s cities — Joshua 15:20–63

The inheritance is named again, now as towns *with their villages*, counted by district (20–62). Allotment and possession remain distinct: Ekron, Ashdod, and Gaza are assigned (45–47) though 13:2–3 counted them as remaining, and the list closes on Jerusalem's Jebusites living *to this day* among Judah (63).

See [[The Cities of Judah]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 15:20–63.

### Achsah’s springs — Joshua 15:13–19

Inheritance moves from Judah’s tribal boundaries to Caleb’s portion and Achsah’s request for water (13–19). A land grant and the resources needed to use it are distinct gifts; the request for springs makes that practical distinction visible.

See [[Achsah Asks for Springs]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 15:13–19.

### Judah’s borders — Joshua 15:1–12

The lot for Judah is delimited on four sides and framed by reference to its families (1, 12). Allotment defines an inheritance; it does not by itself assert occupation of every named place.

See [[The Borders of Judah]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 15:1–12.

### Caleb’s inheritance — Joshua 14:6–15

The promised land becomes Caleb’s named inheritance, Hebron, for him and his children (9, 13–14). Legal allocation and actual removal of inhabitants are distinguished: the latter is still proposed in verse 12.

See [[Caleb Claims Hebron]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 14:6–15; comparisons as cited.


### The western division — Joshua 14:1–5

Nine tribes and a half receive the western inheritance by lot; Moses’ eastern grants remain in force (1–3). Levi’s cities and surrounding land qualify the denial of a territorial share (4). Allocation does not erase the remaining-land report of Joshua 13:1–7.

See [[The Division of the Land Begins]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 14:1–5; comparisons as cited.


### Eastern allotments — Joshua 13:8–33

Moses’ eastern grants are recorded by families (15–32), yet Geshur and Maacath remain (13). Levi’s inheritance is described through offerings and Yahweh rather than a territorial share (14, 33). The Ammon wording in verse 25 remains in tension with Deuteronomy 2:19, 37.

See [[The Inheritance Beyond the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 13:8–33; comparisons as cited.

### The land that remains — Joshua 13:1–7

Inheritance is allocated before possession is complete: Yahweh identifies very much land remaining and nevertheless orders its division (1, 6–7). The claim that Joshua took the whole land in 11:23 remains in explicit tension with this inventory; neither statement should replace the other.

See [[The Land That Remains]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 13:1–7.

### The kings struck down — Joshua 12:1–24

The land on both sides of the Jordan is set in one list. Moses *gave it for a possession* to the eastern tribes (6); Joshua *gave it … for a possession according to their divisions* (7). The east bank is mapped by borders; the west is given by regions — hill country, lowland, Arabah, slopes, wilderness, and the South (8).

See [[The Kings Struck Down]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 12:1–24.

### The whole land taken — Joshua 11:16–23

*So Joshua took the whole land … and Joshua gave it for an inheritance to Israel according to their divisions by their tribes* (23). The summary anticipates the allotment of chapters 13–21, while 13:1 at once says *there remains yet very much land to be possessed*. Both claims stand.

See [[The Whole Land Taken]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 11:16–23.

### The northern coalition — Joshua 11:1–15

Captured cities on mounds remain unburned except Hazor (13), and plunder is retained (14). Capture does not itself demonstrate lasting settlement of every region reached in pursuit; compare the remaining northern territory in Joshua 13:4–6.

See [[The Northern Kings at the Waters of Merom]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 11:1–15.

### The southern campaign — Joshua 10:28–43

The campaign claims the southern kings and land (40–42), yet Joshua 13:1–3 says much land remains and 15:13–17 reports further action at Hebron and Debir. Military victory and enduring settlement are not simply interchangeable; the narrative tension remains.

See [[The Southern Cities Taken]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 10:28–43; comparisons as cited.

### The Gibeonite deception — Joshua 9:1–27

The distinction between distant foreigners and neighbors living within the land determines the
treaty question (6–7, 16). Gibeon’s survival means that receiving the land is not narrated as the
immediate removal of every prior community.

See [[The Gibeonite Deception]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 9:1–27; comparisons as cited.


### The law read at Ebal — Joshua 8:30–35

The law is inscribed and heard in the land after Ai’s capture (30–35). Foreigners stand with natives (33), showing participation in the hearing without identifying the foreigners’ origins or settling every question of legal status.

See [[The Law Read at Mount Ebal]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 8:30–35.

### The fall of Jericho — Joshua 6:1–27

A shut city becomes a conquered city (1–20), but it is burned rather than immediately occupied as an ordinary settlement (24–26). The gift of land does not here authorize unrestricted use of its wealth or rebuilding.

See [[The Fall of Jericho]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 6:1–27; comparisons identified above.


### Circumcision at Gilgal — Joshua 5:1–9

The children have crossed into the land sworn to the fathers, while the disobedient military generation was denied it (6–7). Entry is followed by circumcision and recovery before conquest.

See [[The Second Circumcision at Gilgal]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 5:1–9; comparisons as cited.

### The twelve stones — Joshua 4:1–24

Entry has a dated camp at Gilgal and a memorial (19–20), while conquest and the other tribes’ rest
remain unfinished.
See [[The Twelve Stones from the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 4:1–24.

### Crossing the Jordan — Joshua 3:1–17

The river crossing makes entry possible while the expulsion of the seven peoples remains a promise (10, 17). Crossing the boundary must not be equated with completing possession.

See [[Crossing the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 3:1–17.


### Rahab and the spies — Joshua 2:1–24

Rahab acknowledges that Yahweh has given Israel the land (9), and the spies repeat that certainty to Joshua (24). A resident’s report of fear becomes encouragement to the people who are about to enter.

See [[Rahab and the Spies]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 2:1–24.

### Rest before the land — Joshua 1:10–18

*Yahweh your God gives you rest, and will give you this land* (13). For the eastern tribes rest has come first and east of the river, outside Canaan as Numbers 34 draws it; Deuteronomy 12:9 had said Israel *haven't yet come to the rest and to the inheritance*. Their land is *the land of your possession* (15), but they may not settle in it until the others possess theirs.

See [[Rest from All Your Enemies - Deuteronomy 12, Joshua 1, 21-23, and Hebrews 4]].

See [[The Eastern Tribes Pledge to Cross]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 1:10–18.

### The land given — Joshua 1:1–9

*I have given you every place that the sole of your foot will tread on, as I told Moses* (3), in the border of Deuteronomy 11:24. Deuteronomy's *shall be yours* becomes *I have given you*, while Joshua 13:1 will later say much land remains; the gift and its possession are not the same event.

See [[After the Death of Moses]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 1:1–9.

### The death of Moses — Deuteronomy 34:1–12

Moses sees the whole land sworn to the patriarchs and dies outside it (1–5). The Torah ends with the people on its edge.

See [[The Death of Moses]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 34:1–12.

### Moses blesses the tribes — Deuteronomy 33:1–29

Land receives dew and fertility; Gad’s portion and Naphtali’s possession sit inside Israel’s
promised safety (13–16, 21, 23, 28). The poem is not a full boundary survey.

See [[Moses Blesses the Tribes]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 33:1–29; comparisons as cited.


### Commanded to ascend Nebo — Deuteronomy 32:48–52

The land remains Yahweh’s gift to Israel (49, 52), yet Moses is denied entry. The people’s
inheritance and their leader’s personal participation are distinguished rather than treated as
identical promises.

See [[Moses Commanded to Ascend Nebo]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 32:48–52.

### The song as witness — Deuteronomy 31:14–23

Entry into the sworn land is assured to Joshua (23), while the people's coming betrayal in that
same fertile land is foretold (16, 20). Receiving the inheritance and maintaining covenant
loyalty are distinguished.

See [[A Song to Witness Against Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:14–23.

### Courage and succession — Deuteronomy 31:1–8

The ancestral land oath outlasts Moses’ leadership: Joshua will go with the people and cause them to inherit (7). The gift remains ahead of the assembly, with the Jordan still uncrossed.

See [[Be Strong as Joshua Takes the Lead]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:1–8; Deuteronomy 31:16–18 and 34:7 where compared.

### Choose life — Deuteronomy 30:11–20

Life is life *in the land* (16, 20); death is not to *prolong your days in the land* across the Jordan (18). The land is the one *Yahweh swore to your fathers* (20).

See [[The Word Is Near and the Choice Is Life]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 30:11–20.

### Return and the circumcised heart — Deuteronomy 30:1–10

Israel driven among the nations (1) is brought back to *the land which your fathers possessed* to possess it again, with increase beyond the fathers (5). Possession lost by exile can be given again.

See [[Return Restoration and the Circumcised Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 30:1–10.

### The covenant in Moab — Deuteronomy 29:1–29

Resident foreigners stand within the covenant assembly (11), while a visiting foreigner later witnesses the ruined land (22). These are distinct roles. Possession can give way to uprooting into another land (28).

See [[The Covenant in Moab and the Hidden Things]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 29:1–29.

### Covenant sanctions — Deuteronomy 28:15–68

The land remains Yahweh’s gift even when its inhabitants are uprooted (52, 63). Scattering brings no secure resting place (64–65); possession does not make covenant accountability disappear.

See [[Curses for Disobedience]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 28:15–68.


### Blessings in the land — Deuteronomy 28:1–14

The land is both Yahweh’s present gift in the promise and the land sworn to the fathers (8, 11). Productive settled life there is tied to obedience; receiving the land is not exemption from responsibility.

See [[Blessings for Obedience (Deuteronomy)]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 28:1–14.

### Stones and an altar — Deuteronomy 27:1–10

The promised fertile land is Yahweh's gift (2–3). Crossing into it brings the task of
inscribing the law and worshiping at Ebal, joining possession to covenant responsibility
rather than treating arrival as an end to obligation.

See [[Stones and an Altar on Mount Ebal]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 27:1–10.

### A foreigner in Egypt remembered — Deuteronomy 23:7

*"You shall not abhor an Egyptian, because you lived as a foreigner in his land"* (7). The sojourn is
made a reason for regard toward the people of the land of bondage. The word is *gēr* (WLC 23:8), as in
10:19, *"you were foreigners in the land of Egypt"*. See
[[A Foreigner in a Foreign Land - Gershom and the Law of the Stranger]].

See [[The Assembly and the Holy Camp]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 23:1–14; 10:19.

### The inheritance kept clean — Deuteronomy 21:23

The pericope ends where 21:1–9 began, with the land: the body on the tree must come down *"that you
don't defile your land which Yahweh your God gives you for an inheritance"* (23).

See [[Captives, Firstborn Sons, and Public Shame]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 21:10–23.

### A body in the given land — Deuteronomy 21:1–4

The case opens *"in the land which Yahweh your God gives you to possess"* (1), and the liability is mapped
onto it: the town *"nearest to the slain man"* (3). The heifer is taken to a valley *"which is neither
plowed nor sown"* (4), ground untouched by work. Compare Numbers 35:33–34, where shed blood *"pollutes the
land"* in which Yahweh dwells.

See [[An Unsolved Killing and the Broken-Necked Heifer]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 21:1–9; Numbers 35:33–34.

### House, vineyard, and inheritance — Deuteronomy 20:5–6, 16, 19–20

A man who has built a house or planted a vineyard and not yet enjoyed it is sent home from war (5–6).
The cities *"that Yahweh your God gives you for an inheritance"* are under the *ḥērem* (16). And the
fruit trees of a besieged city are spared *"for you may eat of them"* (19).

See [[Laws for Going to War]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 20:1–20.

### The neighbour's landmark — Deuteronomy 19:14

*"You shall not remove your neighbor's landmark, which they of old time have set, in your inheritance"*
(14). The *gəbûl* is the same word as the national *"border"* Yahweh may enlarge (19:8; WLC). The
boundary stone keeps each family's share of the allotment Yahweh gives, and moving it undoes that allotment. Deuteronomy 27:17
will curse it.

See [[Landmarks, Witnesses, and False Testimony]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 19:14–21. Hebrew from WLC (morphhb), 2026-11-07.

### The land divided, enlarged, and kept clean — Deuteronomy 19:1–13

The land is divided *"into three parts"* (3) around the three cities, its border may be enlarged
*"as he has sworn to your fathers"* (8), and innocent blood shed in it leaves guilt on its people (10).

See [[Refuge for the Unwitting Killer]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 19:1–13.

### Renewed tablets and onward journey — Deuteronomy 10:1–11

Israel is to possess the sworn land (11), but Levi has no tribal portion: Yahweh is its inheritance
(9). Numbers 18:21–24 supplies tithes for service, so lack of a territorial share does not mean lack
of support.

See [[New Tablets and the Journey Onward]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 10:1–11.

### Possession without merit — Deuteronomy 9:1–6

Possession fulfills the ancestral oath and follows judgment on the nations (5). The repeated denial of Israel’s merit prevents reading the land as proof of the recipients’ moral superiority.

See [[Not Because of Your Righteousness]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 9:1–6.


### Wilderness and wealth — Deuteronomy 8:1–20

The promised land is described by water, crops, iron, and copper (7–9). Its abundance calls for
blessing the giver (10); settlement is not an exemption from covenant obligations.

See [[Remember the Wilderness and the Giver of Wealth]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 8:1–20.


### Fullness and remembrance — Deuteronomy 6:10–25

The land is sworn to the fathers and supplied with improvements Israel did not produce (10–11).
Entry and the displacement of enemies remain linked to doing right (18–19), so gift does not
eliminate obligation.

See [[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 6:10–25.

### Setting of the instruction — Deuteronomy 4:44–49

Israel already possesses the eastern territories of Sihon and Og (47–49) when Moses sets the law before it. Teaching takes place in acquired land while the western crossing still lies ahead.

See [[The Setting of the Covenant Instruction]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:44–49.

### Heard and lived — Deuteronomy 4:32–40

The land is given *for an inheritance, as it is today* (38), and held *forever* by keeping the statutes (40). Just before, 4:26 warned that idolatry would end their days on it; the two conditions stand side by side.

See [[Has Any People Heard God and Lived]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:32–40.

### No form and no image — Deuteronomy 4:15–31

Moses must die outside the land; Israel goes over and possesses *that good land* (21–22). Being *long in the land* is the setting of the predicted apostasy, and loss of the land its penalty (25–26). The promised return in these verses is *to Yahweh* (30), not explicitly to the land; Deuteronomy 30:1–5 (stored WEB, not yet ingested) is the passage that adds the gathering.

See [[No Form Seen and No Image Made]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 4:15–31.

### Moses’ plea — Deuteronomy 3:23–29

Seeing the land and entering it are separated: Moses receives the prospect, Joshua the charge to
bring the people into their inheritance (25–28). The people’s future outlasts their present
leader’s permitted journey.

See [[Moses Pleads to Cross the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:23–29.

### Eastern inheritance — Deuteronomy 3:12–22

The eastern grant is already possession and rest, yet its recipients must help their brothers
possess the western land before returning home (18–20). Land given to one group carries
responsibility toward the whole people.

See [[The Eastern Inheritance and the Charge to Joshua]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:12–22.

### Og’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 3:1–11

Victory becomes possession of the two kings’ territory from the Arnon to Hermon (8–10). Territorial
acquisition is narrated before the following unit’s tribal allocation.

See [[The Defeat of Og Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 3:1–11.

### Sihon’s defeat recalled — Deuteronomy 2:24–37

A request for transit toward the Jordan becomes possession of Sihon’s cities (27–36). Yahweh grants
this territory but forbids approach to Ammon’s land (37); gift and boundary belong to the same
account.

See [[The Defeat of Sihon Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:24–37.

### Protected neighbors and the wilderness generation — Deuteronomy 2:1–23

The land giver also sets territorial limits: Israel receives none of Seir, Moab, or Ammon here (5,
9, 19). Purchasing food and water recognizes another people’s possession (6).

See [[Around Seir and Past Moab and Ammon]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 2:1–23.

### The sentence and the ascent — Deuteronomy 1:34–46

The oath to the fathers continues through the children even while their parents lose entry (35–39).
Caleb’s descendants receive a particular promise within that continuing inheritance.

See [[The Sentence and the Presumptuous Ascent]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:34–46; Numbers 14 and 20 for comparisons.

### The spies and the refusal — Deuteronomy 1:19–33

At the border the land is *set before* them again (21). The twelve's whole report is *It is a good land which Yahweh our God gives to us* (25). *Yet you wouldn't go up* (26).

See [[The Spies and the Refusal to Go Up]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:19–33.

### Leave Horeb and possess the land — Deuteronomy 1:6–8

Yahweh's remembered command at Horeb lists the land by kinds of country — hill country, Arabah, lowland, South, seashore, Canaanite land, Lebanon, Euphrates — and declares it already *set before you* (7–8). The gift is stated as done; going in and possessing is what remains. The oath to the fathers who sojourned is the ground of the possession.

See [[Leave Horeb and Possess the Land]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 1:6–8.

### Marriage and tribal inheritance — Numbers 36:1–13

The individual inheritance granted in Numbers 27 must remain within its tribal allotment (36:7–9).
Family continuity and tribal continuity are protected together, but at the cost of restricting
heiresses' marriage choices.

See [[The Marriage of the Daughters of Zelophehad]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 36; comparisons as cited.

### Levitical cities and refuge — Numbers 35:1–34

Tribes contribute cities according to the size of their inheritance (8). Refuge serves Israelites, strangers, and resident foreigners alike (15). Levite holdings and the killer’s eventual return to his own possession (28) qualify any account of land distribution as unrestricted tribal ownership.

See [[The Levitical Cities and the Cities of Refuge]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 35; comparisons as cited.

### The borders of the land — Numbers 34:1–29

Canaan’s boundary (2–12), the eastern exception (13–15), and the named allotment officers (16–29) give the land gift geographical and administrative form. Lot and human administration coexist; the text still does not explain their precise coordination. Genesis 15:18 describes a wider grant toward the Euphrates; the descriptions should not be collapsed into one identical boundary.

See [[The Borders of the Land]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 34.

### Drive out the inhabitants — Numbers 33:50–56

The gift of land grounds the command to take possession and dwell (53). Allotment combines
family and tribal belonging, proportional shares, and lot (54), as in Numbers 26:52–56.
The procedure coordinating size and lot remains unspecified. The closing threat (56) prevents
reading the gift as an unconditional guarantee of continued occupation.

See [[Drive Out the Inhabitants]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 33:50–56.

### The stages of the journey — Numbers 33:1–49

The movement ends in the plains of Moab, with an extended camp beside the Jordan (48–49). Arrival at the border is distinguished from entry and possession.

See [[The Stages of the Journey]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 33:1–49.

### The daughters’ inheritance — Numbers 27:1–11

Following the census allotment instructions, a family without sons receives an explicit right to
inherit through daughters (7–8). The grant is commanded before possession of the land; the hearing
is not a narrated distribution of fields.

See [[The Petition of Zelophehad's Daughters]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 27:1–11.


### The second census — Numbers 26:1–65

Allotment is to reflect numbers, while land is divided by lot (52–56). Both instructions stand;
their administrative coordination is not explained. Levi receives no inheritance among the other
tribes (62).

See [[The Second Census]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 26.


### The journey to Moab — Numbers 21:10–20

The Arnon is explicitly a boundary between Moab and the Amorites (13). Israel’s movement is narrated
through named territories without a conquest being reported within this pericope.

See [[The Journey to Moab]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:10–20.


### The death of Aaron — Numbers 20:22–29

Yahweh describes the land as given to Israel while excluding Aaron from entering (24).
The journey continues beyond the lifetime of one of its leaders.

See [[The Death of Aaron]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 20:22–29.

### The waters of Meribah — Numbers 20:1–13

The congregation protests the absence of seed, figs, vines, pomegranates, and water
(5). Yahweh still describes the land as given to them (12), while excluding the two
leaders from bringing them in. Promised inheritance and individual exclusion coexist.

See [[The Waters of Meribah]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 20:1–13.

### The rebellion of Korah — Numbers 16:1–40

The promised inheritance becomes an accusation against Moses (13–14). The opponents call the land
left behind fertile and desirable, while presenting the lack of fields and vineyards as his failure;
Numbers 14 supplies the preceding refusal of the land.

See [[The Rebellion of Korah]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 16:1–40; comparisons as cited.

### Covenant warnings and remembered promises — Leviticus 26:14–46

Israel is scattered and the land remains. *The land of your enemies will eat you up* (38), while
Yahweh says *I will remember the land* (42). The unit does not state a return; Deuteronomy 30:1–3
does.

See [[Covenant Warnings and Remembered Promises]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Blessings for obedience — Leviticus 26:1–13

*Dwell in your land safely* (5) comes one chapter after *you are strangers and live as foreigners
with me* (25:23), and the two are not reconciled by the text. Security here is conditional tenancy.

See [[Blessings for Obedience (Leviticus)]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### The blasphemer and one law — Leviticus 24:10–23

The foreigner and native-born face the same blasphemy sanction and legal standard
(16, 22). The offender has an Israelite mother and Egyptian father (10–11), but the
narrator does not explicitly assign him either legal category.

See [[The Blasphemer and One Law for All]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 24:10–23.

### Penalties and separation — Leviticus 20

Israel’s inheritance of the land (24) stands alongside the warning of expulsion (22). The Molech
prohibition applies explicitly to resident foreigners as well as Israelites (2); separation does
not exempt Israel from judgment.

See [[Penalties and a People Set Apart]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 20; comparisons as cited.


Going somewhere and not arriving; living in a place that belongs to someone else.

The theme begins in a travel notice. *"They went from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of
Canaan. They came to Haran and lived there"* (Genesis 11:31). It is the first time in the Bible
anyone sets out for [[Canaan]], and the sentence records both the intention and the failure in the
same breath. Two verses later the man who set out is dead in the wrong city. Then Genesis 12:1 —
*"Leave your country, and your relatives, and your father's house, and go to the land that I will
show you"* — turns the unfinished journey into a command, and the rest of the Torah is about
getting there.

Two Hebrew words carry it. ***ʾereṣ***, land or earth, which Genesis has used for the whole world
(1:1) and now begins using for one territory; and **_gûr_**, *to sojourn*, to live somewhere as a
resident alien with no title — the root behind *gēr*, the stranger whom Israelite law will protect
precisely because Israel was one (Exodus 22:21; Leviticus 19:34). The patriarchs are *gērîm* in the
land they are promised. Abraham owns nothing in Canaan until he buys a grave (Genesis 23), and the
one piece of the promise he holds in his hand is a field with a cave in it.

**The structure the theme creates is a permanent gap between promise and possession.** The land is
promised to Abraham and he is a sojourner in it; promised to Isaac and Jacob and they leave it for
famine and for exile; promised to Israel and they spend forty years outside it; given at last under
Joshua and lost again in 587 BC. Hebrews 11 reads the whole sequence as intentional: *"By faith he
lived as an alien in the land of promise, as in a land not his own … for he looked for the city which
has foundations, whose builder and maker is God"* (11:9–10) — which sets the patriarchs' tents
against the city of Genesis 11:4 that had none.

## Development across Scripture

### Passover and the end of manna — Joshua 5:10–12

Israel now eats Canaan’s produce (11–12). The land promise becomes a source of food before
conquest and allotment are complete; entry and completed possession remain distinct.

See [[Passover at Gilgal and the End of the Manna]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 5:10–12; comparisons as cited.

### Reading to all Israel — Deuteronomy 31:9–13

Resident foreigners join the men, women, and children who hear and learn the law (12). Life in the promised land depends on transmitting its obligations, not on treating settlement as the end of instruction (13).

See [[Read the Law to All Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 31:9–13.

### No portion, and a Levite who sojourns — Deuteronomy 18:1–8

Levi has *"no portion nor inheritance with Israel"*; *"Yahweh is their inheritance"* (1–2). The Levite of 18:6 *gār*, *"lives as a foreigner"*, in his own town — the verb of the *gēr*, used of an Israelite without land. Yet 18:8 assumes he may have *"family possessions"* to sell, and the noun *mimkār*, *sale*, is otherwise in the Torah only in the land-sale law of Leviticus 25 (WLC). Landless means without tribal territory, not without property; see [[The Priests Who Kept Their Land and the Levites Who Got None]].

See [[The Portion of the Priests and Levites]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 18:1–8.

### The landless within the gates — Deuteronomy 14:22–29

The Levite has *"no portion nor inheritance"* (27, 29), and the *gēr*, the fatherless, and the widow share the third-year tithe with him (29). The produce of the land is shared with those who hold none of it. See [[Within Your Gates - The Levite, the Foreigner, the Fatherless, and the Widow]].

See [[Tithes for Feasting and Provision]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 14:22–29.

### The *gēr* may eat what Israel may not — Deuteronomy 14:21

The animal that dies of itself may be given to *"the foreigner living among you who is within your gates"* (*gēr*) or sold to *"a foreigner"* (*nokrî*; WLC 14:21, morphhb). The WEB translates both words alike. Leviticus 17:15 by contrast requires the *gēr* who eats such meat to wash and be unclean until evening; the tension is recorded on the passage page and in [[Torn Flesh and Cleansing - Exodus 22 and Leviticus 11 and 17]].

See [[A Holy People and Its Food]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 14:21; Leviticus 17:15.

### Rest and inheritance not yet — Deuteronomy 12:1–28

The statutes are for *"the land … all the days that you live on the earth"* (1). The rule of the place waits on arrival: *"you haven't yet come to the rest and to the inheritance"* (9). The enlarged border (20) and the distance from the place (21) are why meat may be eaten at home.

See [[The Place Yahweh Will Choose]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 12:1–28.

### Every place the sole of your foot treads — Deuteronomy 11:18–32

If Israel keeps the commandments, every place the sole of its foot treads will be its own, from the wilderness and Lebanon to the Euphrates and the western sea (24). The land sworn to the fathers is to be possessed and dwelt in (21, 31).

See [[Set These Words Before You]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 11:18–32.

### Remembered deeds and the rain-fed land — Deuteronomy 11:1–17

The promised land is defined against Egypt: a land of hills and valleys that drinks the rain of the sky, cared for by Yahweh, his eyes on it all year (10–12). Long life there depends on obedience (8–9, 17).

See [[Remember His Deeds and Guard Your Hearts]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 11:1–17.

### What Yahweh requires — Deuteronomy 10:12–22

Remembered foreignness in Egypt grounds Israel’s obligation to love the foreigner (19). Food and clothing in verse 18 make the ethic material, extending the prohibitions of Exodus 22:21 and 23:9 and the love command of Leviticus 19:34.

See [[What Yahweh Requires of Israel]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 10:12–22.

### Blessing and removal — Deuteronomy 7:12–26

The sworn land is the setting of blessing (13). Possession is gradual, *little by little*, so that
the land does not go wild with animals before Israel can fill it (22).

See [[Blessing and the Removal of the Nations]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:12–26.

### Chosen in love — Deuteronomy 7:1–11

Entry into the land is paired with displacement and destruction of its seven named peoples (1–5).
Calling Israel chosen and redeemed (6–8) does not remove the human cost of possession.

See [[A People Chosen in Love]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 7:1–11.

### Wholehearted love and daily instruction — Deuteronomy 6:1–9

The instruction is for doing in the land Israel is going over to possess (1–3). The promise to the
fathers and the prospect of increase accompany the call to obedience; the address anticipates
settlement rather than narrating it.

See [[Hear Israel and Love Yahweh]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 6:1–9.

### The request for a mediator — Deuteronomy 5:23–33

The land is God's gift to be possessed (31), and the instruction Moses receives is for practice
there. Prolonged days and well-being are joined to walking in the commanded way (33): possession
carries an ongoing obligation.

See [[The People's Request for a Mediator]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 5:23–33.

### The commandments recounted — Deuteronomy 5:1–22

The resident stranger receives Sabbath rest within the gates (14); the promise attached to honoring parents concerns the land Yahweh gives (16). No new boundary description is supplied.

See [[The Ten Commandments Recounted]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 5:1–22.


### Joshua commissioned — Numbers 27:12–23

Moses is allowed to see *the land which I have given to the children of Israel* (12) but not to
enter it. Seeing without possessing is the form his exclusion takes.

See [[Joshua Commissioned]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 27:12–23.


### Sihon and Og — Numbers 21:21–35

Israel moves from requesting transit to occupying Amorite cities (22–25, 31). The narrator
distinguishes Sihon’s earlier seizure of Moabite territory from Israel’s present conquest (26);
Ammon’s strong border remains a limit (24).

See [[Sihon and Og]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:21–35.


### Arad and Hormah — Numbers 21:1–3

The encounter on the southern approach ends in destruction of cities, without a
report of occupation or settlement (1–3). A local victory should not be treated as
completion of the promised entry into the land.

See [[Arad and Hormah]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 21:1–3.

### Edom refuses passage — Numbers 20:14–21

Israel requests limited transit across Edom, promising no damage and then payment for
water. The refusal is met by withdrawal, not a territorial claim (17–21).

See [[Edom Refuses Passage]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 20:14–21.


### Duties and portions — Numbers 18:1–32

Aaron has Yahweh as his portion, and Levi receives the tithe as inheritance in
return for service (20–24). This lack of territorial inheritance must be held beside the
explicit Levite city houses and fields of Leviticus 25:32–34, not turned into a denial of property.

See [[The Duties and Portions of Priests and Levites]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 18.

### Vows, valuations, and devoted things — Leviticus 27:1–34

Dedicated land still answers to the Jubilee. Its value is prorated to that year, a bought field
returns to its original holder (24), and an unredeemed inherited field goes to the priest (21), an
exception to 25:28 flagged on the passage page.

See [[Vows, Valuations, and Devoted Things]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 27.

### Poor brother and service — Leviticus 25:35–55

The poor brother is named with the nouns of 25:23, *gēr wətôšāb* (35), and the sold brother goes
back to *the possession of his fathers* (41). The resident aliens of 45 have no such return; their
children may be bought, and the word *possession* is applied to them.

See [[The Poor Brother and Redemption from Service]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 25.

### Land and house redemption — Leviticus 25:23–34

Israel holds the land as strangers and foreigners with Yahweh, its owner (23). Family
fields return through redemption or Jubilee; village houses follow the fields, while
walled-city houses can become permanent purchases after one year (25–31).

See [[Redemption of Land and Houses]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 25.

### Land rest and Jubilee — Leviticus 25:1–22

The given land must rest, and Jubilee restores property and family connection (2, 10, 13). The price of a transaction counts the crops remaining before return (15–16); the explicit ownership rationale follows at 23, outside this unit.

See [[The Sabbatical Year and the Jubilee]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 25:1–22; contextual verses as cited.


### Booths and remembered deliverance — Leviticus 23:33–44

After gathering the land’s produce, native-born Israelites dwell in temporary
shelters to remember departure from Egypt (39, 42–43). Settled abundance is thus
paired with the memory of a people whose dwelling Yahweh provided on departure.

See [[The Feast of Booths]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:33–44; comparisons as cited.

### First fruits and harvest provision — Leviticus 23:9–22

The coming land is Yahweh's gift (10), and its harvest is subject to duties toward
God and the foreigner (14, 22). Possession does not confer a right to collect every
part of the crop; foreigners share the protected access to gleanings.

See [[First Fruits and the Feast of Weeks]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 23:9–22; contextual references as cited.


### Field and community — Leviticus 19:19–37

The orchard law anticipates entry into the land (23–25), while the foreigner within it must
receive native-like treatment and love (33–34). Israel’s own foreign residence in Egypt grounds
the obligation.

See [[Holy Living in Field and Community]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19.


### Holiness and the neighbor — Leviticus 19:1–18

Harvest corners, gleanings, and fallen grapes must remain for poor people and foreigners (9–10).
Possession of productive land carries obligations toward residents who need its produce; no named
location is supplied.

See [[Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19:1–18.


### Forbidden unions and the land — Leviticus 18:1–30

The promised destination can expel Israel as well as its predecessors (3, 24–28). Native-born people and resident foreigners share the obligation (26); entry is not unconditional tenure.

See [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18.

### Sacrifice and blood — Leviticus 17:1–16

Resident foreigners are expressly included in the offering and blood rules
(8–14) and in carcass cleansing (15). Verse 3 addresses the house of Israel specifically;
the later inclusive clauses should not be silently substituted for its wording.

See [[The Place of Sacrifice and the Life in the Blood]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 17; comparisons as cited.


### Houses in the promised land — Leviticus 14:33–57

Canaan is named as the possession Yahweh gives (34); the law anticipates settled
houses and cities before entry is narrated. Possession does not exempt a dwelling
from inspection, costly repair, or demolition. Gift of land and regulation of life
in it stand together in this instruction.

See [[Mildew in a House]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 14:33–57; contextual references as cited.

- **Genesis 11:31–32** — the first journey toward Canaan, undertaken by [[Terah (person)]] with no command
  behind it, stopping at [[Haran (city)]]. He dies short of the land.
- **Genesis 12:1, 5–9** — the command, the arrival, and the first promise of the land itself. Three
  things are set here that hold for the rest of the Bible. The destination is **withheld** — *"the
  land that I will show you"*, where Terah's journey had named Canaan and stopped. The land is
  **occupied** when he gets there, and the narrator says so (12:6) before any promise about it is
  made. And the promise, when it comes, is **not to him**: *"I will give this land to your
  offspring"* (12:7). Abram crosses the whole country from Shechem to the Negev and ends the pericope
  *"still going on"*, having built two altars and pitched one tent. Acts 7:5 states the outcome
  flatly — *"he gave him no inheritance in it, no, not enough to set his foot on."* See
  [[The Call of Abram]].
- **Genesis 12:10–20** — the theme's first hard test, and it comes four verses after the promise.
  *"There was a famine in the land"*, and Abram *"went down into Egypt to live as a foreigner
  there"* — **_lāgûr_**, the theme's own verb, now applied to a country that was never promised him.
  The sequence is worth stating flatly: the land is promised at 12:7, and by 12:10 it cannot feed
  him. He is a *gēr* in Canaan because it belongs to somebody else, and a *gēr* in Egypt because he
  is starving; the difference between the two is a promise and nothing else. Genesis offers no
  comment on the descent, and Isaac will be explicitly forbidden the same move at 26:2. See
  [[Abram in Egypt]].
- **Genesis 15:7–21** — the promise finally given machinery, and the gap between title and possession
  written into the machinery itself. The land gets a **border** for the first time (*"from the river
  of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates"*, 15:18, where 12:7 and 13:15 had only pointed)
  and an **inventory** of ten peoples currently on it. And in the same breath it gets a **delay**:
  four hundred years in which Abram's offspring will *live as foreigners* — *gûr*, the theme's own
  verb — *"in a land that is not theirs"*, before they come back to a land that is also, for now, not
  theirs. Abram asks *"how will I know that I will inherit it?"* (15:8) and is told he will be dead
  and buried first (15:15). The reason for the delay is somebody else's moral condition entirely:
  *"the iniquity of the Amorite is not yet full"* (15:16) — the current occupants keep the land until
  they have earned losing it, which Leviticus 18:28 will then turn on Israel. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] and
  [[From the River of Egypt to the Euphrates]].
- **Genesis 22:20–23:20** — the theme's flattest sentence and its one executed clause, in the same
  chapter. *"I am a stranger and a foreigner living with you"* — *gēr wətôšāb*, the two words for a
  man who cannot own ground — said by Abraham in the sentence where he asks to own some. He is
  offered a tomb free twice and refuses twice, because a gift is held at the giver's pleasure and a
  purchase heard at the city gate is not, and pays four hundred shekels for the field of Machpelah
  under the promise's own legal noun, *ʾăḥuzzâ*, **possession** (23:4, 9, 20; compare 17:8's
  *ʾăḥuzzat ʿôlām*). **The only land he ever owns is a burial plot, and he pays full price for it.**
  See [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] and [[Machpelah]].
- **Genesis 47:9** — Jacob to Pharaoh: *"The days of the years of my pilgrimage are one hundred
  thirty years."*
- **Exodus and Numbers** — the land as the destination of the exodus, and the wilderness generation
  that does not enter it; Moses sees it from Nebo and dies outside (Deuteronomy 34:1–5), the same
  ending as Terah's, at the other end of the Torah.
- **Leviticus 25:23** — the theological centre: *"The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the
  land is mine; for you are strangers and live as foreigners with me."* Israel in the land is still a
  sojourner, and the landlord is God.
- **Joshua 21:43–45; Judges 2** — the land given, and immediately not fully held.
- **2 Kings 25; Psalm 137** — the land lost, and sojourning resumed involuntarily.
- **1 Chronicles 29:15; Psalm 39:12** — Israel praying the patriarchs' status back onto itself: *"we
  are strangers before you, and foreigners, as all our fathers were."*
- **Hebrews 11:8–16** — the New Testament's reading: they *"confessed that they were strangers and
  pilgrims on the earth"* and sought *"a better country, that is, a heavenly one."*
- **1 Peter 1:1; 2:11** — the church addressed as *"sojourners of the Dispersion"* and urged to live
  *"as foreigners and pilgrims."*

**The pattern in one line:** in Scripture, the people who are promised land are almost never the
people who possess it, and the ones who possess it are told they hold it as tenants. Genesis 11:31 is
where that starts, in a sentence about a family that got halfway.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:10, 12

The resident stranger within the gates receives the Sabbath's rest (20:10), and honoring parents is
associated with long life in the land God gives (20:12). This is legislation anticipating settled
life while Israel still stands at Sinai. Canaan is the contextual destination; the verse itself
calls it the given land without naming it. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

**Exodus 22:21 is the first law in which the *gēr* is protected because Israel was one:**
*"You shall not wrong an alien or oppress him, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt"* —
*gērîm hĕyîtem*, the status Genesis 15:13 foretold (*kî-gēr yihyeh zarʿăkā*, MAM). The forbidden verb
*lāḥaṣ* is Egypt's verb at 3:9. See [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] and [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]].

### Knowing the alien and letting the land rest — Exodus 23:1–19

Israel's experience in Egypt grounds the prohibition of oppressing an alien (23:9), and the alien is
expressly included among those refreshed by the seventh day (23:12). The cultivation law anticipates
fields, vineyards, and olive groves, with food left for the poor and animals in the seventh year
(23:10–11). This is a commanded future use of land, not a report that Israel already possesses it.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The prepared place and its border — Exodus 23:20–33

The land is *"the place which I have prepared"* (23:20), *"your land"* (23:26, 33). It receives its
second border in the Torah, from the Red Sea to the sea of the Philistines and from the wilderness to
the River (23:31), and a stated pace of entry: *"Little by little I will drive them out from before
you, until you have increased and inherit the land"* (23:30). The land is to be protected from
becoming desolate even as it changes hands (23:29). See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] and
[[From the River of Egypt to the Euphrates]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

The land flowing with milk and honey is still offered (33:1–3), but Moses will not leave without
God’s presence (33:15). Arrival is not an adequate substitute for accompaniment. The passage
qualifies any account in which obtaining territory alone fulfills the purpose of the exodus. See
[[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

God promises to drive out the inhabitants and enlarge the borders, adding that no one will
desire Israel's land while its males go to appear before him three times yearly (34:11, 23–24).
Worship and landholding are joined by a promise of security. The text states a future assurance,
not a report that the people possess the land or that no later invasion will occur under any
circumstances. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The tabernacle raised and filled — Exodus 40:1–38

The tabernacle’s erection is dated to the first day of the first month in the second
year (40:17), and the ending looks across Israel’s journeys (40:36–38). The book reaches
divine indwelling before it reaches settlement in the promised land. Its closing verses
state the rule for departure and waiting rather than narrating a particular departure
from Sinai or arrival at a new destination.

See [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. Source: Exodus 40 checked in `raw/bible/Exodus.md` (WEB), 2026-10-25.

See [[Cloud and Fire - From the Sea to the Filled Tabernacle]] for the cloud’s continuing guidance and differing access.

### The upright dwell, the wicked are torn out — Proverbs 2:21–22

*"For the upright will dwell in the land. The perfect will remain in it. But the wicked will be cut off
from the land. The treacherous will be rooted out of it"* (2:21–22). The wisdom tradition's rule in the
covenant's vocabulary — *cut off* (Genesis 17:14), *rooted out* (Deuteronomy 28:63) — and in Psalm
37's (*"The righteous shall inherit the land, and live in it forever"*, 37:29), which Matthew 5:5
takes up as *"they shall inherit the earth"*. **Tension flagged, not resolved**: this page's own
history is the exception — patriarchs who held the promised land as sojourners, owning a grave — and
so is Job, *tām wəyāšār*, who lost his. See [[If You Seek Her as Silver]] and
[[The Upright Will Dwell in the Land - Proverbs 2.21-22, Psalm 37, and Matthew 5.5]]. Source: Proverbs 2 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:30

The righteous are promised permanence while the wicked will not dwell in the land, restating
Proverbs 2:21–22’s contrast. No country or boundary is named in 10:30; its covenant-land resonance
is a comparison, not an explicit place identification. See
[[The Upright Will Dwell in the Land - Proverbs 2.21-22, Psalm 37, and Matthew 5.5]].

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### The widow's field — Proverbs 15:25

*"He will keep the widow's borders intact"* (25): land at the scale of one field, kept by Yahweh for the person least able to keep it. Proverbs forbids moving *"the ancient boundary stone"* at 22:28 and 23:10, and Job 24:2 lists those *"who remove the landmarks"*. See [[The Widow's Borders - Proverbs 15.25 and the Ancient Boundary Stone]].

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### The ancient boundary stone — Proverbs 22:28; 23:10

*"Don't move the ancient boundary stone, which your fathers have set up"* (22:28). The land is held by
inheritance, and its edge is a stone the fathers set; moving it takes a neighbour's inheritance a
furrow at a time. At 23:10 the command is repeated with its most exposed victim: *"Don't encroach on the
fields of the fatherless"*. Deuteronomy 19:14 and 27:17 (not yet stored) carry the law behind it.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]] and [[The Widow's Borders - Proverbs 15.25 and the Ancient Boundary Stone]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11.

### Born in the land, at Sinai — Numbers 9:14

*ʾEzraḥ hāʾāreṣ*, *him who is born in the land*, is said to a people camped in a wilderness. As at Exodus 12:48, the category is drawn before the land is held.

See [[The Passover in the Second Year]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 9.

### The place he promised — Numbers 10:29

Moses tells Hobab *We are journeying to the place of which Yahweh said, "I will give it to you."* The land is unnamed here, and it is the reason for the march. Hobab answers with his own land (30).

See [[The March from Sinai]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 10.

### The spies — Numbers 13

The land is seen by Israel for the first time since Jacob's family left it. Yahweh calls it *the
land of Canaan, which I give* (2). The spies bring its fruit and say *it flows with milk and honey*
(27), and then call it *a land that eats up its inhabitants* (32). The chapter shows the promise and
the fear side by side at the border, and the fathers' graves at Hebron go unmentioned.

See [[The Twelve Spies]].

### The land refused — Numbers 14

The promise is intact, *the land which I swore to their fathers* (23), and those to whom it was on
offer say they want Egypt. They are said to have *rejected* it (31). Those who reach it will be the
children, and Caleb. See [[The People Refuse the Land]].

### The land, again — Numbers 15

One chapter after the sentence, the formula *when you have come into the land* returns unchanged. See
[[Offerings in the Land and Sins of Ignorance]].

### Reuben, Gad, and the Land Beyond the Jordan — Numbers 32:1–42

The first tribes to settle do so east of the Jordan, outside what the chapter calls *the land of Canaan*
(30, 32). Moses gives it as *a possession before Yahweh* (22), but the condition attached treats crossing
the river as entering *the land which Yahweh has given them* (7, 9). The chapter leaves open whether the
eastern grant is a full inheritance or something less.

See [[Reuben, Gad, and the Land Beyond the Jordan]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 32.

### *"You shall not cause the land to sin"* — Deuteronomy 24:4

A husband's taking back his divorced wife after her second marriage would *"cause the land to sin"*,
the hiphil of *ḥāṭāʾ* (WLC). The land is again *"which Yahweh your God gives you for an inheritance"*,
the phrase of 19:10 and 21:23. Jeremiah 3:1 asks of the same case, *"Wouldn't that land be greatly
polluted?"* See [[Divorce, Remarriage, and the Newlywed]] and [[A Bill of Divorce - Deuteronomy 24.1-4, Isaiah 50.1, and Jeremiah 3]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 24:1–5.

### *"I have come to the land"* — Deuteronomy 26:1–10, 15

The rite assumes the land is held: *"when you have come in … possess it, and dwell in it"* (1). The
farmer tells the priest *"I have come to the land which Yahweh swore to our fathers to give us"* (3),
and recites a father who *"lived there"* in Egypt, *wayyāgor*, as a sojourner (5; the verb of Genesis
12:10, WLC). The prayer that closes the chapter asks blessing on *"the ground which you have given
us"* (15). See [[First Fruits and the Tithe Declaration]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 26:1–15; Genesis 12:10.

- [[The Call of Abram]] — Genesis 12:1–9: the land entered, crossed end to end, promised to somebody
  not yet born, and already somebody else's
- [[Abram in Egypt]] — Genesis 12:10–20: the promised land starves him out of itself four verses
  after the promise, and he sojourns in Egypt instead
- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — Genesis 13:6, 14–18: the land cannot carry two of his households, and
  in the same chapter it is given to him and his offspring *forever*, in four directions, with an
  order to walk its length and breadth. Total title, zero possession — and he answers by pitching a
  tent
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — Genesis 14: the resident alien defends the country he has
  been promised and does not own, is blessed in it by a king who does own a city in it, and then
  refuses to take a thread of it as payment. He is called *the Hebrew* here (14:13) — on either
  etymology, *the one from the other side*
- [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] — Genesis 15:7–21: the promise sworn, bordered, and inventoried,
  with four centuries of servitude in someone else's country written into the same oracle. Total
  title, zero possession, and a stated reason for the delay
- [[From the River of Egypt to the Euphrates]] — the largest land claim in Scripture against the
  smaller map Numbers 34 and Ezekiel 47 actually work from
- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17:8, the land promise in its strongest
  formulation and its sharpest contradiction: *"I will give to you, and to your offspring after you,
  the land where you are traveling, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession."*
  *ʾĂḥuzzat ʿôlām* is the legal phrase for a permanent inherited holding, and it is applied, in the
  same sentence, to a country the recipient is described as *traveling* in and will die owning one
  cave of
- [[Hagar and Ishmael]] — Genesis 16, where the theme turns around and looks at the household from
  outside. *"after Abram had lived ten years in the land of Canaan"* (16:3) is the verse's only
  measurement, and it is a measurement of waiting. And the pericope's other traveller is going the
  other way: an Egyptian slave on *"the way to Shur"*, the road out of the promised land and back to
  the country the covenant has just called four hundred years of servitude (15:13). She is stopped at
  the frontier and sent back; her son ends up ranging *"from Havilah to Shur"* (25:18), on the desert
  margin of a land he has no share in
- [[Machpelah]] — the field, the cave, and the trees inside its borders: the only ground Abraham owns
- Leviticus 25:23 — *"the land is mine; for you are strangers"*
- Deuteronomy 34:1–5 — Moses outside the land
- Hebrews 11:8–16 — the patriarchs as pilgrims looking for a city

- [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] — Genesis 19:9: *"This one fellow came in to live as a
  foreigner, and he appoints himself a judge."* The *gēr* charge in the mouth of a mob, thrown at a
  man after twenty years in the town — and the reason Israel's law will ground protection of the
  resident alien in memory rather than in pity

- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:30: the end of the line. A *gēr* in Sodom, a refugee in
  Zoar, and a cave-dweller in the hills, holding nothing and buying nothing. His descendants are
  given their territory by divine grant in Deuteronomy 2:9 and 2:19, identified as *"the children of
  Lot"*; he himself never owns a foot of ground, where his uncle's one purchase is a cave and a field
  bought in public at full price (23:3–20)
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:1, 15: *"He lived as a foreigner in Gerar"* — *gûr* again,
  the theme's own verb, now at the far southwestern edge of the country he has been promised, between
  Kadesh and Shur. And then the offer that shows exactly whose land it is: *"Behold, my land is
  before you. Dwell where it pleases you"* (20:15) — **Abram's own words to Lot at 13:9, given back
  to him by a king who actually owns the ground**. The man promised the land is granted permission to
  live on part of it by its holder, and he takes it
- [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]] — Genesis 21:14, 20–21. The heir of the land
  promise is born to a man who owns none of it and is at this point living as a foreigner in Gerar;
  and the other son is put out into a wilderness that **will** belong to his descendants (25:18).
  Hagar *"wandered"* — *wattētaʿ*, from *tāʿâ*, the verb Abraham had used one chapter earlier of his
  own call: *"When God caused me to wander from my father's house"* (20:13). The same word for the
  journey he was sent on and the journey he sends her on
- [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] — Genesis 21:22–34, where the theme reaches its sharpest single verse.
  Abraham **signs a non-aggression treaty over the land he has been promised**, with the man who
  currently holds it, binding himself and his son and his son's son not to deal falsely — and
  extending the obligation *"to the land in which you have lived as a foreigner"* (21:23). He
  defends his claim to a well not by the promise but by the labour — *"that I have dug this well"*
  (21:30); and the pericope closes
  *"Abraham lived as a foreigner in the land of the Philistines many days"* (21:34), *wayyāgor*, the
  theme's own verb, for the last time before Moriah. **The promise is never mentioned in the
  negotiation.** And in the middle of it he plants a **tamarisk** — the one act of planting in the
  patriarchal narratives, on ground he has just conceded is not his, invoking the God whose duration
  runs past the horizon. See [[The Trees of the Patriarchs]] and
  [[The Everlasting God - El Olam in Genesis and Isaiah]]
- [[The Binding of Isaac]] — Genesis 22:1–19: three days' walk across the country he owns nothing of,
  to *the land of Moriah* — a region the Torah names only here, and which 2 Chronicles 3:1 will
  identify with the hill the temple stands on. **The one piece of ground in Genesis that Abraham's
  descendants will hold permanently is a place he visits for a day and names after what happened
  there**, and he goes home to a well he holds by treaty. See
  [[Yahweh Will Provide - Moriah and the Temple Mount]]

- [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] — Genesis 23, where the theme stops being a tension and
  becomes a transaction. Eighteen verses of conveyance for one verse of burial; three bows, two
  refused gifts, four hundred shekels weighed to the merchants' standard, an itemized scope clause
  (field, cave, *"all the trees … in all of its borders"*), and witnesses at the gate. **The deed is
  stated twice and the second statement comes after the burial**, so the chapter ends on the title
  rather than on the grave. Acts 7:5 gives the balance without softening: *"he gave him no
  inheritance in it, no, not so much as to set his foot on"*. See
  [[A Stranger and a Foreigner - The Sojourner's Confession]] and [[Burial and the Grave]]
- [[A Wife for Isaac]] — Genesis 24:5–8: the theme stated as a prohibition with no exception. The
  servant is released from the oath if the bride refuses, but *"you shall not bring my son there
  again"* is repeated and never qualified. **The marriage is negotiable and the land is not** — and
  the country Isaac may not enter is the country his wife must be fetched from

- [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] — Genesis 25:6, 9–11, 18: the cycle's answer on
  land, delivered as four facts and no comment. Abraham dies owning one field and is buried in it;
  seven of his sons are sent *"eastward, to the east country"*, the direction Genesis uses for
  removal (3:24; 4:16; 13:11; 36:6); his heir settles at a desert well named by a runaway slave; and
  the son who was put out of the household ranges over four hundred miles of country the covenant
  never claimed

- [[Isaac in Gerar]] — Genesis 26:2–3, 15–22: **the theme's sharpest statement, and God makes it.**
  *"Live in this land"* — ***gûr***, to sojourn as a resident alien — is what the heir of the land
  promise is told to do in the land, in the same breath as *"I will give to you, and to your
  offspring, all these lands"*. **The chapter then itemizes the difference between the two verbs.** He
  farms ground he does not own and reaps a hundredfold; his father's wells are filled in with earth,
  which destroys the asset rather than transferring it and is therefore eviction rather than theft; a
  king tells him to leave and he leaves without invoking the promise; and his herdsmen lose two
  springs to a counter-claim he never contests. **He ends the chapter with a tent, an altar, a well,
  and a treaty — and no title to anything.** The only prohibition in the patriarchal promises is in
  the same speech: *"Don't go down into Egypt"*
- [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]] — Genesis 28:4: ***ʾereṣ məgurêkā***, *the land of your
  sojournings*, promised as an inheritance in the same clause that describes it by the word for not
  owning it. The phrase is God's own at 17:8; here a father uses it over a son who is about to leave
  the country for twenty years, and whose family will still hold nothing in it but a cave when
  Genesis ends.
- [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] — Genesis 28:13, 15: **the grant at its smallest, and the first
  promise of a return.** *"The land whereon you lie, to you will I give it"* — measured by the body
  of a sleeping man, against the four horizons Abram was told to look across and then walk (13:14–17).
  **Jacob is horizontal, unconscious, and in the dark**, and what he owns at that moment is a stone he
  did not buy in a field whose name he does not know. And because he is the first bearer of the
  promise to leave the country carrying it, the promise acquires a clause it never needed before:
  ***wahăšibōtîkā ʾel-hāʾădāmâ hazzōʾt***, *I will bring you again into this land.*
- [[Jacob's Flocks]] — Genesis 30:25: **the first time the bearer of the promise asks to go back, and
  he asks a man rather than God.** *"**Send me away**, that I may go to **my own place, and to my
  country**"* — ***šalləḥēnî***, the piel of *šālaḥ* that Moses will use on Pharaoh — and **the land
  is named by nothing but two possessive pronouns.** No *Canaan*, no *the land of your sojournings*,
  no reference to 28:13 or 28:15. **What he is invoking is his own vow rather than God's promise**:
  28:21's *"so that I come again to my father's house in peace"* is the clause he is moving to
  collect, six years before God commands the return at 31:3. **He is refused**, and the refusal is
  the theme's first case of a foreign household declining to release the family — the thing Exodus
  will be about. See [[Oaths and Vows]] and [[Blessed for His Sake - Laban's House and Potiphar's]]
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:3, 13, 18; **the return commanded, six years after it
  was refused.** *"Return to the land of your fathers, and to your relatives, and I will be with
  you"* (31:3) discharges the last clause of 28:15, and the destination the narrator states is not a
  country but a man — *"to go to Isaac his father, to the land of Canaan"* (31:18). **The pericope
  ends with the household still outside the land**, on a border in [[Gilead]] it has just sworn never
  to recross
- [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] — Genesis 34:10, 21, 30; **the promise's substance offered as
  a commercial arrangement by the people who currently hold it.** [[Hamor]] says *"the land will be
  before you. Live and trade in it, and get possessions in it"* (34:10), and tells his own citizens
  *"the land is large enough for them"* (34:21) — **an open invitation to settle, which is more than
  God's promise has yet delivered to anyone in the family, and it is refused.** And the pericope ends
  on the sojourner's standing fear stated plainly: *"I am few in number. They will gather themselves
  together against me and strike me"* (34:30) — ***mĕtê mispār***, the phrase Psalm 105:12 uses of the
  patriarchs as a whole. **Jacob had bought his first piece of ground one chapter earlier (33:19); by
  the end of this one he cannot stay on it**, and the next chapter moves the household to Bethel
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:12, 27; **the promise and the reality
  in one chapter.** God says *"the land which I gave to Abraham and Isaac, I will give it to you"*
  (35:12); twenty verses later Jacob arrives at Mamre, *"where Abraham and Isaac lived as
  **foreigners**"* — ***gār***, the sojourner's verb — **on ground of which the two of them owned one
  cave.** And [[Rachel]] is buried by the roadside between the two statements, on land nobody in the
  family has title to. See [[A Stranger and a Foreigner - The Sojourner's Confession]]
- [[The Descendants of Esau]] — Genesis 36:6–8, 43: **the one man in Genesis who separates from the
  covenant line and gains a country by it.** Esau leaves because the land cannot carry two
  households, and Deuteronomy 2:5 makes the move permanent in the same voice that grants Canaan —
  *"I have given Mount Seir to Esau for a possession."* **The contrast is drawn in consecutive
  verses**: 36:43 closes *"This is Esau, the father of the Edomites"*, and 37:1 has Jacob living *"in
  the land of his father's travels"*, ***məgûrê***, his **sojournings**. Esau possesses; Jacob
  sojourns. See [[Too Great to Dwell Together - Abram and Lot, Esau and Jacob]] and [[Edom]]
- [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:1–5; **the first son of Jacob to live apart from the household.**
  *"Judah went down from his brothers"* — ***wayyēred***, topographically correct for the descent from
  the Hebron ridge into the Shephelah, **and the same verb the book is using in the same week for
  Joseph's descent to Egypt** (37:25; 39:1). **One goes down under his own power and one is carried.**
  What he goes down into is Canaanite country: a Canaanite friend at [[Adullam]], a Canaanite wife,
  and three sons born in Canaanite towns, one of them at [[Chezib]]. **The covenant line is not living
  among the nations here so much as inside them** — and **the line of David is conceived there**, which
  Genesis reports without a syllable of comment. See [[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]]
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:46–57, **the chapter in which Egypt becomes *the land*.**
  ***ʾEreṣ*** saturates it — *the land of Egypt* a dozen times over — **and [[Canaan]] is not named
  once.** **The sojourner is now the governor**: *"Joseph went out from the presence of Pharaoh, and went
  throughout all the land of Egypt"* (41:46). **And he still calls it *"the land of my affliction"***
  (41:52). **Famine is the reason anyone in Genesis ever leaves anywhere** — it moved [[Abraham]] at
  12:10 and was forbidden to move [[Isaac]] at 26:2 — **and this is the famine that moves the whole
  household permanently**, since 41:57's *"all countries came into Egypt"* is what puts Jacob's sons on
  the road in 42:1. See [[Famine and Plenty]]
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:2–5, 34; **the first descent of the sons of Israel into Egypt as a group**, in
  the verb ***yārad*** that carries every descent in the book (12:10; 39:1; 46:3). *"You shall trade in
  the land"* (42:34) — **a promise Joseph never made, invented by the brothers in the retelling** — is
  the first hint of the settlement 47:27 will report
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:11, 15, 32; **the second descent in two chapters, and the first
  that ends indoors.** *Yārad* again (43:15), but the destination is no longer a granary — it is a house,
  named seven times in nineteen verses. **The *"choice fruits of the land"* leave Canaan in a famine**
  (43:11), and the chapter closes on the fact that the two peoples cannot share a table (43:32) —
  **the first statement of the separateness that keeps Israel distinct through four hundred years of
  living in somebody else's country**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:9–11, 18–20; **the invitation out of the land of the
  promise, and nobody in the chapter raises the difficulty.** *"Come down to me. Don't wait"* (45:9) is
  issued by the one member of the family who has not lived in [[Canaan]] for twenty-two years, and the
  address he assigns is [[Goshen]] — **the word's first appearance in Scripture** (45:10).
  [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] widens it to *"the good of the land of Egypt"* and tells them to leave
  their belongings behind (45:18, 20). **A household called out of Ur and promised Canaan is invited to
  abandon its possessions and move to Egypt**, and Genesis lets the whole chapter pass without a word
  about the promise; **that waits for 46:2–4**
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:3–4; 47:9, 11; **the pericope where the covenant
  family leaves the land of the promise with God's explicit permission.** **[[Jacob]] calls his own
  hundred and thirty years *"my pilgrimage"*** — ***məgûray***, the noun Genesis uses for the
  patriarchs' whole tenure in Canaan (17:8; 28:4; 37:1) — **and applies it to a lifespan rather than a
  place.** **Then [[Joseph]] gives them *"a possession"* in Egypt** (47:11), ***ʾăḥuzzâ***, **a word
  Genesis has used only of the cave at [[Machpelah]]**: **the family's first freehold anywhere but a
  grave is in [[Rameses]]**
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:20, 27, 29–30; **two freeholds move in opposite directions
  in seven verses.** *"The land became Pharaoh's"* (47:20) — **every field in Egypt, in perpetuity**;
  and *"they got themselves possessions therein"* (47:27), ***wayyēʾāḥăzû bāh***, **Israel taking
  hold of one.** **Then the head of the household with the new freehold spends his last scene
  refusing to be buried on it**: *"Please don't bury me in Egypt … bury me in their burying place"*
  (47:29–30). **The only land this family has ever owned in [[Canaan]] is a tomb, and it is the land
  that gets the oath.** See [[The Land Is Mine - Pharaoh's Freehold and the Jubilee]]
- [[Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh]] — Genesis 48:4, 21–22; **three claims on ground, from a man dying in
  [[Egypt]].** God gave the land *"for an everlasting possession"* (48:4); God will *"bring you again"* to
  it (48:21); **and *"I have given to you one portion above your brothers, which I took out of the hand of
  the Amorite with my sword and with my bow"*** (48:22) — **the only conquest [[Jacob]] ever claims, for
  ground the narrator says he bought** (33:19). **Meanwhile the land appears in the pericope only as a
  theophany and a grave** (48:3, 7). See
  [[Bought, Sacked, or Taken by the Sword - How Jacob Got Shechem]]
- [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]] — Genesis 49:13, 15, 26, 29–32; **a man dying in [[Egypt]] distributes
  land he does not own.** [[Zebulun]] gets a harbour and a border on [[Sidon (city)]] **that Joshua
  19:10–16 does not give him**; [[Issachar]] gets a pleasant land and pays for it in forced labour;
  [[Asher]] gets rich food; [[Joseph]] gets *"the boundaries of the ancient hills."* **Then the
  chapter names the only ground the family actually holds** — the cave and field at [[Machpelah]],
  bought and paid for (49:30) — **and the only use it is put to is burial.** **Twelve tribal
  territories assigned in twenty-five verses, and the one deed anyone can produce is for a grave**
- **Genesis 50:13, 24 — the promise recited in full with nobody standing in it**: the land is entered
  once in the book's last chapter and only to dig a grave, and the family leaves it in the next verse
  — [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]]
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 24:2–5; *"There are people who remove the
  landmarks"* — the boundary stone that Deuteronomy 19:14 and 27:17 protect and Proverbs 22:28 and
  23:10 forbid moving, especially against the fatherless. The land-grab comes first in Job's list,
  and its result follows: the poor *"all hide themselves"* and go out into the desert like wild
  donkeys.
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:22; **the *gēr* of Genesis 15:13 said aloud by Abraham's offspring for the
  first time**, and in Midian rather than Egypt: *"I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land."* The
  word becomes the ground of the law of the stranger — *"you know the heart of an alien"* (Exodus
  23:9). See [[A Foreigner in a Foreign Land - Gershom and the Law of the Stranger]]
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:8, 17, 22; ***"a land flowing with milk and honey"*** — **the phrase's first
  occurrence**, a shepherd's measure of good country, said to a shepherd — and in the same verse the six
  peoples who hold it. And the Egyptian woman *"who visits her house"* is *gārat bêtāh*, the one
  *sojourning* in the Hebrew woman's house: the root of Gershom's *gēr* (2:22), reversed
- [[I Am Yahweh]] — Exodus 6:4, 8; ***ʾereṣ məgurêhem ʾăšer-gārû bāh***, ***"the land of their
  travels, in which they lived as aliens"*** — **Canaan described by the fact that the fathers never
  owned it**, using the *məgûrîm* of Genesis 17:8; 28:4; 36:7; 37:1; 47:9. It is promised to Israel
  *"for a heritage"*, *môrāšâ*, the only occurrence of the noun in Genesis, Exodus, and Job
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:19, 25; **the native and the stranger distinguished in a land Israel
  does not yet have.** *"Whether he is a foreigner (gēr), or one who is born in the land (ʾezraḥ)"* —
  the first *ʾezraḥ* in Scripture (WLC) — and *"when you have come to the land which Yahweh will give
  you"*
- [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]] — Exodus 12:40, 48–49; **four hundred thirty years of sojourn ended, and the sojourner
  given a law.** *"The time that the children of Israel lived in Egypt was four hundred thirty years"*;
  and in the same chapter the *gēr* who sojourns *with* Israel is made *"as one who is born in the land"* —
  a law of native and stranger for a people that has just stopped being strangers and has no land yet.
  See [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]]
- [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] — Exodus 13:5, 11; ***"when Yahweh shall bring you into the land"***,
  twice. **Both laws in the pericope are for the land, given a day out of Egypt**: the feast kept *"within
  all your borders"* (13:7) and the firstborn given *"when … he shall give it you"* (13:11). The land is the
  bush's *"milk and honey"*, of five peoples rather than six
- [[The Song of the Sea]] — Exodus 15:9, 13–17; **the land sung in advance.** The enemy boasts *"my hand shall destroy
  (tôrîšēmô) them"*, with *yāraš*, the verb of the land promise (Genesis 15:7); the Song names Philistia,
  Edom, Moab, and Canaan in road order, and ends with Israel *"planted"* on *"the mountain of your
  inheritance"* — unnamed, and not yet reached.
