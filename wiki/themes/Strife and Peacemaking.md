# Strife and Peacemaking

## Definition / overview

Genesis is largely a book about relatives who cannot get along. What it mostly narrates is the
quarrel; what it rarely narrates is anyone stopping one. This theme tracks both — the strife, and the
much smaller set of occasions on which somebody defuses it, and on what terms.

The Hebrew noun in the founding text is *rîb* (Genesis 13:7–8), a word with a legal edge: not a mood
but a **dispute**, the kind that has parties and a cause and could go to a judge. The prophets use it
for God's lawsuit against Israel (Hosea 4:1; Micah 6:2). What Abram proposes at 13:8 is that this one
not be filed.

## Development across Scripture

**Strife first, and it is lethal.** The Bible's first quarrel between brothers ends in a field with a
body in it — *"Cain rose up against Abel, his brother, and killed him"* (4:8), in a passage that uses
the word *brother* seven times. Lamech escalates it into a boast (4:23–24). The corruption that
brings the flood is summarized as *ḥāmās*, **violence** (6:11). Babel's unity is broken by God
himself as a mercy (11:1–9). Nothing in Genesis 1–12 shows a human being resolving a conflict.

**Then Abram, at 13:8–9.** Herdsmen fight over water and grazing; the two households are too heavy
for the land; and the senior man in the story does four things in two verses that the Bible will keep
coming back to. He names the relationship — *"for we are relatives"*, literally *we are men,
brothers*, reaching for the word that failed in chapter 4. He takes the dispute on himself rather
than leaving it with the staff — *"between you and me"*. He proposes the standard equitable division,
one party splits and the other picks. And then he hands his junior both halves of it, giving away a
choice that was legally his twice over, as elder and as the man to whom the land had been promised
(12:7). The narrative rewards him immediately and without saying that it is a reward: the promise is
enlarged four verses later (13:14–17). See [[Abram and Lot Separate]].

**And once, at 21:22–34, he does it the other way — by law rather than by concession.** Abimelech's
servants have seized a well; Abraham *"complained"* (*hôkîaḥ*, the verb for pressing a case to
judgment) at the moment the king most wanted a favour from him; Abimelech denied knowledge; and the
matter closed with sheep and cattle handed over, an oath sworn, seven ewe lambs set apart as
evidence, and the place named for the swearing. **Nobody yields, nobody adjudicates, and nothing is
conceded — the dispute is converted into a document.** It is the only conflict in Genesis settled by
what we would now call legal instrument, and it is settled with an army commander standing there and
no violence of any kind. See [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] and [[Oaths and Vows]].

**Isaac repeats the earlier method, deliberately.** The herdsmen of Gerar quarrel with his over the
same wells; he yields the first (*Esek*, *contention*), yields the second (*Sitnah*, *enmity*), and
digs a third unopposed —
*"He called it Rehoboth. He said, 'For now Yahweh has made room for us, and we will be fruitful in
the land.'"* (26:20–22). Same dispute as his father's, same water, same solution: concede the
ground and let God supply the room.

**Jacob mostly fails at it and then manages it once.** The strife with Esau is bought (25:29–34) and
stolen (27), and resolved twenty years later by a gift, a bow, and a man expecting to be killed
(32–33). With Laban it ends in a heap of stones and a mutual boundary neither will cross (31:44–54).
With Joseph's brothers, strife is the whole engine of chapters 37–50, and its resolution is the
book's last word: *"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring to
pass, as it is today, to save many people alive"* (50:20).

**The Law and the Wisdom books turn it into instruction.** Leviticus 19:17–18 forbids hatred in the
heart and grudge-bearing and commands neighbour-love; Proverbs returns to it constantly — *"The
beginning of strife is like breaching a dam, therefore stop contention before quarreling breaks
out"* (17:14), *"It is an honor for a man to keep aloof from strife"* (20:3), *"A soft answer turns
away wrath"* (15:1).

**The New Testament makes peacemaking a family likeness.** *"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they
shall be called children of God"* (Matthew 5:9); *"If it is possible, as much as it is up to you, be
at peace with all men"* (Romans 12:18); *"Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be defrauded?"*
(1 Corinthians 6:7), which is Abram's argument at 13:9 in one line; and Philippians 2:3–4, which is
its motive. James 4:1–2 traces quarrels to wanting, and Hebrews 12:14 makes pursuing peace a
condition of seeing the Lord.

**Exodus brings the word into Israel's camp.** *Rîb*, the verb of the herdsmen at Isaac's wells (*"The water is
ours"*, Genesis 26:20), is next used of Israel against Moses at Rephidim: *"the people quarreled with Moses"*,
and he asks *"Why do you quarrel with me?"* (Exodus 17:2; WLC lemma search, Genesis–Exodus). **Again the
quarrel is over water**, and again the place is named for it — *Meribah*, *quarreling* (17:7), as Isaac named
*Esek*, *contention*. **But this quarrel is with God's appointed leader and counted as a test of God**; the
water is given anyway. See [[Water from the Rock]].

**What the theme does not say** is that yielding always works or is always right. Abram concedes to
Lot and then arms three hundred eighteen men to get him back (14:14). The prophets prosecute God's
*rîb* rather than dropping it. Genesis presents peacemaking as costly, occasional, and unrewarded on
the page — and then keeps arranging its narratives so that the man who gave ground ends up holding
the land.

### Violence, injury, and liability — Exodus 21:12–36

The quarrel of 21:18–19 ends with an obligation to pay for lost time and healing, while the fight
of 21:22 injures a pregnant bystander. Strife creates liabilities beyond the original disputants.
The matching penalties constrain the response to harm, but do not narrate reconciliation between
the parties. Matthew 5:38–42's instruction to relinquish retaliation is compared in
[[Eye for Eye - Exodus 21, Leviticus 24, and Matthew 5]].
See [[Laws of Violence and Injury]].

### Helping someone who hates you — Exodus 23:1–19

The return of an enemy's animal and help for the donkey of someone who hates the hearer are required
while the enmity is still named (23:4–5). Neither apology nor reconciliation is a condition. The law
does not report that assistance ends the dispute; it requires an act that refuses to let hostility
decide whether another's need matters. See
[[Help for an Enemy - Exodus 23, Proverbs 25, and Matthew 5]].

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### All wisdom's paths are peace — Proverbs 3:17

[[Wisdom's Worth and the Founding of the Earth]] presents peace as the character of
wisdom's ways, alongside pleasantness and life. It narrates no dispute or settlement:
the claim is broader than one successful act of reconciliation. Read beside Proverbs
1:10–19's violent invitation, it offers an alternative picture of a desirable path.
The claim does not require wise people to be free of all externally imposed suffering;
Job remains the wiki's explicit counterexample to that inference.

Source: Proverbs 3 and the cited Genesis and Job comparisons checked in the stored WEB,
2026-10-25.

### A neighbor safe beside you — Proverbs 3:21–35

The neighbor's security must not be exploited by a scheme against him (3:29), and strife
must not be initiated without cause against someone who has done no harm (3:30). Refusing
to envy or copy a violent man (3:31) addresses the attraction behind aggression. These
commands give social content to 3:17's description of wisdom's paths as peace; they do
not abolish legitimate grievances.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### Sowing discord — Proverbs 6:14, 19

The worthless man *"always sows discord"* (6:14, *midyānîm yəšallēaḥ*, *sends out strife*), and
the list of seven ends on the same act, *"he who sows discord among brothers"* (6:19). The climactic
position gives strife-making the greatest weight in the list. See
[[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:10, 12, 18

The wink causes sorrow, hatred provokes strife, and love covers wrongs. Concealed hatred and slander
are both condemned (18). Peacemaking therefore involves a changed response to an offense, not merely
an appearance of friendliness. See
[[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]].

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:9, 12–14, 29

Contempt, destructive speech, and betrayed confidences threaten neighbors and households. Holding
one’s peace and seeking wise counsel sustain relationships. The household troubler’s empty
inheritance gives such conflict a cost to the instigator.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:16, 18, 20, 25

Overlooking an insult interrupts immediate retaliation; promoting peace brings joy. Rash speech
wounds, while wise and kindly speech restores. Restraint is not approval of false testimony or
injustice: the same cluster requires truthful witness in verse 17.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Quarrels bred by pride — Proverbs 13:10

Proverbs 13:10 traces quarrels to pride and sets against them the wisdom of those who take advice; strife is diagnosed by its source rather than by its parties.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### Slow to anger — Proverbs 14:17, 29

The quick-tempered commit folly (17); *"He who is slow to anger has great understanding"* (29). The phrase is, in the WEB, the one Exodus 34:6 uses of Yahweh. See [[Slow to Anger - Proverbs 14.29 and Exodus 34.6]].

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### Wrath turned away — Proverbs 15:1, 18

The gentle answer *"turns away wrath"* (1), and *"one who is slow to anger appeases strife"* (18). The first is a matter of speech, the second of character; both make the peacemaker the one who absorbs the anger rather than returns it. See [[Slow to Anger - Proverbs 14.29 and Exodus 34.6]].

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### Enemies at peace — Proverbs 16:7, 14, 28, 32

Yahweh makes a pleasing man's enemies *"at peace with him"* (7); a wise man pacifies the king's wrath (14); the perverse man stirs strife and the whisperer separates friends (28); and the slow to anger is *"better than the mighty"* (32). See [[Slow to Anger - Proverbs 14.29 and Exodus 34.6]].

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### The quiet morsel and the breached dam — Proverbs 17:1, 9, 14, 19

A dry morsel with quiet is better than a house of *"feasting with strife"* (1) — literally *sacrifices of strife*, *zibḥê-rîb* (Hebrew from Sefaria). Covering an offense seeks love while repeating it *"separates best friends"* (9); the beginning of strife is *"like breaching a dam"*, so stop it *"before quarreling breaks out"* (14); and *"He who loves disobedience loves strife"* (19). See [[Better Is Little - The Better-Than Sayings of Proverbs]] and [[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]].

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### The bars of a fortress — Proverbs 18:1, 6, 17–19

The man who separates himself *yitgallāʿ*, *breaks out* against sound judgment (1), the verb of 17:14's
quarrel (Hebrew from Sefaria); the fool's lips *"come into strife"* (6); the lot ends disputes between
the strong (18); and *"A brother offended is more difficult than a fortified city; and disputes are
like the bars of a fortress"* (19). Genesis twice shows the offended brother reconciled anyway — Esau
running to meet Jacob (33:4), Joseph weeping over his brothers' *pešaʿ* (50:17).

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:11–13, 19

Verse 11 gives honor to the person who overlooks an offense, while 13 likens a wife's quarrels to
persistent dripping. Verse 19 observes that rescuing a hot-tempered man must be done again: rescue
alone has not changed his disposition. Beside the king's dangerous wrath in 12, ordinary
self-restraint matters. See [[Slow to Anger - Proverbs 14.29 and Exodus 34.6]] and
[[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]] .

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:1–3, 19, 22

Keeping aloof from strife is honorable (3), answering the brawling drink of verse 1. Verse 22
goes further than managing temper: it forbids promising repayment of evil and directs the
wronged person toward Yahweh's salvation. Avoiding the indiscreet companion (19) also protects
relationships. The command to wait names no timetable or guarantee of an enemy's destruction.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Contention and the pacifying gift — Proverbs 21:9, 14, 19

Domestic contention drives a man to the roof or the desert (9, 19), and a secret gift pacifies anger
(14). Verse 14 reports that a bribe calms wrath; 17:23 has already judged the secret bribe as
perverting justice. See [[A Bribe Blinds - Proverbs 17.23, Exodus 23.8, and Deuteronomy 16.19]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### The mocker driven out — Proverbs 22:10

*"Drive out the mocker, and strife will go out; yes, quarrels and insults will stop"* (22:10). Here
peace is made by removal: the source of strife is a person, and the remedy is social.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### Anger is learned — Proverbs 22:24–25

*"Don't befriend a hot-tempered man, and don't associate with one who harbors anger: lest you learn his
ways, and ensnare your soul"* (22:24–25). The danger named is not the angry man's blows but his
influence: a temper is caught from company. 15:18 had already said what such a man produces —
*"A wrathful man stirs up contention"*.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11; Proverbs 15:18.

### Needless bruises — Proverbs 23:29; 24:2

Among the drinker's six troubles are *"strife"* and *"needless bruises"* (23:29): fights he did not
need to have. In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read 2026-10-26), *needless* is *ḥinnām*,
the *"without cause"* of the gang's ambush at 1:11 — there the victims were struck for nothing; here
the drinker is. The block closes on men whose *"hearts plot violence"* (24:2).

See [[Do Not Envy Sinners]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 23:12–24:2.

### Settle with the neighbour — Proverbs 25:8–10, 23–24

*"Don't be hasty in bringing charges to court. What will you do in the end when your neighbor shames
you? Debate your case with your neighbor, and don't betray the confidence of another"* (25:8–9). The
counsel is to argue a quarrel out directly, without escalation and without using another's secret,
lest a lasting bad name follow (25:10). The chapter's close adds the *"backbiting tongue"* that brings
an angry face (25:23) and the contentious woman of the housetop corner, repeated from 21:9 (25:24).
Between them, 25:15's *"soft tongue"* and 25:21–22's bread for an enemy show the other way.

See [[The Glory of Kings]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25.

### Fuel for the fire — Proverbs 26:17, 20–21

*"Like one who grabs a dog's ears is one who passes by and meddles in a quarrel not his own"* (17) — a
grip that is dangerous to keep and dangerous to let go. *Meddles* is *miṯʿabbēr*, which 14:16 used of
the hot-headed fool; it may mean *becoming furious* rather than *meddling* (the wiki does not decide).
Then strife as fire: *"For lack of wood a fire goes out. Without gossip, a quarrel dies down"* (20),
and *"As coals are to hot embers, and wood to fire, so is a contentious man to kindling strife"* (21).
The *contentious man* is *ʾîš midyānîm*, the counterpart of 25:24's contentious woman. See
[[The Contentious Woman - Proverbs 19.13, 21.9, 19, 25.24, and 27.15]].

See [[Answer a Fool]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 26.

### Provocation, jealousy, and the dripping roof — Proverbs 27:3–4, 15–16

Chapter 27 weighs the burdens of strife. The fool's provocation outweighs stone and sand (3); wrath
and anger overwhelm, but jealousy cannot be stood against (4). The contentious wife returns as *"A
continual dropping on a rainy day"* (15), with 19:13's *delep̄ ṭôrēḏ*, and restraining her is
*"like restraining the wind, or like grasping oil"* (16): quarrel as something that cannot be held. See
[[The Contentious Woman - Proverbs 19.13, 21.9, 19, 25.24, and 27.15]].

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19:13; 27; Hebrew from Sefaria.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:4, 25

Greed stirs up strife (25), while those who keep the law contend with the wicked (4). The chapter
therefore distinguishes self-serving conflict from principled opposition. Peace cannot mean refusing
every confrontation; the motive and object of the contention matter.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

Wise people turn away anger and govern their own temper, while mockery inflames a city and wrath
multiplies strife. Verse 27 still recognizes moral opposition between upright and wicked;
peacemaking does not require treating wrongdoing as acceptable.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:8–11, 22, 27; the passage page records the
cross-reference sources and interpretive limits.

### The forcing of wrath — Proverbs 30:33

Proverbs 30:33 builds a three-line wordplay on *mîṣ* (*pressing*, read in Sefaria's *Miqra
according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-27): churning milk gives butter, wringing the nose (*ʾap̄*) gives
blood, and forcing wrath (*ʾappāyim*) gives strife. The image makes strife the product of pressure,
and the preceding verse gives the remedy: stop before pressing.

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

### A time for every purpose — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

Peace closes the catalogue as war's counterpart (8). No disputants or negotiations are
narrated, so this adds a reflection on peace's place in human experience rather than another
example of a settlement. It does not say that peace is always impossible, or authorize war
whenever someone claims its time has come.

See [[A Time for Every Purpose]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

*"If the spirit of the ruler rises up against you, don’t leave your place; for gentleness lays
great offenses to rest"* (10:4). The conflict is between unequal parties; the subordinate is told
neither to flee nor to retaliate. Proverbs 15:1 and 25:15 make the same preference for soft speech
before wrath and rulers. See
[[Gentleness Before the Ruler - Proverbs 15.1, 25.15, and Ecclesiastes 10.4]].

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

  hand. Do to her whatever is good in your eyes."* But at 13:9 what he gave away was his own claim to

  opposite cost, and the peace it buys is bought with someone else's money
- [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] — Genesis 18:22–33, the same man standing between
  parties for the fourth time, and the only occasion the other party is God. At 13:8–9 he ended a
  quarrel by conceding what was his; here he concedes nothing and argues, and the concessions all
  come from the other side
- [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] — Genesis 21:22–34; the same man's fifth appearance in this theme, and
  the only one in which he neither concedes nor argues but files a complaint and takes a settlement.
  See also [[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]] for the dispute's second run, a generation
  later, at the same well
- [[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]] — Genesis 25:22–34; **the theme relocated inside a
  body.** *"The children struggled together within her"* — *wayyitrōṣăṣû*, from *rāṣaṣ*, the verb for
  **crushing** — and the oracle explains the sensation by forecasting two nations. Then the strife is
  institutionalised by the parents: *"Isaac loved Esau … Rebekah loved Jacob"* (25:28), **the first
  stated parental favouritism in Scripture**, with a reason given for one side and none for the other.
  And then it is transacted: a price named to a starving man, an oath extracted, and an inheritance
  moved. **Nobody in this pericope makes peace** — Abram at 13:8–9 ended a quarrel by conceding what
  was his, and here his grandson ends one by taking what was not
- [[Isaac in Gerar]] — Genesis 26:17–33; **the theme's clearest single case, and it is Isaac's.** He
  is expelled from Gerar without a hearing, digs [[Esek]] and is contested, digs [[Sitnah]] and is
  contested again, and both times **leaves without an argument** — no claim of prior right, no appeal
  to his father's treaty at this same well, no reference to the promise of *all these lands* he had
  been given nineteen verses earlier. Then [[Rehoboth]], unopposed, and the one thing he says in the
  sequence: *"For now Yahweh has made room for us"* (26:22). **It is Abram's method at 13:9 —
  concede, and let God do the arithmetic — run three times in six verses**, and it ends the way 21:22–34
  ended, with the king who caused the trouble travelling inland to ask for an oath. Isaac makes them
  a feast, does not contest their account of their own conduct, and *"they departed from him in
  peace"*
- [[The Stolen Blessing]] — Genesis 27:1–45; **the household of 25:28 detonating.** Two parents who
  each love a different son, a mother who acts entirely for one of them, a father who tries to bless
  the other behind her back, and a brother who leaves the chapter planning to kill the one who is
  left. **Nothing in the chapter is negotiated and nobody concedes anything** — which is the exact
  inverse of what Abraham did with Lot at 13:9 and Isaac did with the herdsmen of Gerar at 26:22, and
  it is the only quarrel in the cycle that ends in flight. The reconciliation is twenty years and six
  chapters away, and **it is the wronged brother who runs to make it** (33:4)
- [[Wrestling at the Jabbok]] — Genesis 32:3–8; **the theme reduced to arithmetic, with no combatant
  named.** Word of Esau's four hundred men produces fear and a division of the camp into two
  companies, on the sole logic that *"if Esau comes to the one company… the company which is left
  will escape."* **No attack happens in this pericope and none is confirmed as coming** — the entire
  peacemaking apparatus of the chapter (the division, the prayer, the staged gift) answers a threat
  the text never lets the reader verify. The wrestling that follows is a genuine contest, but not with
  Esau
- [[The Reconciliation with Esau]] — Genesis 33:1–20; **the reconciliation the whole cycle from
  chapter 27 has been building toward, and it is resolved in four verses with no negotiation at all.**
  Esau runs, embraces, and weeps before Jacob's diplomacy can be deployed; neither brother mentions
  the birthright, the blessing, or the twenty years apart. **What follows is not total agreement** —
  Esau's two offers of company are both declined, and the brothers part for different destinations,
  one of which (Seir) Jacob had said he would come to and Genesis never shows him reaching. The
  chapter's peace is real and also partial: real enough to end the danger, partial enough that the
  two men do not end up living near each other

- Proverbs 15:1; 17:14; 20:3 — the Wisdom formulation
- Matthew 5:9; Romans 12:18; 1 Corinthians 6:7; Philippians 2:3–4 — the New Testament's

- [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]] — Genesis 29:1–30; **the one well in Genesis nobody fights over**, in
  the chapter that starts a rivalry lasting two generations. Abraham's wells were seized (21:25),
  Isaac's were stopped up and claimed twice over (26:15–22), and here the only dispute at the water
  is about *when* to draw it — settled by the newcomer simply doing it. **The quarrel this pericope
  actually begins is domestic**, and its terms are set in the last verse: *"he loved also Rachel more
  than Leah."* See [[Envy]]
- [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]] — Genesis 30:14–16, **the only conversation the two sisters have
  anywhere in the Bible, and it is an exchange of theft accusations.** *"Is it a small matter that you
  have taken away my husband? Would you take away my son's mandrakes, also?"* — from the woman who was
  substituted into her sister's wedding. **Both of them are right by their own accounting**, and
  Genesis adjudicates neither: [[Rachel]] was married a week late to a man already given to [[Leah]],
  and Leah has a husband whose preference she has never had. **The quarrel is settled by a trade** — a
  night for a plant — **which is the only conflict resolution anyone in the passage attempts**, and it
  produces two more children and no peace
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:36–54; **the fullest peacemaking procedure in Genesis,
  and it begins as a lawsuit.** ***Rîb*** (31:36) is the legal word for bringing a charge, and Jacob
  asks for witnesses to be empanelled — *"set it here before my relatives and your relatives, that
  they may judge between us two"* (31:37). **What ends the quarrel is not agreement.** Neither man
  concedes a point: Laban says *"the daughters are my daughters… and all that you see is mine"* and
  then asks *"what can I do today?"* **The settlement is a border, a heap of stones appointed as
  witness, and a meal** — and the peace it makes is permanent because it is a separation
- [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] — Genesis 34:8–26; **the theme's counter-case, and the wiki's
  clearest one.** Everything the successful settlements of Genesis contain is present here: a stated
  grievance, a negotiation, terms, a public ratification at the city gate, and a rite to seal it.
  **Against [[Abram and Lot Separate]] (13:8–9), [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] (21:22–34) and the heap
  at [[Galeed]] (31:44–54), where the settlement is honoured and the parties separate, this one is
  agreed in order to be broken.** The difference is not in the procedure but in the intent, which the
  narrator states before the procedure begins: *"with deceit"* (34:13). **Genesis 34 is what the
  peacemaking chapters look like when one party never meant it** — and the peace it destroys was
  offered on terms more generous than any patriarch had yet been given
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:4, 14; ***šālôm*** **used three times in a chapter that has
  none of it, and the arrangement is the cruellest thing in it.** The brothers *"couldn't speak peaceably to
  him"* — ***lə-šālōm*** (37:4) — and ten verses later their father sends the boy sixty-five miles to
  *"see whether it is well with your brothers, and well with the flock"* — ***šəlôm ʾaḥeykā*** (37:14).
  **The errand is, literally, to inspect the peace of men the narrator has just said cannot speak peace to
  him**, and Genesis does not point it out. **Both enquiries come back false**: the flock is fine, the
  brothers are not, and the word that comes back is brought by somebody else and is a lie
- [[Bildad's Third Speech]] — Job 25:2; ***ʿōśeh šālôm bimrômāyw***, *"He makes peace in his high
  places"* — **peace as a settlement in heaven**, implying a conflict above (Rahab's helpers, 9:13; the
  sea under guard, 7:12). The line became the closing petition of the Jewish Kaddish and Amidah, which
  add what Bildad does not: that the God who makes peace above make peace *for us*.
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:13–14; **two Hebrews fighting, and a peacemaker refused.** Moses asks the one
  in the wrong *"Why do you strike your fellow?"* and is told he has no right to ask; Stephen renders
  it as Moses *"urged them to be at peace again"* (Acts 7:26). **The first quarrel inside Israel in
  Exodus is a Hebrew striking a Hebrew while Egypt strikes them all**
- [[Water from the Rock]] — Exodus 17:2, 7; **the quarrel over water, inside Israel.** *"The people quarreled
  with Moses"* — *rîb*, the verb of Isaac's wells (Genesis 26:20) — and the place named *Meribah*, *quarreling*.
  **Moses does not answer the quarrel; he turns it into a question about God** — *"Why do you test Yahweh?"*
- [[Jethro's Counsel]] — Exodus 18:16, 23; ***"I judge between a man and his neighbor"***, and a system
  under which *"all these people also will go to their place in peace"*. The disputes are what keep the people
  standing *"from the morning to the evening"*; **the first peacemaking institution in Israel is proposed by the
  man who also gives Exodus all its *šālôm*** (4:18; 18:7, 23; WLC)

- [[Laws of Violence and Injury]] — Exodus 21:12–36; distinctions and remedies for injury and loss
- [[A Good Name]] — Proverbs 22:10; Drive out the mocker and quarrels stop.
- [[Thirty Excellent Things]] — Proverbs 22:24–25; The hot-tempered companion whose ways are learned.
- [[Do Not Envy Sinners]] — Proverbs 23:29; 24:2; Strife and needless bruises at the wine; hearts that plot violence.
- [[The Glory of Kings]] — Proverbs 25:8–10, 21–24; Haste to court refused, the case argued with the neighbour, the enemy fed.
- [[Answer a Fool]] — Proverbs 26:17, 20–21; The meddler holding a dog by the ears; the fire that goes out without wood; the contentious man.
- [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]] — Proverbs 27:3–4, 15–16; The fool's provocation, jealousy, and the contentious wife.
