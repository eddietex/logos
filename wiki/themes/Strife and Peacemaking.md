# Strife and Peacemaking

## Definition / overview

Genesis is largely a book about relatives who cannot get along. What it mostly narrates is the
quarrel; what it rarely narrates is anyone stopping one. This theme tracks both — the strife, and the
much smaller set of occasions on which somebody defuses it, and on what terms.

The Hebrew noun in the founding text is *rîb* (Genesis 13:7–8), a word with a legal edge: not a mood
but a **dispute**, the kind that has parties and a cause and could go to a judge. The prophets use it
for God's lawsuit against Israel (Hosea 4:1; Micah 6:2). What Abram proposes at 13:8 is that this one
not be filed.

## Development across Scripture

**Strife first, and it is lethal.** The Bible's first quarrel between brothers ends in a field with a
body in it — *"Cain rose up against Abel, his brother, and killed him"* (4:8), in a passage that uses
the word *brother* seven times. Lamech escalates it into a boast (4:23–24). The corruption that
brings the flood is summarized as *ḥāmās*, **violence** (6:11). Babel's unity is broken by God
himself as a mercy (11:1–9). Nothing in Genesis 1–12 shows a human being resolving a conflict.

**Then Abram, at 13:8–9.** Herdsmen fight over water and grazing; the two households are too heavy
for the land; and the senior man in the story does four things in two verses that the Bible will keep
coming back to. He names the relationship — *"for we are relatives"*, literally *we are men,
brothers*, reaching for the word that failed in chapter 4. He takes the dispute on himself rather
than leaving it with the staff — *"between you and me"*. He proposes the standard equitable division,
one party splits and the other picks. And then he hands his junior both halves of it, giving away a
choice that was legally his twice over, as elder and as the man to whom the land had been promised
(12:7). The narrative rewards him immediately and without saying that it is a reward: the promise is
enlarged four verses later (13:14–17). See [[Abram and Lot Separate]].

**Isaac repeats it, deliberately.** The herdsmen of Gerar quarrel with his over wells; he yields the
first (*Esek*, *contention*), yields the second (*Sitnah*, *enmity*), and digs a third unopposed —
*"He called it Rehoboth. He said, 'For now Yahweh has made room for us, and we will be fruitful in
the land.'"* (26:20–22). Same dispute as his father's, same water, same solution: concede the
ground and let God supply the room.

**Jacob mostly fails at it and then manages it once.** The strife with Esau is bought (25:29–34) and
stolen (27), and resolved twenty years later by a gift, a bow, and a man expecting to be killed
(32–33). With Laban it ends in a heap of stones and a mutual boundary neither will cross (31:44–54).
With Joseph's brothers, strife is the whole engine of chapters 37–50, and its resolution is the
book's last word: *"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring to
pass, as it is today, to save many people alive"* (50:20).

**The Law and the Wisdom books turn it into instruction.** Leviticus 19:17–18 forbids hatred in the
heart and grudge-bearing and commands neighbour-love; Proverbs returns to it constantly — *"The
beginning of strife is like breaching a dam, therefore stop contention before quarreling breaks
out"* (17:14), *"It is an honor for a man to keep aloof from strife"* (20:3), *"A soft answer turns
away wrath"* (15:1).

**The New Testament makes peacemaking a family likeness.** *"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they
shall be called children of God"* (Matthew 5:9); *"If it is possible, as much as it is up to you, be
at peace with all men"* (Romans 12:18); *"Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be defrauded?"*
(1 Corinthians 6:7), which is Abram's argument at 13:9 in one line; and Philippians 2:3–4, which is
its motive. James 4:1–2 traces quarrels to wanting, and Hebrews 12:14 makes pursuing peace a
condition of seeing the Lord.

**What the theme does not say** is that yielding always works or is always right. Abram concedes to
Lot and then arms three hundred eighteen men to get him back (14:14). The prophets prosecute God's
*rîb* rather than dropping it. Genesis presents peacemaking as costly, occasional, and unrewarded on
the page — and then keeps arranging its narratives so that the man who gave ground ends up holding
the land.

## Key passages

- [[Cain and Abel]] — Genesis 4:1–16; the quarrel nobody stops
- [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] — Genesis 4:23–24; Lamech's escalation
- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — Genesis 13:5–12; the first quarrel anybody stops, and its terms
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — Genesis 14:14–16, the counterpart: one chapter after
  avoiding a fight by giving away what was his, he starts one to recover what is not, for the same
  nephew. The wiki reads the two chapters as a single argument about what Abram will spend himself on
- [[Hagar and Ishmael]] — Genesis 16:5–6, the third quarrel in the household and the one that shows
  what the founding case at 13:8–9 was actually made of. The form is identical: Abram is presented
  with a dispute and responds by conceding rather than adjudicating — *"Behold, your maid is in your
  hand. Do to her whatever is good in your eyes."* But at 13:9 what he gave away was his own claim to
  a country, and here what he gives away is a pregnant woman who is not consulted. Same generosity,
  opposite cost, and the peace it buys is bought with someone else's money
- Genesis 26:17–22 — Isaac's wells, conceded twice *(not yet ingested)*
- Genesis 31:44–54; 32–33 — Laban's heap, and Esau's embrace *(not yet ingested)*
- Genesis 50:15–21 — Joseph and his brothers *(not yet ingested)*
- Proverbs 15:1; 17:14; 20:3 — the Wisdom formulation
- Matthew 5:9; Romans 12:18; 1 Corinthians 6:7; Philippians 2:3–4 — the New Testament's
