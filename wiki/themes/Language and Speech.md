# Language and Speech

## Definition / overview

Words as the medium of everything in the Bible — creation, command, blessing, curse, covenant,
prophecy, prayer — and the one occasion on which God intervenes in the mechanism itself.

**The Bible begins with speech that works.** God says *"Let there be light"* and there is light, ten
times over in [[The Creation of the World]]. The first thing the man does is name the animals
(2:19–20), which is speech doing something. The serpent's opening move is a question about what God
said (3:1). Every hinge of the primeval history is a sentence: a warning, an accusation, a curse, a
blessing, a covenant, a proverb. Genesis 1–10 is a book in which language is transparent and
effective, and nobody in it has any trouble being understood.

**Genesis 11 breaks it.** *"The whole earth was of one language and of one speech"* — *śāpâ ʾeḥāt
ûdəbārîm ʾăḥādîm*, one lip and one set of words — and God's remedy for a project he means to stop is
not to demolish it but to make the builders unintelligible to each other. *"Come, let's go down, and
there confuse their language, that they may not understand one another's speech."* Nothing is
destroyed. The tool is taken out of their hands by being scrambled, and the work halts on its own.
See [[The Tower of Babel]].

**Two things about that are worth holding.**

**1. Language is treated as infrastructure.** God's stated reason at 11:6 is that *"they are one
people, and they have all one language, and this is what they begin to do. Now nothing will be
withheld from them, which they intend to do."* Shared speech is what makes unlimited collective
action possible. The passage is the Bible's clearest statement that what human beings can do
together is a function of whether they can talk, and it is made by a God who removes the capacity
rather than the works.

**2. Multiplicity is not undone anywhere in the canon.** No later text restores the one language.
Zephaniah 3:9 promises a *purified lip* so that the peoples call on God's name together — which is a
change of content, not of number. At Pentecost each hearer hears *"in our own language in which we
were born"*, which keeps every language and makes them transparent; nobody reverts to Hebrew. And the
last book has *"every tribe, language, people, and nation"* standing before the throne (Revelation
7:9), with the plurality intact and worshipping. The Bible's remedy for Babel is translation, not
reunification. See [[The Table of Nations at Pentecost]].

**The other half of the theme is speech as moral act.** Alongside language-as-medium runs a sustained
biblical interest in what people do with their mouths: the first human words are a poem (2:23), the
first recorded human curse and blessing are Noah's (9:25–27), the first quoted proverb is about
Nimrod (10:9), and the first quoted collective speech is *"Come, let's build ourselves a city"*
(11:4). Genesis notices, every time, that a thing was said. The wisdom books and James turn that
noticing into doctrine.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 1:3–29** — creation by speech: *"God said… and it was so"*, ten times.
- **Genesis 2:19–20; 2:23** — the man names the animals, and speaks his first recorded sentence, in
  verse. See [[The Garden of Eden]].
- **Genesis 3:1–5** — the serpent's question, which is a misquotation. See [[The Fall]].
- **Genesis 4:23–24** — the Song of the Sword: the first poem by a human being about himself. See
  [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]].
- **Genesis 9:25–27** — the first human curse and the first human blessing, with no divine
  ratification recorded. See [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].
- **Genesis 10:5, 20, 31** — languages as one of the four axes the nations are sorted by. See
  [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — one lip, and then many; the only time God alters the medium itself.
- **Exodus 4:10–16** — Moses *"slow of speech and of a slow tongue"*, and a brother appointed to be
  his mouth.
- **Numbers 22–24** — Balaam, hired to curse, who can say only what is put in his mouth.
- **1 Kings 19:12** — the still small voice, after wind, earthquake, and fire.
- **Psalm 19:1–4** — speech without words: *"There is no speech nor language where their voice is not
  heard."*
- **Psalm 137:1–4** — singing Yahweh's song in a foreign land, in Babylon, where the language is not
  yours.
- **Proverbs 10:19; 12:18; 18:21** — death and life in the power of the tongue.
- **Isaiah 6:5–7** — *"I am a man of unclean lips"*, and the coal.
- **Isaiah 28:11; 1 Corinthians 14:21** — God speaking to his people *"with stammering lips and
  another language"*: foreign speech as judgment, which is Babel used as a threat.
- **Jeremiah 1:9** — *"I have put my words in your mouth."*
- **Ezekiel 3:5–6** — a prophet sent to his own people rather than to *"a people of a strange speech
  and of a hard language"*, who would have listened.
- **Daniel 1:4; 2:4** — the exiles taught the language of the Chaldeans, and a book that changes
  language mid-sentence.
- **Zephaniah 3:9** — a purified lip given to the peoples, so that they call on Yahweh's name and
  serve him together: the one prophetic text that answers 11:7 in its own vocabulary.
- **Acts 2:4–11** — the languages of the nations spoken and understood at once. See
  [[The Table of Nations at Pentecost]].
- **1 Corinthians 12–14** — tongues, and the argument that intelligibility is the point.
- **James 3:1–12** — the tongue as a fire, a world of iniquity, and the member no one can tame; and
  the observation that blessing and cursing come out of the same mouth, which is Genesis 9:25–27's
  problem stated as ethics.
- **Revelation 5:9; 7:9; 14:6** — *"every tribe, language, people, and nation"*: the plurality of
  Genesis 11 kept, and worshipping.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:1, 7, 16, 19

God's speech establishes the commands (20:1); human speech is accountable both for the use of God's
name and for testimony against a neighbor (20:7, 16). The assembly asks for Moses' voice to carry
God's words because direct hearing terrifies it (20:19). Speech here is legislation, a possible
instrument of harm, and the means of mediation. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

**Exodus 22:28 regulates speech about authority**: *"You shall not blaspheme God, nor curse a
ruler of your people."* Paul quotes the second half before the high priest, in the Septuagint's
wording, *"You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people"* (Acts 23:5). See
[[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] and [[You Shall Not Speak Evil of a Ruler - Exodus 22.28 in Acts 23.5]].

### False reports and other gods — Exodus 23:1–19

False reports, malicious testimony, and testimony shaped by the crowd are prohibited (23:1–2, 7–8).
Speech can deprive another person of justice and even life. Verse 13 also forbids invoking other
gods. The same continuing address regulates what is said in a dispute and what is invoked in
worship.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### Written words — Exodus 24:4, 7, 12

**The covenant is made with a document.** Moses writes *"all Yahweh's words"* (24:4), reads *"the book of
the covenant"* aloud (24:7), and the people answer what they have heard. God then promises tablets he has
written himself (24:12). Speech, writing, and reading follow one another in a single chapter, and the
first writing in Scripture (17:14) now becomes the form the law takes. See [[The Blood of the Covenant]].

### The shining face — Exodus 34:29–35

Speech joins all sides of the scene: conversation with Yahweh accounts for the shining
face (34:29), Moses' call brings back the frightened leaders (34:31), and he communicates
the commands to all Israel (34:32, 34). The account does not transcribe another speech from
God here. Its emphasis is the transmission of what has been received, with the face visible
while the mediator speaks.

See [[The Shining Face of Moses]].

### My words against her words — Proverbs 2:1, 12, 16

The lecture opens *"if you will receive my words"* (2:1, *ʾămāray*) and says wisdom comes *"Out of his
mouth"* — God's (2:6). The two dangers it rescues from are both speech: *"the men who speak perverse
things"* (2:12, *tahpuḵôṯ*, from *h-p-k*, *to overturn*) and the woman *"who flatters with her words"*
(2:16, *ʾămāreyhā heḥĕlîqâ*, her words made smooth). **The father's words are set against hers, using
the same noun.** See [[If You Seek Her as Silver]]. Source: Proverbs 2 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The postponing answer and the mocker — Proverbs 3:21–35

The refusal in Proverbs 3:28 is spoken as a postponement, although the means to help
are already present. Speech can evade an obligation without openly denying it. At 3:34,
mockery receives a divine answer in kind, while humility receives grace. The passage
joins what is said to a neighbor with how the speaker stands before Yahweh.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### A father quotes his father — Proverbs 4:1–9

The first-person teacher becomes a quoted hearer when he recalls his father's words
(4:3–4). In the stored WEB the quotation continues through 4:9. Speech carries instruction
across generations; the heart must retain what the mouth has given. The unit displays a
chain of teaching without identifying the unnamed speakers as particular kings. See
[[Wisdom Handed On - Proverbs 4 and Exodus 13]].

See [[Get Wisdom]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Heart before mouth — Proverbs 4:23–24

Directly after *"Keep your heart … for out of it is the wellspring of life"* (4:23) comes *"Put
away from yourself a perverse mouth. Put corrupt lips far from you"* (4:24). Speech is the heart's
first outflow, as the Gospels later say in one sentence: *"out of the abundance of the heart, his
mouth speaks"* (Luke 6:45, WEB, verified at bible-api.com). See
[[Out of the Heart - Proverbs 4.23, Matthew 15, and Luke 6]].

See [[Keep Your Heart]]. Source: Proverbs checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Lips that keep knowledge, lips that drip honey — Proverbs 5:2–3

The fifth chapter sets the son's lips against hers: *"that your lips may preserve knowledge. For
the lips of an adulteress drip honey. Her mouth is smoother than oil"* (5:2–3). Her speech is
pleasant, and the danger is its end, *"bitter as wormwood"* (5:4). As at 2:16, the strange
woman's power is a power of words. See [[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 5; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### Trapped by your own mouth — Proverbs 6:1–19

The pledge of 6:1 is a speech act, and the trap is verbal: *"You are trapped by the words of your
mouth. You are ensnared with the words of your mouth"* (6:2). The worthless man walks *"with a
perverse mouth"* (6:12, *ʿiqqəšûṯ peh*, the phrase of 4:24) and talks beside his words, with eyes,
feet, and fingers (6:13). Of the seven things Yahweh hates, two are speech — *"a lying tongue"*
and *"a false witness who utters lies"* (6:17, 19). See [[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]].
Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The teaching that talks — Proverbs 6:22–24

The father's words, once kept, speak back: *"When you awake, it will talk with you"* (6:22,
*ṯəśîḥeḵā*). Deuteronomy 6:7 had Israel talk of the commandments; here the commandment talks to the
son. What it guards him from is again speech — *"the flattery of the wayward wife's tongue"* (6:24,
*ḥelqaṯ lāšôn*, *smoothness of tongue*). See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]]. Source: stored WEB
Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

Proverbs 7 is the strange woman's longest speech (7:14–20), and the narrator's verdict on it is
about speech: *"With persuasive words, she led him astray. With the flattering of her lips, she
seduced him"* (7:21). *Persuasive words* is *rōḇ liqḥāh*, *her much learning* — *leqaḥ*, the book's
word for what the wise receive (1:5; 4:2). Her opening words are religious (7:14) and her
invitation is in the Song of Solomon's vocabulary of love (7:18, *ḏōḏîm*). See [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 7; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Nothing crooked in them — Proverbs 8:6–9

Wisdom describes her own speech: *"excellent things"*, *"right things"*, *"truth"*; *"Wickedness is an
abomination to my lips"*; *"There is nothing crooked or perverse in them"* (8:6–8). The two adjectives,
*nip̄tāl wəʿiqqēš*, are Deuteronomy 32:5's *"perverse and crooked generation"*. Her words are *"plain
to him who understands"* (8:9, *nəḵōḥîm*) — not riddles. The portrait answers the smooth words of the
strange woman (7:21) and the *"men who speak perverse things"* (2:12), and 8:13 adds *"the perverse
mouth"* to what she hates. See [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:6, 8, 10–14, 18–21, 31–32

The mouth can be a spring of life, feed many, and produce wisdom; it can also hide hatred, slander,
and bring ruin. Restraint (19) and nourishment (21) belong together: wise speech is measured by its
truth and benefit, not merely by silence. Verse 18 prevents confusing love’s covering of wrongs (12)
with lying.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:9, 11–14

A mouth can destroy a neighbor and overthrow a city. Understanding sometimes speaks through counsel
and sometimes keeps silence; trustworthiness protects a confidence rather than circulating gossip.
Restraint is thus directed toward the neighbor’s good, not treated as an absolute ban on speech.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:6, 13–14, 17–19, 22–23, 25

Words can ambush, trap, rescue, nourish, wound, heal, and relieve anxiety. Truthful testimony and
prudent restraint belong together; silence is not always wisdom, since another person may need a
kind word. Job 16:2–5 exposes speech offered as comfort that instead wounds; see
[[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### The guarded mouth — Proverbs 13:2–3, 5, 17

The fruit of the lips feeds a man (2); *"He who guards his mouth guards his soul"* (3); the righteous *hates lies* (5); and a trustworthy envoy *"gains healing"* where a wicked messenger falls into trouble (17). Speech is treated as something that protects or endangers the speaker himself.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### Witnesses and empty talk — Proverbs 14:3, 5, 7, 23, 25

The fool's talk brings a rod (3); the truthful witness will not lie (5) and *"saves souls"* (25); no knowledge is found on a fool's lips (7); and *"the talk of the lips leads only to poverty"* where work profits (23). See [[Truthful Witness and Lying Lips - Proverbs 12 and Exodus 20-23]].

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### The gentle answer and the harsh word — Proverbs 15:1–2, 4, 7, 14, 23, 26, 28

*"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger"* (1) opens a chapter that is mostly about speech: the gentle tongue as *"a tree of life"* (4), the fool's mouth that *gushes* and *feeds on* folly (2, 14), the righteous heart that *"weighs answers"* (28). Verse 23 adds timing to manner — *"How good is a word at the right time!"* Job's friends claimed the quality for themselves (Job 15:11), and Job named the result *"miserable comforters"* (16:2); see [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### The answer of the tongue — Proverbs 16:1, 21–24, 27–30

The tongue's answer is from Yahweh (1), yet *"The heart of the wise instructs his mouth"* (23); pleasantness of lips *"promotes instruction"* (21), and *"Pleasant words are a honeycomb"* (24). The worthless man's speech is *"like a scorching fire"* (27), the whisperer *"separates close friends"* (28), and the compressed lips plot evil (30). Kings delight in righteous lips (13).

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Spare words, closed lips — Proverbs 17:4, 7, 9, 20, 27–28

The hearer of lies (4), lying lips unfit for a prince (7), the repeated matter that separates friends (9), the deceitful tongue (20), and the close: *"He who spares his words has knowledge"* (27), and *"Even a fool, when he keeps silent, is counted wise"* (28), Job 13:5 made general. See [[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### Death and life in the tongue — Proverbs 18:2, 4, 6–8, 13, 17, 20–21

The chapter's main subject. The fool wants only *"revealing his own opinion"* (2); a man's words are
*"deep waters"* (4); the fool's mouth is his destruction (6–7); the gossip's words are *"dainty
morsels"* that go down to the innermost parts (8, repeated at 26:22). *"He who gives answer before he
hears"* earns shame (13), and the first speaker in a case *"seems right"* until questioned (17). The
mouth's fruit fills the stomach (20), and *"Death and life are in the power of the tongue"* (21). See
[[He Who Answers Before He Hears - Proverbs 18.13, 17 and Elihu's Waiting]].

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:1, 5, 9, 20, 22, 27–28

Verses 5 and 9 repeat the WEB sentence about a false witness not escaping punishment, then vary the
liar's outcome: no escape (5), perishing (9). The corrupt witness mocks justice (28). Listening to
counsel (20) and continuing in instruction (27) provide the counter-practice. See
[[A False Witness Shall Not Be Unpunished - Proverbs 19 and Exodus 23]] .

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:5–6, 9, 14–15, 18–20, 22, 25

Speech can draw out counsel (5), claim loyalty or purity without establishing either (6, 9),
disparage a purchase before boasting (14), or carry knowledge more precious than jewels (15).
The tale-bearer reveals secrets (19); curses against parents (20), pledges of revenge (22), and
rash dedications (25) expose speech's power to harm and bind. Discretion concerns both what one
says and whose company one keeps.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Guarding the mouth — Proverbs 21:6, 23, 28

Treasure by a lying tongue (6), the guarded mouth that keeps the soul from troubles (23), and the
false witness set against the man who listens and *"speaks to eternity"* (28).

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### Speech that wins and speech that traps — Proverbs 22:11–12, 14

Gracious speech makes the king's friend (22:11); Yahweh frustrates the words of the unfaithful
(12); and *"The mouth of an adulteress is a deep pit"* (14). The same organ is the means of favor
and the trap.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### Words ready on the lips — Proverbs 22:18, 21; 23:8–9

The preface to *"the words of the wise"* wants its teaching *"ready on your lips"* (22:18) and aims at
*"truth, reliable words, to give sound answers to the ones who sent you"* (22:21) — the pupil as a
messenger whose report must be accurate. Two later verses name speech wasted: the guest at a stingy
host's table will *"lose your good words"* (23:8), the compliments paid for a meal not meant; and
*"Don't speak in the ears of a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your words"* (23:9).

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11.

### Right lips and mischievous lips — Proverbs 23:16; 24:2

The block opens with lips that gladden — *"when your lips speak what is right"* (23:16) — and closes
with lips that *"talk about mischief"* (24:2). In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read
2026-10-26) the *right* of 23:16 is *mêšārîm*, the same word the WEB renders *"smoothly"* of the wine
at 23:31. The drunkard is given the last speech in the block (23:35), and his words convict him.

See [[Do Not Envy Sinners]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 23:12–24:2.

### The honest answer and the needless witness — Proverbs 24:26, 28

*"An honest answer is like a kiss on the lips"* (24:26) — in Hebrew without *like*: *lips he kisses,
who returns right words*. Two verses later the opposite: *"Don't be a witness against your neighbor
without cause. Don't deceive with your lips"* (24:28). Straight speech is a gesture of loyalty; false
testimony is the instrument of revenge (24:29).

See [[The Field of the Sluggard]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:23–34.

### Words in their place — Proverbs 25:9–15, 18–20, 23

Chapter 25 holds one of the book's densest runs on speech, nearly all of it in comparisons: *"A word
fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver"* (11); a wise reprover is an ornament of
gold *"to an obedient ear"* — Hebrew *ʾōzen šōmaʿaṯ*, 20:12's *hearing ear* (12); a faithful
messenger is like snow's cold at harvest (13); a boaster of false gifts, like rainless clouds (14);
*"A soft tongue breaks the bone"* (15). Speech also wounds: the betrayed confidence (9–10), the false
witness *"like a club, a sword, or a sharp arrow"* (18), songs sung to a heavy heart (20), and the
backbiting tongue — literally *a tongue of secrecy* — that brings an angry face (23).

See [[The Glory of Kings]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25.

### The proverb in a fool's mouth, and the disguised tongue — Proverbs 26:4–9, 18–28

Chapter 26 reflects on its own medium. *"A parable in the mouth of fools"* — *māšāl*, the book's word
for itself — is a lame leg (7) or a thorn in a drunkard's hand (9); a true saying needs a wise speaker,
and the paired commands of 26:4–5 show that even *answer* and *don't answer* need judgment to apply.
The second half of the chapter is about the mouth that does harm: the deceiver who says *"Am I not
joking?"* (18–19); the whisperer, without whom *"a quarrel dies down"* (20), and whose words are
*"dainty morsels"* (22, identical in Hebrew to 18:8); burning lips over an evil heart, like silver
dross glazed on clay (23); the hater who *"disguises himself with his lips"* (24) and whose speech is
*"charming"* (25); and the end of it all, *"A lying tongue hates those it hurts; and a flattering mouth
works ruin"* (28).

See [[Answer a Fool]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 26.

### Praise, rebuke, and the loud blessing — Proverbs 27:2, 5, 11, 14

Chapter 27's speech sayings are about whose mouth and when. Praise belongs in another's mouth, *"not
your own"* (2). Rebuke should be open rather than love hidden (5). A wise son lets his father *"answer
my tormentor"* — literally *return a word* (11). And the right words at the wrong time turn into their
opposite: *"He who blesses his neighbor with a loud voice early in the morning, it will be taken as a
curse by him"* (14), as the song to a heavy heart was vinegar on soda (25:20).

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25:20; 27; Hebrew from Sefaria.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:13, 23–24

Confession is joined to renunciation, rebuke to eventual favor, and flattery contrasted with
correction. The thief’s claim that robbing parents is not wrong shows speech used to evade
responsibility (24). Verse 23 develops the costly rebuke commended in 27:5–6; its benefit may become
apparent afterward rather than in the first response.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

Flattery traps, lies corrupt government through a willing listener, and haste in speaking is worse
than folly. Verse 24 adds culpable silence: the thief’s accomplice takes an oath but will not
testify. Speech ethics includes hearing and answering as well as truthful assertion.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:5, 9, 12, 19–20, 24; the passage page records
the cross-reference sources and interpretive limits.

### Agur’s words — Proverbs 30:5–6, 8–11

God’s flawless word is set against human additions, falsehood, slander, and a child’s cursing.
Speech can misrepresent God or expose a vulnerable servant to harm. The remedy joins restraint in
teaching with a prayer for truthfulness.

See [[The Words of Agur]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:1–14 and the comparisons documented there.

### The hand over the mouth — Proverbs 30:32–33

The last speech-saying in Agur's words is a command to stop speaking. The one who has exalted
himself or thought evil should put a hand over the mouth (32), because pressing anger, like pressing
a nose, brings out what it presses for (33). Silence is not presented as weakness but as the way
to prevent strife.

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

### Lemuel’s mother — Proverbs 31:1–2, 8–9

Agur’s final counsel was to put a hand over the mouth to prevent strife (30:32–33); Lemuel’s
mother twice commands the opening of the mouth for the vulnerable (31:8–9). These are contrasting
uses of speech, not competing universal rules. Wisdom restrains self-exalting provocation and
requires advocacy when silence would abandon another person’s cause.

See [[The Words of King Lemuel]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9 and the comparisons documented
there.

### The worthy woman — Proverbs 31:26, 28–31

The woman speaks wisdom and faithful instruction; her children and husband answer with
blessing and praise. The poem ends by calling for recognition at the gates, where her works
figuratively praise her. This is deserved commendation rather than flattery or self-praise;
Proverbs 27:2 similarly asks that praise come from another mouth.

See [[The Worthy Woman]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:10–31 and the comparisons documented there.

### The opening of Ecclesiastes — 1:1–11

Ecclesiastes 1:8 describes weariness beyond uttering and an ear that is not filled with
hearing. The poem's own words articulate a limit to what speech and reception can complete.
This is neither a command to silence nor an indictment of a named liar; it joins speech to
the repeated, unfinished activity surrounding the observer. The WEB wording is the basis of
this reading, without a new claim about an alternate Hebrew translation.

See [[Vanity of Vanities]]. Sources: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and the comparison texts
verified on that passage page.

### A time for every purpose — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

Verse 7 places keeping silence beside speaking. Neither is the universally right response;
the verse gives no timetable for selecting between them. Proverbs 15:23 commends a word at
the right time and 25:11 a fitting word. Read together they locate the good of speech partly
in its occasion. See [[A Time to Keep Silence - Proverbs 15.23, 25.11, and Ecclesiastes 3.7]].

See [[A Time for Every Purpose]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

### Few words before God — Ecclesiastes 5:1–7

The Preacher's first direct instructions are about speech in God's house. The mouth is not
to be rash nor the heart hasty, *"for God is in heaven, and you on earth. Therefore let your words
be few"* (2). A fool's speech is known by its multitude of words, as a dream comes from many
cares (3), and the mouth can lead the flesh into sin through a vow it then calls a mistake (6).
Many words are judged vanities (7). See
[[In the Multitude of Words - Proverbs 10.19, 17.27-28, and Ecclesiastes 5.2-7]].

See [[Let Your Words Be Few]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:1–7; the passage page records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### Who knows what is good — Ecclesiastes 6:10–12

Verse 11's many words create vanity in the stored WEB, and the speaker asks what
they profit humanity. This recalls 5:2–7 without repeating its worship setting or vow
instructions. More words do not establish mastery of good or of what comes next.
It does not follow that all complaint or teaching is forbidden; Job 9–10 recognizes
the unequal contest yet continues to address God. See
[[In the Multitude of Words - Proverbs 10.19, 17.27-28, and Ecclesiastes 5.2-7]].

See [[Who Knows What Is Good]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:10–12; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

### The servant's curse — Ecclesiastes 7:21–22

*"Also don’t take heed to all words that are spoken, lest you hear your servant curse you;
for often your own heart knows that you yourself have likewise cursed others"* (7:21–22).
The counsel concerns hearing, not speaking: not every word deserves attention, and one's own
speech is the measure for forbearance. Proverbs 30:10 imagines a servant's curse from the
other side, as the consequence of slandering him to his master.

See [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29; comparison sources and Hebrew checks identified on the passage page.

### Who can challenge the king — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

The king's word carries force, and the question about challenging his actions (4)
expresses the inequality of power. It does not establish the justice of everything he
says. Job 9:12 uses the same short English question about God, while Proverbs 25:15 allows
patient persuasion of a human ruler. A difficult challenge and impossible speech are
not identical claims. See [[Contending with the Mightier - Job 9 and Ecclesiastes 6.10]].

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

The poor man's *"words are not heard"* (9:16); *"The words of the wise heard in quiet are
better than the cry of him who rules among fools"* (17); the fool on the road says to everyone that
he is a fool (10:3); gentleness before an angry ruler lays offenses to rest (10:4). Speech is
weighed by its quiet and its effect, not its volume. See
[[Gentleness Before the Ruler - Proverbs 15.1, 25.15, and Ecclesiastes 10.4]].

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The fool's many words — Ecclesiastes 10:5–20

The wise man's words are gracious; the fool is swallowed by his own lips (10:12), as in
Proverbs 18:7. His talk runs from foolishness to mischievous madness, and he multiplies words,
though *"Man doesn’t know what will be"* (13–14). The charmer's tongue profits nothing when the
snake bites first (11). Speech also travels: *"a bird of the sky may carry your voice"* (20). See
[[In the Multitude of Words - Proverbs 10.19, 17.27-28, and Ecclesiastes 5.2-7]].

See [[Folly in High Places and Everyday Work]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 10:5–20; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Acceptable and truthful words — Ecclesiastes 12:9–14

The teacher seeks acceptable words and truthful writing (10). The images of goads
and securely fastened nails characterize wise words by their effect (11), while
endless books and much study receive a warning (12). The earlier warnings about
many words (5:2–7) are not a blanket rejection of communication: selected, ordered
teaching is explicitly praised. See [[Words of Truth and the Work of the Teacher]].

See [[The End of the Matter]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 12:9–14 and the
comparison passages identified on that page.

### The final summons — Song of Solomon 8:13–14

In [[Make Haste, My Beloved]], Hearing the beloved is itself desired: the garden-dweller is asked for a voice
with friends present (8:13), and an invitation follows (8:14). The probable exchange
between man and woman makes speech an expression of intimacy, not only instruction
or argument. The WEB supplies no speaker labels.
Source: stored WEB Song 8:13–14.

### Confession and means — Leviticus 5:1–13

Speech can fail by omission of testimony or by rash oath; the corrective response itself includes specific confession (1, 4–5).

See [[Confession and Offerings According to Means]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:1–13.

- [[The Second Test]] — **one passage, three separate things done with words.** *Bārak* used twice
  more to mean its opposite (2:5, 2:9), so that the prologue's most loaded verb has now inverted
  three times. Job's rebuke built to convict an *utterance* rather than a person —
  *kədabbēr ʾaḥat hannəbālôt*, *you speak as one of the foolish women would speak*. And the
  narrator's acquittal narrowed to a body part: *"Job didn't sin **with his lips**"* (2:10), which
  says nothing about the man. **His wife's nine words, meanwhile, are made entirely of other
  people's sentences** — God's *maintains his integrity* from 2:3, and the accuser's *bārak* from
  1:11 and 2:5 — quoted accurately by someone who cannot have heard either
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:11–13; **the theme's one great silence, and the structural claim
  the book makes with it.** Three men sit with Job for a week and *"no one spoke a word to him"*, and
  it is the only conduct of theirs God never rebukes. **The disaster in Job is not that the friends
  came; it is that they eventually spoke** — Job says so himself at 13:5, *"Oh that you would be
  completely silent! Then you would be wise"*, and again at 21:34, *"how can you comfort me with
  nonsense?"* **The friends' verbs of arrival are undone by their mouths**, and the whole forty
  chapters of poetry begin because the silence breaks from the inside: *"After this Job opened his
  mouth"* (3:1). Set beside Proverbs 17:28
- [[The Stolen Blessing]] — Genesis 27:1–45, **the performative word at full strength.** A whole
  chapter of people talking while one of them cannot see: a flat lie about identity told twice, a
  quotation with two words added that the speaker never said (*"before Yahweh"*, 27:7 against 27:4),
  God's name used as a cover story, an idiom of obedience — *šəmaʿ bəqōlî*, *obey my voice* — used
  three times to direct a fraud, and a name re-derived as an accusation (*"Isn't he rightly named
  Jacob? For he has supplanted me"*, 27:36). **And then the chapter's point: what was said cannot be
  unsaid.** Isaac reconstructs the whole deception aloud and concludes *"Yes, he will be blessed"* —
  **the speaker's intention does not travel with the word he has released**, which is the same logic
  that binds Balaam (Numbers 23:20), Joshua's leaders (Joshua 9:19), and Jephthah (Judges 11:35). See
  [[Yes, He Will Be Blessed - The Irrevocable Word]]
- [[The Tower of Babel]] — Genesis 11:1–9: one lip, confused; the only divine intervention in
  language itself
- [[The Table of Nations]] — Genesis 10:5, 20, 31: language as one of the four axes the seventy are
  sorted by
- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — Genesis 9:25–27: the first curse and blessing spoken by a
  human being
- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17, four namings in one chapter and all of
  them God's. *Abram* becomes *Abraham* with a gloss that is a pun and not a derivation
  (*ʾab-hămôn gôyim*, father of a multitude of nations); *Sarai* becomes *Sarah* with **no explanation
  offered at all**, the only renaming in Genesis that comes without one, and between two forms that
  mean the same thing; and a child who does not exist is named *Isaac*, *he laughs*, for what his
  father has just done. Elsewhere in Genesis parents name children and people name places; here the
  naming runs the other way, and one chapter earlier a slave had named God (16:13)
- [[The Creation of the World]] — Genesis 1: the world made by speech
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:47; **the only non-Hebrew utterance in the Torah.**
  Laban names the heap ***Jegar Sahadutha*** in Aramaic and Jacob names it ***Galeed*** in Hebrew,
  and the two phrases mean the same thing — *heap of witness*. **Genesis translates neither**, and
  lets the reader hear that four generations after Terah's household split at Haran the two halves
  need an interpreter. **It is Babel's consequence, arriving inside one family**, and the book makes
  the point by adding no comment at all. See
  [[Jegar Sahadutha - The Only Aramaic in the Torah]]
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:2, 19, 32; **three pieces of speech, and each one does
  something the plain sense does not.** ***Dibbâ rāʿâ***, the *evil report*: **a word that means *whispering*
  or *defamation*** — what the spies bring back at Numbers 14:36–37 — **and the Hebrew will not say whether
  Joseph's report was slander or a true account of evil things.** ***Baʿal ha-ḥălōmôt***, *master of the
  dreams* (37:19): **a phrase found nowhere else in the Bible**, and from that verse **nobody in the family
  says his name again.** And ***hakker-nāʾ***, *"examine it, now"* (37:32): **a question that does the work
  of a lie**, since the sentence the brothers wanted said is then said by the man they said it to
- [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:15, 21–22, 25; **a chapter in which the choice of word is the
  evidence.** At 38:15 [[Judah]] takes her for a ***zônâ***, an ordinary prostitute; **when
  [[Hirah the Adullamite]] is sent back with the goat he goes looking for a *qədēšâ*** — a *consecrated
  woman*, from *qādaš*, *to be holy* — **the errand dignified in the telling by the man sent to run
  it.** (Whether *qədēšâ* implies sacred prostitution at all is disputed; Deuteronomy 23:17–18 forbids
  the office under both forms, and the wiki does not settle what it was.) **The townsmen's answer is
  literally true twice over**: *"there has been no prostitute here"* — there had been a widow in a
  veil. **And the chapter's hinge is a question rather than a statement**: *hakker-nāʾ*, *"discern,
  please, whose are these"*, which is the phrase Judah's own brothers used on their father one chapter
  earlier to install a conclusion they never had to assert. **Used once to make a lie unnecessary, and
  once to make a confession unavoidable.** See
  [[Discern, Please - Hakker-na from Jacob's Coat to Judah's Pledge]]
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:13, 19, 20; **a chapter built on a pun that decides who
  lives.** ***Nāśāʾ rōʾš***, *lift up the head*, three times in eight verses in three senses —
  reinstatement, decapitation, and the neutral census idiom of *taking the sum* — **and the second is
  the first with *mēʿāleykā*, *from off you*, added.** The chapter also holds **two words found nowhere
  else in the Hebrew Bible**, *pātar* and *pitrôn*, and **one phrase found nowhere else at all**,
  *"the land of the Hebrews"* (40:15). See [[Lift Up Your Head - One Idiom and Two Verdicts]]
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — **a chapter built on words nobody can construe, and Genesis lets them
  stand.** ***ʾAbrēk*** (41:43) is **a hapax legomenon** — Hebrew *bārak*, *kneel*; Egyptian *ib r.k*,
  *attention*; or Akkadian *abarakku*, *steward* — **and the WEB's *"Bow the knee!"* is a guess.**
  ***Yiššaq*** (41:40) is a crux: the root ordinarily means ***to kiss***, and *"according to your word
  will all my people be ruled"* is one of several defensible renderings of a verb translators have been
  disagreeing about for two thousand years. ***Ṣāpənat Paʿnēaḥ*** (41:45) is **the only renaming in the
  Bible the text does not translate.** ***Wəḥimmēš*** (41:34) is **a verb coined from a numeral.** And
  ***hayʾōr*** and ***ḥarṭummîm*** are **Egyptian loanwords used as though they were Hebrew.** **Set
  against that, the chapter's most precise verbal effect is a repetition**: Pharaoh's *"it couldn't be
  known"* (41:21), ***lōʾ nôdaʿ***, handed back to him ten verses later as ***lōʾ-yiwwādaʿ*** (41:31)
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:23; **the only verse in Genesis where the barrier between two parties is
  linguistic and is deliberately maintained by the one man who could remove it.** *"They didn't know
  that Joseph understood them; for there was an interpreter between them"* — ***mēlîṣ***, a rare noun
  that elsewhere means *mediator* (Job 33:23; Isaiah 43:27). **He speaks *roughly* (42:7, literally
  *hard things*), swears in Egyptian court idiom, and withholds the one sentence in his own language
  that would end the chapter**
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:3, 7, 16; **three intensified infinitives in fifteen verses** —
  *hāʿēd hēʿid*, *"solemnly warned"*; *šāʾôl šāʾal*, *"asked directly"*; *ṭĕbōaḥ ṭebaḥ*, *"butcher an
  animal"* — **two of them the brothers' account of a conversation the reader has already watched.**
  And **the *mēlîṣ* of 42:23 is never mentioned again**, though nothing says he has gone: the chapter
  runs its whole conversation without telling the reader which language it is in

- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:4–6, 18–34; **a chapter made almost entirely of
  quotation, and no interpreter is mentioned.** [[Joseph]]'s accusation is delivered by
  [[Joseph's Steward]] in Joseph's own words (44:4–6); **[[Judah]]'s seventeen verses are built out of Joseph's terms,
  [[Jacob]]'s three sayings, and his own 43:9 pledge**, with one sentence of his own at the end. The
  *mēlîṣ* of 42:23 is never named again and **the reader is not told how the longest speech in Genesis
  crosses the wall Joseph built.** *"What will we tell my lord? What will we speak? Or how will we
  clear ourselves?"* (44:16) is **three verbs of speech collapsing into one of law**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:1–15, 24, 26; **the chapter in which the language barrier
  comes down and the most important thing still goes unsaid.** *"It is my mouth that speaks to you"*
  (45:12) **is offered as evidence of identity**, and it is the answer to *"there was an interpreter
  between them"* (42:23). The chapter is also **the book's densest run of imperatives** — *come near,
  don't be grieved, hurry, go up, tell him, come down, don't wait, tell my father, don't quarrel*.
  **Against all that speech stand two silences**: the brothers say nothing for twelve verses (45:3–15),
  and *"after that his brothers talked with him"* (45:15) **is the entire record of a conversation
  twenty-two years in coming.** Their one quoted sentence (45:26) omits the sale

- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26; **the silence broken, and the book's hinge.** Seven days of
  saying nothing produce twenty-six verses, and the twenty-six verses produce twenty-five chapters
  of argument. **Three features of the speech are all about speech.** It is addressed to nobody —
  not to God, who is spoken of in the third person, and not to the friends, who answer as though it
  had been. **It curses a day rather than a person**, and with *qālal*, the mild verb, where the
  prologue's whole tension was over whether Job would use the other word about God. And it
  **personifies the night as an announcer** — *"the night which said, 'There is a boy conceived'"*
  (3:3) — so that a piece of news becomes the thing punishable. Job's own account of why he spoke
  comes at 7:11: *"Therefore I will not keep silent"*
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:2–3; ***"Shouldn't the multitude of words be answered? Should a man full of
  talk be justified?"*** — *ʾîš śəpātayim*, a man of lips. The friends' standing complaint that Job
  talks too much, in a speech whose central wish is that God would talk (11:5)
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 12:11; 13:4–6, 12–13, 17, 22; **speech for God and speech to God.**
  The friends *"speak unrighteously for God"* (13:7) and their *"memorable sayings are proverbs of
  ashes"* (13:12); their wisdom would be silence (13:5). Job asks for silence so that he can speak
  (13:13) and offers God the two orders of a hearing — *"call, and I will answer; or let me speak, and
  you answer me"* (13:22). *"Doesn't the ear try words?"* (12:11), which Elihu quotes (34:3)
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:2–5, 14–16, 19, 28, 34; **speech quoted, and silence
  asked for.** *"Listen diligently to my speech. Let this be your consolation"* (21:2) — listening as
  the comfort; *"Lay your hand on your mouth"* (21:5), the gesture Job will make at 40:4 (see
  [[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]]); the wicked's speech quoted (21:14–15),
  and the friends' (21:19, 28); and the verdict on their answers, *hebel* and *maʿal* — breath, and a
  breach of faith (21:34). Eliphaz will quote this speech back at 22:17–18.
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:13–14, 17–18; **misquotation and exact quotation in one
  speech.** Job is made to say *"What does God know?"*, which he never said, and then quoted to the
  letter — *"The counsel of the wicked is far from me"* (21:16 at 22:18) — so that his own
  disclaimer becomes Eliphaz's.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:4–5, 12; 24:25; *"I would set my cause in
  order before him, and fill my mouth with arguments"* — speech as a brief that cannot be filed; the
  words of God's mouth treasured *"more than my necessary food"*; and the dare, *"who will prove me a
  liar, and make my speech worth nothing?"*, which none of the friends takes up.
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:9–10, 21–23; **the gate's order of speech**: princes with a hand on
  the mouth, nobles' tongues stuck to the palate, and men who *"kept silence for my counsel. After my
  words they didn't speak again"*, waiting for Job's words *"as for the rain"*. The dialogue has
  overturned it — three men who will not stop. See
  [[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].
- [[Now They Mock Me]] — Job 30:5, 7, 9; **speech degraded**: the shout raised after a thief (30:5), the
  braying in the bushes (*nāhaq*, otherwise only of the wild donkey, 6:5), and ***"Now I have become
  their song. Yes, I am a byword to them"*** (30:9) — *nəgînātām*, the taunt-song of Lamentations 3:14
  and Psalm 69:12. The gate that fell silent for him (29:9–10, 21–22) has become a place where he is
  sung about.
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 32:6–33:33; **the speaker who is all speech.** *"I am full of words. The spirit within
  me constrains me … like new wineskins it is ready to burst. I will speak, that I may be refreshed"*
  (32:18–20). He promises no *"flattering titles"* (32:21), calls Job by name as no friend does (33:1,
  31), opens his mouth in the phrase of 3:1 (33:2), and is **the first speaker to quote Job** — 33:11 is
  13:27 word for word. And no one answers a word of it.
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:3, 35, 37; 35:16; **the charge of words without knowledge.** *"The ear
  tries words, as the palate tastes food"* (34:3), Job's 12:11 quoted. Then the verdict: *"Job speaks
  without knowledge. His words are without wisdom"* (34:35); he *"multiplies his words against God"*
  (34:37); *"Job opens his mouth with empty talk (hebel), and he multiplies words without knowledge"*
  (35:16). God's first words at 38:2 repeat the charge, and 42:7 calls Job's words right. See
  [[Words Without Knowledge - Job 34.35, 35.16, 38.2, and 42.3]].
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:2; 37:2–5, 19–20; **the voice in the thunder.** Elihu speaks
  *"on God's behalf"* (36:2), the brief Job accused the friends of holding (13:7). The thunder is God's
  *qôl*, five times in four verses (37:2–5), *"the voice of his majesty"*. And speech to God is said to be
  impossible: *"we can't make our case by reason of darkness. Will it be told him that I would speak?"*
  (37:19–20) — Job's *"I have set my cause in order"* (13:18) declared out of reach, one verse before God
  asks Job to answer (38:3). See [[The Voice of His Majesty - Job 37.2-5 and Psalm 29]].
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:2–3; 40:2–5; **speech judged, and speech surrendered.** *"Words
  without knowledge"* (38:2) is the charge; *"I will question you, then you answer me"* (38:3) the
  procedure; and Job's reply is to stop: *"I lay my hand on my mouth. I have spoken once, and I will not
  answer; Yes, twice, but I will proceed no further"* (40:4–5). See
  [[Once, Yes Twice - Job 33.14, 40.5, and Psalm 62.11]].
- [[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]] — Job 42:1–6; **the last words of the speaker who spoke most.** Five verses, two of them
  quotations of God (42:3a from 38:2; 42:4b from 38:3 / 40:7), and one confession about speech itself: *"I have
  uttered that which I did not understand"* (42:3). The man who said *"I will proceed no further"* (40:5)
  speaks once more, and then the book gives him no further lines.
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:7–9; **a verdict on speech.** God judges what was *spoken* — *"you have not
  spoken of me the thing that is right"*, twice — and *ʾēlay* may mean *to me*: Job spoke to God, the friends only
  about him. The friends answer with an offering and no words; Bildad's *"man, who is a worm"* (25:6) stands as the
  last thing any of them says.
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 4:10–16; ***lōʾ ʾîš dəbārîm ʾānōkî***, ***"not a man of words am I"***, **said by the
  man who will speak Deuteronomy**, whose first verse is *"These are the words which Moses spoke"*. *Heavy
  of mouth and heavy of tongue* — *kābēd*, the word of Pharaoh's *heavy* heart (7:14). God's answer is
  the doctrine of speech as gift: *"Who made man's mouth? … I will be with your mouth, and teach you what
  you shall speak"*; and then a chain of mouths — God to Moses, Moses to [[Aaron]], Aaron to the
  people. Stephen's *"mighty in his words"* (Acts 7:22) stands against it; the tension is recorded, not
  resolved
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:9, 23; ***"lying words"*** and ***"to speak in your name"*** —
  **Pharaoh calls Yahweh's word *diḇrê šāqer*; Moses spoke in the name and was answered with
  contempt.** The officers' plain speech to Pharaoh (*"the fault is in your own people"*, 5:16) is
  answered with *"You are idle!"*
- [[I Am Yahweh]] — Exodus 6:9–12; **the speaker who says he cannot speak, and the hearers who
  cannot hear.** *Šāmaʿ* runs through the pericope: God *has heard* (6:5), Israel *did not hear*
  (6:9), and Pharaoh *will not hear* (6:12). Moses' *"uncircumcised lips"* restates 4:10's *heavy of
  mouth* — [[Uncircumcised Lips, Ears, and Heart]]
- [[Aaron's Staff Before Pharaoh]] — Exodus 6:30–7:2; **the objection answered by a spokesman.**
  *"I am of uncircumcised lips"* is met, this time, by the arrangement of 4:16: Aaron will speak.
  God concedes that *"Pharaoh will not listen to you"* (7:4), and at 7:13 *"he didn't listen to
  them"* — the *šāmaʿ* thread of [[I Am Yahweh]] runs on into the contest

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:1, 7, 16, 19; divine command, accountable human words, and
  the request for Moses’ voice
- [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] — Exodus 22:28; not cursing God or a ruler
- [[The Blood of the Covenant]] — Exodus 24:4, 7, 12; the words written, read aloud, and promised on stone
- [[If You Seek Her as Silver]] — Proverbs 2:1, 6, 12, 16; the father's words and God's mouth against perverse speech and smooth words
- [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] — Proverbs 6:22, 24; the teaching that talks with the son, against
  the smooth tongue
- [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] — Proverbs 7:14–21; the strange woman's speech, *leqaḥ* and flattering lips
- [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] — Proverbs 8:6–9, 13; Wisdom's straight, plain, truthful words against the perverse mouth
- [[A Good Name]] — Proverbs 22:11–12, 14; Gracious lips, frustrated words, and the adulteress's mouth.
- [[Thirty Excellent Things]] — Proverbs 22:18, 21; 23:8–9; Words ready on the lips, reliable answers, good words wasted.
- [[Do Not Envy Sinners]] — Proverbs 23:16, 35; 24:2; Lips that speak what is right, the drunkard's speech, and lips that talk mischief.
- [[The Field of the Sluggard]] — Proverbs 24:26, 28; The honest answer as a kiss; the witness without cause.
- [[The Glory of Kings]] — Proverbs 25:9–15, 18–20, 23; The word fitly spoken, the soft tongue, the false witness as a weapon.
- [[Answer a Fool]] — Proverbs 26:4–9, 18–28; The proverb in a fool's mouth, the "joke", the whisperer's morsels, charming speech, the lying tongue.
- [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]] — Proverbs 27:2, 5, 11, 14; Praise from another's mouth, open rebuke, the answer to a reproacher, the loud blessing.
