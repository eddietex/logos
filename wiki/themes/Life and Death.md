# Life and Death

## Definition / overview

### Death and the holy food — Leviticus 22:1–16

Corpse contact and carcasses defile the priest (4, 8), and a priest who profanes the commandment
may "die therein" (9). The death language is a warning attached to the commandment, not a
narrated execution.

See [[Eating the Holy Things]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 22:1–16.


### Priestly holiness — Leviticus 21

Death affects priestly access through corpse defilement and differentiated kinship exceptions
(1–3, 11). Verse 9 also prescribes burning for a priest’s daughter who prostitutes herself; it
is a legal sanction, not a narrated execution.

See [[The Holiness of the Priests]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 21.


### Penalties and separation — Leviticus 20

Death penalties, cutting off, and childlessness are differentiated (2–21, 27). The chapter gives
sanctions, not a narrative of their execution or a description of the offenders’ afterlife.

See [[Penalties and a People Set Apart]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 20; comparisons as cited.


Life in Scripture is not a property things have on their own but something given and sustained by
God, and death is its withdrawal. Genesis establishes both in a single scene.

**Life** arrives in [[The Garden of Eden]] as breath: *"Yahweh God formed man from the dust of the
ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul"* (2:7).
Two points the Hebrew makes that English blurs:

- The man does not *receive* a soul as a separate part. *Nephesh ḥayyāh*, "living soul", is the
  same phrase Genesis 1:20–24 uses of fish, birds, and animals. Dust plus breath *is* a living
  being; take the breath away and what is left is dust (3:19, Psalm 104:29, Ecclesiastes 12:7).
- The breath is God's own and personally given — nostril to nostril, not spoken from a distance
  like everything else in Genesis 1.

**Death** enters the story before anyone dies, as the stated consequence of one act: *"in the day
that you eat of it, you will surely die"* (2:17). It is a penalty attached to a choice, not a
built-in feature of the creation described. Against it stands the **tree of life**, growing in
the middle of the garden, unguarded and unforbidden — until 3:22–24, when access to it is exactly
what the expulsion removes.

The unresolved knot: they eat, and do not die that day. Whether the sentence means mortality
becoming certain, a death that is relational before it is biological, or a sentence God commutes
in the act of pronouncing it, Genesis leaves standing — see the notes on
[[The Garden of Eden]].

**How death actually arrives** is settled in [[The Fall]], and not as a same-day execution. The
man's sentence returns him to his origin: *"By the sweat of your face will you eat bread until you
return to the ground, for out of it you were taken. For you are dust, and to dust you shall
return"* (3:19). Dust plus breath made a living being; death is the sentence read backwards. The
seal on it is not the eating but the exile — the expulsion is given explicitly so that the man
cannot *"take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever"* (3:22). Mortality becomes
irreversible at the moment access to the remedy is cut off, which means the same act that ends the
garden is what makes death permanent, and is also framed as preventing something worse.

**The first death is a killing.** In [[Cain and Abel]] the sentence of 3:19 is carried out ahead of
schedule and by a human hand: not age, not the ground taking back its dust, but a brother in a
field (4:8). The theme's shape changes with it. Death stops being only a condition humanity is
under and becomes something people do to each other, which is why 4:10 gives blood a voice —
*"The voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the ground"* — and why the rest of the Bible
treats bloodshed as a thing the land itself registers (Genesis 9:5–6, Numbers 35:33,
Deuteronomy 21:1–9). The man named *hebel*, "vapour", is the first to prove the word.

**Then the theme becomes a refrain.** [[From Adam to Noah]] is where Genesis stops arguing about
death and simply records it. Ten entries, eight of them ending in the same three words — *"then he
died"* — against lifespans of nine centuries that lose every time. Nothing is explained; the
sentence of 2:17 and 3:19 is converted from a threat into a column of totals, and the reader is made
to feel it by repetition rather than by assertion. Methuselah lives longer than any human being in
Scripture, 969 years, and his entry ends the way all of them end.

The two entries that omit the refrain are what the chapter is for. [[Enoch (son of Jared)]] *"walked
with God, and he was not found, for God took him"* (5:24) — subtracted from the list rather than
removed from it, and the first hint anywhere in the Bible that the sentence can be interrupted. And
[[Noah]] is left open: an age, three sons, and no total, because his story has not happened yet. So
Genesis 5 states the rule eight times and breaks it twice, which is the shape the theme keeps for
the rest of the canon.

**At Sinai death is a threatened consequence of unauthorized approach, not a narrated loss.**
The boundary binds animal and human alike; the offender is to be stoned or shot, not touched
(19:12–13). God repeats the warning so that the people do not perish by breaking through
(19:21–24). No one is said to cross the line or die in this chapter. See [[Israel at Mount Sinai]].

## Development across Scripture

### Holiness and the neighbor — Leviticus 19:1–18

The WEB forbids endangering the neighbor’s life (16), adjacent to its slander prohibition. The
passage protects life within ordinary communal dealings; it does not narrate a death.

See [[Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19:1–18.


### Forbidden unions and the land — Leviticus 18:1–30

Obedience is associated with life (5), child sacrifice is forbidden (21), and prohibited acts bring cutting off (29). The chapter does not define the life promise as immortality.

See [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18.

### Sacrifice and blood — Leviticus 17:1–16

The life of flesh grounds the blood restriction (11, 14). Permission to eat
animal food is bounded by respect for that life: even hunting requires pouring out
and covering the blood (13).

See [[The Place of Sacrifice and the Life in the Blood]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 17; comparisons as cited.


### Lest he die — Leviticus 16:1–34

The chapter opens with the death of two priests (1) and twice frames the high priest's entry
as a matter of survival — *"lest he die"* (2), *"so that he will not die"* (13). Life before the
mercy seat is preserved by the prescribed approach, not assumed.

See [[The Day of Atonement]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 16; comparisons as cited.


### Clean and unclean animals — Leviticus 11:1–47

Leviticus 11:39–40 makes a dead animal of an edible kind a source of uncleanness.
Death changes the conditions of contact even where the species itself is permitted
for food; carrying or eating its carcass entails washing clothes and waiting until evening.

See [[Clean and Unclean Animals]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 11.

- **Genesis 2:7** — life as breath given.
- **Genesis 2:9, 2:17, 3:22–24** — the tree of life, the death sentence, the barred access.
- **Genesis 3:19** — *"to dust you shall return"*: death defined as the reversal of 2:7.
- **Genesis 4:8, 4:10** — the sentence first carried out, by a human hand; and blood given a voice
  that God answers.
- **Genesis 4:23–25** — the second killing, and the theme's first genealogy. A list of names is one
  of the ways a narrative reports death: Genesis 4:17–24 runs seven generations in eight verses,
  which is seven lifetimes passing without a word about any of them, and it ends with a man claiming
  a further killing. Against that, 4:25 puts a birth in the place of a death — Seth given
  *"instead of Abel"*.
- **Genesis 5:1–32** — the genealogy whose refrain is *"then he died"*, eight times, with Enoch
  taken instead (5:24) and Noah's entry left unclosed (5:32). Also the chapter's other quiet
  argument: 5:1–2 puts the whole register under the blessing of 1:28, so the *"other sons and
  daughters"* of each entry are life continuing under sentence, not in spite of it.
- **Genesis 6:3** — *"My Spirit will not strive with man forever, because he also is flesh; so his
  days will be one hundred twenty years"*, three verses after the chapter of nine-hundred-year
  lives. The first numerical limit set on human life, and the first time being **flesh** (*bāśār*,
  the frailty word) is given as the ground of a divine decision. Whether the number is a new cap on
  lifespan or a countdown to the flood is disputed, and the arguments are on the passage page.
- **Genesis 6:7** — the sentence extended past individuals to *"man, along with animals, creeping
  things, and birds of the sky"* — the creatures of days five and six, listed for erasure in
  something close to the reverse of the order they were made in. Death moves here from a penalty
  borne by persons to the undoing of a world. See
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- **Genesis 6:17; 7:21–23** — the sentence executed, and the narrator says it four times in three
  verses rather than once: *"All flesh died that moved on the earth"* (7:21), *"All on the dry land,
  in whose nostrils was the breath of the spirit of life, died"* (7:22), *"Every living thing was
  destroyed"* and *"They were destroyed from the earth"* (7:23). The repetition is the point — this
  is the largest death in Scripture and Genesis refuses to compress it. Note that 7:22 defines what
  is being taken by quoting the making of the man at 2:7: the *breath in the nostrils* God lent to
  dust is named here as the thing that stops. And note the exception clause, *"Only Noah was left"*
  (7:23), whose verb *šāʾar* is the root of the prophets' **remnant** — the idea enters the Bible in
  a sentence about eight survivors. See [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 9:1–17** — the terms on which life resumes, and they are terms about killing. Animals may
  now be killed and eaten (9:3), where 1:29–30 had assigned plants; their blood may not be consumed,
  *"for flesh with its life, that is, its blood"* (9:4), which makes *nephesh* — the word for the
  living being the man becomes at 2:7 — the reason; human life is required back by God from whoever
  takes it, animals included (9:5); and the world itself will not be killed again (9:11). Two of the
  four are concessions to a violence the text expects, and 8:21 has already said why it expects it.
  See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and [[Blood]].
- **Genesis 9:29** — *"All the days of Noah were nine hundred fifty years, and then he died."* Genesis
  5's formula, word for word, closing the entry that chapter left open at 5:32 — the tenth and last
  time the antediluvian obituary is used. It is also the end of an era in the numbers. Noah is the last
  man in Scripture to live past nine hundred; the register in Genesis 11 keeps the ages and drops the
  death clause entirely, and the lifespans in it fall by half and then by half again until they reach
  Psalm 90's seventy. The flood narrative turns out to have been told inside a single genealogical
  entry, between a man's age at fatherhood and his age at death. See
  [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].
- **Genesis 11:10–32** — the second register, and the theme handled by omission. Nine consecutive
  entries and **not one of them says the man died**; Genesis 5's refrain is simply deleted, and no
  totals are given either. What the chapter does instead is let the numbers fall — 950, 600, then
  438, 433, 464, then 239, 239, 230, 148 — so that mortality is registered as a slope rather than as
  a sentence, and 6:3's *"one hundred twenty years"* is carried out without being mentioned. The
  only two deaths in the passage are in Terah's household: a son who dies before his father, which
  has not happened before in Scripture, and a father who dies short of the land he set out for. See
  [[From Shem to Abram]] and
  [[Ten Before and Ten After - The Registers of Genesis 5 and Genesis 11]].
- **Psalm 90:10** — *"The days of our years are seventy, or even by reason of strength eighty
  years"*: the Bible's own settled measure of a life, against Genesis 5's numbers.
- Later strands to trace: the choice of *"life and death, blessing and cursing"* in Deuteronomy
  30:15–20; Sheol in the Psalms and Job; the valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37); resurrection in
  Daniel 12:2; Jesus as "the life" (John 11:25, 14:6); Romans 5:12–21 and 6:23 (*"the wages of sin
  is death"*); 1 Corinthians 15; Revelation 20:14 and 21:4, where death itself dies.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:12–13, 18–21

The unit combines a promise of long life linked to honoring parents (20:12), a prohibition of murder
(20:13), and the assembly's fear that further divine speech will kill them (20:19). Moses answers
the fear by explaining the encounter as a test against sin; no one dies in this scene. The promise
is not a rule by which every early death can be assigned a cause. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### Violence, injury, and liability — Exodus 21:12–36

Intentional killing and an unintentional death have different consequences (21:12–14). Liability
can also follow a failure to restrain a bull whose danger was known (21:29), with a conditional
ransom (21:30). **The life-for-life formula does not describe every remedy in this unit:** a
servant killed by a bull brings thirty shekels to the master (21:32). Human life is protected here
within an explicitly unequal social order.
See [[Laws of Violence and Injury]].
### The thief at night — Exodus 22:2–3

A thief struck dead while breaking in brings no guilt of bloodshed, but *"If the sun has risen on
him, guilt of bloodshed shall be for him"* (22:2–3). The text names only the sunrise; the usual
reading is that once a thief can be seen and made to pay, killing him is no longer defence. The
law continues 21:12–14's attention to circumstances in judging a killing.
See [[Laws of Property and Restitution]].

### The incense altar, the ransom, and the holy oil — Exodus 30:1–38

Death surrounds the holy things in Exodus 30. The census ransom is paid *"that there be no plague
among them"* (30:12). The priests wash *"that they not die"*, said twice (30:20–21). Whoever copies
the oil or the incense *"shall be cut off from his people"* (30:33, 38), the penalty Genesis 17:14
set for the uncircumcised. See [[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]].

### The Sabbath death sanction — Exodus 31

Exodus 31:14–15 expressly requires death for profaning the Sabbath and for working on it. Verse 14
also describes the worker as cut off from the people. Death is explicit here, while the exact
relation of being cut off to execution is not explained. No offender or execution is narrated in
this pericope, and no means of execution is specified.

See [[Bezalel, Oholiab, and the Sabbath Sign]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

The threatened destruction of the nation is averted (32:10–14), but about three thousand are
killed and a later plague is reported (32:28, 35). Moses asks to be blotted from God’s book if
forgiveness is refused; the book’s scope is not explained here. Survival of the people and
liability of sinners coexist. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

God declares that a person cannot see his face and live (33:20), and promises to cover Moses in the
rock while the glory passes (33:21–23). The hand which might strike in judgment here protects the
recipient of revelation. The scene supplies a limit on sight, not an account of an actual death. See
[[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

Exodus 35:2 repeats death for work on the Sabbath before the call for gifts. The passage reports
no offender, execution, or means of carrying out the penalty. Its willing giving must not obscure
the serious sanction with which the assembly's address opens.

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The house that sinks to death — Proverbs 2:18–19

*"For her house leads down to death, her paths to the departed spirits. None who go to her return
again, neither do they attain to the paths of life"* (2:18–19). *Leads down* is *šāḥâ*, *sinks*; the
*departed spirits* are *rəp̄āʾîm*, the shades (as in Job 26:5; Psalm 88:10; Isaiah 14:9). *"None …
return"* (*lōʾ yəšûḇûn*) is Job's description of death, *"where I shall not return from"* (Job 10:21,
*wəlōʾ ʾāšûḇ*). The chapter's two roads end in *death* and in *"the paths of life"*. See
[[If You Seek Her as Silver]] and [[Sheol]]. Source: Proverbs 2 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Wisdom as a tree of life — Proverbs 3:16–18

Wisdom carries length of days and becomes a tree of life for those who grasp and retain
her. [[Wisdom's Worth and the Founding of the Earth]] brings the image associated with
Eden into everyday instruction. Genesis 3:22–24 barred access to the tree whose fruit
would allow unending life; Proverbs 3:18 offers a metaphor for life-giving wisdom,
without announcing immortality or a reopening of Eden. See
[[The Tree of Life - Eden and the New Jerusalem]].

Source: Proverbs 3 and the cited Genesis and Job comparisons checked in the stored WEB,
2026-10-25.

### Wisdom as life in daily activity — Proverbs 3:21–35

Sound wisdom and discretion become life to the son (3:21–22), expressed through secure
steps and sweet sleep (3:23–24). The language continues 3:18's life-giving wisdom without
promising immortality or defining the soul as an independently immortal entity.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### Keep the teaching and live — Proverbs 4:1–9

The remembered father's commands are attached to life (4:4), and wisdom promises to
preserve the learner who will not forsake her (4:6). The unit does not describe resurrection
or abolish mortality. Nor does it justify inferring folly from someone's suffering:
Job 1:1; 2:3 depicts an upright man exposed to undeserved ruin. See [[Retribution]].

See [[Get Wisdom]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Instruction as life — Proverbs 4:10–13

*"The years of your life will be many"* (4:10), and instruction is to be kept *"for she is your
life"* (4:13). As in 3:2, length of years is promised as the ordinary fruit of the way, not as
an exceptionless rule; see [[Retribution]].

See [[The Two Paths]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### The wellspring of life — Proverbs 4:20–23

The father's words are *"life to those who find them"* (4:22), and the heart is kept because
*"out of it is the wellspring of life"* (4:23). Life is located at a source inside the person and
described as flowing out into conduct. Job 31 and 2:7 record a man who kept heart, eyes, and
feet and still lost the body's health; see [[Heart, Eyes, and Feet - Proverbs 4 and Job 31]].

See [[Keep Your Heart]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Her feet go down to death — Proverbs 5:5–6, 23

*"Her feet go down to death. Her steps lead straight to Sheol. She gives no thought to the way of
life"* (5:5–6). The lecture ends with the man who followed her: *"He will die for lack of
instruction"* (5:23). Death here is the end of a road, the opposite of 4:22's words that are
*"life to those who find them"*. See [[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]] and [[Sheol]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 5; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### The precious life hunted — Proverbs 6:23–33

*"Reproofs of instruction are the way of life"* (6:23), and the danger is to life: *"The adulteress
hunts for your precious life"* (6:26, *nep̄eš yəqārâ*), and the adulterer *"destroys his own soul"*
(6:32). The thief steals *to fill his soul* (6:30) and can pay; the adulterer ruins his. See
[[The Lamp of the Commandment]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra
according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

Proverbs 7 runs from *"Keep my commandments and live!"* (7:2) to *"the rooms of death"* (7:27,
*ḥaḏrê-māweṯ*), the word for inner rooms or bedchambers — the bed she prepared in 7:16–17. Between
them the young man *"doesn't know that it will cost his life"* (7:23, *ḇənap̄šô hûʾ*). See
[[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] and [[Sheol]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 7; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

**Proverbs 8:35–36 — two loves.** Wisdom's speech ends on life and death set before the hearer:
*"For whoever finds me, finds life, and will obtain favor from Yahweh. But he who sins against me
wrongs his own soul. All those who hate me love death."* *Ḥōmēs nap̄šô*, *does violence to his own
soul*, uses the root of Genesis 6:11's *ḥāmās*. The chapter that began its promises with *"I love those
who love me"* (8:17) ends with a second love — of death — answering 7:27's house of death. See [[Wisdom at the Creation]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:2, 11, 16–17, 21, 27

Righteousness delivers from death; the righteous mouth is a spring of life and feeds many; accepting
correction marks the way of life. These images include life sustained through other people’s words.
The chapter neither explains bodily immortality nor gives a resurrection timetable; its promises
remain in tension with Job’s losses.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:4, 7–8, 19, 30

Righteousness is aligned with deliverance and life, evil with death. Verse 30 calls the fruit of the
righteous a tree of life, extending the image from wisdom in Proverbs 3:18 to the good produced by
righteous living. This is life-giving imagery, not a promise of bodily immortality or a narrated
reopening of Eden.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:6, 10, 28

The chapter includes both human blood threatened by speech and the life of an animal entrusted to
human care. Verse 28 closes on life in righteousness and no death in its path. In the WEB this is an
emphatic life-giving road image; it does not explain resurrection or guarantee that a righteous body
never dies.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Spring of life, snares of death — Proverbs 13:3, 12, 14

The guarded mouth guards the soul (3); fulfilled longing is *"a tree of life"* (12); the teaching of the wise is *"a spring of life, to turn from the snares of death"* (14). Life is the outcome of instruction received, stated without any account of what lies after death.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### A way to death, a fountain of life, a refuge in death — Proverbs 14:12, 27, 30, 32

*"There is a way which seems right to a man, but in the end it leads to death"* (12; repeated at 16:25). The fear of Yahweh is *"a fountain of life"* (27); *"The life of the body is a heart at peace"* (30); and *"in death, the righteous has a refuge"* (32) — the WEB's reading, which some translations render differently; the alternative is not verified here. It stands beside Job 21:26's righteous and wicked lying *"alike in the dust"*.

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### The path that leads upward — Proverbs 15:4, 10, 24, 27, 31

The gentle tongue is *"a tree of life"* (4); *"whoever hates reproof shall die"* (10); the hater of bribes *"will live"* (27); the listening ear *"lives"* (31). Verse 24 gives the prologue's descent to Sheol (5:5; 7:27; 9:18) its opposite: *"The path of life leads upward for the wise, to keep him from going downward to Sheol."* The verse says what the path avoids, not where it ends.

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### The king's face and the fountain — Proverbs 16:14–15, 22, 25

The king's wrath is *"a messenger of death"* and the light of his face *"is life"* (14–15); understanding is *"a fountain of life"* (22); and the way that seems right ends in death (25).

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### In the power of the tongue — Proverbs 18:21

*"Death and life are in the power of the tongue; those who love it will eat its fruit"* (21) —
*bəyaḏ-lāšôn*, *in the hand of the tongue*. After the stomach filled with the mouth's fruit (20), speech
is food, and it can kill.

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:8–9, 16, 18, 23

Wisdom benefits one's own soul (8), lies end in perishing (9), keeping the commandment guards life
(16), and correction is framed by hope and avoidance of the son's death (18). Verse 23 promises
life, contentment, and safety to the God-fearing. Job's affliction remains an explicit
counterexample to treating that safety as an unconditional promise; the chapter supplies no
timetable for resolving the tension.

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:2, 7, 20, 22

Provoking the king endangers life (2), parental cursing ends in an extinguished lamp (20), and
waiting for Yahweh replaces revenge with promised rescue (22). The blessing on righteous
offspring (7) cannot serve as a guarantee that their lives are immune to loss beside Job
1:18–19. The chapter gives warnings and assurances without a timetable for every outcome.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### The assembly of the departed spirits — Proverbs 21:6, 16, 21, 25

Lying treasure belongs to *"those who seek death"* (6); the wanderer rests in *"the assembly of the
departed spirits"* (16), the WEB phrase of 2:18 and 9:18; the pursuer of righteousness finds life
(21); the sluggard's desire kills him (25). See [[Sheol]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### The rod and Sheol — Proverbs 23:13–14

*"If you punish him with the rod, he will not die. Punish him with the rod, and save his soul from
Sheol."* The saying answers the fear that correction harms with the claim that neglect is deadlier;
the death in view is the one folly leads to (7:27; 19:18). Later in the block the father calls himself
the one *"who gave you life"* (23:22). See
[[The Rod of Discipline - Proverbs 13.24 and the Father's Correction]].

See [[Do Not Envy Sinners]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 23:12–24:2.

### Those led away to death — Proverbs 24:11

*"Rescue those who are being led away to death! Indeed, hold back those who are staggering to the
slaughter!"* Death here is not the end of the fool's road, as so often in chapters 1–9, but another's
danger that the wise are commanded to interrupt. The text does not say whether the condemned are
innocent; it says the bystander answers to God for doing nothing (24:12).

See [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:3–22.

### Sheol and the barren womb — Proverbs 30:15–17

Sheol heads the list of things that never say *"Enough"*, and the barren womb follows it (16).
Death that keeps taking and a womb that cannot give life are named together as unfilled want.
Verse 17 then gives the mocker of parents an unburied death, his eye left to the birds — a warning
about the end of contempt, not an account of a particular death.

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

### Lemuel’s mother — Proverbs 31:3, 6

The mother warns against what destroys kings and directs strong drink to the person ready to
perish. Verse 6 describes the recipient’s peril, not an instruction to bring about death, and
it promises no recovery. The text’s concern for relief belongs with its demand to protect the
vulnerable, without supplying a medical protocol or an account of the afterlife.

See [[The Words of King Lemuel]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9 and the comparisons documented
there.

### The opening of Ecclesiastes — 1:1–11

The coming and going of generations in Ecclesiastes 1:4 stands against the continuing earth;
1:11 adds the loss of memory among those who come later. The opening describes human
transience without narrating a particular death or defining an afterlife. Job 14:7–12 also
sets human mortality beside nature, but there a tree may renew and waters may fail; the images
are not identical. See [[The Earth Remains - Genesis and Ecclesiastes 1]].

See [[Vanity of Vanities]]. Sources: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and the comparison texts
verified on that passage page.


### The wise man dies like the fool — Ecclesiastes 2:14–17

*"One event happens to them all"* (2:14); *"the wise man must die just like the fool!"* (2:16). The
Preacher applies the common end to himself (2:15), and draws the book's starkest response so far:
*"So I hated life"* (2:17). The text does not correct the feeling here;
[[The Labor Left to Another]] commends enjoyment (2:24), and [[One Event Comes to All]]
prefers *"a living dog"* to *"a dead lion"* (9:4). Job 21:26
makes the same observation of two men of opposite fortune, and uses it against the friends'
theology instead of against his own wisdom. See
[[One Event Happens to Them All - Job 21.23-26 and Ecclesiastes 2.14-16]].

See [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:14–17; 2:24; 9:4; Job 21:23–26.

### Labor left to another — Ecclesiastes 2:18–26

After the shared death of wise and fool in 2:16, the speaker faces what happens to his work:
it must pass to another (2:18–21). The present unit does not narrate a death or describe an
afterlife; mortality is its preceding context. Eating, drinking, and enjoying labor (2:24)
name a good within life without promising control beyond it.

See [[The Labor Left to Another]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:18–26.

### A time for every purpose — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

The poem begins with a time to be born and a time to die (2), and then names killing and
healing (3). These are recurring conditions and actions within life; the poem does not give
an individual a date of death or instruct anyone to end a life. The WEB's eternity in human
hearts (11) is immediately paired with limited understanding, and is not an explicit promise
of an immortal soul or an account of what happens after death.

See [[A Time for Every Purpose]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

### From dust to dust — Ecclesiastes 3:16–22

The common end of wise and fool in 2:14–16 broadens to humans and animals (3:19–20).
The denial of human advantage is set within their shared death and dust, not an explicit
revocation of Genesis 1:26–28's image and dominion. Verse 21 asks about the spirits' destinations
in the WEB; it does not assert either that different destinations are known or that further
life is impossible. The later affirmation of 12:7 remains a distinct claim in stored text.
See [[Dust and Breath - Genesis 2-3 and Ecclesiastes 3.18-21]].

See [[From Dust to Dust]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–22; comparisons as identified above.

### The tears of the oppressed — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

After seeing oppression, the Preacher praises the dead above the living and the one who
has not yet been above both (2–3), because the latter has not seen evil. This is not a command
to die or a description of the dead's conscious condition. It stands in explicit tension
with enjoyment in 3:22 and the hope of the living in 9:4 in [[One Event Comes to All]] (checked in
stored WEB). The stillborn comparison in 6:3–5 is distinct from 4:3's wording.

See [[The Tears of the Oppressed]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–4:3; the passage page
records the additional comparison sources.

### Wealth and enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 5:8–20

A person arrives naked from the womb and departs with nothing of his labor in hand (15).
The symmetry is a limit on possession rather than a description of the afterlife. Verse 16
laments it, while 18–20 affirm enjoyment during the days God gives. Mortality and a good
within life remain together. See
[[Naked I Came - Job 1.21 and the Empty Hands of Ecclesiastes and Paul]].

See [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20;
comparisons as identified on the passage page.

### Riches without enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

A hundred children and many years do not outweigh a life unfilled with good and without
burial (3). The stillborn child has more rest (4–5); even two thousand years cannot prevent
the common destination (6). **Tension:** the book also commends enjoyment in 3:22 and
attaches hope to the living in 9:4 in [[One Event Comes to All]] (checked in stored WEB). These
judgments have different settings and remain distinct; no universal command to prefer
death is inferred. See [[Cursing the Day of Birth - Job 3 and Jeremiah 20]] and
[[One Event Happens to Them All - Job 21.23-26 and Ecclesiastes 2.14-16]].

See [[Riches Without Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9; comparison
sources are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Who knows what is good — Ecclesiastes 6:10–12

Life is spent like a shadow, and the speaker asks who can tell what follows a person
under the sun (12). The image expresses transience rather than an exact lifespan.
The stated question concerns what happens under the sun; it does not supply a doctrine
of the person's postmortem consciousness. See
[[Life Like a Shadow - Job 14.1-2 and Ecclesiastes 6.12]] for Job's related image,
used in a plea for relief rather than in Ecclesiastes' question about good.

See [[Who Knows What Is Good]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:10–12; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

### The house of mourning — Ecclesiastes 7:1–14

The day of death is ranked above the day of birth (7:1), and the house of mourning above
feasting because death is *"the end of all men"* (2). Yet wisdom's excellence is that it
preserves the life of the one who has it (12). The text does not explain how; set beside
2:14–16, where the wise die like fools, it is a qualified advantage, not an exemption.

See [[The House of Mourning]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:1–14; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

### Dying before one's time — Ecclesiastes 7:15–29

The righteous man perishes and the wicked man lives long (7:15); folly and wickedness risk
dying before one's time (17), and overmuch righteousness or wisdom risks destroying oneself
(16). The ensnaring woman is found *more bitter than death* (26). Length of life is treated
as something conduct can affect, yet 7:15 denies that it reliably follows righteousness.

See [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29; comparison sources and Hebrew checks identified on the passage page.

### No power over the day of death — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

No man has power to contain the spirit or to control the day of death (8). The WEB's
spirit is retained without a claim about underlying Hebrew. Read after the king's apparent
freedom to act (3–4), the general limit includes rulers. Protection associated with wise
obedience (5) does not confer immortality, just as wisdom's advantage in 2:13–16 does not
prevent its possessor's death. See [[No Power over the Day of Death - Job 14 and Ecclesiastes 8.8]].

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Long life and the wicked — Ecclesiastes 8:10–17

Burial (10), the sinner's long life (12), and denial that the wicked will lengthen
days like a shadow (13) keep mortality beside moral evaluation. **Explicit tension:**
12 and 13 do not give a single rule from which a person's lifespan can be predicted.
The days of life are nevertheless God's gift and the setting of commended joy (15).

See [[The Wicked Buried and the Work Beyond Finding]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:10–17;
comparisons are identified on the passage page.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

The common event encompasses righteous and wicked, ritual distinctions, and oath-taking
(2–3). The living know they will die; the dead know nothing and have no further portion
under the sun (5–6). **Explicit tension:** 4:2 praises the dead above the living, whereas
9:4 prefers a living dog to a dead lion. The settings differ, but neither ranking is
withdrawn. The categorical language about the dead must be retained beside the stated
under-the-sun horizon, not silently converted into a complete account of every biblical
afterlife text. See [[A Living Dog and the Praise of the Dead]].

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Youth, decline, and the return to God — Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8

The invitation to rejoice leads through failing household activity, fear, mourners, and
broken vessels to dust returning to earth and spirit to its giver (11:7–12:7).
**Explicit tension:** 3:21 asks about the spirit's destination in the WEB; 12:7 affirms its
return to God. Neither statement should replace the other. Likewise, 9:5–10's account of
the dead's lost knowledge and earthly portion remains beside 12:7; the latter does not
specify consciousness, resurrection, or the duration of an intermediate state. See
[[Dust and Breath - Genesis 2-3 and Ecclesiastes 3.18-21]].

See [[Remember Your Creator]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8; comparison sources
identified on the passage page.

### Love strong as death — Song of Solomon 8:5–7

In [[Love Is Strong as Death]], death supplies the measure of love’s strength (8:6); Sheol supplies that of jealousy’s
cruelty. The poem does not claim that love is stronger than death, describe an
afterlife, or reverse the mortality described in Job and Ecclesiastes.

Source: stored WEB Song 8:5–7; comparisons use the stored Song and Proverbs 6:30–35.

- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:9–19; **two dreams given on the same night in the same

  interpreter does not soften, delay, or decline it. See

- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:36, 54–57, **famine as the form death takes in this book.**

  covenant verb**, the one used of *cutting* a covenant and, in the passive, of a line being
  extinguished. **It is applied here to a nation outside the covenant**, by a man who does not yet know
  the policy will also keep his own family alive. **45:5 and 50:20 are where he says what it was for** —
  *"God sent me before you to preserve life"*. **And the dream's image of death is the cruellest in
  Genesis**: seven fat years eaten with nothing to show for it, *"it couldn't be known that they had
  eaten them, but they were still ugly, as at the beginning"* (41:21)
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:2, 20, 38; **the chapter states its stakes at both ends and they are
  literal.** *"So that we may live, and not die"* opens it (42:2) and *"you won't die"* closes Joseph's
  terms (42:20); **Jacob's last word is his own grave**, [[Sheol]] — *"you will bring
  down my gray hairs with sorrow to Sheol"* — **and the son he is mourning is alive and sent the
  food**
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:8, 14; **both men state the stakes in the same exchange and
  neither is talking about the same danger.** [[Judah]]: *"so that we may live, and not die, both we,
  and you, and also our little ones"* — **[[Jacob]]'s own phrase from 42:2, handed back to him widened

  šākāltî***, **the doubled perfect of a man conceding the worst outcome in advance.** See
  [[If I Am Bereaved, I Am Bereaved - Jacob and Esther]]

- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:9, 20, 22, 31; **four deaths in the chapter: one
  proposed, one believed, two predicted — and none of them occurs.** The brothers swear *"let him
  die"* over a cup none of them took (44:9); [[Judah]] reports *"his brother is dead"* to the brother
  (44:20); he quotes the family's answer that *"his father would die"* if the boy leaves (44:22); and
  he predicts it outright — *"when he sees that the boy is no more, that he will die"* (44:31).
  **[[Joseph's Steward]] converts the first into slavery (44:10) and [[Joseph]]'s disclosure cancels
  the rest.** The chapter's stakes are entirely mortal and its body count is zero

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:5, 7, 26–28; **five statements about being alive in a
  chapter whose premise is a man everyone believed dead.** ***Ləmiḥyâ***, *to preserve life* (45:5);
  ***ûləhaḥăyôt***, *to save you alive* (45:7); *"Joseph is still alive"* twice (45:26, 28); and
  ***wattəḥî rúḥ yaʿăqōb***, *"the spirit of Jacob… revived"* (45:27). **The chapter also contains the
  book's most precise description of a body giving out** — ***wayyāpag libbô***, *his heart fainted*,
  from a rare verb for going slack — **and its recovery is caused by the sight of a cart.** See
  [[The Spirit of Jacob Revived - Ruach Returning]]
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:4, 12, 30; 47:9; **four deaths handled in a pericope where
  nobody dies.** ***"Joseph will close your eyes"*** (46:4) — **the only tender sentence God speaks in
  the cycle**, and a promise about a bedside. **[[Er]] and [[Onan]] are named in the register in order
  to be subtracted from it** (46:12). ***"Now let me die, since I have seen your face"*** (46:30), and
  ***"few and evil have been the days of the years of my life"*** (47:9) — **a release and a verdict,
  from a man who then lives another seventeen years**
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:15, 19, 29; ***die*** **five times in nineteen verses, and
  four of them are Egyptian.** *"Give us bread, for why should we die in your presence?"* (47:15);
  *"Why should we die before your eyes, both we and our land? … Give us seed, that we may live, and
  not die"* (47:19). **The appeal is not to justice or to mercy but to the vizier's sightline**, and
  it works twice. **The fifth is [[Jacob]]'s** — *"The time came near that Israel must die"* (47:29),
  **the narrator's sentence, not his** — **and where a country begs not to die and sells itself, the
  patriarch takes it as settled and arranges the freight.** **A nation buys life with its liberty in
  the same paragraph in which an old man spends his last strength on a grave**
- [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]] — Genesis 49:33; **a man speaks for thirty-two verses and dies in the
  thirty-third, with no narration in between.** ***"He gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded
  up the spirit, and was gathered to his people"*** — **three verbs, and the first is physical.** **He
  has been sitting on the edge of that bed since 48:2**, and **the last thing he does is lie down.**
  ***Wayyigwaʿ wayyēʾāsep*** **is the formula used of [[Abraham]]** (25:8) **and [[Isaac]]** (35:29),
  **said here of a man about to be buried four hundred miles from all of them.** **The chapter records
  no reaction from anyone**: nobody weeps and nobody speaks, and the mourning waits for 50:1. See
  [[Gathered to His People]]
- **Genesis 50:20 — *ləhaḥăyōt ʿam-rāb*, *to save many people alive***, said over a coffin; the same
  hiphil the Hebrew midwives get at Exodus 1:17–18 — [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]]

- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26; **the Bible's most attractive description of death, spoken by
  a living man who is not asking to die.** The distinction is exact and usually lost. Job asks that
  he had never been born (3:3–10), that he had died at birth (3:11–19), and — of other people, in
  the third person — why those who *"long for death"* are kept alive (3:20–22). **He never asks God
  to kill him and never contemplates killing himself.** What 3:13–19 commends is not extinction but
  **rest and the end of rank**: *"There the wicked cease from troubling. There the weary are at
  rest… The small and the great are there. The servant is free from his master."* And the word
  *light* does duty for *life* twice (3:20, 3:23), so that the complaint is framed as one about an
  unwanted gift rather than an unbearable state
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:8–9 and 7:15–16, **the death wish that chapter 3 did not
  contain, now stated and addressed.** *"That it would please God to crush me"*; *"my soul chooses
  strangling, death rather than my bones. I loathe my life. I don't want to live forever."* That
  last phrase is the tree of life's (*ləʿōlām*, Genesis 3:22). And 7:9–10 states Sheol as a one-way
  road — *"he who goes down to Sheol shall come up no more"* — the floor from which 14:14 and 19:25
  will rise. Job still never speaks of taking his own life; he asks God to take it
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 10:18–22; the death wish of chapter 3 put to God as a question, and the land of the
  dead described as the one place left uncreated — *"without any order, where the light is as
  midnight."*
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:17, 20; *ḥeled*, *lifespan*, promised *"clearer than the noonday"* against
  10:20's *"Aren't my days few?"*; and the wicked whose only hope is *mappaḥ-nāpeš*, *"the giving up
  of the spirit"*
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 14:1–14; ***"Man, who is born of a woman, is of few days, and full of
  trouble"***, the flower cut down and the fleeing shadow (14:1–2); the tree that revives and the man
  who *"lies down and doesn't rise"* (14:7–12); and ***"If a man dies, shall he live again?"***
  (14:14), the resurrection question asked in so many words, expecting *no* and then imagining *yes*
  as a wish. Psalm 90:5–6, Psalm 103:15–16 and Isaiah 40:6–8 have the same flower. See
  [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]]
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:13, 23–26, 32–33; **two deaths, and death the only leveller.**
  The wicked *"go down to Sheol"* *"in an instant"* (21:13) after a long life; one man *"dies in his
  full strength, being wholly at ease"* — *bəʿeṣem tummô*, *in the bone of his completeness* — and
  another *"in bitterness of soul, and never tastes of good"*, and *"They lie down alike in the dust.
  The worm covers them"* (21:23–26). The leveller of Ecclesiastes 9:2–3, without Ecclesiastes'
  shrug: for Job, a death that ignores the difference between men is the proof that nothing before it
  marked the difference either.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 24:19–20, 24; **the forgetting womb and the common
  harvest.** *"The womb shall forget him. The worm shall feed sweetly on him. He shall be no more
  remembered"* — Sheol as the swallower of the wicked, in verses the wiki reads as the friends' view
  cited or cursed — and then Job's own: the mighty *"are taken out of the way as all others, and are
  cut off as the tops of the ears of grain"*. Death as the harvest that takes everyone, as at
  21:23–26.
- [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]] — Job 26:5–6; 27:3, 5; the shades (*rəpāʾîm*) trembling
  beneath the waters and Sheol naked before God; *"the length of my life is still in me, and the spirit
  of God is in my nostrils"* — life as God's breath, Genesis 2:7 — and *"Until I die"* as the term of
  the oath
- [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] — Job 28:13, 22; wisdom *"not found in the land of the living"* — *ʾereṣ haḥayyîm* —
  and the dead no better placed: *"Destruction and Death say, 'We have heard a rumor of it with our
  ears.'"* **Neither side of the boundary holds it**
- [[Now They Mock Me]] — Job 30:19, 23; ***"I have become like dust and ashes"*** — *ʿāpār wāʾēper*,
  Abraham's pair (Genesis 18:27) and the words of 42:6 — and ***"you will bring me to death, To the
  house appointed for all living"*** — *bêt môʿēd ləkol-ḥāy*, death as the one appointment every living
  thing keeps, said by a man who has asked for twenty chapters for a different appointment, with God.
  See [[Sitting in Dust and Ashes]].
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 33:4, 18–30; ***"The Spirit of God has made me, and the breath of the Almighty gives me
  life"*** (33:4), Genesis 2:7 in the first person; and *šaḥat*, *the pit*, five times (33:18, 22, 24,
  28, 30) — the death a warning, a mediator, and a ransom keep a man from, *"that he may be enlightened
  with the light of the living"* (33:30).
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:14–15; ***"If he gathered to himself his spirit and his breath, all
  flesh would perish together, and man would turn again to dust."*** Genesis 2:7 and 3:19 joined in one
  conditional, and the ground of Elihu's best argument: a God who keeps every creature breathing cannot be
  indifferent to the world. Psalm 104:29 says the same of all creatures, and Ecclesiastes 12:7 of the
  spirit's return.
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:6, 12, 14, 20; God *"doesn't preserve the life of the wicked"*
  (36:6); the deaf *"die without knowledge"* (36:12); the godless *"die in youth"* (36:14); and *"Don't desire
  the night, when people are cut off in their place"* (36:20) — Job's longing for death in chapter 3,
  forbidden.
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:17, 39–41; 39:1–4, 30; **death inside the order God describes.**
  *"Have the gates of death been revealed to you?"* (38:17) to a man who has wished himself dead since
  chapter 3; births no one watches (39:1–3); prey for the lioness and the raven; and the speech's last
  line, *"Where the slain are, there he is"* (39:30). See
  [[Where the Slain Are - Job 39.30 and Matthew 24.28]].
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:16–17; **a patriarch's death.** A hundred and forty years after the restoration — twice
  the seventy of Psalm 90:10 — and four generations seen; *"So Job died, being old and full of days"*, Isaac's
  formula (Genesis 35:29). The man who wished he had died at birth (3:11) dies full. See
  [[Buried in a Good Old Age - The Promise at Genesis 15.15 and Its Fulfilment]].
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:16–22; ***ḥāyâ* runs the scene.** Pharaoh: *"if it is a daughter, then she
  shall live"*; the midwives *"saved the baby boys alive"* (Joseph's verb, Genesis 50:20); their
  excuse, that Hebrew women are *ḥāyôt*, *vigorous*. Then the river decree (1:22)
- [[The Birth of Moses]] — Exodus 2:1–10; **the son put into the river of death and drawn out of
  it.** The mother obeys 1:22 to the letter — the river — but in a sealed *tēbâ*, an ark; the
  princess *spares* (*ḥāmal*) the child her father condemned; and his name says *the one who draws
  out*
- [[Moses Returns to Egypt]] — Exodus 4:19, 23–24; ***bāqaš*, to seek a life, three times.** Pharaoh sought to kill
  Moses (2:15); *"all the men who sought your life are dead"* (4:19); and five verses later *"Yahweh …
  sought to kill him"* (4:24, WEB *"wanted"*). Between them, *"I will kill your son, your firstborn"*
- [[The Heads of the Fathers' Houses]] — Exodus 6:16, 18, 20; **the Genesis formula *šənê ḥayyê*, *"the years of the
  life of"*, used three times and nowhere else in Exodus**: Levi 137, Kohath 133, Amram 137 — the
  generations that lived and died in Egypt between the descent and the exodus
- [[Water Turned to Blood]] — Exodus 7:18–24; **the river of life made a river of death.** The fish
  die, the water stinks, and a country fed by one river digs in the mud around it for something to
  drink — the river Pharaoh made the grave of Hebrew sons (1:22)
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:17, 28; **"this death."** Pharaoh calls the locusts *hammāwet hazzeh*,
  *"this death"* — the plague named by what it means for a country that lives on its crops. And he
  threatens Moses in the form of the garden warning: *"in the day you see my face you shall die"*
  (10:28; compare *"in the day that you eat of it, you will surely die"*, Genesis 2:17)
- [[The Last Plague Announced]] — Exodus 11:4–6; ***"all the firstborn in the land of Egypt shall
  die."*** The death Pharaoh threatened Moses with (10:28) is announced against Pharaoh's own heir,
  with the hour — *"About midnight"* — and the cry that will follow
- [[The Crossing of the Sea]] — Exodus 14:11–12, 22, 30; **dry ground that kills and dry ground that saves.**
  The people fear *"to die in the wilderness"*; the only other Torah *ḥārābâ*, *dry ground*, is the flood's
  *"All on the dry land … died"* (Genesis 7:22), and here it is where Israel walks through alive (14:21;
  WLC); and Israel sees *"the Egyptians dead (mēt) on the seashore"*, on the *śəpat hayyām*, the phrase of the
  promise of seed *"as the sand which is on the seashore"* (Genesis 22:17; WLC)
- [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] — Exodus 19:12–13, 21–24; death sanctions and preventive warnings

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:12–13, 18–21; life promised, murder forbidden, and a
  frightened assembly left alive

- [[Laws of Violence and Injury]] — Exodus 21:12–36; distinctions and remedies for injury and loss
- [[Laws of Property and Restitution]] — Exodus 22:2–3; the thief killed at night and after sunrise
- [[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]] — Exodus 30:12, 20–21, 33, 38; no plague at the census, washing that they not die, and being cut off.
- [[If You Seek Her as Silver]] — Proverbs 2:18–19; the strange woman's house sinking to death and the shades; *the paths of life*
- [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] — Proverbs 6:23, 26, 32; the way of life, and the precious life
  hunted and destroyed
- [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] — Proverbs 7:2, 23, 27; *"Keep my commandments and live!"*, and the rooms of
  death
- [[Wisdom at the Creation]] — Proverbs 8:35–36; *"whoever finds me, finds life"*; *"All those who hate me love death"*
- [[The Two Feasts]] — Proverbs 9:6, 11, 18; *"Leave your simple ways, and live"*, days multiplied, and Folly's
  guests *"in the depths of Sheol"*: the prologue ends on the two tables as life and death
- [[Do Not Envy Sinners]] — Proverbs 23:13–14, 22; The rod that does not kill and saves from Sheol; the father who gave life.
- [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]] — Proverbs 24:11; Those led away to death, to be rescued.
