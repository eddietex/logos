# Integrity

## Definition / overview

**Being all of one piece — the same thing on every side, and the same thing whether or not anyone
is looking.** The Hebrew root is ***t-m-m***, whose plain sense is *complete, whole, finished,
entire*, and which is used of a full day, an uncut stone, and a sum that has been paid out. It is a
word about **wholeness before it is a word about morality**, and every ethical use in Scripture
carries that literal sense inside it.

Four forms of the root matter, and the wiki keeps them distinct:

| Form | Sense | First and defining uses |
|---|---|---|
| ***tāmîm*** | complete, unblemished, sound | Noah at Genesis 6:9; Abram commanded at 17:1; sacrificial animals *without blemish* |
| ***tām*** | whole, of one piece | Job at 1:1, 1:8, 2:3; **Jacob at Genesis 25:27**, where WEB renders it *quiet* |
| ***tōm*** | uprightness, guilelessness | Abimelech's *"integrity of my heart"* at Genesis 20:5–6 |
| ***tummâ*** | integrity held onto | **Job 2:3 and 2:9**, God and Job's wife, six verses apart; Job's oath at 27:5 |

**Integrity in Scripture is not sinlessness, and the texts are careful to say so.** Noah is *tāmîm*
in 6:9 and drunk and naked in 9:21, in the same book and with no comment on the contradiction. Job
is *tām* four times and spends twenty-eight chapters accusing God of hunting him, and at 42:7 is the
one commended. **The word describes a man without a seam — no hidden compartment, no second account
of himself — not a man without a fault.** Its opposite is not wickedness but duplicity: the divided
heart of Psalm 12:2, the double weights of Deuteronomy 25:13–15 (which the law calls, exactly,
*a whole and just weight*, *ʾeben šəlēmâ wāṣedeq*).

**And it is a thing one *holds onto*.** *Tummâ* takes the verb *ḥāzaq* in the hiphil — to grasp,
keep a grip on. That grammar is the theme's centre of gravity: **integrity in the book of Job is not
a possession but a hold, and the question the book asks is what it costs to keep hold of it when
letting go is the only relief on offer.**

## Development across Scripture

**Genesis 6:9 — the first *tāmîm* applied to a person.** *"Noah was a righteous man, blameless among
the people of his time."* The verdict is given before Noah does anything, is stated in the same
breath as *ṣaddîq*, righteous, and is immediately qualified by a phrase — *in his generations* —
that has been read for two thousand years as either a superlative or a limitation. See
[[Noah and the Flood]] and [[Blameless Before Anyone Is Watching]].

**Genesis 17:1 — the first *tāmîm* commanded.** *"Walk before me, and be blameless."* Not a
description but an imperative, attached to the covenant and to the sign of it, and addressed to a man
who has just fathered a son by a slave on his wife's advice. **Integrity enters Scripture as a
verdict and is then reissued as a demand.** See [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].

**Genesis 20:5–6 — the only time God confirms a man's *tōm* to his face.** Abimelech, who has taken
Sarah on Abraham's word, protests *"in the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands I
have done this"*, and God answers *"Yes, I know that in the integrity of your heart you have done
this."* **The man whose integrity God personally certifies in Genesis is a Philistine king outside
the covenant**, and the man who deceived him is the patriarch. See [[Abraham and Abimelech]] and
[[Sinning Unwittingly - Integrity, Ignorance, and Guilt]].

**Genesis 25:27 — the wrinkle.** Jacob is *ʾîš tām*, and WEB translates it *"a quiet man, living in
tents."* It is the same adjective as Job 1:1. Whether it means *settled*, *wholesome*, or
*straightforward* is disputed, and it is applied to the man who will buy a birthright off his
starving brother and impersonate him to a blind father. **The wiki records the collision and does
not resolve it**; at minimum it is a warning that *tām* is a narrower word than English *blameless*
suggests. See [[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]].

**Job — the word made into a plot.** The book takes *tām* from the narrator's mouth in the first
verse, has God repeat it twice, and then subjects it to a test designed to find out whether it is
load-bearing or bought. At **2:3** God announces the result — *"He still maintains his integrity"* —
and at **2:9** Job's wife throws the identical phrase back as an objection: *"Do you still maintain
your integrity? Renounce God, and die."* **Six verses apart, in the only two mouths in the book that
use the word, as praise and as reproach.** See [[The Second Test]] and [[Job's Wife]].

**Job then makes it his oath.** *"Far be it from me that I should justify you. Until I die I will
not put away my integrity from me. I hold fast to my righteousness, and will not let it go"*
(27:5–6). This is the position he refuses to trade: the friends offer restoration in exchange for a
confession, and taking it would require him to say something about himself he believes false.
**What he will not surrender is not his innocence but his account of his innocence** — and the
book's verdict at 42:7 is that the refusal was the right speech.

**The Psalms make it a way of walking.** *"Yahweh, who shall dwell in your sanctuary? … He who walks
blamelessly"* (Psalm 15:1–2); *"I will walk within my house with a perfect heart"* (Psalm 101:2);
*"Let integrity and uprightness preserve me"* (Psalm 25:21). **Psalm 26 is the closest thing outside
Job to Job's posture**: *"Examine me, Yahweh, and prove me. Try my heart and my mind… I have walked
in my integrity."*

**Proverbs makes it a road.** *"He who walks in integrity walks surely, but he who perverts his ways
will be found out"* (10:9); *"Better is the poor who walks in his integrity than he who is perverse
in his lips and is a fool"* (19:1, and again at 28:6). **The wisdom tradition's claim is that
integrity is safe**, and Job is the counter-example that tradition had to absorb.

**The law makes it a physical property.** The sacrificial animal must be *tāmîm*, without blemish
(Leviticus 1:3, 22:19–21), and the altar's stones must be uncut (Exodus 20:25). **The same word
governs the beast on the altar and the man before God**, and the New Testament reaches back through
that usage when it calls Christ a lamb *"without blemish and without spot"* (1 Peter 1:19).

**The New Testament keeps the wholeness sense and changes the vocabulary.** *Haplous*, single —
*"if therefore your eye is single, your whole body will be full of light"* (Matthew 6:22); *teleios*,
complete — *"you shall therefore be perfect, as your Father in heaven is perfect"* (Matthew 5:48),
which is the Septuagint's word for *tāmîm* and which means *finished, brought to its end*, not
*flawless*. **James 1:4's *"that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing"* stands at the
end of a sentence about the testing of faith**, which is where Job's *tummâ* started.

## Key passages

- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:1–10; ***tummâ*** twice in six verses, from God and from Job's wife,
  and the test designed to find out which of them is right. **The theme's defining passage**: an
  integrity announced as proven, and then tested again anyway
- [[Job of Uz]] — Job 1:1–5; *tām wəyāšār*, blameless and upright, the narrator's opening verdict
  before a single event
- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — Job 1:6–22; the same verdict from God at 1:8,
  and the accuser's counter-theory that it was purchased
- [[Noah and the Flood]] — Genesis 6:9; the first *tāmîm* said of a person, and the qualifier *in his
  generations*
- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17:1; *"walk before me, and be blameless"*,
  the first time it is commanded
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:5–6; *tom-ləbābî*, and **the only time in Genesis God
  confirms a man's integrity in direct speech** — to a foreign king, about a patriarch's deception
- [[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]] — Genesis 25:27; *ʾîš tām* of Jacob, the same adjective
  as Job 1:1, rendered *quiet* by WEB
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — Job 16:17; *"there is no violence in my hands,
  and my prayer is pure"*, the claim stated in its narrowest and most defensible form
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:11–13; **the word is absent, and the men who will spend the book
  trying to make Job put it down are introduced here doing nothing of the kind.** *Tummâ* is the
  issue at 2:3 and 2:9 and will be Job's oath-word at 27:5; between those points three friends arrive
  with no position on it at all, and their first act is to mourn him. **The theme's antagonists are
  established before they have a doctrine**, which is what makes their eventual demand — confess, and
  be restored — a matter of argument rather than of character
- **Job 27:5–6** — *"until I die I will not put away my integrity from me"*: the oath, and the thing
  the friends' offer would cost
- **Job 31:6** — *"let him weigh me in an even balance, that God may know my integrity"*: the oath of
  innocence, and the honest weights of Deuteronomy 25:13–15 turned on the man himself
- **Psalm 15:1–2; 25:21; 26:1–3, 11; 41:12; 101:2** — integrity as the condition of dwelling with God
  and as a thing one asks to be examined for
- **Proverbs 2:7; 10:9; 11:3; 19:1; 20:7; 28:6** — the wisdom tradition's claim that it is safe
- **Leviticus 1:3; 22:19–21; Exodus 12:5** — *tāmîm* of the animal, the physical sense behind the
  moral one
- **Deuteronomy 18:13; 2 Samuel 22:24; 1 Kings 9:4** — the command reissued to Israel, to David, and
  to Solomon
- **Matthew 5:48; 6:22; James 1:4; 1 Peter 1:19** — *teleios*, *haplous*, and the unblemished lamb
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 4:6, *tōm dərākekā*, *the integrity of your ways*: the prologue's
  word in the mouth of a man who never heard the prologue, and treated as an asset — something that
  should be Job's *hope* — rather than as the thing under test
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:10, *"that I have not denied the words of the Holy One"*:
  **integrity held as consolation, not as hope.** Eliphaz had told Job his integrity should be his
  hope (4:6); Job says it is the one thing he can die holding. In the same speech he swears to the
  friends' faces that he will not lie (6:28) and says *"my cause is righteous"* (6:29) — the
  beginnings of the oath of chapter 27 and the defence of chapter 31
- [[Bildad's First Speech]] — Job 8:6, 20; **the narrator's two words for Job in 1:1, set as
  conditions.** *"If you were pure and upright (yāšār)"*; *"God will not cast away a blameless (tām)
  man."* Bildad offers as an *if* what the reader has been told three times is a fact, and Job
  completes the syllogism with Bildad's own words: *"I am blameless… I despise my life… he destroys
  the blameless and the wicked"* (9:21–22).
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 9:20–22; ***tām-ʾānî*, "I am blameless"** — the first time Job says the word of
  himself, after the narrator (1:1), God (1:8; 2:3), and Bildad's conditional (8:20). He says it
  in the same breath as *"I despise my life"* and *"he destroys the blameless (tām) and the
  wicked."* Integrity held, and declared no protection.
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 12:4; 13:15–16; *tāmîm* (12:4), and ***"Nevertheless, I will maintain
  my ways before him"*** — integrity held on either reading of 13:15, whether Job has no hope or
  hopes in God. 13:16 makes it evidence: *"a godless man shall not come before him"*, so the
  readiness to appear is itself the defence. See
  [[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]]
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:23; **the root of integrity used of a wicked man's body.**
  *"One dies in his full strength"* — *bəʿeṣem tummô*, *in the very bone of his completeness*. *Tōm*
  is the root of *tām* (1:1, 8; 2:3) and of the *tummâ* Job's wife told him to let go (2:9). The
  wicked man dies *whole*, in the physical sense; the man who held on to wholeness in the moral sense
  dies, as Job says of the second man, *"in bitterness of soul"* (21:25).
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:3; ***tattēm dərākêkā***, *"that you make your ways perfect"*
  — the *t-m-m* root in a question: does a man's blamelessness benefit God? Eliphaz assumes no. The
  council of 1:8 and 2:3, where God boasts that Job *"still maintains his integrity"*, is the book's
  answer.

## Related themes

- [[Righteousness]] — the neighbouring word, and the one that sits beside it at Genesis 6:9 and Job
  1:1. **Righteousness is a verdict about conduct; integrity is a verdict about consistency**, and
  the Bible pairs them because neither implies the other
- [[Testing]] — what converts a stated integrity into a demonstrated one, and the only instrument
  that can tell the two apart
- [[The Fear of God]] — its companion in Job 1:1's fourfold description, and the thing the accuser
  attacks in place of attacking the integrity directly
- [[Deception]] — its exact opposite in the Genesis material, and the family trait of the house the
  *ʾîš tām* of 25:27 heads
- [[Guilt and Conscience]] — the internal register integrity is measured against, and Abimelech's
  ground of appeal at Genesis 20:5
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the form Job's integrity finally takes at 27:5 and 31:1–40, where he stakes
  it under curse
- [[Suffering]] — the pressure under which integrity either holds or is revealed to have been a
  purchase
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — where *tāmîm* is a physical requirement, and the origin of the word's
  force

## Related people

- [[Job (person)]] — *tām* four times before he speaks, and the only man in Scripture who is told by
  his own wife to give it up
- [[Job's Wife]] — who uses *tummâ* as an objection, in the only human mouth in the book that uses it
- [[God]] — who certifies it at Job 1:8 and 2:3, commands it at Genesis 17:1, and confirms

- [[Noah]] — *tāmîm bədōrōtāyw*, and the qualifier that has never stopped being argued about
- [[Abraham]] — commanded to be *tāmîm* at 17:1, and the man whose deception makes Abimelech's *tōm*
  necessary
- [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] — the outsider whose integrity God affirms directly
- [[Jacob]] — *ʾîš tām* at 25:27, and the hardest single data point for any tidy definition of the
  word
