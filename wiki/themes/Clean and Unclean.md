# Clean and Unclean

## Definition / overview

A distinction between what may approach the holy and what may not — applied in the Torah to animals,
foods, bodies, discharges, disease, corpses, and objects. It is a **ritual** category rather than a
moral or a hygienic one, and confusing it with either produces most of the bad readings of Leviticus.

The Hebrew pair is **ṭāhôr** (clean, pure) and **ṭāmēʾ** (unclean, defiled). Two things are worth
fixing at the start. Unclean does not mean *dirty*, and it does not mean *sinful*: burying a parent
makes a person unclean (Numbers 19:11–14), and so does childbirth (Leviticus 12), and neither is a
wrong. What uncleanness does is disqualify from the sanctuary until it is dealt with, and most
uncleanness is dealt with by washing and waiting a day. And the categories are not symmetrical with
good and evil — the pig is unclean and the locust is clean, which no moral scheme predicts.

**The distinction enters the Bible at Genesis 7:2**, in [[Noah and the Flood]], and its arrival
there is genuinely odd: *"You shall take seven pairs of every clean animal with you, the male and
his female. Of the animals that are not clean, take two, the male and his female."* No definition is
given. No criterion is offered. No law exists — Sinai is many centuries downstream — and the
narrative simply assumes that Noah knows which animals are which. Genesis 1:31 had said everything
God made was very good, and Genesis 9:3 will shortly hand *"every moving thing that lives"* to
humanity as food without qualification. Yet here, unannounced, the world's animals are already
sorted into two kinds.

Three readings of that, all defensible:

- **Anachronism from the author's world.** The narrator writes for readers who live under Leviticus
  and does not stop to explain a distinction they take for granted. On the source-critical analysis
  this is the J strand, whose sacrificial interests show throughout.
- **A pre-Mosaic distinction, later codified.** Sacrifice exists in Genesis before any law regulates
  it — Abel's offering, Noah's altar — so a received sense of which creatures are fit for an altar
  may well precede the legislation that fixes it. Leviticus 11 would then be codifying rather than
  inventing.
- **Narrative necessity.** The plainest reason is the one the story itself supplies four chapters
  later. At Genesis 8:20 Noah *"took of every clean animal, and of every clean bird, and offered
  burnt offerings on the altar"* — and a species preserved as one breeding pair cannot survive
  having one of them burned. The surplus of 7:2 exists so that 8:20 is possible.

These are not exclusive, and the third is the one the text argues for by itself.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 7:2–3, 8** — the first occurrence, assumed and undefined, with a sevenfold surplus of
  the clean. See [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 8:20** *(not yet ingested)* — what the surplus was for: burnt offerings of *every* clean
  animal and bird, the first altar in Scripture after the flood.
- **Leviticus 11; Deuteronomy 14:3–21** — the distinction codified. Land animals must chew the cud
  *and* have a split hoof; sea creatures need fins and scales; a list of birds is simply given.
  Reasons are not stated, and the attempts to supply them — hygiene, anti-Canaanite polemic, Mary
  Douglas's argument in *Purity and Danger* that the unclean are the creatures that violate their
  own category boundaries — are all reconstructions. Leviticus's own stated ground is different and
  is theological: *"You shall therefore be holy, for I am holy"* (11:45).
- **Leviticus 10:10** — the priest's job description: *"you are to make a distinction between the
  holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean"*. The whole system exists to guard the
  sanctuary.
- **Leviticus 12–15; Numbers 19** — uncleanness from childbirth, skin disease, discharges, and
  corpses, with the remedies. Note that the gravest source of uncleanness in the system is contact
  with **death**, which is what ties the category back to Genesis: the God of the sanctuary is the
  God of the living.
- **1 Samuel 20:26; Hosea 9:3–4** — the distinction as ordinary social fact, and as a description of
  exile: to eat in a foreign land is to eat unclean bread.
- **Isaiah 6:5** — *"I am a man of unclean lips"*: the category turned inward, into a confession of
  sin rather than of ritual state.
- **Isaiah 52:11; Ezekiel 44:23** — purity as the mark of the returning and the restored.
- **Mark 7:1–23** — Jesus on what defiles: *"there is nothing from outside of the man that going
  into him can defile him"*, with Mark's own editorial gloss, *"thus he declared all foods clean"*.
- **Acts 10:9–16, 28; 11:1–18** — Peter's sheet of unclean animals and the command *"Rise, Peter,
  kill and eat"*, and the interpretation Peter himself draws, which is not about food: *"God has
  shown me that I shouldn't call any man unholy or unclean."* The clean/unclean boundary is
  dismantled as the gate to the Gentile mission.
- **Romans 14:14, 20; 1 Corinthians 8; Colossians 2:16–17** — the pastoral aftermath: nothing is
  unclean of itself, and the argument shifts to how the strong should treat the scrupulous.
- **Hebrews 9:13–14** — the ashes of a heifer that sanctify to the cleanness of the flesh, against a
  blood that cleanses the conscience.
- **Revelation 21:27** — *"there will in no way enter into it anything profane"*: the category's last
  appearance, applied to the new Jerusalem, where the sanctuary is the whole city.
