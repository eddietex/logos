# Clean and Unclean

## Definition / overview

A distinction between what may approach the holy and what may not — applied in the Torah to animals,
foods, bodies, discharges, disease, corpses, and objects. It is a **ritual** category rather than a
moral or a hygienic one, and confusing it with either produces most of the bad readings of Leviticus.

The Hebrew pair is **ṭāhôr** (clean, pure) and **ṭāmēʾ** (unclean, defiled). Two things are worth
fixing at the start. Unclean does not mean *dirty*, and it does not mean *sinful*: burying a parent
makes a person unclean (Numbers 19:11–14), and so does childbirth (Leviticus 12), and neither is a
wrong. What uncleanness does is disqualify from the sanctuary until it is dealt with, and most
uncleanness is dealt with by washing and waiting a day. And the categories are not symmetrical with
good and evil — the pig is unclean and the locust is clean, which no moral scheme predicts.

**The distinction enters the Bible at Genesis 7:2**, in [[Noah and the Flood]], and its arrival
there is genuinely odd: *"You shall take seven pairs of every clean animal with you, the male and
his female. Of the animals that are not clean, take two, the male and his female."* No definition is
given. No criterion is offered. No law exists — Sinai is many centuries downstream — and the
narrative simply assumes that Noah knows which animals are which. Genesis 1:31 had said everything
God made was very good, and Genesis 9:3 will shortly hand *"every moving thing that lives"* to
humanity as food without qualification. Yet here, unannounced, the world's animals are already
sorted into two kinds.

Three readings of that, all defensible:

- **Anachronism from the author's world.** The narrator writes for readers who live under Leviticus
  and does not stop to explain a distinction they take for granted. On the source-critical analysis
  this is the J strand, whose sacrificial interests show throughout.
- **A pre-Mosaic distinction, later codified.** Sacrifice exists in Genesis before any law regulates
  it — Abel's offering, Noah's altar — so a received sense of which creatures are fit for an altar
  may well precede the legislation that fixes it. Leviticus 11 would then be codifying rather than
  inventing.
- **Narrative necessity.** The plainest reason is the one the story itself supplies four chapters
  later. At Genesis 8:20 Noah *"took of every clean animal, and of every clean bird, and offered
  burnt offerings on the altar"* — and a species preserved as one breeding pair cannot survive
  having one of them burned. The surplus of 7:2 exists so that 8:20 is possible.

These are not exclusive, and the third is the one the text argues for by itself.

**Readiness for Sinai includes washing and temporary abstinence.** Moses sanctifies the people;
they wash their garments and are told to abstain from sexual relations until the encounter
(19:10–15). This anticipates the concern with preparedness for holy presence, but Exodus 19
does not itself provide the later impurity code or call marital intercourse a sin. Holiness
and ritual readiness should not be collapsed into moral innocence. See [[Israel at Mount Sinai]].

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 7:2–3, 8** — the first occurrence, assumed and undefined, with a sevenfold surplus of
  the clean. See [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 8:20** — what the surplus was for: burnt offerings of *every* clean animal and bird, on
  the first altar in Scripture. The chapter also lets the distinction pick the birds: an unclean
  raven for the errand that does not come back, a clean dove for the one that does. See
  [[The Waters Recede]].
- **Genesis 9:3–4** — and then the distinction is not used. Two chapters after 7:2 sorted the animals
  and one chapter after 8:20 burned the clean ones, God hands over *"every moving thing that lives"* as
  food with no qualification whatever, and attaches exactly one restriction — not a species but a
  substance: *"flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat."* The first dietary law in
  Scripture is about blood, not about clean and unclean, and Leviticus 11 will later restrict for
  Israel what Genesis 9 grants to humanity. Genesis does not reconcile the two. See
  [[The Covenant with Noah]] and [[Blood]].
- **Leviticus 11; Deuteronomy 14:3–21** — the distinction codified. Land animals must chew the cud
  *and* have a split hoof; sea creatures need fins and scales; a list of birds is simply given.
  Reasons are not stated, and the attempts to supply them — hygiene, anti-Canaanite polemic, Mary
  Douglas's argument in *Purity and Danger* that the unclean are the creatures that violate their
  own category boundaries — are all reconstructions. Leviticus's own stated ground is different and
  is theological: *"You shall therefore be holy, for I am holy"* (11:45).
- **Leviticus 10:10** — the priest's job description: *"you are to make a distinction between the
  holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean"*. The whole system exists to guard the
  sanctuary.
- **Leviticus 12–15; Numbers 19** — uncleanness from childbirth, skin disease, discharges, and
  corpses, with the remedies. Note that the gravest source of uncleanness in the system is contact
  with **death**, which is what ties the category back to Genesis: the God of the sanctuary is the
  God of the living.
- **1 Samuel 20:26; Hosea 9:3–4** — the distinction as ordinary social fact, and as a description of
  exile: to eat in a foreign land is to eat unclean bread.
- **Isaiah 6:5** — *"I am a man of unclean lips"*: the category turned inward, into a confession of
  sin rather than of ritual state.
- **Isaiah 52:11; Ezekiel 44:23** — purity as the mark of the returning and the restored.
- **Mark 7:1–23** — Jesus on what defiles: *"there is nothing from outside of the man that going
  into him can defile him"*, with Mark's own editorial gloss, *"thus he declared all foods clean"*.
- **Acts 10:9–16, 28; 11:1–18** — Peter's sheet of unclean animals and the command *"Rise, Peter,
  kill and eat"*, and the interpretation Peter himself draws, which is not about food: *"God has
  shown me that I shouldn't call any man unholy or unclean."* The clean/unclean boundary is
  dismantled as the gate to the Gentile mission.
- **Romans 14:14, 20; 1 Corinthians 8; Colossians 2:16–17** — the pastoral aftermath: nothing is
  unclean of itself, and the argument shifts to how the strong should treat the scrupulous.
- **Hebrews 9:13–14** — the ashes of a heifer that sanctify to the cleanness of the flesh, against a
  blood that cleanses the conscience.
- **Revelation 21:27** — *"there will in no way enter into it anything profane"*: the category's last
  appearance, applied to the new Jerusalem, where the sanctuary is the whole city.

**Exodus 22:31 is the first food rule at Sinai that states its reason.** (The goring bull's meat
*"shall not be eaten"* at 21:28, with no reason given.) Flesh *"torn
by animals in the field"* — *ṭərēpâ*, the noun of Genesis 31:39 — is not eaten, and the reason is
holiness, not uncleanness: *"You shall be holy men to me"*. Later law calls the eater unclean until
evening (Leviticus 17:15) — a difference recorded here and not harmonized. See
[[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

### The goat and its mother’s milk — Exodus 23:1–19

Exodus 23:19 prohibits boiling a young goat in its mother's milk but gives no declaration of
uncleanness or rationale. The same rule appears after first fruits in Exodus 34:26 and beside food
and holiness laws in Deuteronomy 14:21 (verified WEB). These contexts are related without being
identical. Neither an unnamed fertility ceremony nor a general meat-and-dairy ban is stated in the
verses. See [[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]].

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The ordination of the priests — Exodus 29:1–46

The priests are washed with water at the tent's door before they are clothed (29:4). The new altar
is *cleansed* for seven days with a daily sin offering (29:36–37), though the chapter never calls it
unclean or gives a cause. See [[The Ordination of the Priests]].

### The incense altar, the ransom, and the holy oil — Exodus 30:1–38

A brass basin stands between the tent and the altar. Aaron and his sons wash hands and feet there
before entering the tent or approaching the altar to serve, *"that they not die"* (30:19–21). The
ordination washing of 29:4 was once; this washing is repeated at every approach, *"a statute
forever"* (30:21). See [[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Exodus 34:26 repeats the young-goat rule immediately after first fruits, as 23:19 did. It
supplies neither a declaration of uncleanness nor a rationale. Its later placement among food
rules is compared in [[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]]; neither a foreign fertility rite nor a universal
meat-and-dairy ban should be read into this verse as its explicit explanation. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

The basin and base required for hands-and-feet washing in 30:18–21 are made from the women’s mirrors
(38:8). Making a washing vessel is not itself a washing rite. Water placement and use by Moses,
Aaron, and his sons appear in 40:30–32, now ingested in [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. See
[[The Basin Made and Used - Exodus 30, 38, and 40]].

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The tabernacle raised and filled — Exodus 40:1–38

The basin made in 38:8 is installed and filled in 40:30; Moses, Aaron, and the sons wash
hands and feet before entering or approaching the altar (40:31–32). This enacts the use
prescribed in 30:18–21 and explicitly includes Moses. The initial washing commanded in
40:12 belongs to priestly preparation; it should not be collapsed into every subsequent
hand-and-foot washing. No particular moral fault or bodily uncleanness is diagnosed here.

See [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. Source: Exodus 40 checked in `raw/bible/Exodus.md` (WEB), 2026-10-25.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:9, 11, 30

The WEB uses purity and cleansing for heart, conduct, and the removal of evil (9, 11, 30).
These are moral contexts, distinct from ritual eligibility or washing rules; the chapter gives
no sanctuary procedure. Verse 9 questions self-claimed freedom from sin, while verse 11 still
allows conduct to be judged pure and right. No equivalence of Hebrew terms across those
settings is asserted.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Agur’s words — Proverbs 30:12

Being pure in one’s own eyes while unwashed from filth is a moral image of self-deception. This is
distinct from the ritual classifications described in this page’s overview: the saying supplies
neither a purity rite nor a rule about animals, bodily contact, or cultic access.

See [[The Words of Agur]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:1–14 and the comparisons documented there.

- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:7's ***šəḥîn***, the eruption of Leviticus 13:18–23 that a priest
  examines and pronounces clean or unclean. **The book of Job has no priest, no law and no Israel**,
  so the contamination Job carries is social rather than cultic — nobody can declare him anything.
  He sits outside among the ashes (the Septuagint makes it a dung-heap outside the city), and the
  three men who arrive at 2:12 *"didn't recognize him"*. See
  [[The Boil of Deuteronomy 28 on a Blameless Man]]
- [[The Waters Recede]] — the distinction load-bearing at last: what 7:2's surplus was for, and
  which bird gets sent where
- [[The Covenant with Noah]] — the distinction conspicuously *not* invoked: every moving thing granted
  as food, with blood as the only restriction
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:2; **the Sinai preparation performed
  four centuries early by a householder.** *"Put away the foreign gods that are among you, ***purify
  yourselves***, change your garments"* — ***hiṭṭahărû***, the *ṭ-h-r* root that Leviticus will build
  its whole vocabulary on, **here in its first imperative use in Scripture** — and the pairing with
  changed garments is exactly Exodus 19:10, 14, where Israel is consecrated before the mountain.
  **There is no priest, no sanctuary, and no law**, and Genesis does not say what the purification
  consisted of
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:32; ***tôʿēbâ*, the Bible's first *abomination*, and it belongs to
  the Egyptians.** *"The Egyptians don't eat bread with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the
  Egyptians"* — **not a divine prohibition but a national distaste**, reported by the narrator without
  judgment. The word that will govern Israel's own table, diet and sexual law enters Scripture
  describing somebody else's dining custom, and **the separateness it produces is what keeps the family
  a family in Egypt.** See
  [[An Abomination to the Egyptians - Separate Tables in Genesis and Exodus]]
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:4, 15; *zak* and *bar*, ***"My doctrine is pure. I am clean in your eyes"*** —
  **a claim Job never made**, built from Bildad's *pure* (8:6) — and *mûm*, the blemish that
  disqualifies a sacrifice (Leviticus 22:20–21), as what Job's face will be without once he
  repents
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 14:4; ***"Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one."***
  **The friends' anthropology (4:17–19) granted, and turned into a reason for God to look away**
  (14:6). The Septuagint expands it — *"not even if his life on earth be one day"* — and in that form
  it became a patristic text on inherited sin. See [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]
- [[Bildad's Third Speech]] — Job 25:4–5; *zākâ* and *zakkû*, *be clean* and *are not pure*,
  **purity applied to the moon and stars** — which no law of purity covers — so that it can then be
  denied to *"he who is born of a woman"*. The third and last of the friends' purity a-fortioris
  (4:17–19; 15:14–16). See [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 4:6–7; **a hand made *leprous, as white as snow*, and restored within a verse.**
  *Ṣāraʿat*, the skin disease Leviticus 13–14 treats as uncleanness; *as white as snow* is the formula
  of Miriam's punishment (Numbers 12:10) and Gehazi's (2 Kings 5:27). **Moses' is the only such hand in
  Scripture with no fault attached** — a sign that the God who sends him makes clean and unclean at
  will
- [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]] — Exodus 9:9–11; ***šəḥîn***, *"a boil breaking out with boils"*, on
  man and animal. **The same word is the boil the priest examines in Leviticus 13:18–23**, where a
  healed *šəḥîn* can turn into *ṣāraʿat* (WLC), and the *"painful sores"* of Job 2:7. Exodus does not
  call the boils unclean; the link is the word —
  [[The Boil of Deuteronomy 28 on a Blameless Man]]

- [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] — Exodus 22:31; torn meat refused for holiness' sake
