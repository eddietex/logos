# Clean and Unclean

## Definition / overview

A distinction between what may approach the holy and what may not — applied in the Torah to animals,
foods, bodies, discharges, disease, corpses, and objects. Ritual impurity need not imply wrongdoing or poor hygiene. Leviticus 18 also applies
defilement to prohibited conduct and the land; the distinction must be made by context.

The Hebrew pair is **ṭāhôr** (clean, pure) and **ṭāmēʾ** (unclean, defiled). Two things are worth
fixing at the start. Unclean does not mean *dirty*, and it does not mean *sinful*: burying a parent
makes a person unclean (Numbers 19:11–14), and so does childbirth (Leviticus 12), and neither is a
wrong. What uncleanness does is disqualify from the sanctuary until it is dealt with, and most
uncleanness is dealt with by washing and waiting a day. And the categories are not symmetrical with
good and evil — the pig is unclean and the locust is clean, which no moral scheme predicts.

**The distinction enters the Bible at Genesis 7:2**, in [[Noah and the Flood]], and its arrival
there is genuinely odd: *"You shall take seven pairs of every clean animal with you, the male and
his female. Of the animals that are not clean, take two, the male and his female."* No definition is
given. No criterion is offered. No law exists — Sinai is many centuries downstream — and the
narrative simply assumes that Noah knows which animals are which. Genesis 1:31 had said everything
God made was very good, and Genesis 9:3 will shortly hand *"every moving thing that lives"* to
humanity as food without qualification. Yet here, unannounced, the world's animals are already
sorted into two kinds.

Three readings of that, all defensible:

- **Anachronism from the author's world.** The narrator writes for readers who live under Leviticus
  and does not stop to explain a distinction they take for granted. On the source-critical analysis
  this is the J strand, whose sacrificial interests show throughout.
- **A pre-Mosaic distinction, later codified.** Sacrifice exists in Genesis before any law regulates
  it — Abel's offering, Noah's altar — so a received sense of which creatures are fit for an altar
  may well precede the legislation that fixes it. Leviticus 11 would then be codifying rather than
  inventing.
- **Narrative necessity.** The plainest reason is the one the story itself supplies four chapters
  later. At Genesis 8:20 Noah *"took of every clean animal, and of every clean bird, and offered
  burnt offerings on the altar"* — and a species preserved as one breeding pair cannot survive
  having one of them burned. The surplus of 7:2 exists so that 8:20 is possible.

These are not exclusive, and the third is the one the text argues for by itself.

**Readiness for Sinai includes washing and temporary abstinence.** Moses sanctifies the people;
they wash their garments and are told to abstain from sexual relations until the encounter
(19:10–15). This anticipates the concern with preparedness for holy presence, but Exodus 19
does not itself provide the later impurity code or call marital intercourse a sin. Holiness
and ritual readiness should not be collapsed into moral innocence. See [[Israel at Mount Sinai]].

**Qualification of the overview:** ritual uncleanness and moral wrongdoing are distinct,
but the opening distinction is too absolute if read as excluding any liability for
unclean contact. Leviticus 5:2–6 explicitly joins these particular contact cases to guilt
and confession. The text does not explain them by an unstated sanctuary violation. See
[[Confession and Offerings According to Means]].

## Development across Scripture

### Forbidden unions and the land — Leviticus 18:1–30

The earlier nonmoral definition needs qualification: Leviticus 12 and 15 describe bodily conditions without necessarily identifying an offense, while 18:20–30 expressly associates defilement with prohibited conduct and the land. Menstruation itself is not identified as sin; sexual approach during it is forbidden (19).

See [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18.

### Sacrifice and blood — Leviticus 17:1–16

Eating an animal that died or was torn requires washing clothes, bathing, and
waiting until evening (15). Failure to wash incurs iniquity (16). This cleansing
procedure does not explicitly repeal Exodus 22:31’s prohibition on torn meat.

See [[The Place of Sacrifice and the Life in the Blood]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 17; comparisons as cited.


### The sanctuary cleansed — Leviticus 16:1–34

After chapters 11–15, chapter 16 cleanses the sanctuary itself *"because of the uncleanness of
the children of Israel"* (16, 19): the Tent of Meeting *"dwells with them in the middle of their
uncleanness"* (16). The people too are cleansed: *"from all your sins you shall be clean"* (30),
joining the purity vocabulary to sin. Those who handle the goat and the carcasses wash before
re-entering the camp (26, 28).

See [[The Day of Atonement]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 16; comparisons as cited.


### Bodily discharges — Leviticus 15:1–33

Discharge, seminal emission, menstruation, and prolonged bleeding have distinct durations
and remedies (2–30). Contact can communicate uncleanness through bodies, beds, seats,
and vessels. Ordinary emission requires washing and waiting until evening; prolonged
discharges involve seven days after cessation and eighth-day offerings. Ritual status
does not itself identify an offense or establish a modern medical diagnosis.

See [[Bodily Discharges and Cleansing]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 15; comparisons as cited.

### Houses in the promised land — Leviticus 14:33–57

A house can be unclean, repaired, condemned, or pronounced clean (37–48). Emptying it
before inspection protects its contents from uncleanness (36). Entry during closure
brings uncleanness until evening; lying down or eating also requires washing clothes
(46–47). Cleanness after repair is followed by the prescribed bird rite (48–53).

See [[Mildew in a House]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 14:33–57; contextual references as cited.

### Cleansing after healing — Leviticus 14:1–32

Healing precedes the priest’s cleansing rite (3). Cleanness and access unfold in stages:
sprinkling and washing permit camp entry, seven days outside the personal tent follow, and
eighth-day offerings complete the prescribed process (7–20). The law supplies neither a cure nor
an accusation that the disease was caused by a personal sin.

See [[The Cleansing of the Leper]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 14:1–32; comparisons as cited.


### Mildew in garments — Leviticus 13:47–59

The distinction includes wool, linen, and skins. A persistent unchanged stain remains
unclean even without spread (55), while an article cleared of the plague is washed again
and becomes clean (58). No offense by its owner is specified. Ritual status is neither
a verdict on personal character nor a modern identification of the material condition.

See [[Mildew in Garments]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 13:47–59; contextual references as noted.

### Examining skin conditions — Leviticus 13:1–46

Visible skin change does not automatically mean uncleanness: dull white spots and ordinary
baldness are clean (38–41). Whole-body whiteness can be clean while raw flesh is unclean
(12–17). The specified signs and their development govern the ruling. Unlike the contact
cases in 5:2–6, these rules name neither guilt nor confession. The distinction between
ritual status and moral fault remains necessary without erasing the guilt clauses elsewhere.

See [[Examining Diseases of the Skin]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 13:1–46.

### Purification after childbirth — Leviticus 12:1–8

Childbirth brings a temporary ritual status: seven days followed by thirty-three for a son,
and fourteen followed by sixty-six for a daughter. Holy things and sanctuary access are
restricted until the purification days end. Unlike the guilt-and-confession cases of 5:1–6,
this law identifies no offense by the mother. The distinction on this page therefore remains
qualified: uncleanness is not automatically wrongdoing, though some contact cases incur guilt.

See [[Purification After Childbirth]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 12.


### Clean and unclean animals — Leviticus 11:1–47

Leviticus 11 now supplies the dietary criteria anticipated earlier on this page, and
distinguishes forbidden species from impurity transmitted by carcasses. Even an edible
animal's carcass defiles (39–40); water sources and dry sowing seed have exceptions
(36–38). **Tension retained:** 5:2–6 attaches guilt, confession, and an offering to its
hidden-contact cases; chapter 11 specifies waiting and, in stated cases, washing, without
prescribing an offering. Neither unit explains the complete relationship. The earlier
claim that the distinction is simply non-moral therefore still requires its qualification.

See [[Clean and Unclean Animals]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 11.

- **Genesis 7:2–3, 8** — the first occurrence, assumed and undefined, with a sevenfold surplus of
  the clean. See [[Noah and the Flood]].
- **Genesis 8:20** — what the surplus was for: burnt offerings of *every* clean animal and bird, on
  the first altar in Scripture. The chapter also lets the distinction pick the birds: an unclean
  raven for the errand that does not come back, a clean dove for the one that does. See
  [[The Waters Recede]].
- **Genesis 9:3–4** — and then the distinction is not used. Two chapters after 7:2 sorted the animals
  and one chapter after 8:20 burned the clean ones, God hands over *"every moving thing that lives"* as
  food with no qualification whatever, and attaches exactly one restriction — not a species but a
  substance: *"flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat."* The first dietary law in
  Scripture is about blood, not about clean and unclean, and Leviticus 11 will later restrict for
  Israel what Genesis 9 grants to humanity. Genesis does not reconcile the two. See
  [[The Covenant with Noah]] and [[Blood]].
- **Leviticus 11; Deuteronomy 14:3–21** — the distinction codified. Land animals must chew the cud
  *and* have a split hoof; sea creatures need fins and scales; a list of birds is simply given.
  Reasons are not stated, and the attempts to supply them — hygiene, anti-Canaanite polemic, Mary
  Douglas's argument in *Purity and Danger* that the unclean are the creatures that violate their
  own category boundaries — are all reconstructions. Leviticus's own stated ground is different and
  is theological: *"You shall therefore be holy, for I am holy"* (11:45).
- **Leviticus 10:10** — the priest's job description: *"you are to make a distinction between the
  holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean"*. The whole system exists to guard the
  sanctuary.
- **Leviticus 12–15; Numbers 19** — uncleanness from childbirth, skin disease, discharges, and
  corpses, with the remedies. Note that the gravest source of uncleanness in the system is contact
  with **death**, which is what ties the category back to Genesis: the God of the sanctuary is the
  God of the living.
- **1 Samuel 20:26; Hosea 9:3–4** — the distinction as ordinary social fact, and as a description of
  exile: to eat in a foreign land is to eat unclean bread.
- **Isaiah 6:5** — *"I am a man of unclean lips"*: the category turned inward, into a confession of
  sin rather than of ritual state.
- **Isaiah 52:11; Ezekiel 44:23** — purity as the mark of the returning and the restored.
- **Mark 7:1–23** — Jesus on what defiles: *"there is nothing from outside of the man that going
  into him can defile him"*, with Mark's own editorial gloss, *"thus he declared all foods clean"*.
- **Acts 10:9–16, 28; 11:1–18** — Peter's sheet of unclean animals and the command *"Rise, Peter,
  kill and eat"*, and the interpretation Peter himself draws, which is not about food: *"God has
  shown me that I shouldn't call any man unholy or unclean."* The clean/unclean boundary is
  dismantled as the gate to the Gentile mission.
- **Romans 14:14, 20; 1 Corinthians 8; Colossians 2:16–17** — the pastoral aftermath: nothing is
  unclean of itself, and the argument shifts to how the strong should treat the scrupulous.
- **Hebrews 9:13–14** — the ashes of a heifer that sanctify to the cleanness of the flesh, against a
  blood that cleanses the conscience.
- **Revelation 21:27** — *"there will in no way enter into it anything profane"*: the category's last
  appearance, applied to the new Jerusalem, where the sanctuary is the whole city.

**Exodus 22:31 is the first food rule at Sinai that states its reason.** (The goring bull's meat
*"shall not be eaten"* at 21:28, with no reason given.) Flesh *"torn
by animals in the field"* — *ṭərēpâ*, the noun of Genesis 31:39 — is not eaten, and the reason is
holiness, not uncleanness: *"You shall be holy men to me"*. Later law calls the eater unclean until
evening (Leviticus 17:15) — a difference recorded here and not harmonized. See
[[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

### The goat and its mother’s milk — Exodus 23:1–19

Exodus 23:19 prohibits boiling a young goat in its mother's milk but gives no declaration of
uncleanness or rationale. The same rule appears after first fruits in Exodus 34:26 and beside food
and holiness laws in Deuteronomy 14:21 (verified WEB). These contexts are related without being
identical. Neither an unnamed fertility ceremony nor a general meat-and-dairy ban is stated in the
verses. See [[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]].

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The ordination of the priests — Exodus 29:1–46

The priests are washed with water at the tent's door before they are clothed (29:4). The new altar
is *cleansed* for seven days with a daily sin offering (29:36–37), though the chapter never calls it
unclean or gives a cause. See [[The Ordination of the Priests]].

### The incense altar, the ransom, and the holy oil — Exodus 30:1–38

A brass basin stands between the tent and the altar. Aaron and his sons wash hands and feet there
before entering the tent or approaching the altar to serve, *"that they not die"* (30:19–21). The
ordination washing of 29:4 was once; this washing is repeated at every approach, *"a statute
forever"* (30:21). See [[The Incense Altar, the Ransom, and the Holy Oil]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Exodus 34:26 repeats the young-goat rule immediately after first fruits, as 23:19 did. It
supplies neither a declaration of uncleanness nor a rationale. Its later placement among food
rules is compared in [[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]]; neither a foreign fertility rite nor a universal
meat-and-dairy ban should be read into this verse as its explicit explanation. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

The basin and base required for hands-and-feet washing in 30:18–21 are made from the women’s mirrors
(38:8). Making a washing vessel is not itself a washing rite. Water placement and use by Moses,
Aaron, and his sons appear in 40:30–32, now ingested in [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. See
[[The Basin Made and Used - Exodus 30, 38, and 40]].

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The tabernacle raised and filled — Exodus 40:1–38

The basin made in 38:8 is installed and filled in 40:30; Moses, Aaron, and the sons wash
hands and feet before entering or approaching the altar (40:31–32). This enacts the use
prescribed in 30:18–21 and explicitly includes Moses. The initial washing commanded in
40:12 belongs to priestly preparation; it should not be collapsed into every subsequent
hand-and-foot washing. No particular moral fault or bodily uncleanness is diagnosed here.

See [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. Source: Exodus 40 checked in `raw/bible/Exodus.md` (WEB), 2026-10-25.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:9, 11, 30

The WEB uses purity and cleansing for heart, conduct, and the removal of evil (9, 11, 30).
These are moral contexts, distinct from ritual eligibility or washing rules; the chapter gives
no sanctuary procedure. Verse 9 questions self-claimed freedom from sin, while verse 11 still
allows conduct to be judged pure and right. No equivalence of Hebrew terms across those
settings is asserted.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Agur’s words — Proverbs 30:12

Being pure in one’s own eyes while unwashed from filth is a moral image of self-deception. This is
distinct from the ritual classifications described in this page’s overview: the saying supplies
neither a purity rite nor a rule about animals, bodily contact, or cultic access.

See [[The Words of Agur]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:1–14 and the comparisons documented there.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

Clean and unclean appear among the pairs sharing one event (2), next to those who offer
sacrifice and those who do not. The observation denies exemption from death; it neither
abolishes purity rules nor equates uncleanness with wickedness. No purification rite or
cause of uncleanness is specified.

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The burnt offering — Leviticus 1:1–17

Water washes the livestock victim’s innards and legs (1:9, 13), and the bird’s crop with its filth
is discarded beside the altar (16). These are prescribed preparations, not a declaration that the
offerer is ritually unclean. This chapter does not introduce the later classifications of personal
impurity.

See [[The Burnt Offering]] . Source: stored WEB Leviticus 1; comparisons identified on the passage
page.

### Priest and congregation — Leviticus 4:1–21

The carcass is burned outside the camp at a clean place where ashes are deposited (12). Outside
is not a synonym for unclean, and the offering’s relation to sin does not make the prescribed
disposal site unclean. This unit does not give a general impurity classification for carcasses.

See [[The Sin Offering for Priest and Congregation]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 4:1–21;
contextual comparisons documented on the passage page.

### Confession and means — Leviticus 5:1–13

The ritual/moral distinction needs qualification: these particular hidden-contact cases incur guilt and require confession and a sin offering (2–6). This does not make every unclean condition a moral offense.

See [[Confession and Offerings According to Means]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:1–13.

### Altar fire and priestly portions — Leviticus 6:8–23

Ashes go outside the camp to a clean place after the priest changes garments (10–11). Outside the camp is therefore not a synonym for unclean; no cleansing rite for the ashes is prescribed here.

See [[The Altar Fire and the Priests’ Meal Offerings]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6:8–23; comparisons documented there.

### Holy flesh and priestly portions — Leviticus 6:24–7:10

Garments stained with sin-offering blood are washed in a holy place; earthen cooking vessels are
broken and bronze ones scoured and rinsed (6:27–28). These are handling rules for most holy flesh,
not a statement that the flesh is unclean or merely a hygiene prescription.

See [[The Priests’ Portions of Sin and Trespass Offerings]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6:24–7:10.

### Thanksgiving and the peace-offering meal — Leviticus 7:11–21

Meat contaminated by contact must burn, and an unclean person must not eat otherwise
edible peace-offering meat (7:19–21). This reinforces the explicit qualification of
the overview: ritual state and moral wrongdoing are distinct, but eating while
unclean is a sanctioned act. The passage does not supply a cleansing remedy or
specify how the cutting-off sanction is executed.

See [[Thanksgiving, Vows, and the Peace Offering]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 6–7.

### Fat, blood, and priestly shares — Leviticus 7:22–38

Fat from an animal that dies of itself or is torn may be used for other service but may not be
eaten (24). This limited permission neither pronounces the carcass clean nor removes impurity
from contact; the unit provides no cleansing rite.

See [[Fat, Blood, and the Priests’ Share]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 7:22–38.

### Aaron and his sons consecrated — Leviticus 8:1–36

Washing precedes clothing (6), and blood purifies the altar (15). No prior personal
contamination or wrongdoing is specified. Purification and consecration overlap in the
rite without making ritual preparation identical to moral acquittal.

See [[Aaron and His Sons Consecrated]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 8; Exodus 28:1; 29; Leviticus 7:38.

### Strange fire and the surviving priests — Leviticus 10:1–11

Priestly instruction explicitly requires distinguishing unclean from clean alongside holy from common (10). The text does not label the brothers’ deaths a consequence of a named impurity or supply a new corpse-purification rite.

See [[Nadab and Abihu and the Strange Fire]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 10:1–11.

### A clean place — Leviticus 10:12–20

The breast and thigh are eaten "in a clean place" (14), a requirement distinct from the holy place for most holy food (12–13). Aaron's reluctance to eat the sin offering is not explained as impurity; the text gives no reason beyond what had happened that day.

See [[The Uneaten Sin Offering]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 10:12–20.
