# Vengeance

## Definition / overview

### The blasphemer and one law — Leviticus 24:10–23

Matching injury is prescribed as a legal response (19–20), not a report of private
revenge. Alongside Leviticus 19:18’s prohibition of vengeance, the texts distinguish
communal judgment from a personal grudge without denying either instruction.

See [[The Blasphemer and One Law for All]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 24:10–23.

Harm returned for harm — who may return it, on what scale, and on whose authority. The Bible's
first two statements on the subject stand three verses apart in the same chapter, and the second is
a corruption of the first.

**The protection (4:15).** Cain, sentenced for murder, says he expects to be killed himself.
Yahweh's answer is not a denial of the danger but a guarantee against it: *"Therefore whoever slays
Cain, vengeance will be taken on him sevenfold."* Two features set the theme's terms. The vengeance
is **God's**, stated in his own mouth, not licensed to any human agent. And its function is to
**stop** a killing rather than to require one — the sevenfold threat exists so that nobody strikes
Cain at all, and it comes with a sign to make sure of it. The Bible's first mention of retribution
is a murderer being kept alive.

**The boast (4:23–24).** Five generations later Lamech puts the same number in his own mouth and
inflates it: *"I have slain a man for wounding me, a young man for bruising me. If Cain will be
avenged seven times, truly Lamech seventy-seven times."* Everything has moved. The vengeance is now
a man's to declare. Its function is no longer protection but deterrence-by-threat. It is claimed in
advance rather than granted after the fact. And the proportion has collapsed entirely: Cain's
sevenfold answered a *murder*, while Lamech's seventy-sevenfold answers a *wound*, and he has
already killed over it. That inflation, sung in careful verse to two wives, is the last thing said
in the line of Cain, and it is Genesis's way of showing what has happened to the world between 4:15
and 4:24 without saying a word about it.

Everything the Torah later legislates on the subject reads as a response to Lamech. *"Eye for eye,
tooth for tooth"* (Exodus 21:23–25) can be read as a restraint on escalating revenge: a
**ceiling** — one eye, not a family, not seventy-seven. Its legal application requires the
distinctions recorded below at Exodus 21:12–36. The cities of refuge (Numbers 35:9–34) take
the avenger of blood, an institution the culture already had, and put a trial between him and the
killer. Both are Lamech's arithmetic being capped.

Then the claim is withdrawn from human hands altogether — *"Vengeance is mine, and recompense"*
(Deuteronomy 32:35), quoted by Paul at Romans 12:19 as the reason a Christian does not retaliate —
and finally the number itself is turned over. Peter proposes forgiving seven times; Jesus answers
with the Septuagint's exact phrase for Lamech's figure (Matthew 18:22). The Bible's high-water mark
for revenge becomes its measure for pardon. See
[[Seventy-Sevenfold - Lamech's Boast and Jesus on Forgiveness]].

## Development across Scripture

### Holiness and the neighbor — Leviticus 19:1–18

Taking vengeance and bearing a grudge against one’s people are prohibited, and love of the
neighbor is commanded (18). Private retaliation is rejected; the same passage still requires
righteous judgment (15).

See [[Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19:1–18.


- **Genesis 4:15** — sevenfold vengeance promised by God on anyone who kills Cain; a protection, not
  a permission.
- **Genesis 4:23–24** — Lamech's Song of the Sword: a man claiming the number for himself and
  raising it eleven-fold, over a wound.
- **Genesis 9:5–6** — after the flood, the first rule: *"Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will be
  shed by man, for God made man in his own image."* Retribution is finally regulated rather than
  boasted, and the striking thing is the arithmetic: Cain's sevenfold and Lamech's seventy-sevenfold
  are both cancelled by **one**. The rule is exactly proportionate, and it is *lex talionis* four books
  before Exodus states it. Two other changes. It is **universal** — given to Noah before any nation
  exists, addressed to everyone — and it **delegates**: 9:5 still has God saying *"I will surely
  require"* three times, and 9:6 says the shedding will be *by man*. What was God's own act in Genesis
  4 becomes a human responsibility here, and the Torah then spends four chapters supplying the
  procedure Genesis conspicuously lacks. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and
  [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: *lex talionis* as a limit (Exodus 21:23–25, Leviticus
  24:19–20, Deuteronomy 19:21); the *gōʾēl haddām*, the avenger of blood, and the cities of refuge
  (Numbers 35, Deuteronomy 19, Joshua 20); David refusing to strike Saul (1 Samuel 24, 26); the
  imprecatory psalms, which hand the demand to God rather than acting on it (Psalms 58, 94, 137);
  *"Vengeance is mine"* (Deuteronomy 32:35, Romans 12:19, Hebrews 10:30); Jesus on turning the
  cheek and on the seventy-seven (Matthew 5:38–42, 18:21–35); the martyrs' *"How long?"*
  (Revelation 6:10).

### Violence, injury, and liability — Exodus 21:12–36

The matching sequence in 21:23–25 has no escalating multiplier. **The earlier description of this
as a ceiling needs precision:** proportionality supports that reading, but Exodus does not report
how courts historically applied each term or establish that every bodily penalty was commuted to
money. Judges are named in 21:22; payments and emancipation are explicit in adjacent cases.
The passage should not be reduced either to unrestricted private revenge or to a system of cash
payments alone. See [[Eye for Eye - Exodus 21, Leviticus 24, and Matthew 5]].
See [[Laws of Violence and Injury]].

### The day of vengeance — Proverbs 6:34–35

The wronged husband of Proverbs 6 is an avenger: *"For jealousy arouses the fury of the husband. He
won't spare in the day of vengeance"* (6:34, *yôm nāqām*), and he will take no *kōp̄er*, ransom, and
no gifts (6:35). Proverbs neither commends nor condemns his vengeance; it is a consequence the son
is warned of, and the lecture names no court beside it, though the law sets death for adultery
(Leviticus 20:10). The sevenfold of the thief's repayment (6:31) uses Cain's number (Genesis 4:15).
See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] and
[[No Ransom for the Adulterer - Proverbs 6.29-35, Leviticus 20.10, and Deuteronomy 22.22]]. Source:
stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Evil for good — Proverbs 17:13

*"Whoever rewards evil for good, evil shall not depart from his house."* The phrase *rāʿâ taḥat ṭôbâ* is the steward's charge at Genesis 44:4 (Hebrew from Sefaria). The proverb names no avenger; the evil simply stays. See [[Rewarding Evil for Good - A False Charge That Is Exactly True]].

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:22

Verse 22 prohibits promising to pay back evil and tells the wronged person to wait for Yahweh's
salvation. It promises rescue, not explicitly the enemy's destruction. Genesis 50:15–21 offers
a narrative comparison in the refusal to repay the brothers and the provision of food instead.
Job 31:29–30 denies rejoicing at an enemy's ruin or cursing him to death. See [[Wait for Yahweh - Proverbs 20.22 and the Refusal of Revenge]].

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### No gladness at an enemy's fall — Proverbs 24:17–18

*"Don't rejoice when your enemy falls. Don't let your heart be glad when he is overthrown; lest
Yahweh see it, and it displease him, and he turn away his wrath from him."* The ban reaches the
heart. The reason is the text's own: the enemy's fall is Yahweh's business, and gloating is seen. It
sits beside 17:5 (*"He who is glad at calamity shall not be unpunished"*), Job 31:29, and 24:29's ban on
saying *"I will do to him as he has done to me."* See
[[Help for an Enemy - Exodus 23, Proverbs 25, and Matthew 5]].

See [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:3–22.

### As he has done to me — Proverbs 24:29

*"Don't say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me; I will render to the man according to his
work'"* (24:29). The law's retaliation formula (Leviticus 24:19) is forbidden as a private resolve, and
its last clause is what 24:12 says God does. See
[[As He Has Done to Me - Proverbs 24.29 and the Law of Retaliation]].

See [[The Field of the Sluggard]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:23–34.

### The enemy fed — Proverbs 25:21–22

Where 20:22 forbade *"I will pay back evil"* and 24:29 forbade *"I will do to him as he has done
to me"*, 25:21–22 commands the opposite action: food and water for *the one who hates you*. In
Hebrew the reward is *yəšallem*, *Yahweh will repay* — the verb 20:22 forbade to the son. Paul quotes
the couplet after *"Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay, says the Lord"* (Romans 12:19–20). See
[[Help for an Enemy - Exodus 23, Proverbs 25, and Matthew 5]] and
[[Wait for Yahweh - Proverbs 20.22 and the Refusal of Revenge]].

See [[The Glory of Kings]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25.

- [[The Stolen Blessing]] — Genesis 27:41–45, **the fourth fratricide in the book and the only one
  that is planned, dated, and abandoned.** ***Wayyiśṭōm ʿĒśāw ʾet-Yaʿăqōb*** — *Esau bore a grudge
  against Jacob* — from *śāṭam*, cousin to the *śiṭnâ* whose first occurrence in the Bible was a well
  one chapter earlier, and the verb that will close the book: *"It may be that Joseph will hate us"*
  (50:15). **He says it *"in his heart"***, which is where Genesis puts the thought Cain acted on in
  a field (4:8), and he defers it out of filial scruple — *"The days of mourning for my father are at
  hand"* — mistiming it by more than forty years. **He never carries it out**; at 33:4 he runs to
  meet his brother and weeps on his neck. And Rebekah's answer is the theme's own arithmetic: *"Why
  should I be bereaved of you both in one day?"* (27:45) — **a killing costs two sons, the murdered
  one and the one who must answer for the blood**, which is the world of 4:14 and of 9:6. See
  [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]]
- [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] — Genesis 34:25–31, **the theme's largest Genesis case and
  the one the book never totals.** One act against one woman is answered by **every male of a town**,
  its flocks, herds and donkeys, its wealth, and its women and children taken captive (34:25–29).
  **Set against the theme's arithmetic** — Cain's sevenfold as a protection, Lamech's
  seventy-sevenfold as a boast, and Noah's exactly one-for-one at 9:6 — **Genesis 34 states no ratio
  at all**, and the two brothers who act give the only justification in the chapter as a question:
  *"Should he deal with our sister as with a prostitute?"* (34:31). **Nobody answers it.** Three
  further things the wiki holds together without resolving them: the narrator endorses their reading
  of the offence (*folly in Israel*, 34:7) and never endorses their response; **the remedy the Torah
  will later prescribe was on the table** (the bride-price and marriage of Exodus 22:16–17 and
  Deuteronomy 22:28–29, offered at 34:11–12) **and was refused**; and [[Jacob]]'s objection at 34:30
  is about danger, not about proportion, so that **the book's verdict has to wait until 49:5–7 and
  falls on the *anger*, not on the killing's scale**. See
  [[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]] and
  [[Dinah and Tamar - Two Rapes and Two Avenging Brothers]]
