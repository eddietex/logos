# Vengeance

## Definition / overview

Harm returned for harm — who may return it, on what scale, and on whose authority. The Bible's
first two statements on the subject stand three verses apart in the same chapter, and the second is
a corruption of the first.

**The protection (4:15).** Cain, sentenced for murder, says he expects to be killed himself.
Yahweh's answer is not a denial of the danger but a guarantee against it: *"Therefore whoever slays
Cain, vengeance will be taken on him sevenfold."* Two features set the theme's terms. The vengeance
is **God's**, stated in his own mouth, not licensed to any human agent. And its function is to
**stop** a killing rather than to require one — the sevenfold threat exists so that nobody strikes
Cain at all, and it comes with a sign to make sure of it. The Bible's first mention of retribution
is a murderer being kept alive.

**The boast (4:23–24).** Five generations later Lamech puts the same number in his own mouth and
inflates it: *"I have slain a man for wounding me, a young man for bruising me. If Cain will be
avenged seven times, truly Lamech seventy-seven times."* Everything has moved. The vengeance is now
a man's to declare. Its function is no longer protection but deterrence-by-threat. It is claimed in
advance rather than granted after the fact. And the proportion has collapsed entirely: Cain's
sevenfold answered a *murder*, while Lamech's seventy-sevenfold answers a *wound*, and he has
already killed over it. That inflation, sung in careful verse to two wives, is the last thing said
in the line of Cain, and it is Genesis's way of showing what has happened to the world between 4:15
and 4:24 without saying a word about it.

Everything the Torah later legislates on the subject reads as a response to Lamech. *"Eye for eye,
tooth for tooth"* (Exodus 21:23–25) is universally misremembered as a licence for revenge; it is a
**ceiling** — one eye, not a family, not seventy-seven. The cities of refuge (Numbers 35:9–34) take
the avenger of blood, an institution the culture already had, and put a trial between him and the
killer. Both are Lamech's arithmetic being capped.

Then the claim is withdrawn from human hands altogether — *"Vengeance is mine, and recompense"*
(Deuteronomy 32:35), quoted by Paul at Romans 12:19 as the reason a Christian does not retaliate —
and finally the number itself is turned over. Peter proposes forgiving seven times; Jesus answers
with the Septuagint's exact phrase for Lamech's figure (Matthew 18:22). The Bible's high-water mark
for revenge becomes its measure for pardon. See
[[Seventy-Sevenfold - Lamech's Boast and Jesus on Forgiveness]].

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 4:15** — sevenfold vengeance promised by God on anyone who kills Cain; a protection, not
  a permission.
- **Genesis 4:23–24** — Lamech's Song of the Sword: a man claiming the number for himself and
  raising it eleven-fold, over a wound.
- **Genesis 9:5–6** — after the flood, the first rule: *"Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will
  be shed by man, for God made man in his own image."* Set the three numbers side by side and the
  point is unmistakable — sevenfold for Cain (4:15), seventy-sevenfold in Lamech's mouth (4:24),
  and here **one**. The escalation that ran through Cain's line is not denounced; it is stopped by
  being converted into arithmetic. Two further changes. The demand is God's own (*"I will surely
  require"*, *dāraš*, three times in 9:5), so what human beings exact is delegated rather than
  owned. And the ground shifts from protection of a person to the [[Image of God]] in every person,
  which is why the later law forbids ransom for a murderer (Numbers 35:31). In Hebrew 9:6a is six
  words that mirror each other exactly, so the *talion* is performed by the syntax before any law
  states it. See [[The Covenant with Noah]] and [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: *lex talionis* as a limit (Exodus 21:23–25, Leviticus
  24:19–20, Deuteronomy 19:21); the *gōʾēl haddām*, the avenger of blood, and the cities of refuge
  (Numbers 35, Deuteronomy 19, Joshua 20); David refusing to strike Saul (1 Samuel 24, 26); the
  imprecatory psalms, which hand the demand to God rather than acting on it (Psalms 58, 94, 137);
  *"Vengeance is mine"* (Deuteronomy 32:35, Romans 12:19, Hebrews 10:30); Jesus on turning the
  cheek and on the seventy-seven (Matthew 5:38–42, 18:21–35); the martyrs' *"How long?"*
  (Revelation 6:10).
