# Kingship and Empire

## Definition / overview

### The summary of a reign — 1 Samuel 14:47–52

*When Saul had taken the kingdom over Israel*, *lāḵaḏ hamməlûḵâ* (14:47, WLC), the narrator steps back and sums
him up as later books sum up kings: his wars (47–48), his sons and daughters (49), his wife and his commander (50),
his genealogy (51), and his court (52). It is the first such notice for an Israelite king in the books stored so
far. A king now has a household that matters to the state, and a commander, [[Abner]], who is his kinsman. The
notice comes after the sentence on Saul's kingdom (13:13–14) and before chapter 15, and records his military success
in full. David's notice in 2 Samuel 8 has the same frame and adds *justice and righteousness* and a list of officers
(WEB at bible-api.com). See [[Saul's Wars and Household]] and
[[Saul's Wars and David's Wars - 1 Samuel 14.47-52 and 2 Samuel 8]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 13:13–14;
14:47–52. Hebrew WLC.

### The king fights their battle, and the kingdom is renewed — 1 Samuel 11:1–15

The people asked for a king to *go out before us, and fight our battles* (8:20). Saul does: the Spirit comes on
him, he musters Israel, and Jabesh Gilead is rescued (11:6–11). Then Samuel calls the people to Gilgal to *renew
the kingdom*, *nəḥaddēš … hamməlûḵâ* (11:14, WLC), and *they made Saul king before Yahweh* (11:15). The verb
*hiṁlîḵ*, *make king*, last had the lords of Shechem as subject, making Abimelech king (Judges 9:6, WLC); here the
people do it *before Yahweh*. Saul has now been made king three times, by oil (10:1), by lot (10:24), and by
acclaim after victory (11:15), and the text does not relate the three. The king's first act after the battle is
to refuse a killing and credit the rescue to Yahweh (11:13). Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:20; 11:6–15; Judges
9:6; Hebrew WLC, 2026-11-18.

### *Long live the king!* — 1 Samuel 10:17–27

The secret anointing becomes a public choosing. Samuel opens by saying the request is a rejection: *you have today
rejected your God, who himself saves you* (10:19). Then he holds the lot anyway. The first acclamation of a king of
Israel, *yəḥî hammeleḵ* (10:24, WLC), follows Samuel's *Do you see him whom Yahweh has chosen …?*, which uses
Deuteronomy's *whom Yahweh … chooses* (17:15). The *way of the king*, *mišpaṭ hammeleḵ* (8:9, 11), was a warning
of what a king would take. Now there are *the regulations of the kingdom*, *mišpaṭ hammelūḵâ* (10:25, WLC), written
in a book and laid up before Yahweh. The contents are not given. The king's first test is the doubters' question,
*How could this man save us?* (10:27), which asks whether he can do what the people wanted a king for, to *fight our
battles* (8:20). See [[Saul Chosen by Lot at Mizpah]].

Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:9, 11, 20; 10:17–27; Deuteronomy 17:15. Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb),
2026-11-17.

### Prince over his inheritance, in secret — 1 Samuel 9:26–10:16

The first king of Israel is anointed with no witnesses. The servant is sent ahead, and Samuel pours *the vial of
oil* on Saul's head at the edge of the city: *Hasn't Yahweh anointed you to be prince over his inheritance?*
(10:1). The title is *nāḡîḏ* again, and Israel is called Yahweh's *naḥălâ* (WLC). The office belongs to someone
else, and the people are someone else's inheritance. The people asked for a king *like all the nations* (8:5), but
what Saul receives is oil, a kiss, signs, the Spirit, and an order to wait for the prophet at Gilgal (10:8). The
narrator names it *the matter of the kingdom*, *dəḇar hamməlûḵâ*, the first use of *məlûḵâ* in the WLC files
searched (10:16). Saul tells no one. See [[Samuel Anoints Saul]].

Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:5; 9:26–10:16. Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-17.

### A prince sent from Benjamin — 1 Samuel 9:1–25

The king asked for in chapter 8 arrives in chapter 9 as someone Yahweh sends. *Tomorrow about this time I will send
you a man out of the land of Benjamin, and you shall anoint him to be prince over my people Israel. He will save my
people out of the hand of the Philistines* (9:16). Yahweh's title is *nāḡîḏ*, not the *meleḵ* of the people's
request; in the WLC files searched (Genesis through 2 Samuel, Ruth) *nāḡîḏ* first occurs here, and next at 10:1;
13:14; 25:30 and 2 Samuel 5:2; 6:21; 7:8, of Saul and then of David (lemma 5057). Its precise sense is debated
(general knowledge, not checked against a lexicon). The reason given is the Exodus reason, *I have looked upon my
people, because their cry has come to me* (Exodus 3:7, 9). Chapter 8 said the people would cry out *because of your
king whom you will have chosen* (8:18); chapter 9 makes the king the answer to their cry. Both stand in the text, and
the wiki does not reconcile them. Samuel's first words of kingship to Saul are not *king* but *all that is desirable
in Israel* (9:20). See [[Saul Comes to the Seer]] and [[Saul]].
Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:18; 9:16–20; Exodus 3:7, 9. Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-17.

### Israel asks for a king — 1 Samuel 8:1–22

The demand comes from *all the elders of Israel*, with two reasons: Samuel is old, and his sons, judges at
Beersheba, *took bribes, and perverted justice* (8:3–5). They ask for *a king to judge us like all the nations*
(5), in the words Deuteronomy put in Israel's mouth (17:14). Yahweh grants it three times, *Listen to their
voice* (7, 9, 22), and names it: *they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me as the king over them*,
*mimməlōḵ ʿălêhem*, *from reigning over them* (7, WLC). Samuel's warning, *the way of the king*, *mišpaṭ
hammeleḵ* (9, 11), is a list of what the king will take: sons for chariots and fields, daughters for his
kitchen, the best land for his servants, a tenth of seed and flock, *and you will be his servants* (11–17).
*Whom you will have chosen*, *bəḥartem* (18), reverses Deuteronomy's *whom Yahweh your God chooses* (17:15,
WLC). The people insist: *that our king may judge us, and go out before us, and fight our battles* (20), the
two jobs Deuteronomy's king is not given. See [[Israel Asks for a King]],
[[Like All the Nations - Deuteronomy 17.14 and 1 Samuel 8.5, 20]],
[[Yahweh Shall Rule Over You - Judges 8.23 and 1 Samuel 8.7]], and
[[A Fifth to Pharaoh - Joseph's Tax and the Rights of the King]].
Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:1–22; Deuteronomy 17:14–15. Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-17.

### Deliverance without a king — 1 Samuel 7:13–17

The chapter before the demand for a king shows Israel delivered without one. Under Samuel *the Philistines were
subdued*, lost towns are restored, and *Yahweh's hand was against the Philistines all the days of Samuel* (7:13–14),
the formula of the judges (Judges 2:18). The people will ask for a king to *go out before us, and fight our
battles* (8:20), after a battle that Yahweh fought with thunder while Samuel
prayed. See [[Samuel Judges Israel at Mizpah]] and [[Between Ramah and Bethel - Deborah and Samuel as Judges]].
Source: stored WEB Judges 2:18; 1 Samuel 7:10–17; 8:20. Hebrew WLC, 2026-11-17.

### *Before my anointed* — 1 Samuel 2:35

The second royal *māšîaḥ* in 1 Samuel comes in a priestly oracle: the faithful priest *will walk before my
anointed forever* (2:35). As with Hannah's *his anointed* (2:10), Israel has no king yet; the priest's
future is already set in relation to one. See [[A Man of God Against the House of Eli]] and
[[His King and His Anointed - Hannah's Song and David's]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 2:10, 35.

### *His king … his anointed* — 1 Samuel 2:10

The first king named in 1 Samuel is in a song, before Israel has one: *He will give strength to his king,
and exalt the horn of his anointed* (2:10). *Māšîaḥ*, *anointed*, occurs in the WLC files of Genesis
through Ruth only of *the anointed priest* (Leviticus 4:3, 5, 16; 6:22 WEB); this is its first royal use.
The king here is *his*, Yahweh's, and his strength is given. Chapter 8 will treat the people's request for a
king as a rejection of Yahweh (8:7); the song presents a king as Yahweh's gift. The book holds both. David's
song at the end of 2 Samuel closes on the same pair, *his king … his anointed, to David* (22:51). See
[[The Song of Hannah]] and [[His King and His Anointed - Hannah's Song and David's]]. Source: stored WEB
1 Samuel 2:10; 8:7; Leviticus 4; 6:22. 2 Samuel 22:51 at bible-api.com, 2026-11-17. Hebrew WLC
(OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-17.

### The king's name without the word — Ruth 4:18–22

Ruth ends on ten generations from Perez to David, and the last word of the book is *David* (4:22). Judges
had ended *In those days there was no king in Israel* (Judges 21:25); Ruth names the king and never uses
the word. 1 Chronicles 2:15 and Matthew 1:6 give the same line, Matthew adding *King David* (WEB at
bible-api.com, 2026-11-17). See [[The Generations of Perez]] and [[From Perez to David - Ruth 4, 1 Chronicles 2, Matthew 1, and Luke 3]]. Source: stored WEB Ruth 4:18–22; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-17.

### The fourth refrain — Judges 21:25

*In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes* (21:25). The
full form of 17:6 returns word for word (WLC) as the book's last sentence, directly after the abduction at
Shiloh. The epilogue is framed by the long form at both ends, with the short form at 18:1 and 19:1. What
follows in the canon is 1 Samuel, where Israel asks for a king; the first king will be a Benjamite of
Gibeah. See [[Wives for Benjamin]] and
[[Right in His Own Eyes - Deuteronomy 12.8 and Judges 17.6 and 21.25]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 17:6;
18:1; 19:1; 21:25; Hebrew WLC, 2026-11-17.

### The third refrain — Judges 19:1

*In those days, when there was no king in Israel* (19:1), the short form, introducing a story of violence
rather than worship. The town where it happens, Gibeah, becomes the home of Israel's first king (1 Samuel
10:26, WEB checked at bible-api.com), and Saul's muster with cut-up oxen repeats the Levite's summons
(1 Samuel 11:7). See [[The Levite's Concubine at Gibeah]] and
[[Cut Limb by Limb - The Burnt Offering, the Concubine, and Saul's Oxen]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 19:1,
29; 1 Samuel 10:26; 11:7 from bible-api.com.

### *There was no king in Israel* — Judges 17:6

The first of the four refrains (17:6; 18:1; 19:1; 21:25): *In those days there was no king in Israel.
Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes*. It comes directly after Micah's shrine is furnished, so
the absence of a king is the narrator's frame for a private image and a private priest. See
[[Micah's Idol and Levite]] and [[Right in His Own Eyes - Deuteronomy 12.8 and Judges 17.6 and 21.25]].
Source: stored WEB Judges 17:6.

### The Philistines are rulers over us — Judges 15:11

*Don't you know that the Philistines are rulers over us?* (15:11). The men of Judah use *māšal*, the verb
of the narrator's *the Philistines ruled over Israel* (14:4) and of Gideon's refusal, *I will not rule
over you … Yahweh shall rule over you* (8:23, WLC). Judah accepts the foreign ruler and hands over the
deliverer. See [[Samson Against the Philistines at Lehi]] and
[[Who Made You a Prince and a Judge - The Rejected Deliverer]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 8:23; 14:4;
15:11; Hebrew WLC, 2026-11-16.

### Households without a king — Judges 12:8–15

Ibzan's sixty marriages (12:9) and Abdon's forty sons and thirty grandsons *who rode on seventy donkey
colts* (12:14) describe judges with households on the scale of the great men of the land, after
Gideon's seventy sons (8:30) and Abimelech's attempt to rule alone (9:5–6). Neither man is called king,
and the text draws no comparison; the juxtaposition is the wiki's observation. See
[[Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 8:30; 9:5–6; 12:9, 14.

### Chief, then head — Judges 11:6–11

The elders of Gilead first offer Jephthah *chief*, *qāṣîn*, a field commander (11:6; in the WLC of
Genesis–Judges elsewhere only Joshua 10:24). When he refuses to come on those terms, they offer *head
over all the inhabitants of Gilead* (11:8), the words of 10:18. He asks for it in plain terms, *will I be
your head?* (11:9). The people make him *head and chief* (11:11). Rule in Gilead is given by contract,
sworn before Yahweh, and made conditional on victory. See [[Jephthah Called from Tob]].

Source: stored WEB Judges 11:1–11; comparisons as cited; Hebrew from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### Head over Gilead — Judges 10:18

*Who is the man who will begin to fight against the children of Ammon? He shall be head over all the inhabitants of Gilead* (18). For the first time a deliverer is to be found by an offer of rule. Earlier, Yahweh raised up the savior. The princes ask one another, not Yahweh. Jephthah will take the offer (11:8–11).

See [[Israel's Repentance Under Ammon]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 10:6–18; comparisons as cited; Hebrew from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### Thirty sons on thirty colts — Judges 10:1–5

After Abimelech's three years, two judges rule forty-five years and nobody calls either one king.
Tola is counted *after Abimelech* (10:1), as if Abimelech belonged to the line of Israel's leaders.
Jair's thirty sons ride thirty donkey colts and hold thirty cities (10:4). The Song had called on
*you who ride on white donkeys* (5:10). Gideon had seventy sons and refused to rule (8:23, 30).
Abimelech killed his brothers to rule alone (9:5). Jair shares his cities among thirty sons and
*judged*. The text draws no comparison. The juxtaposition is the wiki's.

See [[Tola and Jair]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 10:1–5; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### Prince over Israel three years — Judges 9:22–57

*Abimelech was prince over Israel three years* (9:22). The verb is *śārar*, *to be prince*, not
*mālak*. In Genesis–Judges it occurs only here, and the narrator does not call him king again (WLC). He
rules Shechem through an officer, [[Zebul]], and lives at [[Arumah]] (9:28, 41). The town that made him
king turns against him when God sends *an evil spirit* between them (9:23), and a rival asks *Who is
Abimelech … that we should serve him?* (9:28). He answers by destroying the town that made him and
burning the tower whose god paid for him (9:45–49). He dies besieging another Israelite town. When he
is dead, *the men of Israel … each departed to his place* (9:55). No successor is named, and no one
mourns him. Israel's first attempt at kingship ends after three years in civil war, and Judges does not
try it again. See [[The Fall of Abimelech]] and
[[Draw Your Sword - The Deaths of Abimelech and Saul]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 9:22–57; Hebrew WLC
(OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### The trees seek a king — Judges 9:7–21

The Bible's first fable is about kingship. *The trees set out to anoint a king over themselves* (9:8):
it is the first anointing of a king in Scripture, and the ones doing it are trees. Up to here every
*māšaḥ* in Genesis–Judges is of a priest, the tabernacle, offerings, or Jacob's pillar (WLC). The olive,
fig, and vine each refuse to leave their oil, fruit, or new wine *to wave back and forth over the
trees*. Only the bramble accepts, and its terms are a king's: *take refuge in my shade*, or fire. Jotham
then asks whether Shechem made Abimelech king *truly and righteously* (9:16). Whether the fable condemns
kingship as such, or this king and the way he was made, is read both ways; the fable mocks the office,
and the application judges the choice. See [[Jotham's Fable]] and
[[The Bramble and the Thistle - Plant Fables in Judges 9 and 2 Kings 14]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 9:7–21; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### One rule over you — Judges 9:1–6

Gideon's son asks the question his father refused: *Is it better for you that all the sons of
Jerubbaal, who are seventy persons, rule over you, or that one rule over you?* (9:2). The verb is
*māšal*, the verb of *Yahweh shall rule over you* (8:23), and *Yahweh* is not among the choices. Shechem
chooses him because *He is our brother* (9:3), pays him from the house of Baal Berith, and *made
Abimelech king* by the oak of the pillar (9:6). The hiphil of *mālak*, *make king*, occurs in
Genesis–Judges only here and in 9:16, 18 (WLC): it is the first time in those books anyone is made king.
Deuteronomy 17:15 asks for a king *whom Yahweh your God chooses* and *from among your brothers*; Shechem
meets only the second. See [[Your Bone and Your Flesh - Genesis 29.14, Judges 9.2, and 2 Samuel 5.1]].

See [[Abimelech Made King at Shechem]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 9:1–6; Hebrew from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### Yahweh shall rule over you — Judges 8:22–23

After Midian, Israel offers Gideon a dynasty: *Rule over us, both you, and your son, and your son's son
also; for you have saved us* (8:22). He refuses in three clauses, *I will not rule over you, neither
shall my son rule over you. Yahweh shall rule over you* (8:23). The verb is *māšal*, *rule*; neither
*king* nor *reign* occurs in the exchange (WLC). It is the only verse in Genesis–Judges in which Yahweh
is the subject of *māšal* (WLC). The pericope then shows Gideon with the gold, the *many wives*, and the
household Deuteronomy 17:17 denies a king, and with a son he names *Abimelech*. See
[[Yahweh Shall Rule Over You - Judges 8.23 and 1 Samuel 8.7]].

See [[Gideon's Ephod and Death]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 8:22–35; Hebrew from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-15.

### Seventy kings under a table — Judges 1:1–7

Judges begins without a leader: Joshua is dead, no successor is named, and Israel asks Yahweh who
will go up first (1). The first king in the book is a Canaanite. Adoni-Bezek claims to have kept
seventy kings, thumbs and big toes cut off, scavenging under his table (7). That may be a boast, and
the narrator does not confirm it. It still shows the kind of overlord the land had. The book's
refrain, *In those days there was no king in Israel* (17:6; 21:25), comes only at the end; at the
beginning, Israel has no king and no leader, and asks Yahweh who will go first.

See [[Judah Goes Up First]]. Source: stored WEB Judges 1:1–7; 17:6; 21:25.

### The kings struck down — Joshua 12:1–24

The land is measured by its rulers. Sihon and Og are named with their realms; the thirty-one western kings are unnamed, each reduced to a city and the word *one* (9–24). Even kings the narrative named — Adoni-Zedek, Jabin, Horam — are listed by city only.

See [[The Kings Struck Down]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 12:1–24.

### The northern coalition — Joshua 11:1–15

Jabin organizes the northern rulers from Hazor, the former head of their kingdoms (1, 10). Royal coordination and military resources fail to prevent defeat. The text does not define the constitutional relation between these kingdoms.

See [[The Northern Kings at the Waters of Merom]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 11:1–15.

### The southern campaign — Joshua 10:28–43

Horam’s attempt to help Lachish fails (33); the kings of Makkedah, Libnah, Hebron, and Debir fall with their cities. The unnamed king of Hebron in 37 creates a tension with Hoham’s prior death, not a securely identified succession.

See [[The Southern Cities Taken]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 10:28–43; comparisons as cited.

### The five kings at Makkedah — Joshua 10:16–27

Five city kings are publicly subjected to Israel’s commanders, then killed and displayed (23–26).
Royal status offers no protection; Joshua uses their humiliation as a visible assurance about future
enemies (25).

See [[The Five Kings in the Cave at Makkedah]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 10:16–27.


### At Gibeon — Joshua 10:1–15

Adoni-Zedek draws four other city kings into a coalition against Gibeon (3–5). Gibeon is compared
with royal cities, but no king of Gibeon is named (2). The defeated rulers’ capture and execution
belong to the next unit.

### Moses blesses the tribes — Deuteronomy 33:1–29

Verse 5 speaks of a king in Jeshurun when the tribes gather. The English pronoun alone does not

### Covenant sanctions — Deuteronomy 28:15–68

The king whom Israel will appoint shares the people’s deportation (36). Royal office offers no immunity from covenant judgment; neither the king nor the invading empire is named.

Human beings ruling other human beings at scale — and the Bible's long, unresolved argument about
whether that is a good idea.

The theme has an exact starting point. *Mamlākâ*, **kingdom**, occurs for the first time in Scripture
at Genesis 10:10: *"The beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of
Shinar."* Before that verse Genesis has a city (4:17), crafts (4:20–22), a sentence of hereditary
servitude (9:25), and no ruler at all. Nobody in the primeval history commands anybody. Then
[[Nimrod]] *begins to be a mighty one*, and four cities are named as his.

**Three things about that first occurrence are worth holding.**

**1. It is founded by a *gibbōr*.** The word Genesis 10:8 uses of Nimrod is the word 6:4 used of the
pre-flood *"mighty men who were of old, men of renown"* — the last generation before God resolved to
blot out humanity. Genesis does not draw the line; it just reuses the noun. See
[[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].

**2. Its cities are the ones that will destroy Israel and Judah.** Babel is Babylon. And on the
reading Genesis 10:11 most naturally supports, the same man goes north and founds Nineveh and Calah,
which makes one figure the origin of both empires of the exile. Micah 5:6 pairs *"the land of
Assyria"* with *"the land of Nimrod"* as one place. See
[[Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria]].

**3. Genesis passes no verdict.** As with the city, the harp, the forge, and the vineyard, the book
names an origin and declines to evaluate it. What it does instead is place the origin: the first
kingdom in Scripture begins at Babel, one chapter before Babel is a byword.

**The argument that follows is genuinely two-sided.** Israel is promised kings before it wants one
(Genesis 17:6, 16; 35:11; 49:10), given a law regulating the office (Deuteronomy 17:14–20), and then
told that asking for one is a rejection of God (1 Samuel 8:7) — in the same books, without
reconciliation. Kingship then produces David, the covenant of 2 Samuel 7, and the messianic hope; and
it produces Solomon's forced labour, the schism, and the two exiles. The New Testament keeps both
halves: Jesus is proclaimed a king and refuses to be made one (John 6:15), Romans 13 calls the
governing authority God's servant, and Revelation 13 draws it as a beast.

**A kingdom can be defined by priestly service.** Exodus 19:6 offers Israel a kingdom of priests,
following Yahweh's claim to all the earth (19:5). No human king is installed or constitution of
monarchy given in the scene. The expression supplies a corporate vocation for the people
brought out of Egypt, not a new Pharaoh. See [[Israel at Mount Sinai]].

## Development across Scripture

### Balaam’s last oracles — Numbers 23:27–24:25

Israel’s king is exalted above Agag (24:7); the star and scepter portray future dominion (24:17–19).
The final sayings also threaten the powers that subdue others (24:22–24). No particular historical
ruler is identified here.

See [[Balaam's Last Oracles]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 23:27–24:25.


### The first two oracles — Numbers 22:36–23:26

Balak can supply honor, victims, and new vantage points, but cannot command the divine answer. The
shout of a king among Israel accompanies Yahweh’s presence (23:21); no human Israelite monarch is
named here.

See [[Balaam's First Two Oracles]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 22:36–23:26.


### Balak’s embassies — Numbers 22:1–21

Balak responds to fear with diplomacy, rewards, and promised honor (2–17). Royal patronage cannot
secure divine authorization for his military aim.

See [[Balak Sends for Balaam]]. Source: stored WEB Numbers 22:1–21.

- **Genesis 10:8–12** — the first *kingdom*, the first ruler, and eight cities; no evaluation. See
  [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the first collective political project, and what happens to it. The passage
  uses no political vocabulary at all — no king, no kingdom, no ruler, nobody named — but *"one
  people… one language"* pursuing one work on one site is what an empire is for, and it is set in the
  capital 10:10 has already assigned to the first kingdom. The prophets read it that way: Isaiah
  14:13–14 and Daniel 4:30 put Genesis 11:4's ambition into the mouth of Babylon's king. See
  [[The Tower of Babel]] and [[Babel and Babylon the Great]].
- **Genesis 14:1–24** *(not yet ingested)* — nine kings at war, and Melchizedek, the first king in
  Scripture who is also a priest.
- **Genesis 17:6, 16; 35:11; 49:10** — kings promised out of Abraham, Sarah, Jacob, and Judah.
- **Deuteronomy 17:14–20** — the law of the king: no accumulation of horses, wives, or silver, and a
  copy of the law written out by hand.
- **Judges 8:22–23; 9:7–15** — Gideon refuses the crown; Jotham's fable of the trees who want a king
  and get a bramble.
- **1 Samuel 8:4–22** — Israel asks for a king *"like all the nations"*, and is told what one costs,
  and is given one.
- **2 Samuel 7:8–16** — the dynastic promise to David, which is where the theme turns from a concession
  into a hope.
- **1 Kings 9:20–21; 12:1–19** — Solomon's forced labour out of the Canaanite peoples, and the schism
  it produces under Rehoboam.
- **Isaiah 10:5–19** — Assyria as *"the rod of my anger"*, used and then broken for taking credit.
- **Daniel 2, 4, 7** — the empires as a statue and as beasts, and *"the Most High rules in the kingdom
  of men"*.
- **Mark 10:42–45** — *"those who are recognized as rulers over the nations lord it over them… it
  shall not be so among you."*
- **Romans 13:1–7; Revelation 13; 17–18** — the authority as God's servant, and the authority as the
  beast, and Babylon falling.

### By me kings reign — Proverbs 8:15–16

*"By me kings reign, and princes decree justice. By me princes rule; nobles, and all the righteous
rulers of the earth"* (8:15–16). Wisdom claims every grade of ruler — *məlāḵîm*, *rōzənîm*,
*śārîm*, *nəḏîḇîm*, *šōp̄əṭîm* — as hers. The claim fits the book's attachment to Solomon, who asked
for *"an understanding heart to judge your people"* (1 Kings 3:9, WEB at bible-api.com), and the
verse before it gives Wisdom the counsel and might that Isaiah 11:2 gives the coming king. Where
Job 12:17–18 saw God's wisdom stripping counsellors and loosening the bonds of kings, Proverbs 8 sees
wisdom establishing them. Where the Masoretic text has *šōp̄əṭê ṣeḏeq*, *judges of righteousness*,
the WEB's *"of the earth"* reflects a reading the wiki has not traced. See
[[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] and [[Counsel and Might - Job 12.13, Proverbs 8.14, and Isaiah 11.2]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### The king's glory and the king's favor — Proverbs 14:28, 35

*"In the multitude of people is the king's glory"* (28): a ruler's honour depends on his people. The king favours the servant who deals wisely and turns his wrath on one who causes shame (35). The king is a type, not an identified ruler.

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### The king's lips and face — Proverbs 16:10–15

The collection's first royal cluster: inspired judgments on the king's lips (10), wrongdoing an abomination for kings because *"the throne is established by righteousness"* (12), truthful lips their delight (13), their wrath *"a messenger of death"* (14), and *"In the light of the king's face is life. His favor is like a cloud of the spring rain"* (15). Job used the same two images of himself in the months of old (Job 29:23–25). See [[The Light of the King's Face - Proverbs 16.15 and Job 29.23-25]].

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:6, 10, 12

Many seek a ruler's favor (6); verse 12 pictures royal wrath as a lion's roar and favor as dew on
grass. The image describes royal power over others' well-being without naming or unconditionally
approving a king. Verse 10 resists a servant ruling princes. **Internal tension:** 17:2 commends a
wise servant ruling a shameful son; competence and inherited rank do not always point in the same
direction. The offices and circumstances differ, so the two sayings cannot simply be made identical
rules.

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:2, 8, 26, 28

The king's terror is a lion's roar and provoking him threatens life (2). His judging gaze
scatters evil (8); the wise king winnows the wicked and drives a threshing wheel over them
(26). Verse 28 locates the throne's security in love and faithfulness. The violent imagery is
retained, but the text gives no named king or historical execution procedure and does not
equate every royal anger with justice.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### The king's heart in Yahweh's hand — Proverbs 21:1

The most powerful heart in the land is pictured as water in Yahweh's hand. The saying neither
flatters nor excuses the king: his decisions are his, and they are not final. See
[[The King's Heart in Yahweh's Hand - Proverbs 21.1, Exodus, and Ezra 6.22]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### The king's friend — Proverbs 22:11

*"He who loves purity of heart and speaks gracefully is the king's friend"* (22:11), the last royal
saying of the Solomonic collection, continuing 14:35 and 16:13: the king values the wise and
truthful servant.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### Before kings and at the ruler's table — Proverbs 22:29; 23:1–3

The skilled worker *"will serve kings"* (22:29), and the next verse seats him at the table: *"When you
sit to eat with a ruler, consider diligently what is before you; put a knife to your throat, if you are a
man given to appetite. Don't be desirous of his dainties, since they are deceitful food"* (23:1–3). The
court is assumed to be where a capable pupil will end up; the danger there is appetite, and the ruler's
hospitality may carry obligations the guest does not see.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11.

### The king feared beside Yahweh — Proverbs 24:21–22

*"My son, fear Yahweh and the king. Don't join those who are rebellious: for their calamity will rise
suddenly; the destruction from them both—who knows?"* The king is feared under one verb with Yahweh,
but named after him; 21:1 has already put the king's heart in Yahweh's hand. The rebels' ruin comes
*"from them both"*. See [[Fear Yahweh and the King - Proverbs 24.21, Exodus 22.28, and 1 Peter 2.17]].

See [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:3–22.

### The searching king — Proverbs 25:1–7

The Hezekian collection opens with kings. Their glory is to *"search out a matter"* (25:2), yet
their own hearts are *"unsearchable"* as heaven and the deep (25:3). The throne is *"established in
righteousness"* when the wicked are removed from the king's presence as dross from silver (25:4–5),
echoing 16:12. And the subject is taught his place: not to exalt himself before the king, since
*"Come up here"* is better than a public demotion (25:6–7; see
[[Come Up Here - Proverbs 25.6-7 and Luke 14.7-11]]). 25:15 adds that *"By patience a ruler is
persuaded."* The heading itself names a king of Judah, [[Hezekiah]], whose men copied the
collection (25:1).

See [[The Glory of Kings]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:2, 12, 15–16, 28

Rebellion multiplies rulers while understanding sustains order. Wicked rule is compared with
predatory animals over helpless people (15), and tyranny with deficient judgment (16). The repeated
hiding of people when the wicked rise (12, 28) makes political conditions visible from below. Verse
16 places hatred of ill-gotten gain beside tyranny as a contrasting disposition; no historical ruler
is named.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

Justice stabilizes a land; bribery tears it down. A ruler who listens to lies corrupts his
officials, and the just treatment of the poor measures a throne’s legitimacy. Verse 26 locates
justice finally in Yahweh, limiting royal patronage rather than making government unnecessary.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:2, 4, 12, 14, 26; the passage page records the
cross-reference sources and interpretive limits.

### Kings among the creatures — Proverbs 30:22, 27–28, 31

Kings appear four times in a passage mostly about animals. A servant who becomes king shakes the
earth (22). Locusts have no king and still advance in ranks (27). A lizard you can catch with your
hands lives in kings' palaces (28). And the king *"against whom there is no rising up"* closes the
list of stately things, after the lion and the goat (31); the Hebrew of that line (*ʾalqûm*) is
uncertain. Order is possible without a king, and access to a king is possible for the weak.

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

### Lemuel’s mother — Proverbs 31:3–9

The king’s appetite is a public matter because his impaired judgment can deprive the afflicted
of justice (4–5). The mother’s warning about women (3) and her command to speak for the poor (8–9)
make restraint and responsibility inseparable. This is an instruction to a named king, not a
report that Lemuel either obeyed or disobeyed. Deuteronomy 17:17–20 likewise restrains royal
acquisition and places the king under instruction; see
[[A King Under Instruction - Proverbs 31.1-5 and Deuteronomy 17.17-20]].

See [[The Words of King Lemuel]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9 and the comparisons documented
there.

### The opening of Ecclesiastes — 1:1–11

Ecclesiastes 1:1 identifies its speaker as the son of David, king in Jerusalem. The elevated
position introduces a question common to all workers and generations; it does not exempt
royalty from the poem's limits. Solomon is not named. The wiki records the royal literary
identity without converting the heading into a confirmed historical identification or a
new narrative event in David's life.

See [[Vanity of Vanities]]. Sources: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and the comparison texts
verified on that passage page.

### The king as inquirer — Ecclesiastes 1:12–18

The Preacher's first-person claim, *"I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem"* (1:12),
introduces an inquiry only a king's position could plausibly claim to undertake: all that is done
under the sky. Royal means and unequalled wisdom (1:16) do not exempt him from the verdict of
vanity or from grief. See [[The Burden of Wisdom]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:12–18.

### The king's resources — Ecclesiastes 2:1–11

The experiment of 2:1–11 needs a king's means: servants bought and born in the house, *"the
treasure of kings and of the provinces"* (2:8), and greatness *"more than all who were before me in
Jerusalem"* (2:9). The royal speaker can test pleasure at a scale no ordinary person can reach, and
reports that it produced no profit (2:11). Whether that scale is meant to settle the question for
everyone else is an inference the text does not state. See [[The Trial of Pleasure]]. Source:
stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:7–11.


### The king's successor — Ecclesiastes 2:12

*"For what can the king's successor do? Just that which has been done long ago"* (2:12). A king who
has tried everything leaves his successor nothing new to try, which echoes 1:9's *"no new thing under
the sun."* The successor is unnamed; he returns as the heir of 2:18–19. See [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:12–17.

### The youth and the old king — Ecclesiastes 4:13–16

A short exemplary story: a foolish old king who no longer takes warning, a poor and wise youth
who comes out of prison to reign, and a crowd *"without end"* around the successor — whom *"those
who come after shall not rejoice in"* (16). Whether verse 15's *"the youth, the other"* is the same
youth or a further one is left open on the passage page. No historical king is identified; the
Joseph comparison is a shared motif with real differences, since Joseph rules under Pharaoh's
throne. See [[The Youth and the Old King]] and
[[Out of Prison to Reign - Joseph and Ecclesiastes 4.13-14]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes
4:13–16; Genesis 41:39–43.

### Wealth and enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 5:8–20

Officials stand above other officials (8), and the king profits from the field (9).
The king is unnamed, not an identified historical ruler or certainly the Preacher himself.
The WEB establishes royal participation in agricultural profit, but does not settle whether
the hierarchy protects subjects or perpetuates extraction. No successful correction of
oppression is narrated. These lines keep royal power within the material economy.

See [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20;
comparisons as identified on the passage page.

### Royal command and mortal power — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

The king's command is to be kept because of the oath to God (2), and his word is supreme
in the court's unequal contest (3–4). Yet no man controls the day of death (8), and human
rule can bring hurt (9). These limits qualify the picture of power without making it
harmless. The unnamed king is not identified with the Preacher or a historical dynasty.
**Tension:** obedience here stands beside the approved refusal of a killing order in
Exodus 1:16–21. See
[[Fear Yahweh and the King - Proverbs 24.21, Exodus 22.28, and 1 Peter 2.17]].

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

Three rulers appear: the great king who besieges (9:14), the one who *"rules among
fools"* and whose cry is worse than the quiet words of the wise (17), and the ruler whose spirit
rises against a subordinate (10:4). The counsel for the last is to hold one's place, for
gentleness lays great offenses to rest. See
[[Gentleness Before the Ruler - Proverbs 15.1, 25.15, and Ecclesiastes 10.4]].

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Folly in high places — Ecclesiastes 10:5–20

The Preacher traces folly in high office to *"the sort of error which proceeds from the
ruler"* (10:5). A land is pitied when its king is a child and its princes feast in the morning,
and called happy when its king is the son of nobles and its princes eat for strength (16–17).
The king is not to be cursed, even in thought (20). Proverbs 31:4–5 keeps wine from kings
altogether; Ecclesiastes faults drunkenness and timing. See
[[Woe to the Land - Proverbs 31.4-5, Isaiah 3.4, 5.11, and Ecclesiastes 10.16-17]] and
[[Servants on Horses - Proverbs 19.10, 30.21-22, and Ecclesiastes 10.5-7]].

See [[Folly in High Places and Everyday Work]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 10:5–20; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Love better than wine — Song of Solomon 1:1–4

The heading names Solomon (1:1), and the woman says that the king brought her into
his rooms (1:4). Royal imagery enters a love poem, without legislation, warfare, or
an act of public government. The unit does not name the king again or establish that
he and the beloved must be the same person. Attribution and character identity remain
distinct; no separate king's biography is inferred.

See [[Love Better Than Wine]]; comparisons are developed in
[[The Wisdom of Solomon - 1 Kings 3-4 and the Headings of Proverbs]].
Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 1:1–4 and the comparison references on those pages.

### The king at his table — Song of Solomon 1:9–2:7

Royal images serve the love poem. The woman is compared to a steed in Pharaoh's chariots
(1:9), and her perfume spreads while the king sits at his table (1:12). Solomon is not named,
and the king is not identified with the beloved. Pharaoh's chariots, which Exodus casts into
the sea, here become a compliment.

See [[Delight and the Unawakened Love]] and
[[A Steed in Pharaoh's Chariots - Exodus 14-15, 1 Kings 10.28-29, and Song of Solomon 1.9]].
Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 1:9–2:7; comparison sources are identified on the passage page.

### A king's carriage — Song of Solomon 3:6–11

Royal splendor seen as a public display. *"King Solomon made himself a carriage of the wood of
Lebanon"*, with silver pillars, a gold base, and a purple seat (3:9–10). Sixty of *"the mighty men of
Israel"* surround it, armed against *"fear in the night"* (3:7–8), and the king is shown wearing the
crown his mother gave him on his wedding day (3:11). The materials echo 1 Kings' account of his
building with Lebanon timber (1 Kings 5:6; 7:2, checked at bible-api.com). The Song's earlier *king*
(1:4, 12) was unnamed; here the king is Solomon by name.

See [[Solomon's Wedding Procession]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 3:6–11; Hebrew read from
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### Queens and concubines as a scale of praise — Song of Solomon 6:4–10

Sixty queens, eighty concubines, and virgins without number provide the backdrop to the
unique beloved (6:8–9). Royal abundance supplies a scale of comparison, and the royal women
join her praise. The verse says such women exist; it does not explicitly make them the
speaker's household. No king is named here, so the counts cannot establish a stage of
Solomon's reign or settle the male beloved's historical identity.

See [[My Dove Is Unique]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 6:4–10; comparisons use the stored WEB
Song 1:5–6; 4:1–9 and Proverbs 31:28–31 where noted.

### A king captive in her hair — Song of Solomon 6:11–7:9

Royal chariots (6:12), prince's daughter (7:1), and a king captive in her tresses
(7:5) bring royal language into desire. Neither a named father nor the identification
of the man with Solomon follows. The captivity is a poetic image of attraction.

See [[Return, Return, Shulammite]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 6:11–7:9; internal comparisons use the stored Song.

### Family voices and the own vineyard — Song of Solomon 8:8–12

Solomon’s leased vineyard at Baal Hamon gives royal possession an economic form
(11–12). The speaker puts her own vineyard beside it. A contrast with royal control
is possible, but a harem or a rejected royal marriage is not explicitly described.

See [[Our Sister and My Own Vineyard]]. Source: stored WEB Song 1:6; 4:12–16; 8:7–12.

### Ruler and individual — Leviticus 4:22–35

Leviticus 4:22–26 puts the ruler under the commands of Yahweh his God and requires
a sin offering when an unwitting offense is made known. Leadership carries accountability,
not immunity. The WEB calls him a ruler; identifying this office specifically as monarchy
would go beyond these verses.

See [[The Sin Offering for Ruler and Individual]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 4:22–35; contextual
comparisons documented on the passage page.

### The law of the king, ingested — Deuteronomy 17:14–20

The Torah's only law of the king is now ingested. The request is quoted in advance, *"I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me"* (17:14), and granted: the king must be the one *"whom Yahweh your God chooses"* and *"one from among your brothers"* (15). He shall not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold (16–17). His one duty is to copy *"this law"* and read it all his life (18–19), *"that his heart not be lifted up above his brothers"* (20). He is given no judicial, military, or priestly task. See [[Like All the Nations - Deuteronomy 17.14 and 1 Samuel 8.5, 20]] and [[Kings Will Come Out of You]].

See [[The Law for the King]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 17:14–20.

### When Yahweh your God was your king — 1 Samuel 12

Samuel's farewell closes the account of the request for a king. He puts it in the cycle of Judges: whenever Israel cried, Yahweh sent a deliverer, but when Nahash came, *you said to me, 'No, but a king shall reign over us;' when Yahweh your God was your king* (12:12). The king is then shown as both *the king whom you have chosen* and the one *Yahweh has set … over you* (12:13), and placed under the same condition as the people: *both you and also the king who reigns over you* (12:14), *you will be consumed, both you and your king* (12:25). Kingship is allowed into the covenant without being exempted from it. See [[Samuel's Farewell Address]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 12.

### A dynasty forfeited — 1 Samuel 13:13–14

*Now Yahweh would have established your kingdom on Israel forever. But now your kingdom will not continue*
(13:13–14). *Mamlaḵtəḵā … ʿaḏ-ʿôlām* (WLC) is the language of the promise to David's house, *Your house and your
kingdom will be made sure forever* (2 Samuel 7:16, WEB checked at bible-api.com, 2026-11-18; not stored). Saul
keeps the throne for now; the house is what he loses, and the title *prince*, *nāḡîḏ*, passes to *a man after his
own heart* (13:14). See [[Saul's Sacrifice at Gilgal]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 13:13–14; Hebrew WLC,
2026-11-18.

### Rejected from being king — 1 Samuel 15

The second sentence on Saul removes what the first left. At Gilgal in chapter 13 he lost a dynasty (*your kingdom will
not continue*, 13:14); at Gilgal in chapter 15 he loses the kingship: *he has also rejected you from being king* (15:23),
*from being king over Israel* (26), *Yahweh has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today, and has given it to a
neighbor of yours who is better than you* (28). Saul's own account explains the failure in terms of the monarchy's
origin: *I feared the people, and obeyed their voice* (24), when the king had been given because the people would
not listen to Yahweh (8:7, 19). He keeps the throne and asks to be *honored … before the elders of my people, and before
Israel* (30); Samuel never sees him again alive (35). See [[Saul Rejected over Amalek]] and
[[The Torn Robe - 1 Samuel 15.27-28, 24.4-5, 28.17, and 1 Kings 11.29-31]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:7, 19;
13:13–14; 15:23–35.

### A second king anointed — 1 Samuel 16:1–13

Yahweh says *I have provided a king for myself*, *lî meleḵ* (16:1, WLC), in contrast with the king the people asked
for *like all the nations* (8:5). The law of the king allowed only *him whom Yahweh your God chooses … one from among
your brothers* (Deuteronomy 17:15). Samuel says three times *Yahweh has not chosen* (16:8–10) and anoints the chosen
one *in the middle of his brothers* (16:13). The new king is anointed while the old one reigns, and the book now has
two anointed men; Saul remains king until his death (31:4–6, stored WEB; not yet ingested). See
[[Samuel Anoints David]] and [[Like All the Nations - Deuteronomy 17.14 and 1 Samuel 8.5, 20]]. Source: stored WEB
Deuteronomy 17:15; 1 Samuel 8:5; 16:1–13; 31:4–6. Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-20.

### The successor in the king's service — 1 Samuel 16:14–23

The man anointed in secret enters the house of the king he is to replace, as harpist and armor bearer, *stood
before him* (16:21–22). Saul loves him and does not know. Kingship passes in this book not by a coup but by
overlap, and the overlap begins inside Saul's own court. See [[David Plays the Harp for Saul]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 16:14–23; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-21.

### The king who was to go out — 1 Samuel 17:1–11

The people asked for a king who would *go out before us, and fight our battles* (8:20, *wəyāṣāʾ ləp̄ānênû*, WLC).
In the valley of Elah the one who *went out* is the Philistine champion (17:4, *wayyēṣēʾ*), who calls Israel
*servants to Saul* and asks for *a man* (17:8). Saul, taller than all the people (9:2), hears and is *dismayed, and
greatly afraid* with all Israel (17:11). The narrator does not comment; the gap is left open for the next pericope.
See [[Goliath Defies Israel]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 8:20; 9:2; 17:1–11; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb),
2026-11-21.

**The king's reward and the king's armor — 1 Samuel 17:25–39.** The king promises riches, a daughter, and freedom
for the champion's killer (17:25), but does not go down himself. He calls David *a youth*, sends him with *Yahweh will
be with you*, and dresses him in his own armor, which David cannot use (17:33, 37–39). See [[David Comes to the Camp]].
Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 17:12–40; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb).

- [[The Table of Nations]] — Genesis 10:8–12: the first kingdom in Scripture, and the two capitals of
  the exile founded in the same breath
- [[The Tower of Babel]] — Genesis 11:1–9: the first collective project, in the first kingdom's
  capital, with no ruler in it
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — Genesis 14:1–24: nine kings, twelve years of tribute, a
  rebellion, a punitive campaign — and a man with no throne who beats four of them and takes nothing.
  It is also the chapter that shows what a king could be: [[Melchizedek]] holds the crown and the
  priesthood together in one city, which Israel's own law will keep apart

- [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] — Genesis 17:6, 16: the Bible's first promise of
  monarchy, made by God, unprompted, as a blessing, to a childless ninety-nine-year-old and a
  ninety-year-old woman. *"Kings will come out of you"* and *"Kings of peoples will come from her"* —
  the second the only time a woman in Scripture is told it directly. Everything the Old Testament
  later says against kingship, from Judges' refrain to 1 Samuel 8, has to be held beside this. See
  [[Kings Will Come Out of You]]
- [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] — Genesis 21:22–34: a king with an army who negotiates instead. He
  arrives with his commander, concedes that God is with the other party, asks for a
  three-generation non-aggression clause, pleads ignorance of his own servants' seizure, takes the
  livestock, swears, and goes home. **Genesis has now shown four kinds of king in eight chapters** —
  the empire that collects tribute (14:1–11), the priest-king who blesses (14:18–20), the vassal who
  bargains for bodies (14:21), and the small king who buys peace with a sojourner by treaty. Only the
  last one is still standing at the end of his chapter
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:11; ***"kings will come out of your
  body"*** — **the third and last of the promise's three occurrences in Genesis**, after 17:6 to
  Abraham and 17:16 to Sarah. **All three are made to people who will never see one**, and the promise
  sits unredeemed for the whole of the Torah and the conquest. Paired here with *"a nation and a
  company of nations"*, it makes the covenant line's future political shape explicit for the first
  time since chapter 17. See [[Kings Will Come Out of You]]
- [[The Descendants of Esau]] — Genesis 36:31–39: **eight kings in Edom before Israel has one**, and
  the Bible's only sustained description of how a neighbouring monarchy actually worked. **No son
  succeeds a father anywhere in the list** — the formula is *X died, and Y reigned in his place*,
  with a fresh patronymic or home town each time and eight different cities — so this is an elective
  or imposed chieftainship rather than a dynasty, which is a poor model for what 2 Samuel 7 promises
  David and a good illustration of why Deuteronomy 17:14–20 hedges the office instead of simply
  granting it. The dating clause at 36:31 is also one of the most discussed verses in the Torah. See
  [[Kings Will Come Out of You]], [[Bela (son of Beor)]], and [[Hadar]]
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:8; ***"will you indeed reign over us? Or will you indeed have
  dominion over us?"*** — ***hămālōk timlōk … ʾim-māšôl timšōl***, two infinitive-absolute constructions,
  **the Hebrew of scorn. And it is the first time *mālak*, to reign as king, is used of a descendant of
  Abraham.** The kings-promise has been made three times — 17:6, 17:16, and 35:11, *"kings will come out of
  your body"* — **and the first Israelite the verb lands on is a seventeen-year-old, sarcastically, by the
  men about to sell him.** *Māšal* is also **the Eden verb** (3:16; 4:7), the vocabulary of contested
  mastery inside a family. See [[Kings Will Come Out of You]]
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:1–4, 20–22; **the first interior view of a royal
  household in Scripture.** Two named offices — the chief cupbearer, who handles what the king drinks
  and is therefore the last defence against poison, and the chief baker — a court that detains at the
  king's displeasure with no hearing recorded, **a royal prison inside a minister's private residence**,
  a birthday feast for the whole staff, **a muster of servants described with the census idiom**, and an
  execution by exposure. **The king never appears, speaks, or knows that a prisoner predicted his own
  verdicts**
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:37–57, **the Bible's first investiture scene and its most
  complete.** The signet ring off the king's own hand (*ṭabbaʿat*, the power to sign in his name), robes
  of fine linen, a gold chain, **the second chariot**, and a herald crying ***ʾabrēk*** — **and the one
  thing reserved is the throne**: *"only in the throne I will be greater than you"* (41:40). **The same
  regalia recur for Mordecai and Daniel**; see
  [[The Signet Ring and the Second Chariot - Joseph, Mordecai, and Daniel]]. **What the chapter shows
  about monarchy is how much of it is delegable**: Pharaoh gives away the administration, the grain, a
  name, and a wife in one afternoon, and at the famine's height **sends his own starving people to
  someone else** — *"Go to Joseph. What he says to you, do"* (41:55). **And the state's reach is
  established by a tax**: the fifth part of 41:34, proposed by a prisoner, which 47:20–26 turns into the
  permanent law of Egypt after the crown has bought the population. **Genesis reports all of it as
  competent government and passes no verdict**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:8, 16–20, 26; ***"a father to Pharaoh, lord of all his
  house, and ruler over all the land of Egypt"*** (45:8) — **three titles, and the first is an office.**
  ***ʾĀb ləparʿōh*** is the vizier as the king's counsellor, the usage Isaiah gives to Eliakim (22:21),
  **and Genesis sets it four words away from *my father*** — a father to the king of Egypt and a son to
  a man in Canaan who believes him dead. **The chapter's imperial power is exercised entirely as
  hospitality**: the king above him raises every term (45:18–20), and the brothers' report home names
  the rank before anything else, *"he is ruler over all the land of Egypt"* (45:26). **This is the
  friendliest Egypt ever is to Israel, and Exodus 1:8 is written against it**
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 47:1–11; **the family meets the throne, and Egypt is as
  hospitable here as it will ever be.** [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] grants [[Goshen]] unasked,
  **offers Hebrew shepherds charge of the royal livestock** (47:6) — **the trade he is said to find
  abominable** — **and takes a blessing from a landless old man without recorded reaction** (47:7, 10).
  **The address he hands over is [[Rameses]]**, which Exodus will make a store-city built by slaves
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:13–26; **the clearest description in the Bible of how an
  absolute monarchy is actually assembled.** A crisis, a state monopoly on grain, four instalments,
  and a statute: **the money into the king's house** (47:14), **the herds** (47:17), ***"the land
  became Pharaoh's"*** (47:20), **the people his servants** (47:19, 25), **and a permanent fifth**
  (47:26). **[[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] is named eleven times in nineteen verses and does not
  appear, order anything, or say a word** — **the whole transfer is conducted for him by someone
  else.** **1 Samuel 8:11–18 is this passage in the future tense and at half the rate**, and 1 Kings
  12:4 is the same demand producing the opposite result. See
  [[A Fifth to Pharaoh - Joseph's Tax and the Rights of the King]]
- [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]] — Genesis 49:8–10; ***"The scepter will not depart from Judah, nor the
  ruler's staff from between his feet"*** — **the Bible's first attachment of rule to a named tribe.**
  **[[Abraham]] was told *"kings will come out of you"*** (17:6) **and [[Jacob]] the same** (35:11),
  **with no tribe named**; **49:10 gives the promise an address**, three centuries before Israel has a
  king and spoken by a man dying in [[Egypt]]. **The saying also puns the throne into the tribe**:
  ***šēbeṭ*** is both *scepter* and *tribe*. **What the verse's last three words say is genuinely
  undecided** — see [[The Scepter and Shiloh - Genesis 49.10 and Its Readings]] — **but what they
  attach is not**
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 12:17–24; **the longest catalogue of the powerful humbled in the
  Bible.** Counselors, judges, kings, priests, the mighty, the trusted, elders, princes, the strong,
  and the chiefs of the earth — each stripped, unbelted, silenced, or sent wandering; *"He increases
  the nations, and he destroys them"* (12:23). **Two of its half-verses are Psalm 107:40 word for
  word**, where the next line raises the needy; Job's hymn has no next line. Hannah (1 Samuel 2:7–8),
  Daniel 2:21 and Mary (Luke 1:52) belong to the same tradition. See
  [[He Pours Contempt on Princes - Job 12.21-24 and Psalm 107.40]]
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:25; *"I chose out their way, and sat as chief. I lived as a king in
  the army"*. Not a king, but a judge at the gate described in royal terms, robe and turban (29:14) —
  and doing the royal duty the ancient Near East assigned to kings: delivering the poor, the widow and
  the fatherless (29:12–17). See [[I Put On Righteousness - Job 29.14 and the Garments of Justice]].
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:13, 17–20, 24, 30; **the king no one appointed.** *"Who put him in
  charge of the earth?"* (34:13): God's rule is underived, and so cannot be corrupt. *"Shall even one who
  hates justice govern?"* (34:17). He *"says to a king, 'Vile!' or to nobles, 'Wicked!'"*, *"doesn't
  respect the persons of princes"* (34:18–19), breaks mighty men *"and sets others in their place"*
  (34:24), *"that the godless man may not reign"* (34:30) — Job's own hymn of the humbled powerful
  (12:17–24) turned from complaint into theodicy.
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:7; the righteous set *"with kings on the throne … forever, and
  they are exalted"* — Hannah's picture of the poor made *"to sit with princes, and inherit the throne of
  glory"* (1 Samuel 2:8); and God *"exalted in his power"* (36:22), whose way no one has prescribed
  (36:23).
- [[Behemoth and Leviathan]] — Job 40:10; 41:34; **the royal robe offered, and a beast enthroned.** *"Array yourself
  with honor and majesty"* (*hôd wəhādār*, 40:10), the regalia of the Psalms' king; and the last line of the
  speeches, Leviathan *"king over all the sons of pride"* (41:34).
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:8–22; **the king who does not know.** *"A new king over Egypt, who didn't know
  Joseph"*: acknowledgment withheld, a blessing read as a threat, and three measures each widening
  the guilt — officers, two midwives, then *"all his people"*. Nameless throughout. See
  [[Pharaoh (of the oppression)]] and [[Every Son Who Is Born - Pharaoh's Decree and Herod's]]
- [[The Birth of Moses]] — Exodus 2:5–10; **the king's house rears the king's undoing.** Pharaoh's
  own daughter breaks the decree, pays a Hebrew mother from the royal purse, and adopts the
  deliverer; the king appears only as a title in *"Pharaoh's daughter"*
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:14–15, 23; ***"a prince and a judge"*** — **the first question about
  authority inside Israel is who gave a man the right to rule it**, and Moses has no answer until the
  bush. The king who *"sought to kill Moses"* dies, and **the bondage does not die with him** — the
  empire outlasts the emperor
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:6–14; **the chain of command.** Pharaoh → taskmasters (Egyptian)
  → officers (Israelite) → people, and the orders go down it as *kōh ʾāmar parʿōh*, *"This is what
  Pharaoh says"* (5:10) — **the messenger formula of 5:1, used for the other king.** Two sovereigns
  issue contradictory decrees in the same form
- [[The Song of the Sea]] — Exodus 15:18; **Yahweh's reign, first.** *"Yahweh shall reign forever and ever"* — *mālak* with
  Yahweh as subject for the first time in Scripture; in Genesis the verb belonged to Edom's kings (36:31)
  and to the brothers' *"Will you indeed reign over us?"* (37:8) (WLC lemma search). **Pharaoh, called
  *"king of Egypt"* in the prose (14:5, 8), is not called king anywhere in the Song.**
- [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] — Exodus 19:5–6; the kingdom offered as a priestly vocation
