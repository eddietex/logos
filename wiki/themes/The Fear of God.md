# The Fear of God

## Definition / overview

The Old Testament's most compact name for right relation to God — and a phrase that resists the
two easy translations. It is not terror, because the people commended for it are not described as
frightened, and God repeatedly tells the people he is addressing not to be afraid in the same
breath. It is not mere reverence either, softened into respect, because the word is the ordinary
Hebrew verb for being afraid (*yārēʾ*) and the Bible uses it of God in contexts where something
genuinely dangerous is present.

The best available account is that fearing God names **a settled orientation of the whole life
toward God as the one who is really there and really weighty** — an awe that produces conduct.
That is why the phrase so often sits inside a list of behaviours rather than feelings. Job 1:1 is
the pattern case: *"blameless and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from evil."*
The fear is named alongside integrity and beside a refusal, and in Job 1:5 it is shown as an
action — a father rising early to offer sacrifices for sins his children may not have committed.

In the wisdom books it becomes a technical term: the beginning, the first principle, and the
whole of wisdom. Proverbs 1:7, *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge"*; Job 28:28,
the closing line of the hymn to wisdom, *"Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart
from evil is understanding"* — which is Job 1:1 restated as a definition after twenty-seven
chapters of argument.

## The question the book of Job asks about it

Job is the one book of the Bible that makes the fear of God a problem rather than a premise, and
it does so with a single question. The accuser, told that Job fears God, replies:

> **1:9–10** “Does Job fear God for nothing? Haven’t you made a hedge around him, and around his
> house, and around all that he has, on every side? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his
> substance is increased in the land.”

The charge is not that Job's fear is fake. It is that it is **purchased** — that under a system in
which piety is reliably rewarded, no one can tell devotion from a good investment, and God himself
cannot know whether he is loved or paid for. The accuser is attacking the doctrine of
[[Retribution]] from the other end: the friends will use it to convict Job, but the accuser uses
it to point out that it makes disinterested faith unprovable and perhaps impossible.

The entire plot follows from that question. The hedge comes down so that the answer can be
observed. And what the book demonstrates is not that Job's fear survives intact and serene — he
curses the day of his birth, accuses God of hunting him like a beast, and demands a trial — but
that it survives *at all*, without the payments, and while shouting. See [[Job (person)]].

**The trembling at Sinai must not be equated automatically with settled obedience.** The
assembly promises obedience (19:8), then trembles at the storm and trumpet (19:16). The
narrative reports bodily fear and a real boundary, not an evaluation that every frightened
person is faithful. The distinction qualifies any simple opposition between terror and the
fear of God; the next pericope will state it directly in 20:20. See [[Israel at Mount Sinai]]
and [[Sinai and Zion - Exodus 19 and Hebrews 12]].

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 15:1** — the other side of the word, and its first appearance in Scripture on God's own
  lips: ***"Don't be afraid, Abram."*** *ʾAl-tîrāʾ*, the same verb *yārēʾ* the theme is built on, in
  the negative — the beginning of the formula that runs through the theophanies, the prophetic
  commissions (Isaiah 41:10; Jeremiah 1:8), and the annunciations and the empty tomb. It is worth
  holding beside the theme's own vocabulary rather than folded into it: the Bible commands the fear
  of God and forbids being afraid of him, with one verb, and Exodus 20:20 states the paradox flatly
  rather than resolving it. Genesis 15:1 also does not say Abram *was* afraid; the reassurance
  arrives before any fear is reported, and what follows in the same chapter is *"terror and great
  darkness"* falling on him (15:12) while God is doing him nothing but good. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].
- **Genesis 22:12** — *"now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son"*. The
  fear of God demonstrated by a withholding refused, and the first place the phrase is used as a
  verdict on a man.
- **Genesis 20:11; 42:18** — fearing God as the thing that makes a stranger safe to deal with. Its
  absence is what Abraham assumes about Gerar; its presence is what Joseph offers his brothers as
  a reason to trust him. In both, the phrase functions as a claim about **ethics**, not piety.
- **Job 1:1, 8, 9; 2:3** — the narrator's verdict, God's repetition of it twice, and the accuser's
  question about its motive.
- **Job 28:28** — [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]]: the hymn to wisdom ends where the book began.
- **Exodus 20:20** — the paradox stated outright at Sinai: *"Don't be afraid, for God has come to
  test you, and that his fear may be before you, that you won't sin."* Do not fear; fear.
- **Deuteronomy 10:12** — fear, walk, love, serve: the four verbs given as one demand.
- **Proverbs 1:7; 9:10; 8:13** — the beginning of knowledge and of wisdom, and defined as *"to hate
  evil"*. See [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]] and [[The Two Feasts]]: 9:10 closes the prologue on the
  motto that opened it, with *təḥillaṯ ḥoḵmâ* for 1:7's *rēʾšîṯ dāʿaṯ*, and pairs it with *"the knowledge
  of the Holy One"* (*daʿaṯ qədōšîm*, as at 30:3).
- **Proverbs 1:29** — [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]]: *"they hated knowledge, and didn't choose the
  fear of Yahweh"* — *wəyirʾaṯ YHWH lōʾ bāḥārû*. **The fear of Yahweh as a choice**, refusable; and
  the same speech ends with the listener free *"without fear of harm"* (1:33, *mippaḥad*), so the
  chapter sets *yirʾâ*, the fear chosen, beside *paḥad*, the dread that comes to those who did not
  choose it.
- **Psalm 111:10; 34:11; 25:14** — the same formula in worship, and the promise that *"the
  friendship of Yahweh is with those who fear him."*
- **Ecclesiastes 12:13** — *"Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of
  man"*, offered as the conclusion after everything else has been found to be vapour.
- **Isaiah 11:2–3** — the shoot from Jesse delighting in the fear of Yahweh, which makes it a
  messianic quality rather than merely a human duty.
- **Luke 1:50; Acts 10:2, 22** — carried into the New Testament, where *God-fearer* becomes a
  standing term for a Gentile attached to the synagogue, Cornelius the first named.
- **1 John 4:18** — *"There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear"*, the New
  Testament's sharpest-looking counter-statement, and one that uses *phobos* for punishment-dread
  rather than for the Old Testament orientation.
- **Hebrews 5:7; 12:28–29** — Christ heard *"because of his godly fear"*, and worship offered
  *"with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire"*: the phrase retained, not dissolved.
- **Exodus 1:17, 21 — the midwives.** Abraham assumed the fear of God was absent in Gerar (Genesis
  20:11); Joseph claimed it (42:18); Job embodied it (Job 1:1). **[[Shiphrah and Puah]] are the first
  in whom it is civil disobedience**: they fear God, and so do not fear the king. The reward —
  *bāttîm*, households — is the thing the king meant to prevent. See [[The Oppression in Egypt]].
- **Exodus 9:20, 30 — Pharaoh's servants, and Pharaoh.** At the warning of hail, *"those who feared
  Yahweh's word among the servants of Pharaoh"* bring their people and animals indoors and are spared;
  **the fear of God now runs through Pharaoh's own court.** Pharaoh confesses *"I have sinned"* but
  Moses tells him, *"I know that you don't yet fear Yahweh God"*, and the confession does not last
  (9:34). **Confession without fear is shown failing.** See [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]].

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:18–21

Moses tells the people not to be afraid and says that God's fear is to remain before them so that
they will not sin (20:20). The immediate contrast is between dread of being killed by further speech
(20:19) and an awareness of God that governs conduct. The danger is not dismissed: the people keep
their distance, while Moses approaches (20:21). The explanation of fear is given to the frightened
people themselves. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### God's terror sent ahead — Exodus 23:20–33

*"I will send my terror before you, and will confuse all the people to whom you come"* (23:27). The
word for *terror* is *ʾêmâ* (*ʾêmātî*, MAM), the word of Abram's covenant night (Genesis 15:12), of
the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15:16), and of Rahab (Joshua 2:9). Here God promises it in advance; the
peoples' fear is God's weapon rather than Israel's reverence. See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] and
[[Terror Has Fallen on Us - The Song of the Sea in Rahab's Mouth]].

**Correction (2026-10-23).** The entry for Genesis 35:5 below previously said the root *ḥ-t-t* of
*ḥittat ʾĕlōhîm* is the one used in Exodus 23:27 and Joshua 2:9. A check of the Hebrew (MAM, through
Sefaria) shows it is not: Exodus 23:27 and Joshua 2:9 have *ʾêmâ*, and Deuteronomy 11:25 has *paḥad*
and *môrāʾ*. The theme connection holds; the lexical claim did not, and the entry has been revised.

### The shining face — Exodus 34:29–35

Aaron and all Israel are afraid to come near the shining Moses (34:30). Moses calls them,
the leaders return, and then the people approach (34:31–32). This is fear at the visible effect
of divine encounter, not an explicit verdict on their moral reverence. Compare the frightened
assembly in Exodus 20:18–21: mediation makes hearing possible without making fear itself proof
of either faithfulness or rebellion.

See [[The Shining Face of Moses]].

### The motto of Proverbs — Proverbs 1:7

*"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge; but the foolish despise wisdom and instruction."* The
first proverb in the book and the first use of the divine name, at the close of a preface that has
offered wisdom to everyone. *Rēʾšît* can mean first in time or first in rank, and both senses fit: the
fear of Yahweh is where knowledge starts and what governs it. **Its opposite is contempt, not
ignorance** — the fool has been offered *"wisdom and instruction"* (1:2) and despises them. Job 28:28 goes
further, making the fear of the Lord wisdom itself rather than its beginning. Hebrew checked against
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*. See [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]] and [[Folly]].

### Understood at the end of the search — Proverbs 2:5

*"then you will understand the fear of Yahweh, and find the knowledge of God"* (2:5, *yirʾaṯ YHWH
wəḏaʿaṯ ʾĕlōhîm*). The fear of Yahweh and the knowledge of God stand in parallel, two sides of one
relation — the phrase *knowledge of God* is Hosea's too (Hosea 4:1; 6:6, WEB, checked against
bible-api.com). **The motto's direction is reversed**: at 1:7 the fear of Yahweh is where knowledge
begins; at 2:5 the search for wisdom is how one comes to understand the fear of Yahweh. The book needs
both — fear as starting point and as goal. See [[If You Seek Her as Silver]]. Source: Proverbs 2 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Fear Yahweh, and depart from evil — Proverbs 3:7–8

*"Don't be wise in your own eyes. Fear Yahweh, and depart from evil. It will be health to your
body"* (3:7–8). *Yərāʾ ʾeṯ-YHWH wəsûr mērāʿ* is Job 1:1's description of Job (*wîrēʾ ʾĕlōhîm wəsār
mērāʿ*) made a command, and the fear of Yahweh is set against self-assessed wisdom. The promise of
health is stated without exception; Job, who did both, was struck with sores (Job 2:7). **Tension
flagged, not resolved**; see [[Retribution]], [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]], and
[[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]]. Source: Proverbs 3 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### To hate evil — Proverbs 8:13

*"The fear of Yahweh is to hate evil"* (8:13, *yirʾaṯ YHWH śənōʾṯ-rāʿ*). After *"the beginning of
knowledge"* (1:7) and *"Fear Yahweh, and depart from evil"* (3:7), a definition: the fear of Yahweh
is a hatred. Wisdom names at once what she hates — pride, arrogance, the evil way, the perverse
mouth — so the fear of Yahweh and Wisdom's own dislikes coincide. Job 1:1's *"turned away from
evil"* and Job 28:28's *"To depart from evil is understanding"* describe the same posture as a
movement away; 8:13 describes it as an affection. See [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] and
[[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:27

Fear of Yahweh is said to prolong life, in contrast to the shortened years of the wicked. Job 1:1
commends precisely that fear, while Job 21:7 describes the wicked growing old. The promise and the
counter-observation remain side by side; the proverb is not a reliable calculation of an individual
lifespan.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Fortress, fountain, and an upright walk — Proverbs 14:2, 16, 26–27

Uprightness of walk *is* fearing Yahweh, and perversity despises him (2). *"In the fear of Yahweh is a secure fortress"* (26), and it is *"a fountain of life, turning people from the snares of death"* (27) — the sentence 13:14 spoke of *"The teaching of the wise"*, now said of the fear of Yahweh, which puts the motto of 1:7 as an equivalence. Verse 16's wise man *"fears and shuns evil"*, close in the WEB's English to Job 1:1; the Hebrew identity is not verified here. See [[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]].

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### Little with the fear of Yahweh; the fear that teaches — Proverbs 15:16, 33

*"Better is little, with the fear of Yahweh, than great treasure with trouble"* (16) ranks the fear of God above wealth, and *"The fear of Yahweh teaches wisdom. Before honor is humility"* (33) makes it the teacher rather than only the beginning (1:7; 9:10). See [[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]] and [[Better Is Little - The Better-Than Sayings of Proverbs]].

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### Departing from evil — Proverbs 16:6

*"By the fear of Yahweh men depart from evil"* (6), the second half of the atonement saying, joins the fear of God to the conduct Job 1:1 and 28:28 attach to it. Verse 17 makes departing from evil *"the highway of the upright"*.

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:23

Verse 23 joins fear of Yahweh to life, contentment, and freedom from trouble. **Contradiction if
read as an exceptionless guarantee:** Job 1:1, 8 calls Job God-fearing, while 1:13–19 and 2:7
narrate his losses and sores; 2:3 explicitly calls his ruin without cause. The wiki retains the
proverb's strong wording and Job's counterexample rather than making suffering evidence that fear
was absent. See [[Untouched by Trouble - Proverbs 19.23 and the Suffering of Job]] .

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

## Key passages

- [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] — Proverbs 19:23; Fear of Yahweh promises life and
  safety, explicitly in tension with Job’s undeserved affliction.

- [[The Plans of the Heart]] — Proverbs 16:6, 17.

- [[A Gentle Answer]] — Proverbs 15:16, 33.

- [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]] — Proverbs 10:27; Fear of Yahweh is said to prolong life, in
  contrast to the shortened years of the wicked.

- [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]] — Proverbs 1:1–7; the motto placed at the head of Proverbs, opposed by
  the fool's contempt

- [[The Shining Face of Moses]] — Exodus 34:29–35; Fear of approaching the shining mediator gives way to hearing.

- [[Job of Uz]] — the phrase in its first Joban form, as one of four terms in the narrator's
  verdict, and shown in practice in the sacrifices of 1:5
- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — **Job 1:9 is the only verse in the Bible that
  asks what the fear of God is *for*.** *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* — Hebrew *ḥinnām*,
  gratis, without return. Everywhere else in Scripture the fear of God is commanded, commended, or
  described; here a member of the heavenly court asks whether the thing has ever once existed
  unpurchased, and the rest of the book is the answer. Note what the question concedes: **nobody
  disputes that Job fears God.** The attack is on the motive, and therefore not really on Job — a God
  who can only be feared for pay has never been feared at all
- [[The Second Test]] — **the question quietly withdrawn.** God repeats the fourfold description at
  2:3, *"one who fears God, and turns away from evil"*, for the third and last time in the book — and
  the accuser's reply does not mention the fear of God at all. *"Skin for skin… all that a man has he
  will give for his life"* (2:4) asks what a man will trade, not what he loves without pay. **The
  second question is answerable and the first one is not, which is why the first one is the book**
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:11–13; **the theme's conspicuous absence, and the reason it is
  worth logging.** Three men arrive who will argue about Job for twenty-five chapters, and **not one
  of them ever raises the accuser's question.** They ask what Job did; they never ask what his piety
  is for. The one term the narrator, God, and the accuser all agree is the point of the book drops
  out of the dialogue the moment human beings take it over, and does not return until God speaks
- [[Noah and the Flood]] — Genesis 6:9's *"walked with God"* is the Genesis idiom for the same
  settled orientation, in a book that does not yet use this vocabulary
- [[Abram in Egypt]] — Genesis 12:10–20, where the phrase is absent but the reasoning behind it is
  the whole plot. Abraham supplies it retrospectively at 20:11, explaining the same ruse to
  Abimelech: *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place. They will kill me for my wife's sake."*
  That is a working assumption about foreigners, used to justify a deceit — and in both episodes it
  proves false. Pharaoh returns the wife and asks for nothing; Abimelech is told by God himself that
  he acted *"in the integrity of your heart"* (20:6). The theme's sharpest Genesis observation is
  here: the man who has the covenant misjudges who fears God, and the kings outside it behave better
  than he expected and better than he did.
- [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] — Genesis 15:1 and 15:12: the Bible's first *"Don't be
  afraid"*, and eleven verses later *"terror and great darkness"* falling on the man it was said to.
  The two halves of *yārēʾ* in one chapter, with no attempt to reconcile them
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:11, where the assumption is finally said out loud —
  *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place"* — **inside the very pericope that refutes it**. The
  king of Gerar addresses God as *ʾĂdōnāy*, argues from righteousness, is credited by God with
  integrity of heart, and tells his whole household at dawn — *"The men were very scared"*
  (20:8). Abraham is the only person in the chapter whose conduct the fear of God does not visibly
  govern, and he is the one who raises the subject
- [[The Binding of Isaac]] — Genesis 22:12, the theme's **decisive Genesis passage** and the only
  place in the book where God says of anyone that he fears God: *"For now I know that you fear God,
  since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me."* Three things follow from it.
  **First, the phrase is *yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm*, exactly what the narrator says of Job at 1:1, 1:8, and
  2:3** — and both books establish it the same way, by taking everything away while the reader
  watches and the man cannot see the frame. **Second, it is demonstrated rather than declared**: what
  the chapter produces is not a state of mind but an act, which is what this theme has meant since
  Job's sacrifices at 1:5. **Third, it lands on the man who had used the phrase to excuse himself.**
  At 20:11 Abraham explained the Gerar ruse with *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place"*, of
  a city that turned out to be full of it; two chapters later God says the words to him. See
  [[Testing]]
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — the exact inverse of 1:1, delivered as a verdict: *"This is the
  place of him who doesn't know God"* (18:21), spoken in a room containing one candidate and the
  wrong one
- [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] — Genesis 28:17, ***"How dreadful is this place!"*** — ***mah-nôrāʾ
  hammāqôm hazzeh***, the niphal of *yārēʾ*, *awe-inspiring*; the WEB's *dreadful* is the old sense,
  *full of dread*, not *bad*. **It is his first response to an unconditional promise**, and it comes
  before anything else he says about the place is true. The theme's Genesis instances until now have
  been fear **produced by** an act (Abraham at 22:12) or fear **claimed absent** in a foreign city
  (20:11); here it is simple proximity — nothing has been asked of him and nothing threatened, and
  the reaction to being addressed at all is dread
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:42, 53; ***paḥad yiṣḥāq***, ***the fear of Isaac*** —
  **a divine title occurring twice in Scripture and both times in this chapter**, and **the only name
  for God in the Bible built on a patriarch's fear rather than on a covenant, a place, or an
  attribute.** Jacob swears by it in preference to the joint formula Laban has just offered, which
  would have set *the God of Nahor* beside the God of Abraham. **The reading against Arabic *faḫd*,
  thigh and hence clan — *the Kinsman of Isaac* — is a proposal the wiki records and does not
  adopt.** *"Because I was afraid"* (31:31) is also, by Jacob's own account, why he ran
- [[Wrestling at the Jabbok]] — Genesis 32:7, 30; **the vocabulary runs the theme's usual pattern
  backward.** *Yārēʾ*, the ordinary verb, names Jacob's fear of a brother who turns out not to be a
  threat (32:7, 11) — a fear the chapter spends thirty verses on and never confirms was warranted.
  **The encounter that actually was dangerous, by the book's own logic — wrestling a figure Jacob
  will call God — produces no fear-vocabulary at all**, only a claim to have survived: *"my life is
  preserved"* (32:30). Contrast 28:17's *mah-nôrāʾ*, dread named as the immediate response to a far
  gentler theophany. **The chapter names fear where the danger is uncertain and states only survival
  where, on Jacob's own account, it was greatest**
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:5; ***ḥittat ʾĕlōhîm***, **a phrase
  that occurs nowhere else in the Bible.** *"A terror of God was on the cities that were around them,
  and they didn't pursue the sons of Jacob."* The idea, though not the root *ḥ-t-t*, is the conquest's
  — *"I will send my terror before you"* (Exodus 23:27, *ʾêmâ*), *"all the inhabitants of the land
  melt away"* (Joshua 2:9, *mûg*; MAM; corrected 2026-10-23, see above) — **spent here on a single family fleeing a town its own sons have just sacked.** Nobody asks
  for it, nobody is told about it, Genesis reports it in a subordinate clause, and **[[Jacob]] is
  never shown learning it happened.** It is the exact answer to his complaint at 34:30, which named no
  wrong and asked for nothing. **This is the fear of God as something God puts on other people**,
  distinct from every other use on this page
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:18; ***"Do this, and live, for I fear God"*** — **Joseph's only theological
  sentence in the chapter**, offered to frightened foreigners as a guarantee of restraint, in the same
  breath that keeps [[Simeon]] in chains. **It is the phrase's Genesis function exactly**: at 20:11
  Abraham said its absence was why he lied. **Joseph names God to his brothers once and names Pharaoh
  twice**
- **Genesis 50:19, 21 — *ʾal-tîrāʾû*, *don't be afraid*, twice**: in Genesis this is God's line
  (15:1; 21:17; 26:24; 46:3), and [[Joseph]] is the first human being in the book to say it —
  [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]]
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 4:6, *"Isn't your piety (yirʾâ) your confidence? Isn't the
  integrity of your ways your hope?"* **The accuser's question asked kindly.** Where the accuser
  asked whether Job's fear was bought (*"for nothing?"*, 1:9), Eliphaz assumes it should pay, and
  counsels Job to rely on the return. The book's whole test is whether Job's fear is anything other
  than a confidence, and the first piece of pastoral advice he receives is that it should be one
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:14, *"To him who is ready to faint, kindness should be
  shown from his friend; even to him who forsakes the fear of the Almighty."* **The fear of God
  comes back into the dialogue as the condition the friends have attached to their loyalty.**
  Whatever the verse's exact sense — the Hebrew is hard, and an older reading makes the man who
  withholds kindness the one who forsakes the fear — it sets friendship's *ḥesed* beside *yirʾâ* and
  says the first should not wait on the second
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — Job 11:15, 19; ***"you shall not fear"*** and ***"no one shall make you afraid"*** —
  fear promised away, on condition of repentance, to the man God called *"one who fears God"* (1:8)
  and who had said *"the thing which I fear comes on me"* (3:25). See
  [[None Shall Make You Afraid - The Covenant Blessing of Security]]
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 13:11, 21; ***"Shall not his majesty make you afraid, and his dread
  fall on you?"*** — Job telling the friends they do not fear God enough to stop lying for him, and
  then asking God to take his own terror away: *"don't let your terror make me afraid"* (13:21). The
  God-fearing man of 1:1 frightened of God, and warning God's defenders that they ought to be
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:14–16; **the fear of God renounced, and paying.** *"They tell
  God, 'Depart from us, for we don't want to know about your ways. What is the Almighty, that we
  should serve him? What profit should we have, if we pray to him?'"* The accuser's question of 1:9,
  *"for nothing?"*, asked by men who did the sum, concluded religion was unprofitable — and are
  thriving. Job distances himself at once: *"The counsel of the wicked is far from me"* (21:16). The
  fear of God that the prologue tested is shown here unrewarded in the one place the friends' theory
  most needs it rewarded.
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:4; *"Is it for your piety (yirʾâ) that he reproves you?"* —
  **the one question in the dialogue whose true answer is yes.** The test of the prologue exists
  because the accuser disputed Job's *yirʾâ* (1:9), and Eliphaz, who had called the same piety Job's
  *confidence* at 4:6, now treats it as the one possible cause he can rule out.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:15–16; *"Therefore I am terrified at his presence.
  When I consider, I am afraid of him … The Almighty has terrified me."* **Fear as terror, not
  reverence** — *bāhal* and *paḥad*, the *paḥad* of 3:25, not the *yirʾâ* the narrator credited Job
  with at 1:1. The man who wants to find God's seat is afraid of who sits in it, and does not
  withdraw the wish.
- [[Bildad's Third Speech]] — Job 25:2; *"Dominion and fear (paḥad) are with him."* **Dread as an
  attribute God possesses rather than a posture a man takes** — and the root of Job's *"I am afraid of
  him"* (23:15), handed back as a reason for silence.
- [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] — Job 28:28; ***"Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart from evil is
  understanding."*** **Not the *beginning* of wisdom, as in Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10, but wisdom itself** —
  the whole of what a human being is given, after a poem that has placed wisdom proper beyond reach. The
  words are 1:1's: *yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm wəsār mērāʿ* there, *yirʾat ʾădōnāy … wəsûr mērāʿ* here. **The hymn
  defines wisdom as the thing the accuser said Job had only for pay (1:9)**, and the divine title is
  *ʾădōnāy*, found nowhere else in the book. See
  [[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]]
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 37:1, 24; **the last human word before God speaks.** Elihu's heart
  *"trembles"* at the thunder (37:1), and his speech ends *"Therefore men revere him (yərēʾûhû). He doesn't
  regard (lōʾ-yirʾeh, does not see) any who are wise of heart"* (37:24) — *fear* and *see* played against
  each other, and 28:28's *"the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom"* restated as a warning. The narrator
  had said Job feared God (1:1); God regards him in the next verse (38:1).
- [[Behemoth and Leviathan]] — Job 41:9–11, 33; **fear argued from a creature to its maker.** *"None is so fierce
  that he dare stir him up. Who then is he who can stand before me?"* (41:10). Leviathan himself is *"made
  without fear"* (41:33) — the one creature with no fear in it, held up to teach the fear of God.
- [[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]] — Job 42:1–6; **fear for nothing.** The accuser's question was whether Job fears God *ḥinnām*
  (1:9). Job's last words are spoken diseased, childless, and on the ash-heap, before the restoration of
  42:10: confession, sight, and abasement, with nothing given back.
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:17, 21; **the fear that outranks the king's.** *"But the midwives feared God, and
  didn't do what the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the baby boys alive."* The first time in
  Scripture the fear of God is the ground for refusing a king's command, and the only reason the
  narrator gives for God's acting in the chapter: *"Because the midwives feared God, he gave them
  families."* Compare Acts 5:29; and Leviticus 25:43, where *fear your God* is set against Egypt's
  *harshness*
- [[The Birth of Moses]] — Exodus 2:1–10 with Hebrews 11:23; **the parents *"not afraid of the
  king's commandment"*** — the midwives' fear of God (1:17) restated as the absence of the fear of
  Pharaoh. Exodus itself names no fear in the passage
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:14; ***"Moses was afraid"*** — **the fear of a man**, which the midwives were
  praised for not having (1:17). Hebrews 11:27 says *"By faith, he left Egypt, not fearing the wrath of
  the king"*; either it means the exodus itself, or the motive beneath the flight. Both readings are
  recorded on the passage page and neither adopted
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:6; ***"Moses hid his face; for he was afraid to look at God."*** **The fear of 2:14
  turned the other way**: there it made Moses run from Pharaoh, here it makes him stay and cover his
  face. It is the fear of holiness, not of punishment — the ground has just been called holy — and it
  anticipates *"man may not see me and live"* (33:20)
- [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]] — Exodus 9:20–21, 30; **the fear that saves, found in Pharaoh's
  court.** ***"Those who feared Yahweh's word among the servants of Pharaoh made their servants and
  their livestock flee into the houses"*** — the first Egyptians in Exodus said to fear Yahweh, and
  spared by it; the rest *"didn't respect Yahweh's word"*, literally *did not set their heart* on it.
  And Moses to Pharaoh, after his confession: *"I know that you don't yet fear Yahweh God"* —
  **confession without fear, which lasts only until the storm stops** (9:34)
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:7; **"Don't you yet know."** Pharaoh's servants — some of whom *"feared
  Yahweh's word"* at the hail (9:20) — ask him *hăṭerem tēdaʿ kî ʾābədâ miṣrāyim*, *"Don't you yet know
  that Egypt is destroyed?"*: the *ṭerem*, *not yet*, of Moses' *"you don't yet fear Yahweh God"*
  (9:30). **The fear Moses said Pharaoh lacked, his own court now presses on him as knowledge** —
  [[Pharaoh's Servants (of the Exodus)]]
- [[The Crossing of the Sea]] — Exodus 14:10, 13, 31; **from fearing Egypt to fearing Yahweh, in one verb.**
  *"They were very afraid"* (*wayyîrəʾû*, 14:10) of the chariots; Moses' *"Don't be afraid"* (*ʾal-tîrāʾû*,
  14:13); *"the people feared (wayyîrəʾû) Yahweh"* (14:31) (WLC). **The fear changes object without changing
  word**
- [[The Song of the Sea]] — Exodus 15:11, 14–16; **fear in praise, and terror on the nations.** God is *"fearful in
  praises"* (*nôrāʾ*, Jacob's word at Bethel, Genesis 28:17); *"Terror (ʾêmâ) and dread (paḥad) falls on"*
  the nations — *ʾêmâ* falling as on Abram (Genesis 15:12), *paḥad* the word of *"the fear of Isaac"*
  (31:42, 53) (WLC). See [[Terror Has Fallen on Us - The Song of the Sea in Rahab's Mouth]].
- [[Jethro's Counsel]] — Exodus 18:21; **the first qualification for office.** *"Able men which fear God"* —
  *yirʾê ʾĕlōhîm*, the adjective of the angel's *"now I know that you fear God"* (Genesis 22:12; WLC) — set beside
  truth and hatred of *"unjust gain"*. A Midianite priest makes the fear of God a requirement for Israel's judges,
  as the midwives' fear of God (1:17) was the first resistance to Pharaoh
- [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] — Exodus 19:16–24; trembling and warnings in the presence of the deliverer

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:18–21; reassurance against death-terror accompanies fear
  intended to prevent sin
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; *"I will send my terror before you"*.
- [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]] — Proverbs 1:29; not choosing the fear of Yahweh is what hating knowledge means; *yirʾâ* chosen beside *paḥad* suffered (1:26–27, 33)
- [[If You Seek Her as Silver]] — Proverbs 2:5; the fear of Yahweh understood at the end of the search, in parallel with the knowledge of God
- [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]] — Proverbs 3:7–8; Job 1:1's two verbs as a command, with health promised
- [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] — Proverbs 8:13; *"The fear of Yahweh is to hate evil"*, the motto restated as a definition

## Related themes

- [[Folly]] — the refusal the motto names in its second line (Proverbs 1:7)

- [[Wisdom]] — of which this is called the beginning, the whole, and the definition
- [[Righteousness]] — the verdict that Job 1:1 pairs it with
- [[Retribution]] — the system that makes the accuser's question possible, since a fear that is
  always rewarded is a fear whose motive cannot be read
- [[Walking with God]] — Genesis's own idiom for the same thing, used of Enoch and Noah
- [[Sin]] — *"turned away from evil"*, the negative half of the definition in both Job 1:1 and
  Job 28:28
- [[Judgment]] — what the fear takes seriously, and why it is not reducible to respect
- [[Deception]] — Abraham's stated reason for the wife-sister ruse is that the fear of God is not in
  the place he is entering, and he is wrong both times

## Related people

- [[Job (person)]] — the man the phrase is used of more than any other, and the test case for
  whether it can be disinterested
- [[Noah]] — the pre-covenant parallel: righteous, blameless, walking with God
- [[God]] — the object, and in Job the one whose interest in the answer sets the plot going
- [[Abraham]] — who assumes its absence in two foreign kings, and acts on the assumption
- [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] — the first of the two kings, and the first to prove the assumption
  wrong
- [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] — the second, and the one against whom the assumption is stated
  in so many words while the chapter shows him doing the opposite
- [[Elihu the Buzite]] — whose last sentence is *"Therefore men revere him"* (Job 37:24)
