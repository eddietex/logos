# The Fear of God

## Definition / overview

The Old Testament's most compact name for right relation to God — and a phrase that resists the
two easy translations. It is not terror, because the people commended for it are not described as
frightened, and God repeatedly tells the people he is addressing not to be afraid in the same
breath. It is not mere reverence either, softened into respect, because the word is the ordinary
Hebrew verb for being afraid (*yārēʾ*) and the Bible uses it of God in contexts where something
genuinely dangerous is present.

The best available account is that fearing God names **a settled orientation of the whole life
toward God as the one who is really there and really weighty** — an awe that produces conduct.
That is why the phrase so often sits inside a list of behaviours rather than feelings. Job 1:1 is
the pattern case: *"blameless and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from evil."*
The fear is named alongside integrity and beside a refusal, and in Job 1:5 it is shown as an
action — a father rising early to offer sacrifices for sins his children may not have committed.

In the wisdom books it becomes a technical term: the beginning, the first principle, and the
whole of wisdom. Proverbs 1:7, *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge"*; Job 28:28,
the closing line of the hymn to wisdom, *"Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart
from evil is understanding"* — which is Job 1:1 restated as a definition after twenty-seven
chapters of argument.

## The question the book of Job asks about it

Job is the one book of the Bible that makes the fear of God a problem rather than a premise, and
it does so with a single question. The accuser, told that Job fears God, replies:

> **1:9–10** “Does Job fear God for nothing? Haven’t you made a hedge around him, and around his
> house, and around all that he has, on every side? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his
> substance is increased in the land.”

The charge is not that Job's fear is fake. It is that it is **purchased** — that under a system in
which piety is reliably rewarded, no one can tell devotion from a good investment, and God himself
cannot know whether he is loved or paid for. The accuser is attacking the doctrine of
[[Retribution]] from the other end: the friends will use it to convict Job, but the accuser uses
it to point out that it makes disinterested faith unprovable and perhaps impossible.

The entire plot follows from that question. The hedge comes down so that the answer can be
observed. And what the book demonstrates is not that Job's fear survives intact and serene — he
curses the day of his birth, accuses God of hunting him like a beast, and demands a trial — but
that it survives *at all*, without the payments, and while shouting. See [[Job (person)]].

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 15:1** — the other side of the word, and its first appearance in Scripture on God's own
  lips: ***"Don't be afraid, Abram."*** *ʾAl-tîrāʾ*, the same verb *yārēʾ* the theme is built on, in
  the negative — the beginning of the formula that runs through the theophanies, the prophetic
  commissions (Isaiah 41:10; Jeremiah 1:8), and the annunciations and the empty tomb. It is worth
  holding beside the theme's own vocabulary rather than folded into it: the Bible commands the fear
  of God and forbids being afraid of him, with one verb, and Exodus 20:20 states the paradox flatly
  rather than resolving it. Genesis 15:1 also does not say Abram *was* afraid; the reassurance
  arrives before any fear is reported, and what follows in the same chapter is *"terror and great
  darkness"* falling on him (15:12) while God is doing him nothing but good. See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].
- **Genesis 22:12** — *"now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son"*. The
  fear of God demonstrated by a withholding refused, and the first place the phrase is used as a
  verdict on a man.
- **Genesis 20:11; 42:18** — fearing God as the thing that makes a stranger safe to deal with. Its
  absence is what Abraham assumes about Gerar; its presence is what Joseph offers his brothers as
  a reason to trust him. In both, the phrase functions as a claim about **ethics**, not piety.
- **Job 1:1, 8, 9; 2:3** — the narrator's verdict, God's repetition of it twice, and the accuser's
  question about its motive.
- **Job 28:28** — the hymn to wisdom ends where the book began.
- **Exodus 20:20** — the paradox stated outright at Sinai: *"Don't be afraid, for God has come to
  test you, and that his fear may be before you, that you won't sin."* Do not fear; fear.
- **Deuteronomy 10:12** — fear, walk, love, serve: the four verbs given as one demand.
- **Proverbs 1:7; 9:10; 8:13** — the beginning of knowledge and of wisdom, and defined as *"to hate
  evil"*.
- **Psalm 111:10; 34:11; 25:14** — the same formula in worship, and the promise that *"the
  friendship of Yahweh is with those who fear him."*
- **Ecclesiastes 12:13** — *"Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of
  man"*, offered as the conclusion after everything else has been found to be vapour.
- **Isaiah 11:2–3** — the shoot from Jesse delighting in the fear of Yahweh, which makes it a
  messianic quality rather than merely a human duty.
- **Luke 1:50; Acts 10:2, 22** — carried into the New Testament, where *God-fearer* becomes a
  standing term for a Gentile attached to the synagogue, Cornelius the first named.
- **1 John 4:18** — *"There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear"*, the New
  Testament's sharpest-looking counter-statement, and one that uses *phobos* for punishment-dread
  rather than for the Old Testament orientation.
- **Hebrews 5:7; 12:28–29** — Christ heard *"because of his godly fear"*, and worship offered
  *"with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire"*: the phrase retained, not dissolved.

## Key passages

- [[Job of Uz]] — the phrase in its first Joban form, as one of four terms in the narrator's
  verdict, and shown in practice in the sacrifices of 1:5
- [[Noah and the Flood]] — Genesis 6:9's *"walked with God"* is the Genesis idiom for the same
  settled orientation, in a book that does not yet use this vocabulary
- [[Abram in Egypt]] — Genesis 12:10–20, where the phrase is absent but the reasoning behind it is
  the whole plot. Abraham supplies it retrospectively at 20:11, explaining the same ruse to
  Abimelech: *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place. They will kill me for my wife's sake."*
  That is a working assumption about foreigners, used to justify a deceit — and in both episodes it
  proves false. Pharaoh returns the wife and asks for nothing; Abimelech is told by God himself that
  he acted *"in the integrity of your heart"* (20:6). The theme's sharpest Genesis observation is
  here: the man who has the covenant misjudges who fears God, and the kings outside it behave better
  than he expected and better than he did.
- [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] — Genesis 15:1 and 15:12: the Bible's first *"Don't be
  afraid"*, and eleven verses later *"terror and great darkness"* falling on the man it was said to.
  The two halves of *yārēʾ* in one chapter, with no attempt to reconcile them
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — the exact inverse of 1:1, delivered as a verdict: *"This is the
  place of him who doesn't know God"* (18:21), spoken in a room containing one candidate and the
  wrong one

- [[Righteousness]] — the verdict that Job 1:1 pairs it with
- [[Retribution]] — the system that makes the accuser's question possible, since a fear that is
  always rewarded is a fear whose motive cannot be read
- [[Walking with God]] — Genesis's own idiom for the same thing, used of Enoch and Noah
- [[Sin]] — *"turned away from evil"*, the negative half of the definition in both Job 1:1 and
  Job 28:28
- [[Judgment]] — what the fear takes seriously, and why it is not reducible to respect
- [[Deception]] — Abraham's stated reason for the wife-sister ruse is that the fear of God is not in
  the place he is entering, and he is wrong both times

## Related people

- [[Job (person)]] — the man the phrase is used of more than any other, and the test case for
  whether it can be disinterested
- [[Noah]] — the pre-covenant parallel: righteous, blameless, walking with God
- [[God]] — the object, and in Job the one whose interest in the answer sets the plot going
- [[Abraham]] — who assumes its absence in two foreign kings, and acts on the assumption
- [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] — the first of the two kings, and the first to prove the assumption
