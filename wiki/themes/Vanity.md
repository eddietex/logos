# Vanity

## Definition / overview

The verdict of Ecclesiastes that challenges the lasting gain, satisfaction, and remembrance
human beings expect from their activity. In the opening, the WEB's vanity is not a description
of personal conceit: labor continues, nature recurs, the senses remain unsatisfied, and
generations pass from memory. Futility and transience describe aspects of this context without
settling every later use of the word.

The theme does not by itself mean that creation is evil or that every deed is morally
indifferent. Those claims go beyond Ecclesiastes 1:1–11.

## Development across Scripture

**Ecclesiastes 1:1–11.** [[Vanity of Vanities]] places the verdict before an unanswered question
about labor's gain. The poem tests human accomplishment against recurring nature and
forgetting. It gives the book its starting problem rather than summarizing every conclusion.

**Tension with Proverbs.** Proverbs 14:23 affirms profit in work; Proverbs 10:7 affirms the
blessed memory of the righteous. Ecclesiastes 1:3, 11 questions gain and denies enduring
remembrance. Practical fruit and permanent advantage may differ, but that distinction is an
interpretation and cannot erase the competing formulations. See [[Profit and Remembrance - Proverbs and Ecclesiastes 1]].

**Nature as a comparison.** Genesis 8:22's reliable seasons sustain life; Ecclesiastes 1:4–7
makes persistence the backdrop to passing generations. See [[The Earth Remains - Genesis and Ecclesiastes 1]].

**Ecclesiastes 1:12–18.** [[The Burden of Wisdom]] applies the verdict to *"all the works that
are done under the sun"* (1:14) and adds a companion phrase, *"a chasing after wind"*
(1:14, 17). The second verdict falls on the Preacher's study of wisdom, madness, and folly
itself, so the inquiry is not exempt from its own conclusion. The saying of 1:15, that the
crooked cannot be made straight, explains why seeing clearly does not produce gain.

Sources: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–18; Genesis 8:22; Proverbs 10:7; 14:23.

**Ecclesiastes 2:1–11.** [[The Trial of Pleasure]] announces the verdict before the evidence —
*"this also was vanity"* (2:1) — and confirms it after the catalogue of works, wealth, and
pleasure: *"all was vanity and a chasing after wind, and there was no profit under the sun"*
(2:11). The verdict does not deny the joy of 2:10; it denies that the joy remains as gain. See
[[Mirth and Its Verdict - Proverbs 14.13, 21.17, and Ecclesiastes 2.1-11]]. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 2:1–11.

**Ecclesiastes 2:12–17.** [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]] applies the verdict to wisdom's advantage in the face of death:
*"why was I then more wise?"*, and *"this also is vanity"* (2:15). The unit closes
with the full formula and a stated feeling, *"So I hated life"* (2:17). See
[[One Event Happens to Them All - Job 21.23-26 and Ecclesiastes 2.14-16]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:12–17.

### Labor left to another — Ecclesiastes 2:18–26

The verdict attaches to uncertain succession (2:19), skilled labor left to a nonworker
(2:21), and sorrow without nighttime rest (2:23). Enjoyment is then attributed to God's hand
(2:24), but 2:26 still ends in vanity and chasing after wind. Its final *this* may concern the
sinner's accumulation or the wider situation; the WEB does not expressly delimit it. The
verdict must be kept beside enjoyment, without quietly cancelling either statement.

See [[The Labor Left to Another]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:18–26.

### From dust to dust — Ecclesiastes 3:16–22

The verdict returns in 3:19, attached to the shared fate and breath of humans and animals.
Verse 20 explains their return to dust. The statement does not make every action morally
indifferent: God judges righteous and wicked (17). Nor does it cancel rejoicing in work (22).
Mortality, judgment, and enjoyment stand together without an explanation that dissolves them.

See [[From Dust to Dust]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–22; comparisons as identified above.

### Two are better than one — Ecclesiastes 4:4–12

The verdict is pronounced twice: over labor driven by envy (4) and over the man alone whose labor
has no end and whose eyes are not satisfied with wealth (7–8), which the Preacher also calls *"a
miserable business."* The verdict is not extended to companionship; verses 9–12 commend two over
one without calling it vanity.

See [[Two Are Better Than One]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:4–12; the passage page
records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### The youth and the old king — Ecclesiastes 4:13–16

The verdict falls on popularity and succession, not on wisdom. Verse 13's ranking of the poor wise
youth stands; what is *"vanity and a chasing after wind"* (16) is that the countless people around
him are followed by those who *"shall not rejoice in him."* See [[The Youth and the Old King]]. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 4:13–16.

### Many dreams and many words — Ecclesiastes 5:7

For the first time the verdict falls on religious speech: *"in the multitude of dreams there are
vanities, as well as in many words"* (7). The plural *vanities* is the book's keyword *heḇel*
(Mechon-Mamre's Masoretic text, read 2026-10-27). The verse sets against them the command *"you
must fear God"*, which is not called vanity.

See [[Let Your Words Be Few]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:1–7; the passage page records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### Wealth and enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 5:8–20

The explicit vanity verdict attaches to the lover of silver who is not satisfied (10).
Verses 15–16 ask again about profit when nothing can be carried from life, describing labor
for the wind. Yet 18–20 end with enjoyment rather than a fresh vanity refrain. The change
of emphasis does not revoke the earlier judgment: receiving good in given days and failing
to retain labor's product can both be true.

See [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20;
comparisons as identified on the passage page.

### Riches without enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

Vanity marks wealth another consumes (2), the stillborn child's coming (4), and the
closing comparison of sight and wandering desire (9). The uses do not turn the child
into a moral offender or human vanity into personal conceit. Even the better-than saying
of verse 9 is followed by the verdict, so it supplies no simple escape from the problem
of unfilled appetite (7).

See [[Riches Without Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9; comparison
sources are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Who knows what is good — Ecclesiastes 6:10–12

Many words create vanity, and the question of profit follows (11). The designation
also qualifies the life spent like a shadow (12). Here the theme joins fruitless
multiplication of speech to passing days, without declaring every utterance evil
or every experience of good unreal. The question of good remains beside the earlier
affirmations of enjoyment in 3:12–13 and 5:18.

See [[Who Knows What Is Good]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:10–12; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

### The house of mourning — Ecclesiastes 7:1–14

The vanity verdict falls on the fool's laughter, like thorns crackling under a pot (7:6). The
surrounding sayings rank wisdom, mourning, and patience above their opposites, so 7:1–14 is
not a verdict of vanity on everything; it discriminates.

See [[The House of Mourning]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:1–14; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

### My days of vanity — Ecclesiastes 7:15

The Preacher sets his observation of the righteous who perish *"in my days of vanity"*
(7:15) — the vanity word applied to the span of his own life, as 6:12 spoke of the days of
a vain life.

See [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29; comparison sources and Hebrew checks identified on the passage page.
