# Folly

## Definition / overview

Folly in the Old Testament is wisdom's opposite, and it is a moral condition before it is an
intellectual one. The fool is not someone who lacks information. Proverbs introduces him in its
first antithesis as someone who has been offered wisdom and refuses it: *"The fear of Yahweh is the
beginning of knowledge; but the foolish despise wisdom and instruction"* (Proverbs 1:7). The
operative verb is *despise*. Folly is a stance toward instruction, and so — because instruction
begins in the fear of Yahweh — a stance toward God.

Hebrew has several words for the fool, and Proverbs uses them as a graded vocabulary rather than as
synonyms. The glosses below are standard lexical descriptions, offered as orientation rather than as
fixed definitions; the words overlap in use.

- **The simple** — *petî*, plural *pətāʾyim* (Proverbs 1:4, 22). The untaught and impressionable:
  not yet a fool, open to anything, and therefore the person the book is most anxious to reach.
  The preface promises *"prudence to the simple"* (1:4).
- **The fool** — *ʾĕwîl* (1:7) and *kəsîl* (1:22; 10:1). The man who has heard and does not
  listen; *"The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but he who is wise listens to counsel"*
  (12:15). *"A fool has no delight in understanding, but only in revealing his own opinion"* (18:2).
- **The mocker** — *lēṣ* (1:22). Folly hardened into contempt, who *"delight[s] … in mockery"*.
- **The *nābāl*** — the word for the fool of Psalm 14:1, *"The fool has said in his heart, 'There
  is no God'"*, and of a moral and religious senselessness that shades into outrage.

**The sharpest sign of folly in Proverbs is self-assurance.** The fool is right in his own eyes
(12:15), *"hot headed and reckless"* (14:16), and repeats his folly *"as a dog that returns to his
vomit"* (26:11). The book's dry joke on the subject is *"Even a fool, when he keeps silent, is counted
wise"* (17:28) — which the wiki's Job pages have already had occasion to quote against men who did
not keep silent.

## Development across Scripture

- **Proverbs 1:1–7** — [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]]: the book's first antithesis, in its motto.
  What verse 2 offers — *ḥokmâ ûmûsār*, wisdom and instruction — is what verse 7's fools despise. The
  contrast between wise and foolish is set up as a choice before a single saying has been read.
- **Proverbs 1:8–19** — [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]]: the first lecture is addressed to the simple at their weakest
  point. *"If sinners entice you"* — *yəpattûkā*, from *p-t-h*, the root that (on the standard lexical
  account) also gives *pətāʾyim*, *the simple*: the simple are the enticeable, and the first danger the
  book names is not stupidity but suggestibility (Hebrew checked against Sefaria's *Miqra according to
  the Masorah*).
- **Proverbs 1:22, 32** — [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]]: Wisdom crying out to the simple, the
  mockers, and the fools, each paired with its own vice — *pətāyim* who love *peṯî*, *lēṣîm* who
  delight in *lāṣôn*, *kəsîlîm* who hate knowledge — and their end: *"the backsliding of the simple
  will kill them. The careless ease of fools will destroy them."* *Məšûḇâ*, *backsliding*, is from
  *šûḇ*, the *"Turn"* of 1:23: folly here is a turning, the wrong way (Hebrew checked against
  Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*).
- **Proverbs 9:13–18** — [[The Two Feasts]]; Folly personified as a woman, *"loud, undisciplined, and
  knows nothing"* (9:13; *ʾēšeṯ kəsîlûṯ*, *a woman of folly*, and *pəṯayyûṯ*, *simpleness*, for
  *undisciplined*), whose feast competes with Wisdom's. She sits at her door in the same high places,
  calls the simple with Wisdom's own words (9:16 repeats 9:4), and offers *"Stolen water"* and *"Food
  eaten in secret"*; her guests are *"in the depths of Sheol"*. She takes the strange woman's features
  — *loud* (7:11), at her door (5:8), bound for Sheol — and the wiki leaves open whether she is that
  woman or a wider figure including her. The mocker (*lēṣ*) who answers reproof with hatred (9:7–8) is
  folly's other face.
- **Proverbs 26:4–5** — [[Answer a Fool]]: *"Don't answer a fool according to his folly"* beside
  *"Answer a fool according to his folly"*: the book's own admission that dealing with folly takes
  judgment, not a rule.
- **Job 2:10** — [[The Second Test]]: *"You speak as one of the foolish women would speak"*
  (*hannəbālôt*). Job does not call his wife a fool; he says she has spoken like one.
- **Job 5:2–3** — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]: *"resentment kills the foolish man, and jealousy kills
  the simple … I have seen the foolish taking root"*. Eliphaz uses Proverbs' own vocabulary —
  *ʾĕwîl* and *pōteh* — and applies the portrait of the fool to Job.
- **Job 42:8** — [[The Restoration of Job]]: God tells the friends he will not *"deal with you
  according to your folly"* (*nəbālâ*). **The men who applied the fool's portrait to Job are the ones
  whose speech God calls folly.**
- **Psalm 14:1; 53:1** — *"The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.'"* Folly as practical
  atheism.
- **Matthew 7:26** — the *"foolish man, who built his house on the sand"*: the one who hears and does
  not do, which is Proverbs 1:7's fool exactly.
- **Luke 12:20** — *"You foolish one, tonight your soul is required of you"*: the rich man whose
  planning left God out.
- **1 Corinthians 1:18–25** — the cross as *"foolishness"* to the world, and *"the foolishness of God
  is wiser than men"* (1:25): the category turned inside out.

Scriptural quotations outside Genesis, Exodus, Job, and Proverbs above are WEB, checked against
bible-api.com (2026-10-25); the Hebrew words for *Proverbs 1:7, 1:22, Job 2:10, 5:2–3, 42:8*, and
*Psalm 14:1* were checked against Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### The promotion that is shame — Proverbs 3:21–35

The final contrast of Proverbs 3:35 sets the glory inherited by the wise against the
shame that becomes the promotion of fools in the WEB. Verse 34 has already turned the
mockers' contempt back upon them. Folly is evaluated by its outcome before God, not by
the apparent advantage that makes the violent man enviable in verse 31.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### The fool's late confession — Proverbs 5:11–14, 23

Proverbs 5 puts the fool's confession in his own mouth, spoken at the end: *"How I have hated
instruction, and my heart despised reproof; neither have I obeyed the voice of my teachers"*
(5:12–13). It is 1:7's fool, who despises instruction, and the refusers of 1:29–30, now
admitting it too late, *"at the brink of utter ruin, among the gathered assembly"* (5:14). He dies
*"for lack of instruction"* and goes astray *"in the greatness of his folly"* (5:23, *ʾiwwaltô*).
See [[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 5; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

### Void of understanding — Proverbs 6:32

*"He who commits adultery with a woman is void of understanding"* (6:32): *ḥăsar-lēḇ*, *lacking
heart* — the phrase Proverbs 7:7 will give the young man who walks toward the strange woman's house.
The folly is a failure to reckon: he *"destroys his own soul"*, and the reproach *"will not be wiped
away"* (6:32–33). See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read
from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

The young man of Proverbs 7 is *ḥăsar-lēḇ*, *"void of understanding"* (7:7), the description 6:32
gave the adulterer and 9:16 will give Folly's guest. He is not called wicked: he is one of the
*simple* (*pəṯāʾyim*, 7:7), and his folly is that he walks the way to her house at nightfall,
follows her *"immediately"* (7:22), and *"doesn't know that it will cost his life"* (7:23) — the
words 9:18 repeats of the guest of Folly. 7:22b, *"as a fool stepping into a noose"*, is textually
difficult in the Hebrew; see the passage page. See [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 7; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Fools invited — Proverbs 8:5

*"You simple, understand prudence. You fools, be of an understanding heart"* (8:5). In 1:22 Wisdom
rebuked the simple, mockers, and fools; in 8:5 she invites them, offering the simple the *ʿormâ*,
*prudence*, the preface promised them (1:4). The fool, *kəsîl*, is not written off: he is told to
acquire *lēḇ*, a heart, the organ the young man of 7:7 lacked. See [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:1, 8, 10, 13–14, 18, 21, 23

The foolish son grieves his mother; chatter, slander, refusal of understanding, and pleasure in evil
give folly a social cost. The repeated falling fool (8, 10) is contrasted with the person willing to
learn.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:12, 22, 29

Despising a neighbor shows lack of understanding. The gold ring in a pig’s snout exposes the
incongruity of beauty without discretion, and the household troubler inherits wind. The fool
becoming servant to the wise is a proverbial reversal, not an explanation of every enslaved person’s
circumstances.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:1, 11, 15–16, 23

Hating reproof, chasing fantasies, trusting one’s own verdict, and broadcasting annoyance or
foolishness give folly recognizable conduct. The opening insult targets refusal to learn, not
intellectual disability. The wise person’s willingness to listen supplies the counterexample.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### The fool's desire — Proverbs 13:16, 19–20

The fool *"exposes folly"* (16) and harms his companions (20). Verse 19 gives folly a motive: the fool also longs, but *"fools detest turning from evil"* — what he cannot give up is the evil itself.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### The house torn down and the credulous simple — Proverbs 14:1, 3, 7–9, 15–18, 24, 29

The foolish woman tears down her house *"with her own hands"* (1); the fool's talk earns the rod (3); folly is deceit (8), credulity (15), recklessness (16), and a quick temper (17, 29). The simple *"inherit folly"* (18), and folly is its own crown (24).

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### The gushing mouth — Proverbs 15:2, 5, 7, 14, 20–21

The fool's mouth *gushes out* folly (2) and *feeds on* it (14); he despises his father's correction (5) and his mother (20); and folly *"is joy to one who is void of wisdom"* (21). Folly here is an appetite as well as an error.

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### Folly as its own punishment — Proverbs 16:22

*"The punishment of fools is their folly"* (22): the consequence is not added from outside but is the condition itself.

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Worse than a bereaved bear — Proverbs 17:10, 12, 16, 21, 24–25, 28

A hundred lashes reach a fool less than a rebuke reaches the wise (10); better *"a bear robbed of her cubs"* than *"a fool in his folly"* (12); his money cannot buy wisdom (16); his eyes *"wander to the ends of the earth"* (24); his father has no joy and his mother bitterness (21, 25). Only silence makes him seem wise (28).

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### Airing his own opinion — Proverbs 18:2, 6–7, 13

*"A fool has no delight in understanding, but only in revealing his own opinion"* (2). His lips bring
strife and his mouth invites beatings (6); his mouth is *"his destruction"* (7); and answering before
hearing is *"folly and shame to him"* (13). See
[[He Who Answers Before He Hears - Proverbs 18.13, 17 and Elihu's Waiting]].

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:1–3, 10, 13, 25, 29

The foolish person subverts his own way and then rages against Yahweh (3), a diagnosis of this
person's conduct rather than an explanation of every sufferer's anger. Delicate living does not suit
a fool (10), and a foolish son is his father's calamity (13). Verse 25 does not say that flogging
makes the scoffer wise: the simple observer learns, while an understanding person learns from
rebuke. This distinction prevents treating punishment and wisdom as automatic equivalents.

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:1, 3, 4, 13, 25

The person led astray by drink is not wise (1), and every fool quarrels (3). Neglected plowing
and love of sleep bring need (4, 13), while delayed thought about a vow makes commitment a trap
(25). These are particular failures of judgment and timing, not a diagnosis of everyone who
experiences poverty or difficulty.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### The wanderer and the scoffer — Proverbs 21:16, 20, 24

The man who wanders from understanding comes to rest among the departed spirits (16); the fool
swallows what the wise stored (20); the proud man's name is scoffer (24).

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### Folly in the child — Proverbs 22:3, 15

The simple walk into the danger the prudent sees (22:3), and *"Folly is bound up in the heart of a
child"* (22:15). Folly is treated as a default condition of the young, to be driven out by
discipline, not as an incurable nature.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### Words wasted on a fool — Proverbs 23:9

*"Don't speak in the ears of a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your words"* (23:9). The preface of
the collection had sent the pupil out to give *"sound answers"* (22:21); this verse names the audience not
worth answering. 26:4–5 complicates the rule; see below.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11; Proverbs 26:4–5.

### The fool in the gate — Proverbs 24:7–9

*"Wisdom is too high for a fool: he doesn't open his mouth in the gate."* Where the town deliberates,
the fool has nothing to say. Then *"The schemes of folly are sin. The mocker is detested by men"*
(24:9) — *tôʿăḇaṯ ləʾāḏām*, *an abomination to man*, the noun Proverbs usually pairs with Yahweh
(Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, read 2026-10-26).

See [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:3–22.

### The man void of understanding — Proverbs 24:30

The sluggard's vineyard belongs to *"the man void of understanding"*, *ʾāḏām ḥăsar-lēḇ* (24:30) —
the phrase of the *"young man void of understanding"* at 7:7. Folly here is not a scheme but neglect,
and it is read off the state of the man's land.

See [[The Field of the Sluggard]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:23–34.

### Answering the fool — Proverbs 26:1–12

Chapter 26 opens with the longest run on the fool in the book: *kəsîl* in eleven of its first twelve
verses. Honour does not fit him, like *"snow in summer"* (1), and giving it to him is like binding a
stone in a sling (8). He is to be driven like a horse or donkey: *"a rod for the back of fools"* (3).
Sending a message by him is *"cutting off feet and drinking violence"* (6). Twice, in the same Hebrew
words, *"a parable in the mouth of fools"* is a lame leg or a thorn in a drunkard's hand (7, 9): the
book's own word for itself, *māšāl*, is useless or dangerous in his mouth. *"As a dog that returns to
his vomit, so is a fool who repeats his folly"* (11), the line 2 Peter 2:22 quotes. Over it all stand
two contradictory commands, side by side: *"Don't answer a fool according to his folly, lest you also
be like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes"* (4–5). Each names
a real danger, and the reader must judge which is greater in a given case — the qualification 23:9
anticipated. The run ends below the fool: *"Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope
for a fool than for him"* (12). See
[[The Dog Returns to His Vomit - Proverbs 26.11 and 2 Peter 2.22]] and
[[Wise in His Own Eyes - Proverbs 3.7, 26.12, Isaiah 5.21, and Romans 12.16]].

See [[Answer a Fool]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 26.

### Ground in a mortar — Proverbs 27:3, 12, 22

Chapter 27 has three fool sayings, none in a run. *"A fool's provocation is heavier than both"* stone
and sand (3), with *kaʿas*, the fool's vexation of 12:16. *"The simple pass on, and suffer for it"*
(12), repeated from 22:3. And the hardest: *"Though you grind a fool in a mortar with a pestle along
with grain, yet his foolishness will not be removed from him"* (22). The fool here is *ʾĕwîl*, not
chapter 26's *kəsîl*. 22:15 had said the rod *"drives it far"* from a child; this is the adult in whom
nothing reaches the folly, not even the pounding that husks grain.

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12:16; 22:3, 15; 27; Hebrew from Sefaria.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:7, 26

The companion of gluttons shames his father; the person who trusts in himself is a fool. The
contrast in verse 26 is between self-trust and walking in wisdom. It addresses refusal of a wiser
standard, not a prohibition of every exercise of personal judgment.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Correction and public justice — Proverbs 29

The fool disrupts a dispute through rage or mockery and vents anger. Yet the hasty speaker has
still less hope (20), repeating the comparison applied to self-assured wisdom in 26:12 without
making the two faults identical.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 29:9, 11, 20; the passage page records the
cross-reference sources and interpretive limits.

### The fool filled with food — Proverbs 30:22, 32

A fool *"filled with food"* is one of the reversals the earth cannot bear (22): plenty does not
make him wise, it makes him worse. The closing command addresses anyone who *"has done foolishly in
lifting up yourself"* — the remedy is a hand over the mouth (32).

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

### Madness and folly as objects of study — Ecclesiastes 1:17

In Proverbs folly is chiefly something to avoid. The Preacher applies his heart *"to know madness
and folly"* (1:17) together with wisdom, as part of one inquiry. The text does not say he
practiced folly here; that begins with the experiment of 2:1–3 in [[The Trial of Pleasure]]. The study
too is judged *"a chasing after wind."* See [[The Burden of Wisdom]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:17; 2:1–3.

### Folly laid hold of, wisely — Ecclesiastes 2:2–3

The Preacher says of laughter, *"It is foolishness"* (2:2), and sets out *"how to lay hold of folly"*
with his heart *"yet guiding me with wisdom"* (2:3). Folly is now practiced, not only studied, but
as an experiment under wisdom's supervision. The purpose is stated: to see what is good for the
sons of men to do. See [[The Trial of Pleasure]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:2–3.

### The fool's darkness and the common end — Ecclesiastes 2:13–16

The fool *"walks in darkness"* while the wise man's eyes are in his head (2:14), and folly is ranked
below wisdom as darkness below light (2:13). Yet the fool's end is the wise man's: the same event, the
same forgetting (2:14–16). The fool here is the measure the wise man fears to be no better than,
not the object of a warning. See
[[One Event Happens to Them All - Job 21.23-26 and Ecclesiastes 2.14-16]].

See [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:12–17.

### Labor left to another — Ecclesiastes 2:18–26

The Preacher does not know whether his successor will be wise or a fool (2:19). The contrast
now concerns control over what someone else has made, not just the shared death of 2:16.
The successor is not declared foolish; uncertainty is the grievance.

See [[The Labor Left to Another]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:18–26.

### Two are better than one — Ecclesiastes 4:4–12

*"The fool folds his hands together and ruins himself"* (4:5). The WEB's *ruins himself* renders a
phrase reading literally *eats his own flesh* (Mechon-Mamre's Masoretic text, read 2026-10-27).
The folded hands are the sluggard's of Proverbs 6:10 and 24:33; see
[[Folding the Hands - Proverbs 6.10, 24.33, and Ecclesiastes 4.5]]. The fool is the foil for
4:6, which cautions against the opposite excess of labor.

See [[Two Are Better Than One]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:4–12; the passage page
records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### The king who no longer takes warning — Ecclesiastes 4:13

The old king of 4:13 is *"foolish"*, and the only folly named is that he *"doesn’t know how to
receive admonition any more."* This is Proverbs' test of the fool (12:15; 15:5, 32) applied to a
ruler, with the added note that the capacity once existed. See [[The Youth and the Old King]] and
[[The Ear That Listens to Reproof - Proverbs and Ecclesiastes 4.13]]. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 4:13; Proverbs 12:15; 15:5, 32.

### Fools in God's house — Ecclesiastes 5:1–4

The fool appears three times in the unit. Fools offer sacrifice without knowing that they do
evil (1); a fool's speech is known by its multitude of words (3); and God *"has no pleasure in
fools"*, the motive given for paying a vow without delay (4). Folly here is a failure of
attention in worship: speaking and offering instead of listening.

See [[Let Your Words Be Few]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:1–7; the passage page records the comparison sources and Hebrew checks.

### Riches without enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

Verse 8 places the fool beside the wise person in a question about advantage after
unfilled appetite (7). It supplies no new foolish act and does not identify the deprived
wealthy man of verse 2 as a fool. Wisdom remains preferred in 2:13, while its power to
secure satisfaction is questioned here. The comparison resembles 2:14–16's shared end
without declaring every difference between wise and foolish conduct meaningless.

See [[Riches Without Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9; comparison
sources are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The house of mourning — Ecclesiastes 7:1–14

The fool's heart is in the house of mirth (7:4); his song is worse than the wise man's rebuke
(5), and his laughter is the crackling of thorns under a pot — vanity (6). Anger rests in the
bosom of fools (9). Folly can also be produced: extortion makes the wise man foolish (7).

See [[The House of Mourning]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:1–14; comparison sources identified on the passage page.

### Folly as madness — Ecclesiastes 7:17, 25

*"Don’t be too wicked, neither be foolish. Why should you die before your time?"* (7:17).
The Preacher seeks *"to know that wickedness is stupidity, and that foolishness is madness"*
(25), joining moral and intellectual failure. Verse 17 does not commend a moderate amount of
folly; the paired warnings of 16–17 are grounded in the fear of God (18).

See [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29; comparison sources and Hebrew checks identified on the passage page.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

Madness resides in human hearts during life (3), alongside evil. The passage moves from
shared mortality to this diagnosis, but supplies no clinical description and no separate
named fool. Its command to work vigorously (10) is not a claim that skill can overcome
time and chance (11).

See [[One Event Comes to All]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:1–12; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### The forgotten wise man — Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

A little folly outweighs wisdom and honor as dead flies spoil the perfumer's oil (10:1).
The fool's heart is at his left hand (2), and on the road his understanding fails and he tells
everyone that he is a fool (3), matching Proverbs 12:23 and 13:16. Whether 10:3 means
self-exposure or calling others fools is noted on the passage page; this page follows the WEB's
plain sense of self-exposure.

See [[The Forgotten Wise Man]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.
