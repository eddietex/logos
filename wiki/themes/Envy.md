# Envy

## Definition / overview

The Bible's word is ***qānāʾ***, and it is one word for two things English separates: **envy** — what
you feel about somebody else's good — and **jealousy** — what you feel about what is yours being
taken. The Hebrew does not distinguish them, which is why the same root that describes the Philistines
watching Isaac's harvest, Rachel watching her sister's children, and Joseph's brothers watching their
father's affection also becomes, at Exodus 20:5, **a name for God**: *ʾĒl qannāʾ*, a jealous God.

**Its first occurrence in Scripture is Genesis 26:14**, and Genesis then runs it through the two
family crises that carry the rest of the book. What the theme tracks is a root that begins as a
neighbour's resentment of a foreigner's prosperity, becomes the engine of the Jacob cycle, and ends
up as a divine attribute — with the Bible never explaining how one word can do all three.

## Development across Scripture

**Genesis 26:14 — the first *qānāʾ*, and it is about a harvest.**

> **12** Isaac sowed in that land, and reaped in the same year one hundred times what he planted.
> Yahweh blessed him.
> **13** The man grew great, and grew more and more until he became very great.
> **14** He had possessions of flocks, possessions of herds, and a great household. The Philistines
> envied him.

***Wayqanʾû ʾōtô Pəlištîm.*** The verb arrives at the end of a three-verse crescendo in which the
root *gādal* is used four times and the narrator has already said *"Yahweh blessed him"*. **The
theme's opening statement in the Bible is therefore that blessing provokes envy**, and the sequence
is immediate and physical: the next verse has the wells filled in with earth, and the verse after
that has the king telling him to leave. **Nobody in the chapter says the envy is wrong.** Genesis
records it as what happened, exactly as it records the lie two verses earlier without comment. See
[[Isaac in Gerar]].

**The scene has a shape the rest of the Bible keeps.** The envied party is a resident alien; the
envying party is the host population; and what the envy produces is not violence against the person
but the destruction of what he lives by. **The wells are stopped rather than seized** — the asset
destroyed rather than transferred — which is eviction rather than economics, and it is the same
logic a king of Egypt applies to the same family at Exodus 1:9–11 with the same comparative in his
mouth. See [[Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt]].

**Genesis 30:1 — inside the family, and it is about children.** *"When Rachel saw that she bore Jacob
no children, Rachel envied her sister."* The same verb, now between two women in one household, and
the object is not property but fertility. **What it produces is the second use of a slave as a
surrogate in the book**, after Sarai's at 16:2. See [[Barrenness]].

**Genesis 37:11 — and it is about love.** *"His brothers envied him, but his father kept this
saying in mind."* The verse stands between the dreams and the pit, and it is the narrator's own
diagnosis of what the brothers are doing. **Genesis has now used the root three times: for a
neighbour, a sister, and ten brothers**, and each time it is followed directly by an act that removes
the envied party — expelled, out-borne, sold. Acts 7:9 gives the same reading: *"The patriarchs,
moved with jealousy against Joseph, sold him into Egypt."*

**Then the turn.** *"You shall not bow yourself down to them, nor serve them, for I, Yahweh your God,
am a jealous God"* (Exodus 20:5; and again 34:14, where *Jealous* is given as a name). **The word for
what the Philistines felt about a harvest is the word God uses of himself about worship.** The logic
of the transfer is that jealousy is proper where the claim is proper: God's *qināʾ* is not resentment
of another's good but refusal to share what is his. **Nothing in the Bible reconciles the two senses;
they are the same word used of different parties.** See [[The Names of God]].

**And the tenth commandment names the appetite rather than the act** — *"You shall not covet"*
(Exodus 20:17), a different root, *ḥāmad*, and the only commandment in the Decalogue that legislates
a wish. The Wisdom books then work the theme as a physical condition: *"envy is the rottenness of the
bones"* (Proverbs 14:30); *"wrath is cruel, and anger is overwhelming; but who is able to stand before
jealousy?"* (Proverbs 27:4); and Ecclesiastes 4:4 makes it the whole motor of human labour — *"every
labor and every skillful work, that for it a man is envied by his neighbor."*

**The New Testament keeps it in the vice lists** (Romans 1:29; 1 Corinthians 3:3; Galatians 5:21;
James 3:14–16; 1 Peter 2:1), and Mark 15:10 makes it the diagnosis of the crucifixion: *"he perceived
that for envy the chief priests had delivered him up."* Paul then takes the Exodus sense and applies
it to himself — *"I am jealous over you with a godly jealousy"* (2 Corinthians 11:2) — and to Israel's
future in Romans 11:11–14. **The one root, running from a well in the Negev to a Roman governor's
judgment about a motive.**

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:5, 17

The WEB calls God jealous in the demand for exclusive worship (20:5), then forbids coveting the
neighbor's household (20:17). These uses must not be treated as the same moral act: the first
asserts God's claim on worship, the second forbids reaching after what belongs to another. The
English comparison does not establish an identity of Hebrew vocabulary. The reach into desire is the
point Paul takes up in [[Coveting and Love - The Commandments in Romans 7 and 13]]. See
[[The Ten Commandments]].

### Do not admire the violent man — Proverbs 3:21–35

Proverbs 3:31 forbids envy of a violent man and immediately forbids choosing his ways.
Here envy can lead to imitation, not only resentment of another's advantage. The answer
is Yahweh's evaluation of the perverse and upright (3:32–35). The passage does not deny
that violence can look successful; Job 21:7–9 separately exposes the danger of assuming
that visible prosperity proves righteousness.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### The husband's jealousy — Proverbs 6:34

*"For jealousy arouses the fury of the husband"* (6:34, *qinʾâ ḥămaṯ-gāḇer*). Here *qinʾâ* is
plainly jealousy, not envy — a husband's claim on his own wife, the passion of the *"spirit of
jealousy"* in Numbers 5:14 (*rûaḥ-qinʾâ*) and of Song of Solomon 8:6, *"Jealousy is as cruel as
Sheol"*. Proverbs gives it no verdict; it is the danger the adulterer brings on himself. The same
lecture forbids the other desire, coveting (*taḥmōḏ*, 6:25), which Exodus 20:17 keeps distinct. See
[[The Lamp of the Commandment]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra
according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Envy rots the bones — Proverbs 14:30

*"The life of the body is a heart at peace, but envy rots the bones"* (30): envy is pictured as a bodily corruption, the opposite of a settled heart.

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### Envy of sinners — Proverbs 23:17–18; 24:1–2

Proverbs forbids envy of the wicked twice in one block. *"Don't let your heart envy sinners; but
rather fear Yahweh all day long"* (23:17) gives the alternative to envy as a direction of the heart,
and its reason as a future: *"there is a future hope, and your hope will not be cut off"* (23:18).
*"Don't be envious of evil men; neither desire to be with them"* (24:1) names where envy goes next —
into wanting their company. In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read 2026-10-26) both
verbs are *qinnēʾ*, the root traced on this page; 24:19 opens with the same four words as Psalm 37:1.
See [[Do Not Fret Because of Evildoers - Proverbs 23.17, 24.19, and Psalm 37.1]].

See [[Do Not Envy Sinners]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 23:12–24:2.

### Do not fret — Proverbs 24:19–20

*"Don't fret yourself because of evildoers; neither be envious of the wicked: for there will be no
reward to the evil man; and the lamp of the wicked shall be snuffed out."* The fourth prohibition in
Proverbs (3:31; 23:17; 24:1), opening with the words of Psalm 37:1, and placed straight after the ban
on gloating at an enemy's fall (24:17–18): the wicked are neither envied while they stand nor gloated
over when they fall. See [[Do Not Fret Because of Evildoers - Proverbs 23.17, 24.19, and Psalm 37.1]].

See [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:3–22.

### Who can stand before jealousy — Proverbs 27:4

*"Wrath is cruel, and anger is overwhelming; but who is able to stand before jealousy?"* (27:4). The
nouns are *ḥēmāh* and *qinʾāh*, the pair of 6:34, where the wronged husband's jealousy *"won't spare in
the day of vengeance"*. Wrath and anger are a flood that passes; jealousy is ranked above both because
it does not. 27:20 adds the eye that is *"never satisfied"*, the appetite behind envy, set beside Sheol;
see [[Never Satisfied - Proverbs 27.20, 30.15-16, Ecclesiastes 1.8, and Habakkuk 2.5]].

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6:34; 27:4, 20; Hebrew from Sefaria.

## Key passages

- [[Do Not Withhold Good]] — Proverbs 3:21–35; The violent man must be neither envied nor imitated.

- [[Isaac in Gerar]] — Genesis 26:12–16; **the first *qānāʾ* in the Bible**, provoked by a hundredfold
  harvest, and answered by wells filled in with earth and an eviction
- Genesis 4:3–8 — Cain and Abel: the thing itself, four chapters before the word for it. *"Yahweh
  respected Abel and his offering, but he didn't respect Cain and his offering"*, and the narrator
  supplies no vocabulary at all — see [[Cain and Abel]]
- Genesis 21:9–10 — Sarah watching Ishmael at Isaac's feast, and the expulsion she demands; again the
  thing without the word — see [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]]
- [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]] — Genesis 30:1, **the Bible's second *qānāʾ***, between two sisters
  in one household, with fertility rather than property as its object. **What the passage adds to the
  theme is that the envy outlives the shortage**: [[Leah]] is not barren, has borne four sons, and
  produces a slave anyway the moment she *"saw that she had finished bearing"* (30:9) — **matching her
  sister's move rather than solving a problem.** The object of *qānāʾ* here is standing, not children,
  and the passage's own word for the contest is [[Rachel]]'s: *"I have wrestled **with my sister**…
  and have prevailed"* (30:8)
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:4, 5, 8, 11; **the third *qānāʾ* in Genesis, and the only one
  the narrator builds up to in stages.** *Hated* (37:4), *hated all the more* (37:5), *hated all the more*
  again (37:8), and then ***wayqannəʾû-bô***, *they envied him* (37:11) — **four escalations in eight
  verses, each one following a fresh display of what the envied party has.** The object is neither property
  nor fertility but **a father's preference**, and the wiki notes that the pattern of the other two holds
  exactly: **the envy is immediately followed by the removal of the envied party** — the wells stopped, the
  sister out-borne, and here a brother sold. **Acts 7:9 supplies the diagnosis Genesis 37 declines to
  state**: *"the patriarchs, moved with jealousy against Joseph, sold him into Egypt"* — *zēlōsantes*, the
  Septuagint's word for this verb. See
  [[Sold for a Servant - Joseph's Sale in Psalm 105 and Acts 7]]
- Exodus 1:9–11 — the same fear of a prospering household, and a labour draft instead of an eviction
- Exodus 20:5; 34:14; Deuteronomy 4:24 — *ʾĒl qannāʾ*, and *Jealous* given as a divine name
- Numbers 5:11–31 — the law of jealousy, the one ordeal in the Torah, built on the same root
- Proverbs 14:30; 27:4; Ecclesiastes 4:4 — the Wisdom books on what it does to a person
- Psalm 73:3 — *"I was envious of the arrogant, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked"*; see
  [[The Prosperity of the Wicked]]
- Mark 15:10; Acts 7:9; Romans 1:29; Galatians 5:21; James 3:14–16 — the New Testament's use
- 2 Corinthians 11:2 — the sense turned around and claimed as proper
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:1–2; **the complaint and the changed face, in the exact
  sequence that followed Isaac's enrichment.** *"Jacob has taken away all that was our father's"*
  from Laban's sons, and then *"the expression on Laban's face… was not toward him as before"* —
  against 26:13–14, where *"the man became great"* is followed immediately by *"the Philistines
  envied him."* **The last face Genesis reported falling belonged to [[Cain]]** (4:5), also over an
  offering and a brother's success
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:4, 38; **the favouritism of 37:3 has simply relocated one place down.** Jacob
  withholds [[Benjamin]] by name — whom the narrator calls *"Joseph's brother"* rather than Jacob's son
  — and ends the chapter saying ***"he only is left"*** **in front of the other ten.** Nothing in
  twenty-two years has changed the household's basic arrangement, **and Genesis does not say what the
  ten make of hearing it**
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:34; **the pit's cause reconstructed at a dinner table.**
  *"Benjamin's portion was five times as much as any of theirs"* — **one son of [[Rachel]], conspicuously
  preferred, in front of the other ten** — is 37:3–4 restaged with the same ingredients by the man it was
  done to. **Genesis does not say [[Joseph]] is watching for the reaction**, and it does not record one:
  *"They drank, and were merry with him."* **The silence of the ten is the chapter's most important
  non-event**, and 44:1–2 only works as a test if this was one

- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:13, 20; **the answer to 37:3–4, and Genesis does
  not say so.** The favouritism is restated in court — *"a child of his old age … and his father loves
  him"* (44:20), **the narrator's own causal chain from 37:3–4 repeated by one of the brothers it
  excluded** — **and this time it is offered as a reason to spare the favourite rather than as a
  reason to be rid of him.** Then ten men with a free road home turn their donkeys around for him
  (44:13). **The envy that opened the cycle is answered by an act the text records without a single
  adjective**

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:22, 24; **the cause of Genesis 37 is reproduced in the
  chapter that resolves it, by the man it was used against.** [[Joseph]] gives the ten one change of
  clothing each and [[Benjamin]] **five, with three hundred pieces of silver** — ***śəmālôt***, the
  same class of object as the coat of 37:3, in the same asymmetry, to the other son of [[Rachel]].
  **Then he says *"see that you don't quarrel on the way"*** (45:24), **which is either an afterthought
  or an admission that he knows exactly what he has just handed them.** Genesis records no quarrel and
  offers no comment on either verse; see [[Five Changes of Clothing - The Coat Given Again]]
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:9–12; *"more and mightier than we"* — Abimelech's comparative (Genesis 26:16),
  and *wayyāquṣû*, *they were grieved*, a dread with revulsion in it

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:5, 17; God’s claim to exclusive worship differs from coveting
  a neighbor’s household
- [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] — Proverbs 6:25, 34; coveting a neighbour's wife forbidden, and
  the husband's *qinʾâ*
- [[Do Not Envy Sinners]] — Proverbs 23:17; 24:1; Sinners and evil men not to be envied, nor their company wanted.
- [[Through Wisdom a House Is Built]] — Proverbs 24:19; Do not fret because of evildoers, nor envy the wicked.
- [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]] — Proverbs 27:4, 20; Jealousy that no one can stand before; the eyes never satisfied.

## Related people

- [[Isaac]] — the first person in Scripture said to be envied, and the reason given is a harvest
- [[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]] — who acts on his people's envy by expelling the household and then
  comes back to sign a treaty with it
- [[Cain]] — the case the theme is defined against, four chapters before its vocabulary arrives
- [[Sarah]] — who watches a boy play at a feast and demands an expulsion
- [[Esau]] — envied nothing and lost everything; the one brother in Genesis whose resentment the text
  attributes to a grievance rather than to *qānāʾ*
- [[Jacob]] — around whom the book's other two occurrences of the root turn, as husband and as father
- [[Rachel]] — the subject of the second *qānāʾ* in Genesis, and the wife whose rank the passage
  before it establishes
- [[Leah]] — the object of it, and the sister who has what is envied
- [[Joseph]] — the third object of the root in Genesis, and the only one who is envied for being loved
  rather than for what he owns or has borne
