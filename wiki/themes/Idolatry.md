# Idolatry

## Definition / overview

### Covenant warnings and remembered promises — Leviticus 26:14–46

The ban of 26:1 comes back as what falls: high places, incense altars, and *the bodies
of your idols*, with Israel's dead on top of them (30). The idols are described in the same word as
corpses.

See [[Covenant Warnings and Remembered Promises]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Blessings for obedience — Leviticus 26:1–13

Idols, carved image, pillar, and figured stone head the chapter (1), with *ʾĕlîlîm* as at 19:4 (MAM).
Figured stone (*ʾeben maśkît*) is listed for the land itself: the concern is what may stand in it.
See [[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]].

See [[Blessings for Obedience]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 26.

### Penalties and separation — Leviticus 20

Giving offspring to Molech defiles the sanctuary and profanes the holy name (2–5). Concealment by
the community does not prevent divine action. Consulting mediums brings divine cutting off (6),
while being a medium or wizard carries stoning (27); these are distinct cases.

See [[Penalties and a People Set Apart]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 20; comparisons as cited.


**The worship, possession, or consultation of other gods, and forbidden images used in worship** — and in Genesis
it is remarkable chiefly for how little the book says about it.

**There is no commandment against it in Genesis, no denunciation of it, and no character who is
condemned for it.** The explicit image prohibition comes in Exodus 20:4–5; the prophets' whole
vocabulary of *idols*, *vanities*, *abominations* and *whoredom* is not yet available. **What Genesis
gives instead is a set of objects, a set of names, and silence.**

**The book's method here is the method it uses everywhere: it reports and declines to judge.** It says
Laban divined (30:27) and does not call it sin. It says Rachel stole her father's *teraphim* (31:19)
and never says why or what happened to them. It has Laban say ***my gods*** (31:30) in a sentence
where he has twice elsewhere named Yahweh correctly, and prints it without comment. **Only once does
anyone in Genesis act against foreign gods**, and it is Jacob, at 35:2–4, burying them under an oak.

## Development across Scripture

### Field and community — Leviticus 19:19–37

Enchantments and sorcery are prohibited (26), and seeking mediums and wizards brings defilement
(31). These are forbidden religious practices; the passage does not name a particular foreign
deity or explain the origin of each custom.

See [[Holy Living in Field and Community]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19.


### Holiness and the neighbor — Leviticus 19:1–18

Israel must neither turn to idols nor make molten gods (4). The prohibition belongs to the call
for the congregation to reflect Yahweh’s holiness, not only to regulations for priests.

See [[Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 19:1–18.


### Forbidden unions and the land — Leviticus 18:1–30

The stored WEB forbids giving children as a sacrifice to Molech and profaning God’s name (21). It specifies neither a ritual procedure nor that the children are firstborn. Molech is treated as an object of forbidden worship rather than a new human person.

See [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18.

### Sacrifice and blood — Leviticus 17:1–16

The purpose of redirecting field sacrifices is that Israel cease sacrificing
to goat idols (5–7). The WEB describes this allegiance as prostitution. The passage
does not supply names or biographies for these objects of worship.

See [[The Place of Sacrifice and the Life in the Blood]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 17; comparisons as cited.


### In Genesis

- **The gods of the fathers beyond the River.** Joshua 24:2 looks back on this family and says it
  plainly — *"your fathers lived of old time beyond the River… and they served other gods"* — **which
  Genesis itself never says about Terah, Nahor, or Laban.** The book leaves the household's religion
  unstated and lets Joshua supply the verdict centuries later. See [[Terah]] and
  [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]].
- **The *teraphim*, at 31:19.** **The first household gods in the Bible**, taken by a matriarch,
  hidden under a camel saddle, never recovered, and never explained. **Their purpose in Genesis is
  left blank** — the standing proposals are value, an inheritance claim, and protection — and the
  narrative's only interest in them is that they were not found. See
  [[The Teraphim - Household Gods in Genesis and After]] and [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]].
- **Divination, at 30:27 and 44:5, 15.** ***Nāḥaš*** enters Scripture in Laban's mouth and returns in
  Joseph's cup, **and both times the practitioner is right.** Leviticus 19:26 and Deuteronomy 18:10
  will forbid it outright. See [[Divination in Genesis - Laban's Nahash and Joseph's Cup]] and
  [[Prophecy and Vision]].
- ***"The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge between us"***
  (31:53), **with a plural verb in the Hebrew.** On one reading it is the Bible's one glimpse of the
  religion of the branch that stayed in Mesopotamia; on another the plural is grammatical agreement
  and means nothing. **The wiki adopts neither.** See [[The Names of God]].
- **The one purge, at 35:2–4.** *"Put away the foreign gods that are among you"* — Jacob collects them
  along with the earrings and buries them under the oak at [[Shechem (city)]]. **Genesis does not
  say whose they were, does not name the *teraphim*, and does not say the burial was commanded.**
  *(Not yet ingested.)*
- **Egypt, throughout the Joseph cycle** — a court, a priesthood at On, and a priest's daughter
  married into the covenant line (41:45), **all reported without a word of religious comment.**

### After Genesis

**The subject becomes the central charge of the rest of the Old Testament**, and the terms are set at
Sinai: other gods and the making of idols are prohibited (Exodus 20:3–5). The golden calf (Exodus 32) makes the failure the nation's founding one; Deuteronomy builds
its whole covenant sanction on it; **Judges runs the cycle seven times**; and the prophets convert it
into a marriage metaphor — Israel as an unfaithful wife (Hosea 1–3; Jeremiah 3; Ezekiel 16, 23).

**The mockery is a later development, not a Genesis one.** *"They have mouths, but they don't speak"*
(Psalm 115:5), Isaiah's carpenter who burns half a log and worships the other half (Isaiah 44:9–20),
and Jeremiah's scarecrow in a cucumber field (Jeremiah 10:5) **all argue that idols are nothing.**
**Genesis never argues this.** Its *teraphim* are simply objects a family owned, and the narrative's
one anxiety about them is whether they will be found under a woman sitting on a saddle.

**The New Testament keeps both registers**: idols are *"nothing in the world"* (1 Corinthians 8:4) and
also what stands behind real spiritual danger (1 Corinthians 10:19–21), **and it extends the word
inward** — greed *"which is idolatry"* (Colossians 3:5; Ephesians 5:5) — and ends with the plainest
imperative in the epistles: *"Little children, keep yourselves from idols"* (1 John 5:21).
**Job 31:26–28 describes the oldest idolatry without an idol.** *"If I have seen the sun when it
shined, or the moon moving in splendor, and my heart has been secretly enticed, and my hand threw a kiss
from my mouth"* — no image, no shrine, a gesture at the sky made in secret. It is the worship
Deuteronomy 4:19 forbids, and the kiss is the act 1 Kings 19:18 and Hosea 13:2 name. Job counts it *"an
iniquity to be punished by the judges; for I should have denied the God who is above"* (31:28): **the
idolatry of the heart is denial of God**, and it is the very thing the accuser predicted of Job (1:11;
2:5). See [[Job's Oath of Innocence]].

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:3–6

The reserve of Genesis gives way to explicit prohibition: Israel is to have no other gods, make no
idol or image, and neither bow to nor serve them (20:3–5). God describes himself as jealous and
joins the warning across generations to loving kindness for those who love him and keep his commands
(20:5–6). **Scope needs care:** the image prohibition stands in a worship context, while the same
book later prescribes cherubim for the ark (25:18–20, checked in raw; see [[Cherubim]] and
[[The Ark, the Table, and the Lampstand]]). Treating this as a ban on
every representational object would leave that prescription unexplained. See
[[The Ten Commandments]].

### Gods alongside him — Exodus 20:22–23

The second statement of the image prohibition argues from hearing: *"You yourselves have seen that I
have talked with you from heaven"*, and therefore *"gods of silver or gods of gold"* are not to be
made *"to be alongside me"* (20:22–23; WLC *ʾittî*, *with me*). **The rival it forbids is a
companion as much as a replacement.** The altar of earth or uncut stone follows at once (20:24–25),
in place of an image. Exodus 32:4 tests the point with a calf called the gods that brought
Israel up from Egypt. See [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]].

**The first commandment receives its penalty at Exodus 22:20.** *"He who sacrifices to any god,
except to Yahweh only, shall be utterly destroyed"* — *yoḥŏrām*, devoted to destruction (*ḥērem*,
MAM 22:19). Beside it stands the sorceress, whom Israel may not *"allow … to live"* (22:18), with the
root of Pharaoh's *"sorcerers"* (7:11). See [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

### Other gods not invoked — Exodus 23:1–19

Between the commands on rest and the festival calendar, Exodus 23:13 requires attention to all God's
instructions and forbids invoking other gods or letting their names be heard from the mouth. The
three feasts that follow are addressed to Yahweh (23:14–17). The placement binds the agricultural
calendar to exclusive allegiance rather than treating harvest as an occasion for rival worship.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

### The peoples' gods as the snare — Exodus 23:20–33

The Book of the Covenant opens with no gods of silver or gold (20:23), and closes on the gods of the
land's peoples. Israel must not bow to them or serve them, must demolish their pillars, may make no
covenant with them or their gods, and may not let the peoples live in the land, *"for if you serve
their gods, it will surely be a snare to you"* (23:24, 32–33). The stated danger is religious. See
[[The Angel Sent Ahead]] and [[Little by Little - The Hornet, the Snare, and the Land Not Taken]].

**The tension between prohibited images and commanded figures extends to textiles.** Exodus 20:4
forbids making an idol or image in the commandment's worship context; 26:1, 31 commands cherubim on
the curtains and inner veil. Both commands are preserved. The latter does not authorize worship
of the figures, and the former cannot be applied as a ban on every representational object without
explaining the cherubim. Exodus 26 gives no argument resolving the tension. See [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]] and [[Cherubim]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Aaron’s feast is expressly proclaimed to Yahweh, yet the calf and its worship violate the
prohibitions already given (32:4–8; 20:3–5, 23). Idolatry here includes forbidden worship using
Yahweh’s name. The image is destroyed at Sinai; Jeroboam’s later calves are different objects. See
[[The Golden Calf Retold - Deuteronomy 9, Psalm 106, and Acts 7]] and [[They Sat Down to Eat and Drink - Exodus 32 in First Corinthians 10]]. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

The renewed covenant expressly prohibits cast idols after the calf episode (34:17; compare 32:4,
8). Verses 12–16 trace the danger from treaties to sacrificial meals and marriages that draw
Israel's sons into other worship; altars, pillars, and Asherah poles are to be destroyed. The
prostitution language describes allegiance to other gods and does not establish a particular
sexual rite. Divine jealousy here concerns exclusive worship (34:14), rather than simply envy of
another's possessions. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

The calf uses collected gold and Aaron's craftsmanship against the commanded way (32:2–8).
The sanctuary receives donated gold and skilled labor for what Yahweh commanded (35:5, 10, 22,
29). Material, expense, and enthusiasm alone do not determine the character of worship. This is
a narrative comparison, not an assertion that every donor's motive is disclosed or that the
calf's destroyed gold is reused. See [[Gold for the Calf and Gold for the Sanctuary]].

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The enclosure made — Exodus 36:8–38

After the calf has been condemned (32:4–8), cherubim are made on the sanctuary curtains and
veil (36:8, 35), fulfilling the instructions of 26:1, 31. This sharpens the existing tension
with the image prohibition in 20:4–5: the narrative condemns the calf but commissions these
figures. It does not describe their worship or offer a general resolution of every question
about representational art. The distinction is retained without erasing either command.

See [[The Tabernacle Built]].

### The furnishings made — Exodus 37:1–29

After the golden calf is condemned (32:4–8), gold cherubim are made as prescribed
(25:18–20; 37:7–9). The tension with the image prohibition of 20:4–5 is real and retained:
the narrative forbids images and their worship while commissioning these figures. Their
worship is not narrated, and no general theory of religious art is offered. The contrast
concerns commanded use; it does not make gold itself either evil or proof of acceptable worship.

See [[The Furnishings Made]]. Source: the cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-24.

### The work completed — Exodus 39:32–43

Moses’ sight of the calf had led to anger and the breaking of the tablets (32:19);
his sight of the finished sanctuary work now leads to blessing (39:43). The comparison concerns
what has been made in relation to Yahweh’s command, not gold or craft skill as inherently evil
or automatically acceptable. The later approval does not withdraw the earlier condemnation.
See [[Gold for the Calf and Gold for the Sanctuary]].

See [[The Work Completed]]. Source: cited passages checked in the stored WEB Genesis and Exodus
texts, 2026-10-25.

## Related themes
- [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] — Genesis 35:2–4; **the only religious reform any
  patriarch conducts, and God did not ask for it.** The command was *arise, go up, live there, build
  an altar*; Jacob's reply to his household opens with a fifth imperative — *"Put away the foreign
  gods that are among you, purify yourselves, change your garments"* — in the vocabulary Exodus
  19:10, 14 will use for Sinai. **He hides them rather than destroying them**, under the oak at
  Shechem, where the household gives them up. Moses later destroys the calf below Sinai
  (Exodus 32:20); Josiah burns bones on the altar at Bethel (2 Kings 23:15–16). **And Genesis never says whose gods they were**, four chapters after telling us
  [[Rachel]] stole her father's. The earrings surrendered with them are *nəzāmîm* — **the word of
  Exodus 32:2–3**, where the same ornaments are collected and made into a calf, whereas a later, different calf is placed at Bethel (1 Kings 12:28–29)
