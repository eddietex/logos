# Wealth and Possessions

## Definition / overview

**Genesis is a book about property, and it never once says whether property is good.**

Livestock, silver, gold, slaves, tents, wells, a cave, a field, a coat. The narrative is full of
inventories, and **the inventories are almost always the evidence of a blessing** — *"Yahweh has
blessed my master greatly, and he has become great. He has given him flocks and herds, silver and
gold, male servants and female servants, and camels and donkeys"* (24:35). **That sentence is the
theme in miniature**: a list of goods offered as proof of divine favour, by a servant, to persuade a
family to hand over a daughter.

Three things frame it:

1. **Wealth in Genesis is nearly always acquired abroad, from a foreigner, and under a shadow.**
   Abram leaves Egypt rich because he passed his wife off as his sister (12:16). Jacob leaves Paddan
   Aram rich because he out-bred his employer. **The book reports the enrichment and the method in
   the same breath and grades neither.**
2. **It is the standard cause of separation.** *"Their substance was great, so that they couldn't
   dwell together"* (13:6) is the reason Abram and Lot part; the wells of Genesis 26 are contested
   until Isaac walks away from three of them; Laban's sons at 31:1 say *"Jacob has taken away all
   that was our father's"*. **Every generation's property produces a boundary.**
3. **And there is a counter-current running the whole length of it.** Abram refuses the king of
   Sodom's goods so that no man can say *"I have made Abram rich"* (14:23). Abraham insists on paying
   four hundred shekels for a cave he is offered free (23:11–16). **The men who are richest in the
   book are also the ones who most conspicuously refuse gifts**, and both refusals are about who gets
   the credit.

**What Genesis never does is moralise.** There is no warning against riches, no praise of poverty,
and no character who is admired for having little. **The nearest the book comes to a verdict is
structural**: possessions are what a promise looks like when it starts arriving, and they are what
the promise is repeatedly mistaken for.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 12:5, 16; 13:1–2** — the first inventory in the Bible. Abram leaves Haran with
  ***rəkûš***, *substance*, acquired there; comes out of Egypt with sheep, cattle, donkeys, slaves,
  and camels **handed over by a Pharaoh he deceived**; and is then described as ***kābēd məʾōd***,
  *very heavy* — *"rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold."* **The adjective is *weight*, and it
  is the same root as *glory*.** See [[Abram in Egypt]].
- **Genesis 13:5–12** — the first consequence. *"The land was not able to bear them, that they might
  dwell together"*, and the herdsmen quarrel. **Abram's answer is to give away the choice** — the
  senior man lets the junior pick, and the junior picks by looking. See [[Abram and Lot Separate]].
- **Genesis 14:21–24** — the first refusal, and the reason given is attribution: *"I will not take a
  thread nor a sandal strap nor anything that is yours, **lest you should say, 'I have made Abram
  rich'**."* **The one time in Genesis a man states why he will not be enriched.** See
  [[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].
- **Genesis 15:14** — property enters the covenant. The nation that goes into slavery *"will come out
  with **great substance**"*, ***rəkûš gādôl*** — **the same noun as 12:5**, promised to a man who
  has just been told his descendants will be slaves for four hundred years. See
  [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].
- **Genesis 20:14–16** — compensation. Abimelech hands over sheep, oxen, slaves, and **a thousand
  pieces of silver** to the man who deceived him, and Genesis lets the transaction stand as a
  vindication of the deceived party rather than of the deceiver. See [[Abraham and Abimelech]].
- **Genesis 23:3–20** — the one purchase. Four hundred shekels of silver, *"current money with the
  merchants"*, weighed out in public for a field and a cave. **The only land the patriarchs ever own
  is a grave, and they pay full price for it in front of witnesses.** See
  [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] and [[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].
- **Genesis 24:10, 22, 35, 53** — wealth as argument. Ten camels, a gold ring and two bracelets
  produced before a single question is asked, an inventory recited as the third sentence of the
  servant's case, and *"jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and clothing"* given to the bride with
  *"precious things"* to her brother and mother. **The negotiation for Rebekah is conducted in
  goods**, and the family's decision is announced immediately after the goods appear. See
  [[A Wife for Isaac]] and [[Laban]].
- **Genesis 25:5–6** — inheritance managed. *"Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac"*, and the sons
  of the concubines are given **gifts** and sent east, away from the son who gets the estate. See
  [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]].
- **Genesis 26:12–16** — the only yield figure in the Bible's patriarchal narratives: **a hundredfold
  in a famine year**, on land Isaac does not own, followed by *"possessions of flocks, and
  possessions of herds, and a great household"*, envy, stopped wells, and an expulsion. **Wealth here
  is explicitly a blessing (*"Yahweh blessed him"*) and explicitly the cause of the trouble.** See
  [[Isaac in Gerar]].
- **Genesis 30:25–43** — the most detailed acquisition in the book. Jacob refuses a wage
  (*"you shall not give me anything"*), takes the odd-coloured animals instead, and ends
  ***wayyipṛōṣ hāʾîš məʾōd məʾōd*** — *the man burst out exceedingly* — **with the verb of the Bethel
  promise** (28:14) and the six-noun inventory of 12:16 and 24:35. **Three generations, three foreign
  patrons, and the same list.** See [[Jacob's Flocks]].
- **Job 1:3; 1:21; 42:10–12** — the theme's one full-dress theological treatment in the wiki so far.
  Job is introduced by his inventory — *"the greatest of all the children of the east"* — loses it in
  four messengers' worth of verses, and answers *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away. Blessed be
  the name of Yahweh."* **The friends then argue the inverse**, that possessions track righteousness,
  and the book spends thirty-five chapters on the claim. See [[Job of Uz]],
  [[Zophar's Second Speech]], and [[The Prosperity of the Wicked]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: Jacob's present to Esau and *"I have enough"* /
  *"I have all"* (32:13–21; 33:9–11); Joseph telling his family *"don't concern yourselves about your
  belongings"* (45:20) and then buying the whole of Egypt for Pharaoh (47:13–26); the plunder of the
  Egyptians (Exodus 12:35–36); the laws of gleaning, release, and jubilee (Leviticus 19:9–10; 25);
  *"you shall remember Yahweh your God, for it is he who gives you power to get wealth"*
  (Deuteronomy 8:17–18); Proverbs on riches and integrity; Ecclesiastes 5:10–15; the rich fool and
  the rich young ruler (Luke 12:13–21; Mark 10:17–27); *"the love of money is a root of all kinds of
  evil"* (1 Timothy 6:10).

## Key passages

- [[Abram in Egypt]] — the first inventory, handed over by a deceived king; *very heavy* in
  livestock, silver, and gold
- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — wealth as the cause of the first separation, and the senior man giving
  away the choice
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — a tenth given to a priest and everything else refused to a
  king, in consecutive verses, for stated reasons
- [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] — *"they will come out with great substance"*: property written
  into the covenant, on the far side of four hundred years of slavery
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — a thousand pieces of silver paid to the man in the wrong
- [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] — the one purchase, the one holding, and a grave paid for
  at full price in public
- [[A Wife for Isaac]] — the marriage negotiated in gold, and an inventory recited as the argument
- [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] — *"all that he had"* to one son, gifts to the
  others, and a direction to travel in
- [[Isaac in Gerar]] — a hundredfold harvest in a famine, on borrowed ground, and the expulsion it
  causes
- [[Jacob's Flocks]] — the book's most detailed acquisition: a refused wage, a self-auditing
  contract, seven verses of husbandry, and the promise's own verb applied to a herd
- [[Job of Uz]] — the inventory as a character's introduction, and the man who blesses the God who
  removed it
- [[Zophar's Second Speech]] — the wisdom tradition's nightmare: *"That for which he labored he shall
  restore, and shall not swallow it down"*
