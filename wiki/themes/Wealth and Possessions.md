# Wealth and Possessions

## Definition / overview

**Genesis is a book about property, and it never once says whether property is good.**

Livestock, silver, gold, slaves, tents, wells, a cave, a field, a coat. The narrative is full of
inventories, and **the inventories are almost always the evidence of a blessing** — *"Yahweh has
blessed my master greatly, and he has become great. He has given him flocks and herds, silver and
gold, male servants and female servants, and camels and donkeys"* (24:35). **That sentence is the
theme in miniature**: a list of goods offered as proof of divine favour, by a servant, to persuade a
family to hand over a daughter.

Three things frame it:

1. **Wealth in Genesis is nearly always acquired abroad, from a foreigner, and under a shadow.**
   Abram leaves Egypt rich because he passed his wife off as his sister (12:16). Jacob leaves Paddan
   Aram rich because he out-bred his employer. **The book reports the enrichment and the method in
   the same breath and grades neither.**
2. **It is the standard cause of separation.** *"Their substance was great, so that they couldn't
   dwell together"* (13:6) is the reason Abram and Lot part; the wells of Genesis 26 are contested
   until Isaac walks away from three of them; Laban's sons at 31:1 say *"Jacob has taken away all
   that was our father's"*. **Every generation's property produces a boundary.**
3. **And there is a counter-current running the whole length of it.** Abram refuses the king of
   Sodom's goods so that no man can say *"I have made Abram rich"* (14:23). Abraham insists on paying
   four hundred shekels for a cave he is offered free (23:11–16). **The men who are richest in the
   book are also the ones who most conspicuously refuse gifts**, and both refusals are about who gets
   the credit.

**What Genesis never does is moralise.** There is no warning against riches, no praise of poverty,
and no character who is admired for having little. **The nearest the book comes to a verdict is
structural**: possessions are what a promise looks like when it starts arriving, and they are what
the promise is repeatedly mistaken for.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 12:5, 16; 13:1–2** — the first inventory in the Bible. Abram leaves Haran with
  ***rəkûš***, *substance*, acquired there; comes out of Egypt with sheep, cattle, donkeys, slaves,
  and camels **handed over by a Pharaoh he deceived**; and is then described as ***kābēd məʾōd***,
  *very heavy* — *"rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold."* **The adjective is *weight*, and it
  is the same root as *glory*.** See [[Abram in Egypt]].
- **Genesis 13:5–12** — the first consequence. *"The land was not able to bear them, that they might
  dwell together"*, and the herdsmen quarrel. **Abram's answer is to give away the choice** — the
  senior man lets the junior pick, and the junior picks by looking. See [[Abram and Lot Separate]].
- **Genesis 14:21–24** — the first refusal, and the reason given is attribution: *"I will not take a
  thread nor a sandal strap nor anything that is yours, **lest you should say, 'I have made Abram
  rich'**."* **The one time in Genesis a man states why he will not be enriched.** See
  [[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].
- **Genesis 15:14** — property enters the covenant. The nation that goes into slavery *"will come out
  with **great substance**"*, ***rəkûš gādôl*** — **the same noun as 12:5**, promised to a man who
  has just been told his descendants will be slaves for four hundred years. See
  [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].
- **Genesis 20:14–16** — compensation. Abimelech hands over sheep, oxen, slaves, and **a thousand
  pieces of silver** to the man who deceived him, and Genesis lets the transaction stand as a
  vindication of the deceived party rather than of the deceiver. See [[Abraham and Abimelech]].
- **Genesis 23:3–20** — the one purchase. Four hundred shekels of silver, *"current money with the
  merchants"*, weighed out in public for a field and a cave. **The only land the patriarchs ever own
  is a grave, and they pay full price for it in front of witnesses.** See
  [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] and [[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].
- **Genesis 24:10, 22, 35, 53** — wealth as argument. Ten camels, a gold ring and two bracelets
  produced before a single question is asked, an inventory recited as the third sentence of the
  servant's case, and *"jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and clothing"* given to the bride with
  *"precious things"* to her brother and mother. **The negotiation for Rebekah is conducted in
  goods**, and the family's decision is announced immediately after the goods appear. See
  [[A Wife for Isaac]] and [[Laban]].
- **Genesis 25:5–6** — inheritance managed. *"Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac"*, and the sons
  of the concubines are given **gifts** and sent east, away from the son who gets the estate. See
  [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]].
- **Genesis 26:12–16** — the only yield figure in the Bible's patriarchal narratives: **a hundredfold
  in a famine year**, on land Isaac does not own, followed by *"possessions of flocks, and
  possessions of herds, and a great household"*, envy, stopped wells, and an expulsion. **Wealth here
  is explicitly a blessing (*"Yahweh blessed him"*) and explicitly the cause of the trouble.** See
  [[Isaac in Gerar]].
- **Genesis 30:25–43** — the most detailed acquisition in the book. Jacob refuses a wage
  (*"you shall not give me anything"*), takes the odd-coloured animals instead, and ends
  ***wayyipṛōṣ hāʾîš məʾōd məʾōd*** — *the man burst out exceedingly* — **with the verb of the Bethel
  promise** (28:14) and the six-noun inventory of 12:16 and 24:35. **Three generations, three foreign
  patrons, and the same list.** See [[Jacob's Flocks]].
- **Job 1:3; 1:21; 42:10–12** — the theme's one full-dress theological treatment in the wiki so far.
  Job is introduced by his inventory — *"the greatest of all the children of the east"* — loses it in
  four messengers' worth of verses, and answers *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away. Blessed be
  the name of Yahweh."* **The friends then argue the inverse**, that possessions track righteousness,
  and the book spends thirty-five chapters on the claim. See [[Job of Uz]],
  [[Zophar's Second Speech]], and [[The Prosperity of the Wicked]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: Jacob's present to Esau and *"I have enough"* /
  *"I have all"* (32:13–21; 33:9–11); Joseph telling his family *"don't concern yourselves about your
  belongings"* (45:20) and then buying the whole of Egypt for Pharaoh (47:13–26); the plunder of the
  Egyptians (Exodus 12:35–36); the laws of gleaning, release, and jubilee (Leviticus 19:9–10; 25);
  *"you shall remember Yahweh your God, for it is he who gives you power to get wealth"*
  (Deuteronomy 8:17–18); Proverbs on riches and integrity; Ecclesiastes 5:10–15; the rich fool and
  the rich young ruler (Luke 12:13–21; Mark 10:17–27); *"the love of money is a root of all kinds of
  evil"* (1 Timothy 6:10).

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:15, 17

The prohibition of stealing protects a neighbor's possessions (20:15), while the prohibition of
coveting reaches the desire to take over another household (20:17). House, wife, servants, ox, and
donkey are listed before the comprehensive ending. The list assumes an unequal household order, not
that everyone named has the same social status.
[[Coveting and Love - The Commandments in Romans 7 and 13]] follows Paul's use of the desire
prohibition. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### Violence, injury, and liability — Exodus 21:12–36

Ownership now carries duties beyond refraining from theft: covering a pit, restraining a bull
known to be dangerous, and paying when the failure causes loss (21:28–36). When no prior dangerous
habit is specified, two bull owners share the remaining animal's sale price and the carcass;
known danger left uncontrolled requires full replacement (21:35–36). The human cases expose the
limits of a property account: the servant is called property (21:21), and the thirty shekels for
a servant killed by a bull go to the master (21:32). That sum is a compensation provision, not
proof of a universal market price for human beings.
See [[Laws of Violence and Injury]].
### Theft, deposit, and loan — Exodus 22:1–15

After the pit and the bull, ownership is treated through theft, damage, and holding goods for
someone else. A thief repays double, or four or five times; grazing and fire damage is made good,
from *"the best"* of one's own field (22:5); a deposit or kept animal lost without fault costs
the keeper nothing, while a borrower pays for loss in the owner's absence (22:7–15). See
[[Theft and Restitution]] and
[[Fourfold Restitution - Exodus 22, Nathan's Parable, and Zacchaeus]].
See [[Laws of Property and Restitution]].

**Exodus 22:25–27 limits what a lender may do.** A loan to a poor member of *"my people"* is to
carry no *nešek*, interest, and the lender is not to act *"as a creditor"*; a cloak taken in pledge
comes back before sunset. **Ownership of the pledge is allowed; using it against the man's need is
not.** See [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] and [[Lending Without Interest - Exodus 22.25 and the Poor Brother]].

### Money cannot buy judgment; land must feed others — Exodus 23:1–19

A bribe perverts judgment and is forbidden (23:8). Land, vineyard, and olive grove must cease
cultivation in the seventh year so that the poor and animals may eat (23:10–11), while first fruits
are brought to Yahweh's house (23:19). The owner's use of resources is limited by justice, others'
need, and worship.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

**Wealth becomes both ornament and support.** In Exodus 26 gold covers the boards and bars and
forms clasps and hooks; silver forms the sockets supporting the boards and inner pillars, while
brass serves the goats’ hair clasps and entrance sockets (26:6, 11, 19–29, 32, 37). Valuable materials
are part of the structure as well as its visible surfaces. The chapter assigns their places but
provides no allegorical meaning for each metal. See [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]].

### The altar, court, and maintained light — Exodus 27:1–21

The materials offered in Exodus 25:3–6 are assigned further uses in 27:1–21: brass covers
the altar and supplies utensils and sockets, silver supplies court fittings, and linen and
dyed thread form hangings. Oil is not simply installed once like a furnishing; Israel must
supply it for the continuing light (27:20–21). The chapter specifies material and quality
without assigning a symbolic value to each metal or color. See [[The Altar and the Court]].

### The priestly garments — Exodus 28:1–43

Gold, blue, purple, scarlet, fine linen, two onyx stones, and twelve precious stones become
the high priest's clothing (Exodus 28:5–21). The wealth offered in 25:1–7 is now worn by the one
who represents the givers. See [[The Priestly Garments]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Gold earrings are surrendered and made into the calf (32:2–4). The sanctuary offering also calls
for gold (25:3), so the narrative contrasts uses of available wealth. Exodus 32 does not trace
these particular earrings to Egyptian donors; that connection is an inference from the larger
exodus story. See [[You Shall Not Go Empty-Handed - The Plunder of Egypt]]. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

Jewelry given for the calf in 32:2–4 is followed by jewelry removed in mourning in 33:4–6. Exodus 33
does not say the remaining ornaments are destroyed or deposited as an offering. Their removal is
obedience to a command in the crisis of presence, not payment for grace. See [[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

Men and women bring gold ornaments, other metals, textiles, skins, and wood; rulers provide stones,
spice, and oil (35:22–28). The text specifies donors and materials without tracing each object back
to Egypt. Gold used for the calf in 32:2–4 is destroyed in 32:20; the offering here must not be
called that same recovered gold. Jewelry removed in 33:4–6 remains available to give, but the text
does not explicitly identify those pieces with these. See [[Gold for the Calf and Gold for the Sanctuary]].

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The makers and the surplus — Exodus 35:30–36:7

The makers' assessment, rather than an indefinite demand for more, ends the collection
(36:4–7). Material abundance supports a specified purpose. The surplus is not condemned, and
its quantity and disposition are not supplied. Nor does the scene promise that each donor will
receive increased private wealth. See [[Enough for the Work - Manna and the Sanctuary Offering]].

See [[Bezalel's Workmen and the Surplus]].

### The enclosure made — Exodus 36:8–38

The supplied materials become curtains, coverings, gold clasps and overlays, silver sockets,
and brass clasps and entrance sockets (36:8–38). Their value is put into the commissioned work;
this is not a new fundraising appeal or a promise of wealth to contributors. The account does
not identify which donor supplied which component or give the disposal of the earlier surplus.

See [[The Tabernacle Built]].

### The furnishings made — Exodus 37:1–29

The contributed material becomes gold overlay, solid beaten work, rings, vessels, and other
furnishings. Exodus 37:24 assigns a talent of pure gold to the lampstand with its vessels,
not to all the chapter's gold. Neither the provenance of individual pieces nor the disposition
of the surplus is given. Costly material serves the commissioned work without purchasing pardon.

See [[The Furnishings Made]]. Source: the cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-24.

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

Gold totals 29 talents and 730 shekels; census silver 100 talents and 1,775 shekels; brass 70
talents and 2,400 shekels (38:24–29). The silver is traced into sockets and fittings, and the brass
into named equipment. The basin’s mirrors have a stated provenance, unlike many other objects. The
metal-use list omits the basin, and no weight for it or disposition of the surplus is given.

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The priestly garments made — Exodus 39:1–31

The contributed materials of 35:21–27 become garments: gold enters the textile work,
stone settings, chains, bells, and crown, while the rulers’ stones are engraved for Israel
(39:2–31). Precious materials are directed to the commanded service. No weight or separate cost
of the garments is given, so the gold total in 38:24 cannot be divided among them from this text.

See [[The Priestly Garments Made]]. Source: cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Greedy for gain — Proverbs 1:13–19

The gang's offer is wealth first and belonging second: *"We'll find all valuable wealth. We'll fill our
houses with plunder. You shall cast your lot among us. We'll all have one purse"* (1:13–14). The
father's summary names the motive and its end: *"So are the ways of everyone who is greedy for gain. It
takes away the life of its owners"* (1:19). *Greedy for gain* is *bōṣēaʿ bāṣaʿ*, from *beṣaʿ* — Judah's
*"What profit is it?"* at Dothan (Genesis 37:26) — and *its owners* is *bəʿālāyw*, from *baʿal*, the word
that made the bird an *"owner of a wing"* in 1:17: **the greedy man's gain owns him.** Compare 15:27,
*"He who is greedy for gain troubles his own house"*, and 1 Timothy 6:9–10 (WEB, checked against
bible-api.com). Hebrew checked against Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*. See [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]] and
[[What Profit Is It - Joseph's Brothers and the Ambush of Proverbs 1]].

### Honouring Yahweh with wealth — Proverbs 3:9–10

*"Honor Yahweh with your substance"* (3:9, *mēhôneḵā*). *Hôn*, *wealth*, is a word Proverbs uses
often. Its first use was the gang's: *"We'll find all valuable wealth. We'll fill our houses with
plunder"* (1:13, *kol-hôn yāqār … nəmallēʾ*). Here the same noun is given first to Yahweh, and the same
verb, *mālēʾ*, *fill*, describes the result: *"your barns will be filled"* (3:10, *wəyimmāləʾû*). **Houses
filled by taking, against barns filled after giving.** The promise is overflow — barns and vats (3:10) — and Jesus' rich fool, whose barns
overflowed and who built bigger ones without being *"rich toward God"* (Luke 12:18–21), is its reverse.
Two verses later the lecture says Yahweh may reprove the son he loves instead (3:11–12). See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]] and
[[Honor Yahweh with Your Substance - First Fruits in Exodus 23, Proverbs 3.9-10, and Malachi 3.10]]. Source: Proverbs 3 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Worth more than the riches she holds — Proverbs 3:14–16

[[Wisdom's Worth and the Founding of the Earth]] values wisdom above silver, gold,
rubies, and every desired thing, then pictures riches and honor in her left hand. Wealth
is a subordinate good, not the measure of wisdom's value. **Tension with Job:** Job 1:1,
13–19 portrays an upright, God-fearing man losing wealth and household; Proverbs 3:16
cannot be used as an infallible test of a person's wisdom from their possessions.
See [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]] and [[Retribution]].

Source: Proverbs 3 and the cited Genesis and Job comparisons checked in the stored WEB,
2026-10-25.

### Available goods and immediate obligation — Proverbs 3:21–35

After honoring Yahweh with possessions in 3:9, the son must give good owed to a neighbor
when he has the ability (3:27–28). Possession entails responsibility: having the resource
already makes the instruction to come back tomorrow blameworthy. The good is broader
than money, and the verses do not specify only charitable gifts or only payment of debts.

See [[Do Not Withhold Good]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3; comparisons as cited above.
The discussion added here makes no claim about exact source-language forms.

### Understanding before all possessions — Proverbs 4:1–9

In the WEB, 4:7 urges getting understanding even if it costs all one's possessions.
The image intensifies 3:14–15's ranking of wisdom above silver, gold, and rubies. It does
not establish a market price or a rule that the wealthy can buy wisdom: no seller or
payment mechanism appears. Reading it as a priority of desire also keeps 2:6's divine
gift in view. See [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]].

See [[Get Wisdom]]. Source: cited verses checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Surety and the sluggard — Proverbs 6:1–11

The first money lesson of Proverbs is about another man's debt: the son who has *"become
collateral for your neighbor"* (6:1) is *"trapped by the words of your mouth"* (6:2) and must go
at once and plead his way free. The wiki records the tension with [[Judah]]'s suretyship for
[[Benjamin]] (Genesis 43:9; 44:32–33) on
[[I Will Be Surety for Him - Judah's Pledge and the Guarantor in Scripture]]. The sluggard's
poverty arrives *"as a robber"* (6:11). Proverbs' sayings are general; the book also says *"Better
is the poor who walks in his integrity"* (19:1), so laziness explains some poverty, not all. See
[[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### Rather than silver, and full treasuries — Proverbs 8:10–11, 18–21

Wisdom tells her hearers to *"Receive my instruction rather than silver; knowledge rather than choice
gold"* (8:10, *wəʾal-kāsep̄*, *and not silver*), and then promises *"riches, honor, enduring wealth"*
(8:18) and *"I fill their treasuries"* (8:21). **The exclusion and the promise stand side by side,
unexplained.** The wiki reads them as an order of desires, as 1 Kings 3:11–13 narrates of Solomon
(riches and honour given though not asked), and records the unqualified promise against Job's losses
(Job 1:13–19). *ʿŌšer wəḵāḇôḏ*, *riches and honour*, is the pair of Proverbs 3:16 and 1 Kings 3:13.
See [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] and [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:2–5, 15–16, 20, 22

Wealth shelters its owner and poverty leaves people exposed (15), but wicked treasure cannot deliver
from death (2). Diligence (4) and Yahweh’s blessing (22) both contribute to flourishing; possessions
alone do not certify righteousness. Job’s losses and Job 24:5–11’s hungry workers are explicit
limits on inferring moral failure from poverty.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:1, 4, 15–18, 24–29

Fair weights, costly guarantees, violent gain, generosity, and withheld grain place wealth inside
relationships. Verse 16 acknowledges that violent men obtain riches; verses 4 and 28 deny that
wealth can secure a person against judgment. Scattering can increase resources, while excessive
withholding impoverishes. This is no unconditional investment promise: the neighbor’s access to
goods matters, and Job’s losses remain a counterexample to guaranteed material security.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:9, 11–12, 14, 24, 27

Modest provision is better than self-honor accompanied by hunger. Productive work and valued
possessions contrast with fantasy and desire for plunder. The chapter praises sufficient resources
without making public prestige the measure of success, or making every poor worker responsible for
deprivation.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### What riches are worth — Proverbs 13:7–8, 11, 18, 21–23

Wealth can be pretended (7), can ransom a life while making its owner a target (8), dwindles when dishonest (11), passes to grandchildren or to the righteous (22), and can be swept away by injustice (23). Verse 22 parallels Job 27:16–17; see [[Stored for the Righteous - Proverbs 13.22 and Job 27.16-17]].

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### Friends of the rich — Proverbs 14:20, 23–24

*"The rich person has many friends"* while the poor is shunned (20) — observed, not approved, as verse 21 shows. Work profits (23), and *"The crown of the wise is their riches"* (24). See [[Shunned by His Neighbor - Proverbs 14.20 and Job 19.13-19]].

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### Much treasure and better little — Proverbs 15:6, 16–17, 27

*"In the house of the righteous is much treasure"* (6), and in the same chapter *"Better is little, with the fear of Yahweh, than great treasure with trouble"* (16). The two forms do not conflict: one states the ordinary run of things, the other ranks goods when they come apart. Greed for gain *"troubles his own house"* (27). See [[Better Is Little - The Better-Than Sayings of Proverbs]].

### Revenues, gold, and plunder — Proverbs 16:8, 11, 16, 19

Little with righteousness above great revenues with injustice (8); the weights in the bag are Yahweh's work (11); wisdom better than gold (16); and the lowly poor above those who divide plunder (19). See [[Better Is Little - The Better-Than Sayings of Proverbs]].

### The bribe and the fool's money — Proverbs 17:1, 8, 16, 23

A poor table in peace outranks a full one in strife (1). The bribe looks like *"a precious stone"* to its giver and *"wherever he turns, he prospers"* (8), but *"A wicked man receives a bribe in secret, to pervert the ways of justice"* (23). And money cannot buy wisdom for a fool who *"has no understanding"* (16). See [[A Bribe Blinds - Proverbs 17.23, Exodus 23.8, and Deuteronomy 16.19]] and [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]].

See [[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17.

### The strong city in the imagination — Proverbs 18:11, 16, 23

*"The rich man's wealth is his strong city"* repeats 10:15, but now with *"like an unscalable wall
in his own imagination"* (11), directly after the name of Yahweh as the strong tower (10). A gift
*"makes room"* for its giver (16), a neutral observation a page after the bribe sayings of chapter 17;
and *"the rich answer harshly"* (23). See
[[The Name of Yahweh Is a Strong Tower - Proverbs 18.10-11, Psalm 61.3, and Genesis 11.4]].

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:1, 4, 6–7, 14, 17, 22

Wealth and gifts attract company (4, 6), while poverty drives it away (7). These observations do not
supply the chapter's standard of worth: integrity and honesty do (1, 22). Inherited houses and
riches are distinguished from the prudent wife given by Yahweh (14), and help for the poor is
pictured as a loan to him (17). The metaphor promises divine reward without naming a rate,
timetable, or guarantee of increased cash.

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:4, 10, 13–17, 21, 23

The chapter links provision with timely work (4, 13), condemns dishonest measurements and
fraudulent gain (10, 14, 17, 23), and values knowledgeable lips above treasures (15). A
guarantor's garment becomes security (16), and hasty inheritance lacks final blessing (21).
Verse 21 does not state the means of acquisition, so theft or a wish for a parent's death must
not be supplied as facts.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Treasure gained, kept, and given — Proverbs 21:5–6, 17, 20, 26

Treasure gained by a lying tongue is a fleeting vapor (6); love of pleasure, wine, and oil
impoverishes (17); the wise house keeps treasure that the fool swallows (20); the righteous give
without withholding (26). Wealth is judged by how it is gained, kept, and given.

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### Riches ranked and limited — Proverbs 22:1–2, 4, 7, 16

The pericope frames wealth from both ends. A good name outranks great riches (1); rich and poor
share one Maker (2); humility and the fear of Yahweh yield wealth, honor, and life (4); the rich
rule the poor and the borrower serves the lender (7); and both the oppressor of the poor and the
giver to the rich come to poverty (16). Wealth is promised, observed as power, and placed beneath
reputation and the Maker's claim. Verse 4's promise is held beside Job, who feared God and lost it.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### The bed and the eagle — Proverbs 22:26–27; 23:4–5

Two warnings about wealth in one block. Standing surety can cost the bed: *"If you don't have means to
pay, why should he take away your bed from under you?"* (22:27). And wealth pursued at the cost of
oneself does not stay: *"Don't weary yourself to be rich … it certainly sprouts wings like an eagle and
flies in the sky"* (23:4–5). The second warns against the effort, not the possession, and sits beside
22:4's promise of wealth to humility and the fear of Yahweh.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]] and [[Riches Sprout Wings - Proverbs 23.4-5 and the Uncertainty of Riches]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11.

### Rags, and the one thing not to sell — Proverbs 23:21, 23

*"For the drunkard and the glutton shall become poor; and drowsiness clothes them in rags"* (23:21):
appetite as a road to poverty, with the sluggard's sleep (6:9–11) as its last stage. Two verses later
the one commodity named is truth: *"Buy the truth, and don't sell it"* (23:23).

See [[Do Not Envy Sinners]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 23:12–24:2.

### Poverty as an intruder — Proverbs 24:34

*"So your poverty will come as a robber, and your want as an armed man"* (24:34, repeating 6:11).
The saying describes the ordinary path of neglect; like the other sluggard sayings, it does not
diagnose every poor man.

See [[The Field of the Sluggard]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:23–34.

### Riches are not forever — Proverbs 27:13, 23–27

Chapter 27 ends with the only sustained poem in chapters 25–29: *"Know well the state of your flocks
… for riches are not forever, nor does the crown endure to all generations"* (23–24). *Riches* is
*ḥōsen*, stored wealth, and *crown* is *nēzer*. What lasts instead is the yearly cycle — hay, new growth,
lambs for clothing, goats to buy a field, milk for the household and its servant girls (25–27). The
saying does not despise wealth; it relocates security from the store to the flock one tends. 27:13
repeats 20:16's warning about the collateral of one who vouches for a stranger. See
[[Riches Sprout Wings - Proverbs 23.4-5 and the Uncertainty of Riches]].

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20:16; 27; Hebrew from Sefaria.

- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — the inventory removed in four sentences, and
  **1:21 as the Bible's most concentrated statement of what ownership is worth at the ends of a
  life**: *"Naked I came out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return there."* The line is not
  a denial that possessions matter — the epilogue counts them back carefully — but a claim about
  **tenure**, and it is aimed at 1:10, where the accuser reads the same fortune as a wage. See
  [[Naked I Came - Job 1.21 and the Empty Hands of Ecclesiastes and Paul]]
- [[The Second Test]] — **the accuser's reply to 1:21, and a concession disguised as an argument.**
  *"Skin for skin. Yes, all that a man has he will give for his life"* (2:4) grants that Job
  surrendered the inventory without breaking and claims that the inventory was never the price. It
  is a market idiom — *ʿôr bəʿad-ʿôr*, hide for hide — **used about a man's dead children**, and it
  is the last valuation anyone in the book puts on Job's possessions until the epilogue doubles them
- [[Zophar's Second Speech]] — the wisdom tradition's nightmare: *"That for which he labored he shall
  restore, and shall not swallow it down"*
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:1, 14–16, 18, 42; **the herd argued over by four
  parties.** Laban's sons call it *"all this ***glory***"* (***kābôd***, the heaviest noun in the
  theological vocabulary, used of livestock); Jacob calls it what *"God has taken away from your
  father"*; **the daughters call it their consumed dowry** — *"he has sold us, and has also used up
  our money"* — and reassign it to themselves and their children; and Laban's last word on it is
  *"the flocks are my flocks… and all that you see is mine."* **Four claims and no adjudication.**
  The chapter's own summary of the alternative is one adverb: ***rêqām***, *empty* (31:42)
- [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] — Genesis 34:23, 28–29; **the same inventory read from both
  sides, twelve verses apart.** At the gate, [[Hamor]] sells the merger to his citizens on what they
  stand to absorb — *"Won't their livestock and their possessions and all their animals be ours?"*
  (34:23). **Three days later the list belongs to the other party**: *"They took their flocks, their
  herds, their donkeys, that which was in the city, that which was in the field, and all their
  wealth"* (34:28–29). **Each side's reason for the treaty was the other side's property**, and
  Genesis reports both without comment. It also records, flatly and for the first time in the Bible,
  **people counted as plunder**: *"they took captive all their little ones and their wives"*
- [[The Descendants of Esau]] — Genesis 36:6–7: **prosperity as the thing that breaks a household
  up**, in the same two clauses Genesis 13:6 used of Abram and Lot — *"their substance was too great
  for them to dwell together, and the land of their travels couldn't bear them because of their
  livestock."* **Genesis's recurring difficulty is not famine but success**, and the recurring remedy
  is distance. The difference from chapter 13 is that Abram spoke and offered Lot the choice, and
  here nobody says anything at all. See
  [[Too Great to Dwell Together - Abram and Lot, Esau and Jacob]]
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:26–28; ***mah-beṣaʿ***, ***what profit*** — **and it is offered
  as the reason not to commit a murder.** ***Beṣaʿ*** is gain, and characteristically **dishonest** gain:
  the word in the qualification for judges, *"men of truth, hating unjust gain"* (Exodus 18:21), and in the
  prophets' complaints about men *"greedy for gain"*. **[[Judah]]'s objection to killing his brother is that
  killing him pays nothing**, and he closes the argument with the chapter's one appeal to kinship. **Twenty
  shekels is the only price Genesis ever puts on a person**, and the chapter does not say who took it. See
  [[Twenty Pieces of Silver - The Price of a Brother]]
- [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:17–23; **a transaction in which every item is misvalued.** A young
  goat is offered as a price; **the *ʿērābôn* taken as security against it is [[Judah]]'s signet,
  cord, and staff** — the instruments by which he signs contracts and holds standing, and worth
  incomparably more than the fee they secure. **He surrenders them without hesitation and then declines
  to redeem them**: *"Let her keep it, lest we be shamed."* **He writes off his own identity to avoid
  being laughed at**, which is the same calculation, in a smaller currency, as *mah-beṣaʿ* — *what
  profit is it if we kill our brother* — one chapter earlier. **The man who priced a brother at twenty
  shekels prices his seal at the cost of an embarrassment**, and three months later it is the whole of
  the evidence against him
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:34, 42, 48–49, 56, **the chapter in which a state
  becomes rich on a forecast.** ***Wəḥimmēš***, *take up the fifth part* (41:34) — **a verb coined from
  the numeral five and used this way nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible** — is **twenty per cent of a
  nation's harvest for seven years, proposed by a prisoner and collected by the same man once he is
  appointed.** The grain is *"as the sand of the sea"* (41:49), **the covenant simile for offspring**
  (22:17; 32:12) **spent on an Egyptian crop** — see
  [[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]]. And **the storehouses are opened to *sell***
  (41:56), not to distribute: **the relief is a market**, and 47:13–26 will show what the market
  eventually costs — money, livestock, land, and finally the people themselves. **Genesis reports the
  whole sequence as good government and does not moralise about it.** Joseph's own wealth is stated
  entirely in regalia: a signet, fine linen, and a gold chain (41:42)
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:11–12, 15, 21–23; **the chapter's economy runs one way and
  everyone in it is wrong about the accounts.** [[Jacob]] sends *"the choice fruits of the land"* —
  balm, honey, spices, myrrh, nuts, almonds — **from a land with no food, to the man who owns all the
  grain**; they are not provisions but the last good things in the house. He adds **double money**, and
  the returned silver, which he calls a ***mišgeh***, an *oversight*, **a noun found nowhere else in the
  Hebrew Bible.** Three sums are in motion and the chapter never balances them: [[Joseph's Steward]]
  calls the first *treasure* from their God and says he was paid anyway
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:13–27; **the pericope is a ledger, and it itemises
  everything an Egyptian owns as it changes hands**: *money* six times, *livestock* five, *seed*
  three, *the fifth* twice, and *land* **twenty-three times in nineteen verses.** *"Joseph gathered
  up all the money that was found in the land of Egypt, and in the land of Canaan … and brought the
  money into Pharaoh's house"* (47:14) — **not a treasury, the king's household.** **The only
  property Genesis reports being *acquired* here is Israel's** (47:27), ***wayyēʾāḥăzû***, **from the
  root of the *ʾăḥuzzâ* at 47:11** — **and the law given to Israel later forbids in its own land
  precisely what Joseph does in Egypt** (Leviticus 25:23). See
  [[The Land Is Mine - Pharaoh's Freehold and the Jubilee]]

- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:14–15; **the rich listed among the dead as company rather than as a
  contrast.** *"with kings and counselors of the earth, who built up waste places for themselves; or
  with princes who had gold, who filled their houses with silver."* **The waste places are the
  point** — what the great built is ruins now, and Job, who had been the greatest man of the east
  (1:3), names their society as the one he would have preferred to join at birth. Set against
  3:19's *"the small and the great are there"*, the passage is the wisdom tradition's levelling
  argument in its bleakest form
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:7–13, 24; **wealth kept to the end.** *"Grow mighty in power"*
  — *gābərû ḥāyil*, where *ḥayil* is both strength and wealth; bulls that breed, cows that calve and
  *"don't miscarry"* (21:10, the covenant blessing of Exodus 23:26); pails full of milk; days spent *"in
  prosperity"*. The direct counter to Zophar's swallowed riches vomited up (20:15): nothing is
  surrendered, and the wealth goes to the grave with its owner.
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:24–25; **gold into the wadi.** *"Lay your treasure in the
  dust, the gold of Ophir among the stones of the brooks. The Almighty will be your treasure"* — a
  pun on *beṣer*, ore, and *ṣûr*, rock, and a charge that Job's wealth had become his god. Job
  answers it under oath at 31:24–25. See [[Ophir]].
- [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]] — Job 27:16–19; silver heaped *"as the dust"* and clothing

  Proverbs 13:22 and Ecclesiastes 2:26 — and in contradiction of Job's own 21:7–13 (flagged on
  [[Retribution]])
- [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] — Job 28:1–2, 15–19; **the most precious things in the world, catalogued and set
  aside.** The miner's silver, gold, iron and copper (28:1–2), and then gold in five words, silver
  weighed, *"the gold of Ophir"*, onyx, sapphire, glass, coral, crystal, rubies and *"the topaz of
  Ethiopia"* — none of which can buy wisdom: *"Man doesn't know its price"* (28:13). Spoken by a man
  who has lost everything he owned, and who was wise, by 28:28's definition, before and after the loss.
  See [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]]
- [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] — Job 31:24–25, 38–40; *"If I have made gold my hope … If I have rejoiced because my wealth was
  great"*: wealth denied as trust and as joy, and the land denied as a thing got *"without money"* or by
  causing *"its owners to lose their life"* (31:39). The answer to 22:24.
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:16, 18–19; the table *"full of fatness"* God would have set for

  relative brings a *qəśîṭâ* and a ring of gold (42:11), the patriarchs' coin. And Job's last recorded act is to give:
  an inheritance to his daughters beside their brothers (42:15). See
  [[Yahweh Turned the Captivity of Job - Restoration and the Double]].
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:21–22; ***"you shall not go empty-handed … You shall plunder the Egyptians."***

  *nāṣal* beside God's *deliver* (3:8). See [[You Shall Not Go Empty-Handed - The Plunder of Egypt]]
