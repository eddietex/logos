# Barrenness

## Definition / overview

A womb that does not open, in a book whose entire structure is a list of children being born.

The theme enters Scripture in six Hebrew words at the end of a genealogy: *"Sarai was barren. She
had no child"* (Genesis 11:30) — *wattəhî śāray ʿăqārâ ʾên lāh wālād*. The statement is doubled, and
both halves are flat. There is no cause given, no prayer, no lament, no promise attached, and no
comment from the narrator. Genesis has just spent twenty verses on nine generations of men fathering
sons, and it stops the mechanism by naming a woman who cannot.

The word is *ʿăqārâ*, from a root meaning **uprooted** or **plucked up** — the same consonants as
*ʿiqqār*, a root or stock. What is being said of her is not that she is unlucky but that the line
stops. In a world where the promise of 1:28 and 9:1 is *be fruitful and multiply*, and where blessing
has been counted in descendants for eleven chapters, barrenness is the exact negation of the thing
Genesis has been describing.

**And then the promise is attached to it anyway.** The next thing in the book, four verses later, is
*"I will make of you a great nation"* (12:2), spoken to the husband of the woman who cannot have
children. From that moment the two facts are held together for ten chapters, and the pattern is set
for the rest of Scripture: the child through whom God keeps his word is repeatedly the child who could
not be had.

## Development across Scripture

- **Sarah** — Genesis 11:30, and then the promise (12:2; 15:4; 17:16), Hagar (16), the laughter
  behind the tent flap (18:12), and Isaac at ninety (21:2). Hers is the longest and the theme's
  template: the wait is twenty-five years, and the human attempt to solve it produces Ishmael.
- **Rebekah** — Genesis 25:21. *"Isaac entreated Yahweh for his wife, because she was barren."*
  Twenty years, resolved by prayer in a single verse, and the twins in her womb struggle before they
  are born.
- **Rachel** — Genesis 29:31; 30:1–24. *"Give me children, or else I die."* The only case in Genesis
  where the barren wife is loved and the fertile one is not, and the rivalry produces most of the
  twelve tribes.
- **Manoah's wife** — Judges 13:2–3. Unnamed, *"barren, and childless"*, visited by the angel of
  Yahweh, and the mother of Samson.
- **Hannah** — 1 Samuel 1–2. The most fully told: the provocation, the vow, the prayer taken for
  drunkenness, and then Samuel — and a song (2:1–10) that turns her private reversal into a political
  one, *"the barren has borne seven"*, which Mary's Magnificat will take up almost line for line.
- **The Shunammite** — 2 Kings 4:8–17, and the son given, lost, and raised again.
- **Elizabeth** — Luke 1:7, 24–25. *"They had no child, because Elizabeth was barren, and they both
  were well advanced in years"* — the formula of Genesis 11:30 and 18:11 together, deliberately, at
  the opening of the New Testament.
- **Israel herself** — Isaiah 54:1: *"Sing, barren, you who didn't give birth … for the children of
  the desolate will be more than the children of the married wife."* The nation in exile is addressed
  as a childless woman, and Paul quotes the verse at Galatians 4:27 to make the point about the
  covenant that the promise runs through the barren line.
- **And the reversal as a standing image of God's character** — Psalm 113:9, *"He settles the barren
  woman in her home as a joyful mother of children"*; 1 Samuel 2:5; Isaiah 66:7–9.

**Two things the pattern is doing.** First, it puts the fulfilment of the promise permanently outside
human capacity: every generation of the patriarchal line arrives by a birth that should not have
happened, so that the nation can never be read as the natural result of a family's fertility.
Second, it hands the narrative to the women. In Genesis 12–35 the men are told the promise and the
women are the obstacle to it — and it is Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel who speak, bargain, scheme, laugh,
and pray in those chapters, and the barrenness is why.

**A caution the text supports.** In every case the fault is stated of the woman. That is the
convention of the period and of the language, not a claim Genesis defends, and Genesis itself shows
what the convention costs: Hagar's contempt and Sarai's retaliation (16:4–6), Rachel's *"Give me
children, or else I die"* and Jacob's angry *"Am I in God's place?"* (30:1–2), Hannah's provocation
by Peninnah *"to irritate her"* (1 Samuel 1:6). The narratives are consistently more sympathetic to
the women than the frame they are written in.

## Related themes

- [[Blessing]] — counted in descendants from 1:28 onward, which is what makes barrenness its exact
  negation
- [[Life and Death]] — Romans 4:19 calls it *"the deadness of Sarah's womb"*, and Hebrews 11:12 calls
  Abraham *"as good as dead"*
- [[Covenant]] — the promise of offspring is the covenant's content, and the barren womb is the
  standing obstacle to it
- [[Hope]] — the wait, which in Sarah's case runs twenty-five years
- [[Marriage]]
- [[Favor and Grace]] — the child is given, never achieved
