# Prayer

## Definition / overview

A human being addressing God, unbidden, and asking for something.

The distinction that matters for Genesis is between **being spoken to** and **speaking first**. God
addresses Adam, Cain, Noah, Abram, Hagar, Abimelech, and Lot; those conversations are theophanies,
and the human replies in them are answers, not prayers. **Prayer in the sense this page tracks is a
human opening the exchange**, and Genesis takes a long time to produce one.

The Hebrew Bible has no single word for it. **ʾĀmar ʾel** — *he said to* — covers most of Genesis.
**Pālal** in the hitpael, *hitpallēl*, is the standard later verb and appears for the first time at
Genesis 20:17, of Abraham praying for Abimelech's household; the noun *təpillâ* is not in Genesis at
all. **Qārāʾ bəšēm YHWH**, *to call on the name of Yahweh* (4:26; 12:8; 13:4; 21:33), is the book's
phrase for public worship rather than for petition — see
[[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]]. **ʿĀtar**, to entreat, is what Isaac does for a barren wife
(25:21).

## Development across Scripture

**Genesis 4:26 — worship before petition.** *"Then men began to call on Yahweh's name."* The first
religious act humanity performs collectively is invocation, and Genesis says nothing about what was
asked for.

**Genesis 18:22–33 — argument, not petition.** Abraham's six rounds over Sodom are the first extended
human speech to God in Scripture, and they are a negotiation begun by God's own disclosure: *"Will I
hide from Abraham what I do?"* (18:17). He asks nothing for himself and nothing for anyone he knows;
he interrogates a principle. It is the closest thing to intercession the book has produced so far,
and the conversation is God's idea. See [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] and
[[Mediation and Advocacy]].

**Genesis 20:7, 17 — intercession, at instruction.** *"He is a prophet, and he will pray for you,
and you will live."* The first occurrence of *hitpallēl* in the Bible, and the first time prayer is
said to accomplish something — and Abraham prays because God told a foreign king that he would. See
[[Abraham and Abimelech]] and [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]].

**Genesis 24:12–14 — the first unbidden petition, and it is a slave's.** *"Yahweh, the God of my
master Abraham, please give me success today, and show kindness to my master Abraham."* Nobody has
prompted him. God does not appear, does not answer in words, and does not speak anywhere in the
chapter. **The prayer is short, it is entirely on somebody else's behalf, it proposes a concrete test,
and it is answered before it is finished**: *"Before he had finished speaking, behold, Rebekah came
out"* (24:15). Fifteen verses later he thanks God in the same terms he used to ask — *ḥesed we-ʾemet*,
loving kindness and truth — which makes it also the first prayer in Scripture with a recorded
answer *and* a recorded thanksgiving. See [[A Wife for Isaac]] and [[Abraham's Servant]].

**That the first one is his and not a patriarch's is the theme's most striking fact in Genesis.** The
man has no name, no standing, and no promise; he asks for nothing for himself; and the book gives him
the first prayer, the first sign asked for, and the first answer.

**The rest of Genesis, briefly.** Isaac entreats Yahweh for barren Rebekah and is answered (25:21).
Jacob prays at the Jabbok in the book's first prayer that argues from a promise back to God —
*"Deliver me, I pray you … you said, 'I will surely do you good'"* (32:9–12) — and then wrestles
until dawn for a blessing he asks for out loud (32:26). Nothing in the Joseph cycle is a prayer at
all, which is consistent with a narrative that reports no divine speech to Joseph either.

**Afterwards.** The sign-asking form recurs at Judges 6:36–40 (Gideon's fleece) and 1 Samuel 14:8–10
(Jonathan at Michmash), both reported without comment. Moses' intercessions (Exodus 32:11–14;
Numbers 14:13–19) argue from God's reputation and his promises, in Jacob's manner. Hannah prays
silently and is accused of drunkenness for it (1 Samuel 1:13) — the servant's *"in my heart"* (24:45)
is the first silent prayer in the Bible. Solomon's dedication prayer (1 Kings 8) makes the temple the
address for petitions. The Psalter turns the whole practice into a genre. Isaiah 65:24 states the
timing of Genesis 24:15 as a principle: *"before they call, I will answer."* And the New Testament's
own instruction — *"when you pray, don't use vain repetitions"*, *"your Father knows what things you
need, before you ask him"* (Matthew 6:7–8) — describes something the first prayer in Scripture
already did.

## Key passages

- [[A Wife for Isaac]] — Genesis 24:12–14, 26–27, 45, 52; the theme's founding text. The first
  unbidden petition, offered by an unnamed slave for his master's benefit, answered mid-sentence, and
  closed with thanksgiving in the vocabulary it opened with
- [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] — Genesis 18:22–33; the longest human speech to
  God in Genesis, and a negotiation God opens
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:7, 17; the Bible's first *hitpallēl*, and the first prayer
  reported to have had an effect
- [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]] — Genesis 4:26; 12:8; 13:4; 21:33: invocation, which is the
  book's other religious speech and is not petition
- Genesis 25:21 — Isaac entreats Yahweh for Rebekah, and the answer is Jacob and Esau
  *(not yet ingested)*
- Genesis 32:9–12, 26 — Jacob at the Jabbok: the first prayer that quotes God's own promise back to
  him *(not yet ingested)*
- Exodus 32:11–14; Numbers 14:13–19 — Moses arguing from God's reputation
- 1 Samuel 1:10–17 — Hannah, silent, and misread
- 1 Kings 8:22–53 — Solomon, and the temple as an address
- Psalms — the practice as a genre, including its complaints
- Isaiah 65:24 — *"before they call, I will answer"*
- Matthew 6:5–15; Luke 11:1–13; 18:1–14 — the instruction, the model, and the two parables about
  persistence and posture
- Romans 8:26–27; Hebrews 4:14–16 — praying badly, and being represented anyway
