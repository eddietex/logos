# Suffering

## Definition / overview

The Bible does not have a single doctrine of suffering; it has a set of accounts that do not
reduce to one another, and it declines to rank them. Any honest treatment has to hold several at
once:

- **Suffering as consequence** — the sentences of Genesis 3, the covenant curses, the harvest of
  Galatians 6:7. Real, and the account most often misapplied.
- **Suffering as discipline** — Deuteronomy 8:5, Proverbs 3:11–12, Hebrews 12:5–11. Also real, and
  the one [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] offers in [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] (Job 5:17) before
  withdrawing it. See
  [[Do Not Despise the Chastening - Job 5.17, Proverbs 3.11-12, and Hebrews 12]].
- **Suffering as test** — [[The Binding of Isaac]], Deuteronomy 8:2, and the prologue of Job. This is
  the one the reader of Job is given and the characters are not; Genesis 22:1 does the same thing in
  four words, and never lets Abraham in on it. See [[Testing]].
- **Suffering as vicarious** — Isaiah 53, and the whole New Testament reading of the cross.
- **Suffering as unexplained** — Psalm 88, which ends *"darkness is my closest friend"*, and much of
  Job. Scripture leaves these standing without resolution, and that is itself a position.

The book of [[Job]] is where the question is put most sharply, because the book gives the reader
the answer and withholds it from everyone inside it. We know from Job 1–2 that the affliction is a
test. Job never learns this. And crucially, **God does not tell him even at the end**: the
whirlwind speeches of chapters 38–41 explain nothing about the wager, and what quiets Job is not
information but encounter — *"I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees
you"* (42:5).

Job 16:1–17:16 supplies the theme's most extreme vocabulary in Scripture, and its distinguishing
feature is the identification of the agent. The laments of the Psalms usually suffer at the hands
of enemies, with God as the one appealed to. Job's assailant *is* God: torn in his wrath, gnashed
at with teeth, taken by the neck and dashed to pieces, set up as an archery target, kidneys split,
gall poured on the ground, broken with breach on breach. It is spoken by the man the narrator and
God have both called blameless, and it is not retracted.

The other thing the book supplies is a picture of how not to attend a sufferer. The friends do one
thing right — *"they sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke
a word to him, for they saw that his grief was very great"* (2:13) — and everything after they open
their mouths is wrong. Job's own diagnosis of the failure is the best one available: *"I also could
speak as you do. If your soul were in my soul's place"* (16:4). The problem is not that their
theology is unusually bad. It is that fluency about someone else's catastrophe is cheap, and
requires only that you be standing outside it.

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 3:16–19** — pain in childbearing, toil in the ground: the first suffering, and the one
  the Bible does explain. See [[The Fall]].
- **Genesis 6:6** — God grieved *"in his heart"*, using the same root as the pain assigned in
  Genesis 3. Suffering is not only something creatures do. See
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]] and [[Divine Grief and Relenting]].
- **Job 1–2** — the cause disclosed to the reader, and to no one else.
- **Job 3** — the curse on the day of his birth: *"Why didn't I die from the womb?"*
- **Job 15** — [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]], where suffering is nothing but evidence.
- **Job 16:6–17** — [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]], the assault described.
- **Job 38–42** — an answer that is not an explanation.
- **Psalm 22; 42–43; 69; 73; 88** — the lament tradition, which is a third of the Psalter and which
  the church's practice has largely lost. Psalm 88 is the one that does not resolve.
- **Lamentations 3:1–20** — God as archer and besieger, the closest parallel anywhere to Job 16, and
  from it the turn at 3:21–23 to *"his compassions don't fail. They are new every morning."*
- **Isaiah 53:3–11** — the servant *"acquainted with disease"*, whose suffering is neither deserved
  nor pointless.
- **Habakkuk 1:2–4, 13** — *"Why do you tolerate those who deal treacherously?"*, the prophetic form
  of Job's question, addressed to God and left standing for two chapters.
- **Luke 13:1–5; John 9:1–3** — Jesus refusing the retributive reading of two specific disasters.
- **2 Corinthians 1:3–7; 12:7–10** — comfort received becoming comfort given, and a thorn not
  removed after three requests: *"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in
  weakness."*
- **Romans 5:3–5; 8:18–25** — suffering producing endurance, and the whole creation groaning.
- **1 Peter 4:12–19** — suffering as expected rather than anomalous.
- **James 5:11** — *"you have heard of the perseverance of Job, and have seen the Lord in the
  outcome"* — the New Testament's one direct appeal to this book, and worth noting that it appeals
  to the *outcome*, not to an explanation.
- **Revelation 21:4** — the end of the theme rather than an account of it.

### Discipline in the lecture's own order — Proverbs 3:11–12

Proverbs 3:11–12 is the canonical statement of suffering as discipline, and its setting is part of
its meaning: it is the sixth of six command-and-reason pairs, and the only one whose reason is not a
good received. After long life, favour, straight paths, health, and full barns (3:2–10), the son is
told that *"whom Yahweh loves, he reproves; even as a father reproves the son in whom he delights"*.
**The lecture qualifies its own promises.** Eliphaz said the same to Job (5:17) without the father;
Hebrews 12:5–6 quotes it to a church under pressure. See [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]] and
[[Do Not Despise the Chastening - Job 5.17, Proverbs 3.11-12, and Hebrews 12]]. Source: Proverbs 3 checked in `raw/bible/Proverbs.md` (WEB); Hebrew checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:3–5, 22, 24–27

The chapter links well-being to righteousness and diligence, but Job’s uprightness and realized fear
(Job 1:1; 3:25) conflict with a universal reading of those outcomes. Job 24:5–11’s hungry workers
likewise show suffering without idleness. The tension is retained, rather than turning proverbial
contrasts into accusations against sufferers.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:8, 15, 17, 21, 31

Suretyship and cruelty can bring suffering through conduct, but the assurance of righteous
deliverance cannot explain every affliction. Job 2:3 describes ruin without cause, and his
children’s death conflicts with an unconditional reading of Proverbs 11:21. First Peter 4:17–19
likewise addresses people suffering while doing good; see
[[Repaid in the Earth - Proverbs 11.31 and 1 Peter 4.18]].

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:4, 18, 21, 25

Pain appears as inward decay, swordlike speech, and anxiety that weighs down the heart. A kind word
offers relief without implying that every distress can be removed by conversation. **Contradiction
in an exceptionless reading:** verse 21’s righteous safety meets Job 2:3, 7’s undeserved suffering;
neither text authorizes treating the sufferer as secretly wicked.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### A crushed spirit — Proverbs 18:14

*"A man's spirit will sustain him in sickness, but a crushed spirit, who can bear?"* (14). Proverbs
asks the question and leaves it open; the book of Job is the long form of it. The chapter's promise that
the righteous who run to the name *"are safe"* (10) stands, as on earlier pages, against Job, who
blessed Yahweh's name (Job 1:21) and was not spared.

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:3–7, 23

Verse 3 describes a person ruining his own way and blaming Yahweh; verses 4 and 7 describe
deprivation intensified by social abandonment. Those are different accounts of distress. **Explicit
contradiction in a universal reading of 19:23:** the God-fearing Job is touched by severe trouble
(Job 1:1, 8, 13–19; 2:3, 7). The book itself calls his ruin without cause. See
[[Untouched by Trouble - Proverbs 19.23 and the Suffering of Job]] .

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:7, 30

Verse 30 assigns a cleansing purpose to wounding blows, but 17:26 condemns punishment of
righteous people. Job's affirmed integrity and undeserved affliction (Job 2:3, 7) prevent
treating pain itself as evidence of needed correction. His children's deaths also contradict an
unconditional safety reading of Proverbs 20:7. Both the proverb's intended benefit and these
counterexamples remain explicit.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Lemuel’s mother — Proverbs 31:5–9

The person ready to perish and the bitter in soul are offered forgetfulness of poverty and
misery. The passage gives no cause for that suffering and makes no charge against the sufferer.
**Explicit tension:** Proverbs 23:29–35 presents wine as a source of woe and renewed craving;
31:6–7 presents it as relief from remembered misery. The differing purposes and recipients are
stated, but the text does not explain a general reconciliation. Its final demand remains justice
for the poor. See [[The Vine and the Cup]].

See [[The Words of King Lemuel]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9 and the comparisons documented
there.

### The grief of the wise — Ecclesiastes 1:13, 18

Elsewhere in the wiki's wisdom texts, suffering often falls on the fool or tests the righteous.
Here it attaches to wisdom itself: the search is given *"to be afflicted with"* (1:13), and *"he
who increases knowledge increases sorrow"* (1:18). The sorrow is not punishment for a fault the
text names; it is the cost of seeing what cannot be repaired. See [[The Burden of Wisdom]] and [[Much Wisdom, Much Grief - Proverbs 3.13-18, Job 28, and Ecclesiastes 1.18]].
Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:12–18.

### Labor left to another — Ecclesiastes 2:18–26

The speaker describes despair over his labor (2:20), days of sorrow and grief, and a heart
without rest at night (2:23). His competent work is the setting, not a named fault that explains
the distress. Although 2:26 describes the sinner's travail, it does not identify the speaker
of 2:18–23 as that sinner. The recognition of enjoyment from God in 2:24 does not erase the
preceding report of grief.

See [[The Labor Left to Another]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:18–26.

### A time for every purpose — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

The poem gives weeping and mourning their times (4), and the Preacher names the burden God
has given people (10). These claims remain beside beauty in its time (11) and enjoyment as a
gift (13). The passage does not explain why a specific person suffers or infer guilt from
suffering. Its assertion of order does not grant people knowledge of the whole work.

See [[A Time for Every Purpose]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

### The tears of the oppressed — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

The suffering in 4:1 has identifiable human agents: power is on the side of the oppressors,
and their victims weep without a comforter. The passage gives no diagnosis of the victims'
guilt and no explanation through discipline or testing. Its response is the Preacher's
severe preference for the dead and the one who has not yet been (2–3). These verses expand
the page's accounts of suffering to include human oppression plainly observed and unresolved.

See [[The Tears of the Oppressed]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–4:3; the passage page
records the additional comparison sources.

### Wealth and enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 5:8–20

The unit moves from oppression of the poor (8) to the rich person's lost sleep (12),
harmful wealth and misfortune (13–14), and darkness, frustration, sickness, and wrath (17).
It does not explain this loss as a divine sentence for an identified sin. Joy given by God
(18–20) remains beside suffering, not as a denial of its reality or proof that every
sufferer has failed to receive a universally available remedy.

See [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20;
comparisons as identified on the passage page.

### Riches without enjoyment — Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

The evil is heavy on people (1), and the wealthy man's inability to eat of what God
gives is called an evil disease (2). This is the WEB's description of the condition, not
a named medical diagnosis. No particular wrongdoing, lesson, or divine purpose is
specified. The stillborn comparison (3–6) measures the severity of life without good;
it is not a claim that all living people fare worse than the dead.

See [[Riches Without Enjoyment]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9; comparison
sources are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Heavy misery and harmful rule — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

Heavy human misery accompanies the affirmation that every purpose has a time and
procedure (6). Verse 9 observes one person's dominion over another to his hurt. The WEB
pronoun does not explicitly settle whether the hurt is the subject's or the ruler's;
harm to the subject fits 4:1, but the verse does not guarantee the ruler's eventual ruin.
**Tension:** 8:5's safety for the commandment keeper stands beside this harm, without a
diagnosis of the sufferer's guilt. See [[Power with the Oppressors - Job 24 and Ecclesiastes 4.1]].

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9; comparison sources
are identified on the passage and connection pages.

### Difficult years without pleasure — Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8

The coming years are anticipated as days in which the person will have no pleasure
(12:1), pictured through trembling, failing activity, fear, and burdens (3–5).
The instruction to put away sorrow and evil (11:10, following the WEB) is not a
claim that decline can always be avoided. Judgment in 11:9 does not identify these
bodily losses as punishment for a particular sin.

See [[Remember Your Creator]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8; comparison sources
identified on the passage page.

### Beaten in the city — Song of Solomon 5:7

The first injury in the Song. Searching for the beloved at night, the woman is found by the watchmen:
*"They beat me. They bruised me"* (5:7). The Hebrew is *hikkûnî pǝṣāʿûnî*, *they struck me, they
wounded me*; *peṣaʿ* is the *wound* of Exodus 21:25 and of Lamech's boast at Genesis 4:23 (Sefaria).
No reason is given, and the poem neither condemns the watchmen nor calls the beating deserved. It
comes after her own delay has lost her the beloved (5:3–6), but the text does not present it as
punishment for that delay. She does not lament it; she turns to the daughters and says she is faint
with love (5:8).

See [[I Opened, but He Had Gone]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 5:2–8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*.

- [[Wine Is a Mocker]] — Proverbs 20:7, 30; Family loss and unjust punishment limit a disciplinary diagnosis.

- [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] — Proverbs 19:3–7, 23; Folly can bring trouble, but
  Job prevents using that diagnosis for every sufferer.

- [[A Strong Tower]] — Proverbs 18:14.

- [[Whoever Loves Correction]] — Proverbs 12:4, 18, 21, 25.

- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:8, 15, 17, 21, 31.

- [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]] — Proverbs 10:3–5, 22, 24–27; The chapter links well-being to
  righteousness and diligence, but Job’s uprightness and realized fear (Job 1:1; 3:25) conflict with a
  universal reading of those outcomes.

- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — **the wiki's one passage where the cause of a
  suffering is disclosed**, and the disclosure turns out not to be an explanation. Knowing *that* a
  question was asked in a heavenly court does not tell the reader why the answer required eleven
  thousand animals, an unnumbered household and ten funerals — and the cost falls entirely on a man
  who is never consulted and never told. **Job's suffering is caused by his righteousness, not
  despite it**, which is the sentence the rest of the book exists to make sayable
- [[The Second Test]] — **the point at which the suffering moves inside the man.** Everything taken
  in chapter 1 was external; from 2:7 it is a body, permanently, and one whose symptom the law
  itself reads as a verdict. Two features of the passage are the wiki's sharpest statement of what
  the theme costs. **God announces at 2:3 that the first test has been passed, and the second is run
  anyway** — so at least one round of this suffering demonstrably settles nothing that was still
  open. And **God calls it what it is**: *ballaʿ*, swallowed up, and *ḥinnām*, without cause,
  refusing on his own behalf the explanation the friends will supply for him
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:11–13; **the theme measured from outside for the only time in the
  book.** Three men who knew Job cannot identify him, weep, perform the rites for the dead over a
  living man, and then sit on the ground for seven days and seven nights unable to say anything. The
  narrator's reason is *"they saw that his grief was very great"* — *kəʾēb*, a word that covers
  physical pain and mental anguish without distinguishing them, which is the right word for a man who
  has both. **Every other account of the suffering in this book is Job's own**; this is what it
  looked like to people who had seen him before. It is also the wiki's clearest statement that **the
  right response to suffering is available and is not an explanation** — see
  [[Mourning and Lament]] and [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]]
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — the Bible's most violent description of
  God's hand on a sufferer, spoken by a man certified innocent
- [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] — suffering treated purely as evidence, with the consolation of the
  first cycle withdrawn
- [[The Fall]] — the suffering the Bible does explain
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — the Bible's fullest account of suffering's *social*
  dimension: eight verses walking outward through every ring of a household — brothers, relatives,
  friends, servants, wife, the children in the street — and finding no one, with the whole
  catalogue attributed to God (19:13)
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] and [[Zophar's Second Speech]] — suffering read as a portrait of the
  sufferer, item by item, and delivered to him as general doctrine
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:40, 42; **the only description of physical hardship any
  patriarch gives of himself**, and it is about weather: *"in the day the drought consumed me, and
  the frost by night; and my sleep fled from my eyes."* **Abraham is never cold in Genesis and Isaac
  is never tired.** And the noun he sums it up with is ***ʿŏnî***, affliction — **the word of Hagar
  in the desert (16:11), of Leah at Reuben's birth (29:32), and of Israel in Egypt (Exodus 3:7)** —
  applied to twenty years of employment by a man who left rich
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:34–35; **the Bible's first sackcloth, worn for a son who is
  alive.** ***Śaq*** enters Scripture here and becomes the standard dress of mourning and repentance for the
  rest of the canon. Jacob *"refused to be comforted"* — ***wayməʾēn ləhitnaḥēm***, from *nāḥam*, the verb
  Genesis uses of God at 6:6 — **by a household that includes the ten men who sold the boy and the one who
  knows they did**, and he is still refusing at 42:38 and 45:28, **twenty-two years later.** **And the
  suffering the chapter does not show is Joseph's**: he is silent from 37:17, and his pleading from the
  cistern is supplied only at 42:21, in the mouths of the men who ignored it
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:15; ***"here also I have done nothing"*** — **Joseph's
  only protest of innocence in the whole book**, made to a fellow prisoner rather than to a court, and
  never repeated. He said nothing after the charge in chapter 39 and is never recorded as cleared of it.
  **The *gam*, *also*, quietly claims he had done nothing in the other place either**, without
  relitigating it. He calls the prison ***habbôr***, *the pit* — **his brothers' word for the cistern at
  37:24**
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:52, **the chapter's sting, and it is one word.** ***ʾEreṣ ʿonyî***,
  ***the land of my affliction*** — **said by the second man in Egypt, with a new name, a wife, a signet
  ring, a chariot and a country.** At the peak of everything the chapter gives him, **Joseph's own word
  for the place is *affliction***. The root is ***ʿānâ***, **and it is the root the Exodus is built
  on**: Genesis 15:13's *"they will afflict them four hundred years"* and Exodus 3:7's *"I have surely
  seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt."* **Joseph names a son with the word of the
  prophecy his family is about to live inside, and Genesis does not point it out.** The other half of
  the same breath is 41:51 — *"God has made me forget all my toil"*, ***ʿămālî*** — **thirteen years and
  a father put in one clause and given thanks for as a thing lifted**
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:21; ***ṣārat napšô***, *"the distress of his soul"* — **the only description
  anywhere in Scripture of what the pit was like for the boy inside it.** It is four words long, it is
  twenty-two years late, **and it is given in a confession rather than a narration**, by the men who
  watched it happen

- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:29–34; **a grief argued in advance, by a man who
  helped cause the last one.** [[Judah]]'s whole case is a prediction about [[Jacob]]: *"his life is
  bound up in the boy's life … when he sees that the boy is no more, that he will die"* (44:30–31).
  **He quotes the father's *Sheol* refrain twice** (44:29, 31) — **the vow of 37:35, which Judah's own
  proposal at 37:26–27 produced** — **and then concedes the agency the second time**: *your servants
  will bring down the gray hairs of your servant, our father.* **The speech ends on the thing he
  cannot face**, *"lest I see the evil that will come on my father"*

- [[Joseph Reveals Himself]] — Genesis 45:5–8, 26; **twenty-two years summarised by the man who lived
  them in four words** — *"whom you sold into Egypt"* — **and then reassigned to God as an errand.**
  [[Joseph]] does not mention the pit, the caravan, [[Potiphar]]'s house, the false charge, or the two
  forgotten years; he says ***"don't be grieved, nor angry with yourselves"*** (45:5). **The wiki reads
  this as pastoral rather than complete**, on the evidence of 50:15–21, where the same ground has to be
  covered again in harder words. **[[Jacob]]'s suffering gets the chapter's only physical description**:
  ***wayyāpag libbô***, *his heart fainted* (45:26), **the language Scripture uses of a body that has
  run out**
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 47:9; ***"Few and evil have been the days of the years of my
  life."*** **The only time a patriarch is asked to assess his own life, and the assessment is bleak.**
  **He says it in the month he gets his son back, to a foreign king, unprompted**, and he is right on
  the arithmetic — a hundred and thirty against [[Abraham]]'s hundred and seventy-five and [[Isaac]]'s
  hundred and eighty. **Genesis neither rebukes the verdict nor endorses it**
- [[Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh]] — Genesis 48:16; ***"the angel who has redeemed me from all evil."***
  ***Mikkol-rāʿ*** — **and *raʿ* is the word he used of his own years to [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]]
  eleven verses earlier**, *"few and **evil** have been the days of the years of my life"* (47:9).
  **The same man calls his life evil to a king and calls himself redeemed from all evil to God, one
  chapter apart, and Genesis reconciles the two not at all.** **Between them sits the third verdict** —
  *"the God who has fed me all my life long to this day"* (48:15). **The wiki records all three and
  harmonises none**
- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26; **the first account of the suffering from inside, and it is
  four lines long.** Chapters 1–2 reported what was done to him and 2:12–13 reported what he looked
  like to three witnesses; **3:24–26 is the only description of what it is like to be him**: *"my
  sighing comes before I eat. My groanings are poured out like water… I am not at ease, neither am I
  quiet, neither have I rest; but trouble comes."* The chapter also contains **the book's one
  admission of a dread that predates the disaster** — *"the thing which I fear comes on me"* (3:25) —
  which reads back against the continual burnt offerings of 1:5, and which Eliphaz answers four
  verses later by telling Job his piety should have been his confidence (4:6)
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 4–5; **the first account of the suffering given by someone other
  than the sufferer, and it is the discipline account**: *"happy is the man whom God corrects.
  Therefore do not despise the chastening of the Almighty. For he wounds, and binds up"* (5:17–18).
  The teaching is Proverbs 3:11–12's and Hebrews 12:5–6's, and the book does not deny it. **It denies
  it of this case**: God called Job's affliction *ḥinnām*, *without cause* (2:3), and correction
  with nothing to correct is not correction. See
  [[Do Not Despise the Chastening - Job 5.17, Proverbs 3.11-12, and Hebrews 12]]
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:1–7:21; **the sufferer's own account, and for the first
  time an argument rather than a cry.** The suffering has a weight — *"heavier than the sand of the
  seas"* (6:3) — a cause, *"the arrows of the Almighty"* (6:4), and a duration, *"months of misery"*
  (7:3), the book's only clue to how long it has lasted. And it has an author: chapter 3 named God
  once in passing (3:23); here he is the archer, and Job the target. Job's complaint is not that God
  is absent but that he will not look away (7:19)
- [[Bildad's First Speech]] — Job 8:1–22; **suffering read backwards from its outcome.** The dead
  must have sinned (8:4); the living sufferer is being given time (8:5–7). The speech divides the
  household into the condemned and the still-redeemable, and offers the survivor his life back on
  condition he accept his children's verdict.
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 9:17; **"he multiplies my wounds without cause (ḥinnām)"** — the accuser's word
  (1:9) and God's own (2:3), reached by the sufferer without having heard either. The suffering
  is now framed as a case that cannot be brought: the cause is undeserved and the court
  unavailable.
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 13:25–28; 14:1, 22; *"Will you harass a driven leaf? Will you pursue
  the dry stubble?"*; the moth-eaten garment (13:28); man *"full of trouble"* (14:1); and the dead
  man whose *"flesh on him has pain, and his soul within him mourns"* (14:22) — suffering imagined as
  outlasting even the awareness of anything else
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:7–13, 23–26; **suffering shown by its negative.** Job
  describes the wicked man's untroubled life, and every item is something he has lost: children in
  sight, a house without fear, herds that do not miscarry, music. Then two deaths, one *"in his full
  strength"* and one *"in bitterness of soul"* (21:23, 25) — his own phrase for himself (3:20; 7:11;
  10:1). *"When I remember, I am troubled. Horror takes hold of my flesh"* (21:6): the argument
  frightens the man making it.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:2, 15–17; 24:4–12; **the first suffering in the book
  that is not Job's.** Chapter 23 is Job's own — a heavy hand, a fainting heart, terror at God's
  presence — and then chapter 24 turns away from him to the poor driven off their land, the cold and
  wet who *"embrace the rock for lack of a shelter"*, the harvesters who carry sheaves hungry, and the
  wounded of the city who cry out (24:12). Job is the first speaker in the dialogue to describe anyone
  else's pain at length, and he is the one the friends accused of causing it.
- [[Now They Mock Me]] — Job 30:1–31; **suffering in every register at once**: social (the young who
  deride him, the spitting, the siege of the rabble, 30:1–15), divine (*"You have turned to be cruel to
  me"*, 30:21), bodily (bones pierced at night, skin blackened and peeling, 30:17, 30), and inward (*"my
  soul is poured out within me"*, 30:16; *"My heart is troubled, and doesn't rest"*, 30:27). Set line
  for line against chapter 29, so that every pain is measured by the honour it replaced.
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 33:19–30; **suffering as speech.** *"For God speaks once, yes twice"* (33:14): by a
  dream, and by *"pain on his bed, with continual strife in his bones"* (33:19) — Job's own body
  described, unnamed, down to the bones that *"stick out"* (33:21). The pain is a warning, sent to
  *"withdraw man from his purpose, and hide pride from man"* (33:17), and it ends with an angel, a
  ransom, and restored flesh *"fresher than a child's"* (33:25). The fullest statement of the discipline
  account in the book, and one the prologue has already excluded for Job (2:3).
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:6; 35:9; *"My wound is incurable"* (34:6) — *ḥiṣṣî*, my arrow, the arrow
  of 6:4 — quoted as evidence against the man who bears it; and *"the multitude of oppressions"* (35:9), the
  suffering of the poor, which goes unanswered, Elihu says, because no one prays.
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 36:8–21; **suffering as the means, not the sentence.** The bound man
  in *"the cords of afflictions"* is shown his pride and has his ear opened (36:8–10), and the doctrine is
  put in one line: *"He delivers the afflicted by their affliction, and opens their ear in oppression"*
  (36:15) — the one idea in Elihu that the friends never had. Then it is turned on Job: *"you have chosen
  this rather than affliction"* (36:21). The prologue has already told the reader that this is not why Job
  suffers (2:3).
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:1–40:5; **the sufferer answered and the suffering unmentioned.** God
  speaks to Job at length and says nothing of his losses, his sores, or the wager; the speech has no
  *because*. Job's response is silence, not resolution (40:4–5), and God speaks again.
- [[Behemoth and Leviathan]] — Job 40:6–41:34; **the second speech, and still no word of the pain.** It answers
  the lawsuit — *"Will you condemn me, that you may be justified?"* (40:8) — and not the suffering that drove
  it; the reason for Job's suffering, which the reader knows, is never given to him.
- [[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]] — Job 42:1–6; **answered and not explained.** Job's last words mention neither his losses nor
  his sores, and ask for no reason. *"Now my eye sees you"* (42:5) is the whole of what has changed, and it is
  said before anything is restored (42:10).
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:7–17; **restored and not explained.** The narrator's last word on the cause is *"all the evil
  that Yahweh had brought on him"* (42:11), with no accuser, no wager, and no reason. The ten dead children are not
  replaced by doubling, and the sores of 2:7 are never mentioned again. The suffering is ended; it is not accounted
  for.
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:11–14; *"they made their lives bitter"* — *mārar*, the root of the bitter herbs
  (12:8) and of Marah (15:23). No lament is recorded until 2:23
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:23–25; **groaning, *nəʾāqâ*, is the first thing God is said to hear in
  Exodus.** The suffering has gone on through a king's whole reign and Moses' whole exile without
  comment; when it reaches God the response is four verbs — *heard*, *remembered*, *saw*, *knew*
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 3:7; ***"I know their sorrows"*** — ***yādaʿtî ʾet-makʾōbāyw*** — **the object that
  2:25's bare *God knew* left out.** God names the suffering as *affliction*, *cry*, *oppression*, and
  *sorrows*, four words in three verses, and says he knows the last
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:6–21; **suffering made worse by the first step of deliverance.**
  The request to worship doubles the labour; the foremen are beaten (*wayyukkû*, the *nākâ* of 2:11–12);
  and the people turn on Moses. By 6:9 they cannot listen *"for anguish of spirit, and for cruel
  bondage"*
- [[I Am Yahweh]] — Exodus 6:9; ***qōṣer rûaḥ***, ***"anguish of spirit"***, literally *shortness of
  spirit* — **suffering that makes promise inaudible.** The noun occurs nowhere else in Genesis,
  Exodus, or Job
- [[Bitter Water at Marah]] — Exodus 15:22–25; **thirst, and the bitterness of Egypt tasted again.** Three
  days without water, then water that is *mārîm*, the root of the lives *"made bitter"* in Egypt (1:14) and
  of the bitter herbs (12:8; WLC). The place keeps the name *Marah* after the water is made sweet
- [[Trust in Yahweh with All Your Heart]] — Proverbs 3:11–12; discipline as a father's, placed after five promises
