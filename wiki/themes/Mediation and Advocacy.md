# Mediation and Advocacy

## Definition / overview

The need for someone to stand *between* — between God and a person, or between two parties one of
whom is God — and the Bible's various answers to it. The theme has two distinguishable strands that
converge late:

- **Mediation** — someone who carries between the two sides: Moses on the mountain, the priest at
  the altar, the prophet with a word. The direction is usually God-to-people and back.
- **Advocacy** — someone who takes a side in a proceeding: a witness, a defender, a guarantor, an
  intercessor. The setting is a court.

Job's contribution is the second, and it is the most audacious version of it in Scripture, because
the party his advocate would have to argue against is God. Four legal offices are reached for
across the book, escalating:

1. **An umpire** — *"There is no umpire between us, that might lay his hand on us both"* (9:33).
   *Môkîaḥ*, an arbitrator with authority over both parties. Job states this as an absence — or,
   on the Septuagint's reading (*lû* for *lōʾ*), as a wish. See [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]].
2. **A witness and a voucher** — *"my witness is in heaven. He who vouches for me is on high"*
   (16:19), asked to *"maintain the right of a man with God"* (16:21). Job states this as a fact.
3. **A guarantor** — *"Now give a pledge, be collateral for me with yourself. Who is there who will
   strike hands with me?"* (17:3). *ʿĀrab*, to stand surety, with the handshake of Proverbs 6:1 and
   17:18. Job asks **God to post bond for Job against God**, and nothing else in the Hebrew Bible
   asks this.
4. **A redeemer** — *"But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives. In the end, he will stand
   upon the earth."* (19:25). *Gōʾēl*, the kinsman with the legal duty to buy back a relative's land or freedom and to
   avenge his blood.

The identity of the figure in 16:19 is genuinely unsettled, and the wiki does not settle it; the
three readings — God himself, a distinct heavenly advocate, and Job's own cry personified — are set
out on [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] and on [[The Witness in Heaven]].

### Where the theme starts — Genesis 18:22–33

**Intercession enters the Bible in an argument, not a prayer.** Abraham does not ask for mercy for
Sodom; he asks whether destroying it would be *right*, and the ground he stands on is a phrase God
had used four verses earlier about him — *ṣədāqâ ûmišpāṭ*, righteousness and justice (18:19). The
standard is handed over and immediately turned around.

Three features of the founding scene set the theme's shape for everything after it.

1. **The advocate has no office.** He is not a priest, a prophet (that word arrives at 20:7), or a
   king; he is a man standing on a road who *"came near"* (*wayyiggaš*) and started talking.
2. **He argues for people he has no tie to** — a Canaanite city, with the one relative inside it
   never named. Where later intercessors plead for their own (Moses for Israel, Job for his
   friends), the first one pleads for strangers.
3. **He asks for the guilty to be spared *because of* the righteous**, not for the righteous to be
   pulled out. That mechanism — a few holding up the many — is what Ezekiel 14:12–20 will later
   refuse by name, and what the New Testament will make its central claim.

**And God concedes six times without resisting once.** Whatever the passage is, it is not a man
wearing God down: it is a man discovering that the door was already open, and stopping at ten. See
[[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].

Two things are worth holding whichever reading is taken. **The heavenly court in this book already
contains someone taking an interest in Job's case, and he is the prosecution.** The accuser of Job
1–2 is the office Job is unknowingly asking for the counterpart of. And **Job wants an advocate
*against* God**, which is not what the New Testament's advocate texts describe; 1 John 2:1 has an
advocate *with the Father* and on the sinner's side, which is a different configuration and a
different problem. Reading Christ straight into Job 16:19 without noticing the difference flattens
what Job is asking for.

**At Sinai mediation carries both words and people.** Moses brings the covenant offer down,
the people's assent back, and the prepared assembly out to meet God (19:3–17). He speaks and
God answers before the people, but access remains differentiated: he is summoned up, sent
back down, and told to bring Aaron (19:19–25). Public hearing does not eliminate mediation.
See [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] and [[Sinai and Zion - Exodus 19 and Hebrews 12]].

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 18:22–33** — Abraham bargaining for Sodom: intercession's first appearance in Scripture,
  and the first person to argue with God on someone else's behalf. See
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Genesis 20:7** *(not yet ingested)* — Abraham called a prophet, *"and he will pray for you"*:
  the office named.
- **Job 9:32–33; 16:19–21; 17:3; 19:25–27** — the four offices above.
- **Job 33:23–24** — Elihu's proposal: *"If there is beside him an angel, an interpreter, one among
  a thousand, to show to man what is right for him; then God is gracious to him"* — the book's own
  candidate for the figure, offered by the speaker nobody answers. See [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]].
- **Job 42:8–10** — the resolution nobody expects: Job intercedes *for the friends*, and God accepts
  him. The man who spent the book asking for an advocate ends it as one — [[The Restoration of Job]].
- **Exodus 32:11–14, 30–32** — Moses standing in the breach, *"blot me, I pray you, out of your book
  which you have written"*: mediation at its costliest in the Torah.
- **Numbers 12:6–8; Deuteronomy 5:5** — Moses' standing office, *"I stood between Yahweh and you at
  that time"*.
- **Leviticus 16** — the Day of Atonement: mediation institutionalized, and made repeatable.
- **1 Samuel 2:25** — *"If a man sins against another, God will judge him; but if a man sins against
  Yahweh, who shall intercede for him?"* — the problem stated as a question, exactly Job's.
- **Zechariah 3:1–5** — Joshua the high priest with the accuser at his right hand, and the Angel of
  Yahweh rebuking the accuser and reclothing him. The closest thing in the prophets to what Job
  asks for, with the roles filled.
- **Isaiah 53:12** — *"he bore the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors."*
- **Jeremiah 15:1; Ezekiel 22:30** — mediation looked for and not found: *"I sought for a man among
  them who would build up the wall and stand in the gap before me for the land."*
- **1 Timothy 2:5** — *"there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ
  Jesus."*
- **Hebrews 7:25; 8:6; 9:15, 24; 12:24** — the letter that makes this its central category: a
  mediator of a better covenant, appearing *"before the face of God for us"*, and blood that
  *"speaks better than that of Abel"*.
- **Romans 8:26–27, 34** — a double intercession, the Spirit's within and Christ's above.
- **1 John 2:1** — *"if anyone sins, we have a Counselor with the Father, Jesus Christ, the
  righteous"* — *paraklētos*, the advocate called alongside.
- **Revelation 12:10** — *"the accuser of our brothers … who accuses them before our God day and
  night"*, thrown down. The office Job 1–2 shows in operation, ended.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:18–21

The people themselves ask Moses to speak, promising to listen while fearing that direct divine
speech will kill them (20:19). He explains the encounter and approaches the thick darkness as they
remain far off (20:20–21). The mediator is requested by the assembly as well as commissioned by God.
Deuteronomy 5:28 approves their request; it should not automatically be called rebellion. See
[[The Ten Commandments Retold - Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5]] and
[[Sinai and Zion - Exodus 19 and Hebrews 12]]. See [[The Ten Commandments]].

### A messenger who must be heard — Exodus 23:20–33

At the close of the Book of the Covenant, the mediator promised is not a human advocate but an
angel. He is sent ahead to keep Israel on the way and bring it to the land, and Israel is told to
*"listen to his voice"*. He also *"will not pardon your disobedience"* (23:20–22). Unlike Moses, who
pleads for the people (32:11–14, 31–32; [[The Golden Calf]]), this mediator is described only as a
guide to be obeyed. After the calf, Moses refuses to go without God’s presence (33:15;
[[Show Me Your Glory]] ). See [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] and
[[An Angel Before You - Genesis 24, Exodus 23, and Exodus 33]].

### The mediator of the covenant — Exodus 24:1–18

Moses carries the words down, the people's answer back, writes the words, reads them, and speaks the
formula over the blood (24:3–8). He alone may *"come near"* (24:2), and he alone enters the cloud
(24:18). Hebrews 9:19–20 recalls this scene by Moses' role — *"when every commandment had been spoken by
Moses to all the people"* — and Hebrews 12:24 calls Jesus *"the mediator of a new covenant"* (WEB; *not
yet ingested*). See [[The Blood of the Covenant]].

**The meeting place now has an inner boundary.** The ark where God promised to speak to Moses
(Exodus 25:22) belongs within the veil (26:33–34). Chapter 26 does not yet appoint the priests or
state who may enter when. Hebrews 9:6–8 later reads the two spaces in terms of restricted priestly
access, and 10:19–22 urges believers to approach through Jesus. This is a later interpretation of
the sanctuary, not a prediction expressly stated in Exodus 26. See [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]] and [[The Veil - Separation in Exodus and Access in Hebrews]].

### The altar, court, and maintained light — Exodus 27:1–21

Exodus 27:20–21 distinguishes the hearer who must command Israel, the people who bring
the oil, and Aaron and his sons who keep the lamp in order on their behalf. This is a
specified representative service rather than an act of intercessory speech. The lamp's
location outside the veil also keeps this duty distinct from the meeting with Moses above
the ark in 25:22. See [[The Altar and the Court]].

### The priestly garments — Exodus 28:1–43

Aaron bears the twelve names on his shoulders and heart *"for a memorial before Yahweh"*,
bears *"the judgment of the children of Israel on his heart"*, and bears *"the iniquity of the
holy things"* so that the people's gifts *"may be accepted"* (28:12, 29–30, 38). The mediator's
role is described through clothing: what he wears is what he carries for others. See [[The Priestly Garments]].

### The ordination of the priests — Exodus 29:1–46

Moses installs the mediators: every act of the ordination is his (29:4–26). The priests then begin
their own mediation at the daily altar, at the door where God says *"I will meet with you"*
(29:42). Atonement is made for the priests before they make it for anyone else (29:33). See [[The Ordination of the Priests]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Moses pleads for a guilty people and secures relief from the threat of total destruction
(32:11–14). Afterward he seeks atonement and asks to be erased if forgiveness is refused
(32:30–32). **Qualification:** treating this as a fully specified offer to pay for others goes
beyond the text; solidarity in their fate is also possible. God’s answer assigns erasure to the
sinner and does not accept Moses as a substitute (32:33). See [[Blot Out - The Flood, Amalek, and God's Book]]. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### Presence after the calf — Exodus 33

Moses’ intimate access is described in 33:7–11 and exercised for the nation in 33:12–17. He insists
that God regard Israel as his people and that both mediator and people require divine accompaniment.
God agrees because Moses has found favor and is known by name (33:17). Mediation here secures
presence after the threatened withdrawal; no sacrifice or substitution is performed. See
[[Show Me Your Glory]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Moses' privileged encounter leads to a request for the whole people: divine accompaniment,
pardon of our iniquity and sin, and acceptance as God's inheritance (34:8–9). He does not claim
Israel has ceased to be stiff-necked. His inclusion of himself expresses solidarity without
narrating his participation in making the calf. Covenant renewal follows (34:10, 27), and no
sacrifice or substitution by Moses is performed in this unit. See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The shining face — Exodus 34:29–35

Moses' mediation continues after covenant renewal: he calls the frightened leaders and
people near and relays all the commands received on Sinai (34:31–32). The veil is put on after
he finishes speaking, not before they hear him (34:33). Verses 34–35 extend this into a
repeated movement before Yahweh and back to Israel. His distinction serves communication,
rather than making the commands a private possession.

See [[The Shining Face of Moses]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

Moses assembles all the congregation and transmits Yahweh's commands (35:1, 4); the final verse
credits the commanded work to Yahweh through Moses (35:29). His mediation now makes shared work
possible. The willing people respond to a communicated instruction, rather than receiving an
independent design or watching Moses manufacture the sanctuary alone.

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

## Key passages

- [[The Freewill Offering]] — Exodus 35:1–29; Moses brings the mountain instructions to the whole assembly.

- [[The Shining Face of Moses]] — Exodus 34:29–35; Moses moves between divine speech and public instruction.

- [[The Covenant Renewed]] — Exodus 34:1–28; Moses intercedes in solidarity after receiving the proclamation.

- [[Show Me Your Glory]] — Exodus 33:1–23; The favored mediator pleads for shared presence.

- [[The Golden Calf]] — Exodus 32:1–35; Moses pleads for a guilty people and secures relief from the threat of total destruction (32:11–14).

- [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] — Genesis 18:22–33, the theme's founding text:
  an advocate with no office, arguing for a city he has no tie to, on a standard he was given four
  verses earlier, against a defendant who never once objects
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — the witness, the voucher, and the demand for
  surety, in eight verses
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — the fourth and last officer, and the only one not drawn from
  the courtroom. A *gōʾēl* is the nearest able kinsman on whom a duty falls by blood, and Job
  asserts one in the verse after eight verses proving he has no kin left
- [[The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood]] — what the word carries: buying back land, redeeming a
  relative out of slavery, marrying a widow to raise a name, and avenging blood
- [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] — Genesis 14:18–20, the office itself arriving: *kōhēn*, one
  who stands between, blessing a man on God's behalf and God on the man's. Hebrews 7:25 takes the
  same figure as the answer to Job's search. See [[Priesthood]]

- [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] — Genesis 19:29, the founding intercession's answer,
  and it is not in the terms it was asked in: the place is not spared, the man the advocate never
  named is carried out of it, and the advocate is never told
- [[Abraham and Abimelech]] — Genesis 20:7, 17, where the theme acquires its vocabulary and its
  office in one speech. ***Hitpallēl*, the Bible's ordinary verb for prayer, occurs here for the
  first time**, and so does *nābîʾ* — and God defines the second by the first: *"he is a prophet,
  and he will pray for you, and you will live."* The intercession is then performed at 20:17 and
  answered. Two things make it unlike Genesis 18: the advocate is the guilty party, and the
  beneficiary is an outsider. See
  [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]]
- [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] — Genesis 37:21–22, 26–27; **two interventions, and between them they show
  how little advocacy can accomplish.** [[Reuben]] argues three times — *"let's not take his life … shed no
  blood … lay no hand on him"* — **and the last clause is the angel's at Moriah** (22:12), spoken here by a
  brother in the one chapter of the cycle where no divine voice speaks. **He wins on the letter and the
  narrator has to supply the intention his speech does not contain.** Then [[Judah]] argues from *mah-beṣaʿ*,
  *what profit*, and *"his brothers listened to him"* — **which the chapter never says of Reuben.** The
  successful advocate saves a life by arranging a sale; the sincere one comes back to an empty pit
- [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:11, 14, 25; **the theme by its absence, which is the sharpest form
  the wiki has recorded it in.** [[Tamar]] is owed a son by a household that will not produce one, and
  **there is nobody to take her part**: no brother, no father who acts, no elder, no forum. **The
  remedy that will eventually exist is the one Deuteronomy 25:7–10 creates — and it makes the widow her
  own advocate**, bringing the refusing brother before the elders herself, pulling off his sandal,
  spitting, and fixing a name of contempt on his house. **None of that machinery exists yet**, and
  Genesis 38 is what the same situation looks like without it. **So she advocates for herself in the
  only forum available**, which is a road, and **the brief she files is three objects and six words**:
  *"by the man, whose these are, I am with child."* She does not name him, in private, while under
  sentence of death — **giving the man who could burn her the chance to convict himself instead.** See
  [[Levirate Marriage - Genesis 38, Deuteronomy 25, and Ruth]]
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:14; 41:9–13; **a man asks for an advocate and does not
  get one.** Joseph's request is precisely for representation — *"make mention of me to Pharaoh"*,
  ***wəhizkartanî***, cause the king to remember me — **from the one person in the building with access
  to a king.** The advocacy eventually arrives two years late and **from a man confessing a fault**:
  *"I remember my faults today"* (41:9). **The theme's usual shape in Genesis is intercession offered
  unasked**; this is the one place it is asked for and withheld
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:9–13, 55, **the advocacy of 40:14 arrives two years
  late, in the wrong frame, and it works.** The cupbearer speaks **only after the king's own
  establishment has failed publicly**, opens with ***"I remember my faults today"*** — **his own
  offences, not Joseph's case** — and **describes the man without naming him**: *a young man, a Hebrew,
  servant to the captain of the guard.* **It is the thinnest possible advocacy and it is sufficient.**
  Then the role inverts: **by the end of the chapter Joseph is the one people are sent to.** Pharaoh's
  own starving subjects cry to him for bread and he answers ***"Go to Joseph. What he says to you, do"***
  (41:55) — **a king referring his people to an intermediary**, which is where the rest of the book's
  brother-scenes begin
- [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]] — Genesis 42:23, 37; ***mēlîṣ***, the noun that elsewhere means *mediator* (Job 33:23), **here
  reduced to a court functionary** — and the chapter's plot depends entirely on him. **[[Reuben]]'s
  surety at 42:37 is advocacy that fails** because it offers the wrong collateral (two grandsons), and
  it sets up [[Judah]]'s offer of himself at 43:9, which is the one that works
- [[The Second Journey]] — Genesis 43:8–10, 23; **the book's turning point for the theme.** [[Judah]]'s
  ***ʿārab*** at 43:9 — *"I'll be collateral for him. From my hand will you require him"* — is
  **the first voluntary suretyship in Scripture**: a man interposing himself, by name and in advance,
  between a debt and the one who would otherwise pay it. Set against [[Reuben]]'s offer of two grandsons
  (42:37), **which Jacob does not dignify with a reply**, it establishes the difference between
  collateral you own and collateral you are. **And [[Joseph's Steward]] stands between the parties in
  both directions** — *"Peace be to you. Don't be afraid"* (43:23), and then the cup at 44:1–12. See
  [[I Will Be Surety for Him - Judah's Pledge and the Guarantor in Scripture]]

- [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]] — Genesis 44:18–34; **advocacy that turns into substitution in
  its last sentence, and the theme's high point in the Old Testament.** [[Judah]] ***draws near***
  (***wayyiggaš***, 44:18, the verb of [[Abraham]] at 18:23) and speaks for seventeen verses — **the
  longest speech in Genesis** — **without once disputing the charge.** He argues from a third party
  who is not present and cannot be produced: *"his life is bound up in the boy's life … he will die"*
  (44:30–31). **Then he stops advocating and offers to be the defendant**: *"please let your servant
  stay instead of the boy"* — ***taḥat hannaʿar*** (44:33), **the preposition of the ram at 22:13.**
  An advocate who cannot win the case takes the sentence, and the disguise does not survive it (45:1)
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 5:1, **the theme raised in Job in order to be dismissed**: *"Call
  now; is there any who will answer you? To which of the holy ones will you turn?"* Every officer
  Job later reaches for — umpire (9:33), witness (16:19), guarantor (17:3), redeemer (19:25) — is an
  answer to this question. **And the book reverses it exactly at 42:8**: Eliphaz is sent to Job, *"my
  servant Job shall pray for you"*, and the man told he had no one to turn to becomes the one his
  counsellor must turn to. See [[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]]
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 9:32–35; **the first of the four officers, and the only one named as missing.** *"For he
  is not a man, as I am… There is no umpire (mōkîaḥ) between us, that might lay his hand on us
  both."* The problem is stated exactly: a dispute between two men goes to a third, and there is
  no third above God. The Septuagint and Syriac read *"would that there were an umpire"* — one
  letter's difference — which makes the absence a wish; the wiki keeps the Masoretic *there is not*
  as primary. Elihu answers the verse twice: as *"one among a thousand"* (33:23, picking up 9:3)
  and by offering himself, *"formed out of the clay"*, as the man-sized party (33:6–7).
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 13:3, 10, 15, 22; **the umpire's verb taken up by the defendant.**
  *Mōkîaḥ*, the umpire Job said did not exist (9:33), is from *yākaḥ*; in chapter 13 Job uses the verb
  himself — *"I desire to reason (hôkēaḥ) with God"* (13:3), *"I will maintain (ʾôkîaḥ) my ways
  before him"* (13:15) — and warns the friends that God *"will surely reprove (hôkēaḥ yôkîaḥ)"* them
  for advocating falsely on his behalf (13:10). Lacking an arbiter, he will plead his own case.
  13:19's *"Who is he who will contend with me?"* is Isaiah 50:8's question, where the Servant has the
  vindicator Job lacks. See [[Who Will Contend with Me - Job 13, Isaiah 50, and Romans 8]]
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:30; ***"He will even deliver him who is not innocent. Yes, he
  shall be delivered through the cleanness of your hands."*** The friends' one statement of
  intercession, made by the friend who denied at 5:1 that Job had anyone to turn to, and fulfilled
  at 42:8 with Eliphaz as the guilty man delivered by Job's hands. See
  [[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]]
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:3–7; **the hearing imagined without a
  mediator.** In chapter 9 a trial with God was hopeless because God would overpower him, and Job
  wished for an umpire (9:33); in chapters 16 and 19 he reached for a witness and a redeemer. Here he
  wants only to find God's *seat*: *"Would he contend with me in the greatness of his power? No, but
  he would listen to me"* (23:6). The obstacle has moved from God's power to God's absence, and no
  third party is invoked. See [[Who Will Contend with Me - Job 13, Isaiah 50, and Romans 8]].
- [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] — Job 31:35–37; *"oh that I had one to hear me! Behold, here is my signature! Let the Almighty
  answer me! Let the accuser write my indictment!"* **The case filed without an advocate.** The umpire
  (9:33), the witness (16:19) and the redeemer (19:25) are not invoked; Job signs his own deposition
  (*tāw*, a mark) and asks only for a hearer and a written charge from *ʾîš rîbî*, the opposing party.
- [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]] — Job 32:12; 33:6–7, 23–24; **the advocate offered, and changed.** The friends had no *mōkîaḥ*
  for Job (32:12, *"no one who convinced Job"*) — 9:33's missing umpire. Elihu offers himself as the
  man-sized party, *"formed out of the clay"*, whose *"terror shall not make you afraid"* (33:6–7), and
  then describes *"an angel, an interpreter, one among a thousand"* (*malʾāk mēlîṣ*, 33:23) — Joseph's
  *interpreter* (Genesis 42:23) — at whose word God says *"I have found a ransom"* (33:24). **Job's
  advocate was to vindicate the innocent; Elihu's rescues the penitent.** See
  [[I Have Found a Ransom - Job 33.24, Psalm 49, and Mark 10.45]].
- [[Elihu on God's Justice]] — Job 34:23; 35:14; **the hearing refused, and the case acknowledged.** *"He
  doesn't need to consider a man further, that he should go before God in judgment"* (34:23): the trial
  Job has asked for since 9:32 is unnecessary to a God who sees. And yet *"The cause is before him, and you
  wait for him!"* (35:14) — *dîn*, the case on the docket, Elihu's one concession that Job's suit has been
  filed.
- [[Elihu on God's Greatness]] — Job 37:19–20; **the hearing declared impossible.** *"Teach us what we
  shall tell him, for we can't make our case (naʿărōk) by reason of darkness. Will it be told him that I
  would speak? Or should a man wish that he were swallowed up?"* — Job's *ʿārak*, *"I have set my cause in
  order"* (13:18; 23:4), ruled out. God's next words ask for it: *"I will question you, then you answer
  me!"* (38:3).
- [[Out of the Whirlwind]] — Job 38:3; 40:2; **no mediator, and the umpire's word given to Job.** The
  hearing Job asked for is granted on his own terms (13:22) with no third party; and God calls Job
  *mōkîaḥ ʾĕlôah*, *"he who argues with God"* (40:2) — 9:33's missing *umpire*. See
  [[The Witness in Heaven]].
- [[The Restoration of Job]] — Job 42:8–10; **the advocate Job asked for, become.** Eliphaz had asked *"To which of the
  holy ones will you turn?"* (5:1); God sends Eliphaz to Job. *"My servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept
  him"* — *pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, I will lift his face — and the restoration follows the prayer. The umpire, witness and
  Redeemer Job sought (9:33; 16:19; 19:25) were to plead his case; he ends pleading theirs. See
  [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]].
- [[Moses Flees to Midian]] — Exodus 2:11–19; **three interventions for the wronged, in widening circles** — an
  Egyptian against a Hebrew, a Hebrew against a Hebrew, shepherds against seven women. **The first two,
  for his own people, fail; the third, for strangers, is rewarded with a home.** The verbs of the third
  are the exodus's own: *yāšaʿ*, *saved* (2:17; 14:30), and *nāṣal*, *delivered out of the hand*
  (2:19; 3:8). See [[Who Made You a Prince and a Judge - The Rejected Deliverer]]
- [[The Burning Bush]] — Exodus 4:14–16; **mediation given a structure.** *"He will be to you a mouth, and you will be
  to him as God"* — God's words to Moses, Moses' to [[Aaron]], Aaron's to the people. **Moses the
  intercessor of later chapters begins as a man who asks to be excused from mediating at all** (*"Please
  send someone else"*), and is given a mediator of his own
- [[Moses Returns to Egypt]] — Exodus 4:28–30; **the chain in use.** Moses tells Aaron *"all Yahweh's words"*, and
  Aaron speaks them to the people and does the signs; Moses is silent in the scene. God → Moses →
  Aaron → the people, exactly as 4:15–16 arranged
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:15–23; **two appeals up two chains.** The Israelite officers
  plead with Pharaoh for their people and are refused; Moses takes their curse to God and pleads for
  *"your people"* — **the people who have just cursed him** — and is answered
- [[Aaron's Staff Before Pharaoh]] — Exodus 7:1–2; **a chain of four links.** God commands Moses,
  Moses speaks to Aaron, Aaron speaks to Pharaoh, and Pharaoh is to let the people go. Moses is
  *ʾĕlōhîm* to Pharaoh and Aaron his *nābîʾ* — mediation built into the shape of the embassy
- [[Frogs, Lice, and Flies]] — Exodus 8:8–13, 28–31; **the mediator prays for the
  oppressor.** Pharaoh asks Moses to entreat Yahweh for him twice, and Moses does, without conditions
  except a warning — *"don't let Pharaoh deal deceitfully any more"* — and is heard both times. **It
  is the first time in Exodus that Moses stands between God and Egypt rather than between God and
  Israel**, as Abraham stood for Abimelech (Genesis 20:7, 17) — [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]]
- [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]] — Exodus 9:27–33; **intercession without illusion.** Moses prays for
  Pharaoh a third time, after a confession — *"I have sinned this time"* — that he openly doubts: *"I
  know that you don't yet fear Yahweh God"*. **He mediates anyway, so that the stopping of the storm
  will teach *"that the earth is Yahweh's"*** — [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]]
- [[Locusts and Darkness]] — Exodus 10:16–18; **Moses still intercedes for the king who will threaten his
  life.** The fourth intercession (8:12, 30; 9:33; 10:18); Pharaoh asks forgiveness of Moses and Aaron
  themselves (10:17). Ten verses later Pharaoh says *"in the day you see my face you shall die!"*
  (10:28). **There is no further intercession for Pharaoh in the plague narrative** —
  [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]]
- [[The Crossing of the Sea]] — Exodus 14:13–16, 21, 26–27, 31; **the mediator speaks first and is believed
  in.** Moses answers the people's terror before God has spoken to him (14:13–14); God addresses the people's
  cry to him (14:15); he stretches out his hand and Yahweh acts (14:21, 27); and the people believe *"in
  Yahweh, and in his servant Moses"* (14:31) — **belief in the mediator named beside belief in God for the
  first time**. Paul's *"baptized into Moses"* (1 Corinthians 10:2) names the same bond — see
  [[Baptized into Moses - The Sea in 1 Corinthians 10 and Hebrews 11]]
- [[Bitter Water at Marah]] — Exodus 15:24–25; **the complaint turned into a prayer.** The people murmur
  *"against Moses"* (the first *lûn*, WLC), and Moses does not answer them; he *"cried to Yahweh"*, and the
  remedy is shown to him, not to them. **The mediator receives the complaint and passes it upward**
- [[Manna and Quail]] — Exodus 16:6–9; **the mediators step aside.** *"Who are we, that you murmur against us?
  … Your murmurings are not against us, but against Yahweh"* — Moses and Aaron refuse to be the object of the
  complaint and pass it through; Moses gives Aaron the words, and Aaron speaks as the glory appears
- [[Water from the Rock]] — Exodus 17:2–6; **the mediator in danger from those he mediates for.** *"Why do you
  quarrel with me? Why do you test Yahweh?"*; *"They are almost ready to stone me"*; sent to *"walk on before
  the people"* with the elders as witnesses. **The quarrel with the mediator is counted as a test of God**
- [[The Battle with Amalek]] — Exodus 17:11–12; **the mediator who needs holding up.** *"When Moses held up his
  hand, Israel prevailed"*; his hands grow *kəbēdîm*, *heavy* — the word of his *"heavy"* mouth (4:10; WLC) — and
  *"Aaron and Hur held up his hands, the one on the one side, and the other on the other side"*. **As Aaron became
  his mouth at the bush, Aaron and Hur become his hands.** The text does not say the raised hand is
  intercession; that reading is reported on the passage page, not adopted
- [[Jethro's Counsel]] — Exodus 18:19; ***"You represent the people before God, and bring the causes to
  God"*** — *hĕyēh ʾattâ lāʿām mûl hāʾĕlōhîm* (WLC). **Jethro narrows Moses' mediation to what only he can do** and
  hands the rest to others: the *"heavy"* mouth (4:10), *"heavy"* hands (17:12), and now the *"too heavy"* work
  (18:18), each shared — [[Too Heavy for You Alone - Exodus 18, Numbers 11, and Deuteronomy 1]]
- [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] — Exodus 19:3–25; Moses relays speech and leads the people to the encounter

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:18–21; the assembly requests the mediator who approaches
  where it will not
- [[The Angel Sent Ahead]] — Exodus 23:20–33; a guide to be obeyed, who does not pardon.
- [[The Blood of the Covenant]] — Exodus 24:1–18; Moses alone comes near, relays, writes, reads, and sprinkles
- [[The Ark, the Table, and the Lampstand]] — Exodus 25:22; ***"There I will meet with you"*** — a fixed meeting place, *"from between the two
  cherubim"*, where God speaks to one man *"all that I command you for the children of Israel"*

- [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]] — Exodus 26:1–37; The meeting place above the ark is situated within a separating veil.

- [[The Altar and the Court]] — Exodus 27:1–21; Moses transmits the rule; Israel provides oil; Aaron and his sons tend it on the people’s behalf.
- [[The Priestly Garments]] — Exodus 28:1–43; the high priest carries Israel's names, judgment, and iniquity before Yahweh.
- [[The Ordination of the Priests]] — Exodus 29:1–46; Moses ordains the priests; the meeting place at the tent's door.
