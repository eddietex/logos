# Work

## Definition / overview

Labour as a human vocation. The Bible's placement of it is the whole argument: **work appears
before the fall, not after it.** *"Yahweh God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to
cultivate and keep it"* (2:15) comes fourteen verses before the prohibition is broken and a
chapter before the ground is cursed. What Genesis 3 adds is not work but *futility* — thorns,
thistles, sweat, and a harvest that fights back. Toil is the curse; work is the commission.

Three things frame it in Genesis 1–2:

1. **God works.** Six days of it, and the seventh day is defined by what it rests *from*. Human
   work is patterned on divine work before it is anything else.
2. **Work is dominion in practice.** Genesis 1:28 grants rule over the creatures; Genesis 2:19–20
   shows the man exercising it by naming them — an act of ordering and knowing, not of force.
3. **Work is service and guardianship.** The pair *ʿābad* / *šāmar*, "cultivate and keep", is
   used elsewhere of the Levites tending and guarding the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7–8, 18:5–7).
   *ʿābad* is also the ordinary verb for serving and for worshipping. The garden is worked the way
   a sanctuary is kept.

Work and rest are a matched pair, not opposites: the same account that gives the man a job gives
the seventh day a blessing. See [[Sabbath]].

## Development across Scripture

- **Genesis 1:28; 2:15** — the mandate to fill, subdue, cultivate, and keep.
- **Genesis 3:17–19** — the ground cursed; the same work now yields thorns and is done by sweat.
  The precision matters: the curse formula in 3:17 falls on *the ground*, not on the man and not
  on his labour. The commission of 2:15 is never withdrawn — he is sent out *"to till the ground
  from which he was taken"* (3:23), which is the same job in a harder field. What is new is
  resistance, and an end: the ground he works will take him back.
- **Genesis 4:2–5, 4:11–12** — two trades, honestly held: *"Abel was a keeper of sheep, but Cain
  was a tiller of the ground."* Both continue Eden's vocabulary — Cain works his father's cursed
  soil, and Abel *keeps* (the sense of 2:15) a flock — and both bring God the produce of their
  labour, which is the first time in Scripture work turns into worship. Then the curse takes its
  next step: *"when you till the ground, it won't yield its strength to you"* (4:12). Where 3:17–19
  made the ground resist, 4:12 makes it refuse, and the sentence ends a vocation rather than
  burdening one. The farmer becomes a wanderer, and the theme acquires its first case of work
  taken away.
- **Genesis 4:17, 4:20–22** — work diversifies. Cain, whose ground has refused him, builds a city;
  four generations later Lamech's three sons found herding, music, and metalwork (*"the father of
  those who dwell in tents and have livestock"*, *"the father of all who handle the harp and pipe"*,
  *"the forger of every cutting instrument of brass and iron"*). These are the Bible's first
  **specialized** trades — the first that are named as institutions rather than as what one man
  happens to do, and the first that are inherited. Genesis attaches no evaluation to any of them,
  and places all of them in the cursed line. See [[City and Civilization]].
- **Genesis 9:20** — the first trade named after the flood, and the register's next entry:
  *"Noah began to be a farmer, and planted a vineyard."* The Hebrew is *ʾîš hāʾădāmâ*, **man of the
  ground** — the same noun that has carried the curse since 3:17, that [[Cain]] worked at 4:2, and that
  Lamech named this son for relief from at 5:29. Two things follow. Viticulture joins the list of
  founded crafts, and it is the first entry that does not belong to Cain's line. And the pattern of
  4:17–24 repeats exactly: Genesis names the origin of a good thing and shows its cost in the same
  breath, this time eleven Hebrew words later. The relief Lamech hoped for from the *ʾădāmâ* arrives as
  a crop, and the crop lays the man out in his tent. See [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] and
  [[The Vine and the Cup]].
- Strands to trace as ingestion reaches them: the sabbath and jubilee legislation that limits
  work and protects workers (Leviticus 25); Ecclesiastes on the vanity and the gift of
  labour (2:18–24, 3:13); Proverbs on diligence and sloth; Jesus as a *tektōn* (Mark 6:3); Paul's
  tentmaking and his rules for it (Acts 18:3, 1 Thessalonians 4:11, 2 Thessalonians 3:10);
  Colossians 3:23.

### The commandments at Sinai — Exodus 20:9–11

Six days of labor stand beside a seventh day on which work ceases for everyone under the hearer's
authority (20:9–10). The prohibition reaches the use of servants and animals as well as the hearer's
own hands. Grounded in God's work and rest (20:11), the command places a recurring limit on the
power to extract labor. See [[The Ten Commandments]].
### The keeper's liability — Exodus 22:7–15

Keeping a neighbor's animal is treated as a duty with limits. The keeper swears an oath for an
unwitnessed loss, repays a theft, and brings the carcass of a torn animal as evidence and pays
nothing (22:10–13). The WEB's *"his neighbor's goods"* (22:8, 11) renders *məlʾeket rēʿēhû*,
using the noun for God's *"work"* in Genesis 2:2 and the Sabbath *"work"* of 20:9–10 (MAM). Jacob
says he bore the loss this law exempts (Genesis 31:39). See
[[The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22]].
See [[Laws of Property and Restitution]].

### A limit on production — Exodus 23:1–19

Six years of sowing are followed by uncultivated land whose produce feeds others (23:10–11), and six
days of work by rest for animals and dependents (23:12). Harvest and Ingathering then make the yield
of labor an occasion of worship (23:16). The field is both a source of offerings and a source of
food the owner must leave accessible.

See [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]].

**Craft includes the joining of separate pieces.** Exodus 26 follows the hammered furniture of
chapter 25 with curtains, loops, clasps, boards, sockets, and bars. The result is a single dwelling
(26:6), although its parts remain distinguishable. Skilled work produces cherubim on the curtains
and veil, and embroidery makes the entrance screen (26:1, 31, 36). The work is governed by what
Moses was shown (26:30), not by an interpretation of the colors or numbers supplied by the maker.
See [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]].

### The altar, court, and maintained light — Exodus 27:1–21

Exodus 27 moves from metalwork, woodwork, and embroidery (27:1–19) to the recurring tasks
of supplying oil and tending a lamp (27:20–21). The design must follow what Moses was shown
(27:8); the continuing service is assigned to Aaron and his sons. Building the sanctuary
and sustaining its use are distinct kinds of work within one command. See [[The Altar and the Court]].

### The priestly garments — Exodus 28:1–43

Weaving, engraving *"like the engravings of a signet"*, setting stones in gold, braiding chains,
and embroidery are specified for the garments (Exodus 28:6–39). See [[The Priestly Garments]].

### Spirit-given craft and Sabbath limits — Exodus 31

God supplies ability for real technical work: designing, metalworking, setting stones, and carving
wood (31:3–5). Oholiab and the wise-hearted company share the commission (31:6–11). The command to
keep Sabbath immediately follows (31:12–17), so sanctuary building receives no stated exemption from
the limit on labor. The gift of skill does not imply that Bezalel had no training, or that the gift
ends when construction ends; Exodus says neither. See
[[Called to Make the Sanctuary - Exodus 31 and Exodus 35-38]].

See [[Bezalel, Oholiab, and the Sabbath Sign]].

### The golden calf — Exodus 32

Aaron receives gold and fashions the calf with a tool (32:4); the tablets and their writing are
explicitly God’s work (32:16). Alongside the commissioned craft of chapter 31, the contrast
concerns what work serves and who authorizes it. Aaron’s claim that the calf simply appeared
suppresses the workmanship the narrator reports. See [[The Golden Calf]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

Moses cuts the replacement stones (34:4), whereas 32:16 attributed the first tablets themselves
to God. Divine writing is promised again (34:1); the final writing notices require the
distinction discussed in [[The Replacement Tablets - Exodus 34 and Deuteronomy 10]]. Agricultural labor is also bounded: neither plowing nor
harvest suspends seventh-day rest (34:21). See [[The Covenant Renewed]].

### The freewill offering — Exodus 35:1–29

The summons to make all the prescribed objects (35:10–19) is followed by actual spinning:
women work with their hands and bring the yarn they have spun (35:25–26). Their labor is part of
the contribution, alongside donated materials and leaders' gifts. The work is shared before the
named supervisors are announced in the next unit, and 35:1–3 places its limits at the start.

See [[The Freewill Offering]].

### The makers and the surplus — Exodus 35:30–36:7

Teaching belongs to Bezalel and Oholiab's gift (35:34); the wider company joins wisdom to
willing participation (36:1–2). The workers are already at their tasks when they report excess
materials (36:4–5). Moses halts contributions, not construction. Skilled labor includes knowing
what the work requires and communicating when its supply is sufficient.

See [[Bezalel's Workmen and the Surplus]].

### The enclosure made — Exodus 36:8–38

The wise-hearted company makes textiles, loops, clasps, boards, sockets, bars, and hangings
(36:8–38). Joining is itself part of the work: distinct curtains become a unit (36:13, 18).
The report begins collectively, so its later singular making descriptions should not erase
the workforce or turn skilled construction into one person's unaided achievement.

See [[The Tabernacle Built]].

### The furnishings made — Exodus 37:1–29

The furnishing account adds detailed metalwork and perfumery to the enclosure's textile and
structural work. Acacia is overlaid, rings cast, gold beaten into integrated forms, and oil and
incense compounded (37:1–29). Bezalel's named credit stands within the shared workforce of
36:1–2. The work includes making equipment for later service; construction and that service
are not the same act.

See [[The Furnishings Made]]. Source: the cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-24.

### The outer equipment and inventory — Exodus 38:1–31

Making the altar and court includes woodwork, casting, metal overlay, and embroidery (38:1–20). The
inventory also names Ithamar’s accounting and Oholiab’s skills (38:21–23). Shared work includes
managing materials as well as making objects; the women’s mirrors supply the basin, though their own
ministry is not described.

See [[The Altar, the Basin, the Court, and the Inventory]].

### The priestly garments made — Exodus 39:1–31

The garment account adds a practical detail to the earlier design: gold is beaten into
thin plates and cut into wires to be worked with yarn and linen (39:3). Weaving, engraving,
setting stones, braiding chains, and embroidery cooperate in the finished clothing. Alternating
singular and plural descriptions do not distribute these tasks among named individuals.

See [[The Priestly Garments Made]]. Source: cited Exodus verses checked in local raw WEB, 2026-10-25.

### The work completed — Exodus 39:32–43

The children of Israel receive collective credit for the work (39:32, 42), alongside the
earlier attribution to named leaders and skilled workers (36:1–2; 38:22–23). The articles are
brought to Moses, who inspects and blesses (39:33–43). Work has an accountable completion rather
than an indefinite demand for more. Completion of manufacture is not completion of installation:
Moses still raises the structure and finishes that work in 40:17–33.

See [[The Work Completed]]. Source: cited passages checked in the stored WEB Genesis and Exodus
texts, 2026-10-25.

### The tabernacle raised and filled — Exodus 40:1–38

The finished manufacture of 39:32 and Moses’ finished work in 40:33 refer to successive
stages: components are made and presented, then raised, arranged, and put into service.
Neither completion statement cancels the other. The final cloud and glory are not another
object the craftspeople make. Completion language invites comparison with Genesis 2:1–3,
without turning the erection into a second creation or claiming a construction day count.

See [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]]. Source: Exodus 40 checked in `raw/bible/Exodus.md` (WEB), 2026-10-25.

### Go to the ant — Proverbs 6:6–11

*"Go to the ant, you sluggard. Consider her ways, and be wise"* (6:6). The ant has *"no chief,
overseer, or ruler"* (6:7) — *šōṭēr*, the word Exodus used for the Israelite officers over the
brickmakers (Exodus 5:6, 14) — and stores her food in summer. The sluggard's *"little sleep, a
little slumber"* (6:10) brings poverty *"as a robber"* (6:11), a doublet repeated at 24:33–34.
Diligence is presented as wisdom and idleness as an assault waiting to happen; the ant's work is
the garden's vocation (Genesis 2:15), not the curse. See
[[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]] and
[[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 6; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father — Proverbs 10:4–5, 16, 26

Diligent hands and gathering in season bring provision; sleeping at harvest shames the household.
The sluggard also irritates those who rely on his errand (26). Labor’s moral direction matters (16),
and Job 24:5–11’s hungry workers prevent reversing the lesson into a claim that poverty proves
laziness.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Proverbs/Job comparisons
checked in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### A False Balance — Proverbs 11:1, 18, 26

The weighing of goods and sale of grain make ordinary commerce morally accountable. Sowing
righteousness and reaping reward use labor as an image of conduct and consequence. The text values
honest exchange and provision, not income detached from the means of acquiring it.

See [[A False Balance]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; comparisons are documented on the passage
and connection pages.

### Correction, speech, and daily conduct — Proverbs 12:9, 11, 14, 24, 27

Cultivation, rewarded hands, diligence, and using one’s game contrast with fantasy and neglect. The
warning that laziness ends in slave labor is not reversible: Exodus 1:11–14 attributes Israel’s
forced labor to oppression, and Job 24:5–11 depicts hungry workers. These texts contradict any claim
that servitude or hunger proves laziness.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 12 and the comparisons documented on
that passage page.

### Desire, gathering, and the swept field — Proverbs 13:4, 11, 23

The sluggard *desires* and has nothing while the diligent is satisfied (4); wealth gathered *"by hand"* grows (11). But 13:23 records productive fields of the poor swept away by *injustice*: in the same chapter, poverty is not always the result of idleness.

See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13.

### The ox and the stall — Proverbs 14:4, 23

*"Where no oxen are, the crib is clean, but much increase is by the strength of the ox"* (4): productive work brings mess with it, and the saying prefers the harvest. *"In all hard work there is profit"* (23), against mere talk.

See [[A Way That Seems Right]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 14.

### The sluggard's thorn patch — Proverbs 15:19

*"The way of the sluggard is like a thorn patch, but the path of the upright is a highway"* (19): laziness pictured not as rest but as a road that tears at whoever walks it, set against the upright rather than the diligent.

See [[A Gentle Answer]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15.

### The labourer's appetite — Proverbs 16:26

*"The appetite of the laboring man labors for him; for his mouth urges him on"* (26): hunger observed as the motive of work, without praise or blame.

See [[The Plans of the Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16.

### Brother to the destroyer — Proverbs 18:9

*"One who is slack in his work is brother to him who is a master of destruction"* (9). Neglect and
destruction are put in one family: the slacker does slowly what the destroyer does at once.

See [[A Strong Tower]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 18.

### Integrity, counsel, and compassion — Proverbs 19:15, 24

Verse 15 connects idle sleep with hunger; verse 24 gives the comic extreme of a hand left in the
dish instead of brought back to the mouth. Food is within reach in that picture. Neither saying
establishes that every hungry person has refused available work or food: 19:1 honors an upright poor
person, and 13:23 already names injustice as a cause of deprivation.

See [[Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity]] . Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19 and the
comparisons documented on that passage page.

### Discernment and accountability — Proverbs 20:4, 13

The sluggard's refusal to plow because of winter leaves him empty at harvest (4); love of sleep
threatens poverty (13). The warning concerns missed work and its time, not every hungry
person's conduct. Proverbs 13:23's productive field destroyed by injustice remains an explicit
alternative cause of need. The WEB gives winter as the excuse; no further agricultural
reconstruction is claimed.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 20; supporting comparisons are documented on the
passage page.

### Haste and the sluggard — Proverbs 21:5, 25

Diligent plans profit while haste rushes to poverty (5), and the sluggard's desire kills him because
his hands refuse to labor (25). Haste and idleness are both failures of steady work. See
[[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; supporting comparisons are documented on
the passage page.

### The sluggard's lion — Proverbs 22:13

*"The sluggard says, 'There is a lion outside! I will be killed in the streets!'"* (22:13, repeated
at 26:13; see [[Answer a Fool]]). The excuse is the joke: an invented danger keeps him indoors.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22; no new source-language claim.

### The skilled worker — Proverbs 22:29

*"Do you see a man skilled in his work? He will serve kings. He won't serve obscure men"* (22:29). An
observation, not a command, set between the boundary stone and the ruler's table: competence brings a
worker into the presence of power, and the next verses tell him how to behave there.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–23:11.

### The field before the house, and the field left to thorns — Proverbs 24:27, 30–34

*"Prepare your work outside, and get your fields ready. Afterwards, build your house"* (24:27): the
means before the comfort. Three verses later the speaker passes the field no one readied — thorns,
nettles, a fallen wall — and draws the lesson of 6:10–11 again: *"a little sleep, a little slumber"*
(24:30–34). See [[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]] and
[[The Vineyard Gone to Thorns - Proverbs 24.30-34, Genesis 3.18, and Isaiah 5.1-7]].

See [[The Field of the Sluggard]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 24:23–34.

### The sluggard's last portrait — Proverbs 26:13–16

Four sayings in a row name the *ʿāṣēl*. His lion in the road (13) varies 22:13; his hand buried in the
dish (15) repeats the first line of 19:24, now because he is *"too lazy"* (*nilʾāh*, *weary*) to lift
it. New are the door on its hinges, as he turns on his bed and goes nowhere (14), and his opinion of
himself: *"wiser in his own eyes than seven men who answer with discretion"* (16). The portrait adds
self-deception to laziness. See [[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]].

See [[Answer a Fool]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 26.

### The fig-tree keeper and the herdsman — Proverbs 27:18, 23–27

*"Whoever tends the fig tree shall eat its fruit. He who looks after his master shall be honored"*
(27:18): two verbs of guarding (*nōṣēr*, *šōmēr*), and the return on care is fruit for one and honour
(*yəḵubbāḏ*) for the other. The chapter closes on the herdsman told to *"Know well the state of your
flocks"* (23), because stored wealth does not last and the flock, tended, provides clothing, land, and
food each season (24–27). Unlike the sluggard portraits of 24:30–34 and 26:13–16, this is work
commended rather than its absence condemned.

See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 27; Hebrew from Sefaria.

### Integrity, law, and public life — Proverbs 28:19–20

Cultivating one’s land brings food; chasing fantasies brings poverty. Verse 19 recalls Proverbs
12:11 but closes with poverty rather than lack of understanding. Verse 20 then favors faithfulness
over haste for riches. These describe responsible conduct; verse 3’s crop-destroying oppression
within the same chapter prevents treating every failed harvest as evidence of idleness.

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28 and the comparisons
documented on the passage and connection pages.

### Agur's ants and locusts — Proverbs 30:25–27

The ants return from 6:6–8, now as *"not a strong people"* who still provide their food in summer
(25). The locusts add a second lesson: without a king they *"advance in ranks"* (27). Diligence and
order are shown in creatures without strength or overseers. See
[[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]].

See [[Four Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 30:15–33 and the comparisons documented there.

- [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]] — Genesis 29:15–30; **the Bible's first employment contract, and it buys
  a wife.** ***ʿābad***, *serve*, runs **seven times** through sixteen verses, and the arrangement is
  labour in place of a bride-price by a man who has nothing else to pay with: seven years, then seven
  more, and at 31:41 his own accounting of it — *"twenty years in your house."* The passage also
  contains **the only feat of physical strength attributed to any patriarch**, the well-stone at
  29:10, and a scene in which a newcomer tells experienced shepherds how to do their job and is
  answered with the rule, twice, patiently (29:7–8)
- [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]] — Genesis 30:16, 18; **the wages theme moved indoors.**
  ***Śākar*** is the root of Laban's question at 29:15 and of the fourteen years it produced — and here
  it is used of a husband, by his wife, twice in three verses: *"I have surely **hired** you with my
  son's mandrakes"* (***śākōr śəkartîkā***, infinitive absolute), and then *"God has given me my
  **hire**"* (30:18), which names [[Issachar]]. **The man Laban put on wages is put on wages again by
  Leah**, and Genesis gives him no reply to either
- [[Jacob's Flocks]] — Genesis 30:25–43; **the only sustained description of a trade anywhere in
  Genesis.** ***ʿĀbad*** three times in one verse (30:26, of the service that bought two wives),
  ***śākār*** three times in the contract (30:28, 32, 33), and then **seven verses of husbandry** —
  peeled rods in the watering-troughs, the young separated, and the rods used on the strong animals
  and withheld from the feeble. **The book that gives Isaac's hundredfold one clause gives Jacob's
  breeding programme a paragraph**, and the only comparable technical passages in it are the ark's
  specifications and the brickmaking at Babel. **It also contains the theme's one worker-designed
  contract**: a man who refuses a wage (*"you shall not give me anything"*), names an unpayable-looking
  one instead, and attaches an audit clause to it — *"so my righteousness will answer for me"*. **The
  employer honours the terms and hollows them out the same day**, and the worker out-engineers him.
  See [[Wealth and Possessions]]
- [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] — Genesis 31:38–41; **the fullest account of a labour contract in
  Scripture, delivered as a complaint.** Twenty years, stated twice; fourteen for two wives and six
  for a flock; wages changed ten times; no miscarriages in the herd and no rams eaten; drought by
  day, frost by night, and no sleep. **And one clause of technical liability**: *"that which was torn
  of animals, I didn't bring to you. I bore its loss"* — **the loss the law of Exodus 22:13 says a
  keeper does not have to bear.** See
  [[The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22]]
- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:9–11, 16–17, 21; **both dreams are simply the job being
  done.** The cupbearer presses grapes and puts the cup in the king's hand; the baker carries the king's
  bread on his head. **Neither dream contains a symbol that is not an office**, which is why the one
  intrusion — birds eating out of the top basket — is the whole of the second interpretation. **And the
  restoration is described entirely as the resumption of a task**: *"he gave the cup into Pharaoh's
  hand"*, the same clause the dream ended on
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:33–36, 46–49, **the longest description of administrative labour in the
  Torah, and the chapter treats competence as evidence.** The programme is unglamorous and complete:
  an officer, ***pəqidim*** (overseers, from *pāqad*), a twenty per cent levy, gathering, **storage city
  by city** — *"the food of the field, which was around every city, he laid up in the same"* (41:48) —
  and distribution by sale. **Joseph *"went throughout all the land of Egypt"*** and *"laid up grain as
  the sand of the sea, very much, **until he stopped counting**"* (41:49). **The proof that the
  interpretation was true is seven years of successful logistics**, not a sign; **the food was never
  multiplied, it was stored.** See [[Famine and Plenty]]
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:32–34; 47:3–6; ***"What is your occupation?"*** **The family's trade
  is simultaneously the thing that wins it land and the thing that makes it untouchable.** **[[Joseph]]
  coaches them to say *keepers of livestock* because *"every shepherd is an abomination to the
  Egyptians"*** (46:34); **they say *shepherds* anyway** (47:3); **and [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]]
  grants [[Goshen]] and then offers them charge of his own herds** (47:6)
- [[Joseph and the Famine]] — Genesis 47:23–24; **the terms of tenancy, stated in full.** *"Behold,
  here is seed for you, and you shall sow the land … you shall give a fifth to Pharaoh, and four
  parts will be your own, for seed of the field, for your food, for them of your households, and for
  food for your little ones."* **The Egyptians go back to the same fields they owned the year
  before**, and the provision Joseph itemises for them — households and little ones — **is word for
  word the provision he gave his own family for nothing** (47:12). **The theme's one Scriptural
  account of a whole nation's labour being repriced in a single decree**
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 7:1–2, ***"Isn't a man forced to labor on earth? Aren't his
  days like the days of a hired hand? As a servant who earnestly desires the shadow, as a hireling
  who looks for his wages…"*** — human life as *ṣābāʾ*, conscript service, and the sufferer as a day
  labourer whose evening never comes. Job uses *ṣābāʾ* of his own life again at 14:14, *"all the
  days of my warfare"*
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 24:5–6, 10–11; **labour without its fruit.** The dispossessed
  forage like wild donkeys and glean the vineyard of the wicked; then *"Being hungry, they carry the
  sheaves. They make oil within the walls of these men. They tread wine presses, and suffer thirst."*
  Deuteronomy 25:4 forbids muzzling the ox that treads the grain; these workers get less than the ox.
- [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] — Job 28:1–11; **the Bible's fullest description of a technology, and one of its
  most admiring.** Shafts sunk far from where people live, men on ropes who *"hang far from men, they
  swing back and forth"* (28:4), rock broken *"as it were by fire"* (28:5), channels cut and water
  dammed (28:10–11) — copper and iron mining of the kind practised in the Arabah. The miner goes where
  the falcon has not looked and the lion has not walked (28:7–8), and *"His eye sees every precious
  thing"* (28:10). **The poem praises the skill without reservation, and then says what it cannot reach**:
  wisdom (28:12)
- [[The Oppression in Egypt]] — Exodus 1:11–14; **work as bondage.** *"Hard service, in mortar and in brick, and in all
  kinds of service in the field"* — Babel's materials (Genesis 11:3), carried for someone else's
  storehouses. See [[Come, Let Us - Babel's Bricks and Pharaoh's]]
- [[The Birth of Moses]] — Exodus 2:9; ***"I will give you your wages"*** — *śəkārēk*, Jacob and
  Laban's word. **A Hebrew mother paid by Pharaoh's house** to keep alive the son Pharaoh ordered
  drowned, in a book whose first chapter made Israel's labour unpaid
- [[Bricks Without Straw]] — Exodus 5:6–19; **the quota.** No straw, *"the same number of bricks"*,
  the people scattered for stubble, the foremen beaten for the shortfall — **labour as pure
  extraction, administered through native overseers** (*šōṭərîm*, their first appearance). Pharaoh
  calls the request to worship idleness, *nirpîm*, twice (5:8, 17). See
  [[Come, Let Us - Babel's Bricks and Pharaoh's]]
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:16, 25–26; ***"no kind of work shall be done in them"***, and
  ***"this service"***. *ʿĂbōdâ*, the word of Egypt's *"hard service"* (1:14; 2:23; 5:9; 6:6, 9), now
  names a rite of worship; after 13:5 it belongs to the tabernacle (WLC)
- [[Jethro's Counsel]] — Exodus 18:18–22; **overwork named and divided.** *"You will surely wear away … the
  thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to perform it yourself alone"*; the cure is delegation — *"So shall
  it be easier for you, and they shall share the load with you"*. *Qāšeh*, *hard*, the word of Egypt's *"hard
  service"* (1:14), now describes the *"hard causes"* (18:26; WLC)

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:9–11; the workweek is bounded by rest for dependents as well
  as the household head
- [[Laws of Property and Restitution]] — Exodus 22:10–15; a keeper's liability limited to what he could prevent
- [[The Offering for the Sanctuary]] — Exodus 25:9; **work to a pattern.** *"According to all that I show you … even
  so you shall make it"* — the materials are given freely, the design is not; the craftsmen are named at 31:1–11
- [[The Ark, the Table, and the Lampstand]] — Exodus 25:18, 31, 36, 40; **hammered work, of one piece.** *Miqšâ*, beaten from one block of
  gold, and *"See that you make them after their pattern"*: craft held to a design the craftsman did not
  make

- [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]] — Exodus 26:1–37; Textile work, joinery, and metalwork serve a specified design (26:1–30, 36–37).

- [[The Altar and the Court]] — Exodus 27:1–21; Making the altar and court leads into the recurring work of maintaining the light.
- [[The Priestly Garments]] — Exodus 28:1–43; skilled weaving, engraving, and embroidery for vestments.
- [[The Priestly Garments Made]] — Exodus 39:1–31; Gold cut into wires and integrated with skilled textile and stone work.

- [[The Work Completed]] — Exodus 39:32–43; Shared craftsmanship is completed, presented, inspected, and approved.

- [[The Tabernacle Raised and the Glory]] — Exodus 40:1–38; Moses finishes erection after manufacture and inspection; filling is a distinct divine act.
- [[A Good Name]] — Proverbs 22:13; The sluggard's lion in the streets.
- [[Thirty Excellent Things]] — Proverbs 22:29; The skilled worker who serves kings.
- [[The Field of the Sluggard]] — Proverbs 24:27, 30–34; The field readied before the house; the sluggard's vineyard gone to thorns.
- [[Answer a Fool]] — Proverbs 26:13–16; The sluggard's lion, his hinge, his dish, and his opinion of himself.
- [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]] — Proverbs 27:18, 23–27; The fig-tree keeper, the servant honoured, the herdsman who knows his flock.
