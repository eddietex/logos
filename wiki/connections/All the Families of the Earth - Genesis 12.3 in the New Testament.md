# All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 12:3** — *"All the families of the earth will be blessed through you."* —
  [[The Call of Abram]]
- **Genesis 18:18** — *"all the **nations** of the earth will be blessed in him"*, with *gôyim* for
  12:3's *families* and *bô* for its *through you* — and spoken by God to himself, as the reason for
  telling Abraham about Sodom. See [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Genesis 22:18** — *"All the nations of the earth will be blessed by your offspring, because you
  have obeyed my voice"*: the fifth and final restatement to Abraham, now *the nations* rather than
  *the families*, now running through the offspring rather than through him, and now grounded in
  obedience. See [[The Binding of Isaac]].
- **Genesis 26:4** — *"In your offspring will all the nations of the earth be blessed"*, restated to
  Isaac in the first words God ever speaks to him, and grounded — like 22:18 — in Abraham's
  obedience, here spelled out as four terms of Torah vocabulary. See [[Isaac in Gerar]] and
  [[Because Abraham Obeyed My Voice - Law Before Sinai]].
- **Genesis 28:14** — the same clause restated to Jacob at Bethel *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 10:5, 18, 20, 31, 32** — *mišpəḥōt*, the families of the nations —
  [[The Table of Nations]]
- **Psalm 72:17** — *"men shall be blessed by him. All nations will call him blessed"*, of the king
- **Isaiah 49:6** — *"a light to the nations, that you may be my salvation to the end of the earth"*
- **Acts 3:25** — Peter: *"in your offspring will all the families of the earth be blessed"*
- **Galatians 3:6–14** — Paul: *"In you all the nations will be blessed"*, called *the Good News
  preached beforehand*

## Analysis

Genesis 12:3 ends on a clause that the rest of the Bible keeps picking up, and it contains a
grammatical ambiguity that the New Testament resolves in one direction and the Hebrew does not
obviously support.

### What the Hebrew says

*Wənibrəkû bəkā kōl mišpəḥōt hāʾădāmâ.* Two things in it are worth slowing down for.

**The nouns are pointed backwards.** *Mišpāḥâ*, family or clan, is the word
[[The Table of Nations]] uses five times to organise the seventy peoples of Genesis 10 — *"by their
families, according to their languages, in their lands, in their nations"*. And *ʾădāmâ* is the
**ground**, the thing God cursed at 3:17 and that Lamech was still complaining about when he named
Noah at 5:29. So the promise is not addressed vaguely to *everyone*; it is aimed by vocabulary at
the two specific casualties of Genesis 1–11, the scattered peoples and the cursed soil. Whoever
composed the clause knew what was in front of it.

**The verb is a niphal, and that is the crux.** *Nibrəkû* can be read:

- **passively** — *shall be blessed*: the nations receive blessing through Abram; or
- **reflexively / middle** — *shall bless themselves*: the nations will use Abram's name as their
  formula of blessing, exactly as Israel does at Genesis 48:20 — *"By you will Israel bless, saying,
  'God make you as Ephraim and as Manasseh.'"*

The internal evidence cuts both ways and it is worth stating plainly which way each piece cuts. The
clause recurs in the **niphal** at 18:18 and 28:14, and in the unambiguously reflexive **hithpael**
at 22:18 and 26:4 — and the hithpael is the strongest single argument that the whole series means
*bless themselves*, since Genesis would hardly switch to a reflexive stem to say something passive.
Against that: the Septuagint renders every occurrence passively (*eneulogēthēsontai*), the Vulgate
follows, and Jeremiah 4:2 uses the hithpael of the nations *"glorying"* in a way that is hard to
keep purely reflexive. Most modern translations, WEB included, keep the passive; most modern
commentators lean reflexive or leave it open.

**The two readings converge further than the argument suggests.** A world in which every family says
*may God make you like Abraham* is a world for which Abram has become the measure of what a blessed
life is — which is not nothing, and is not far from the passive sense in effect. What the readings
do not share is force. The passive makes Abram the channel; the reflexive makes him the standard.
Paul needs the channel.

### What the New Testament does with it

**Peter quotes it with *families* intact.** *"You are the children of the prophets, and of the
covenant which God made with our fathers, saying to Abraham, 'In your offspring will all the families
of the earth be blessed'"* (Acts 3:25). Two features are easy to miss. He is speaking in Solomon's
porch to a Jerusalem crowd, and he uses the promise to tell them what *they* have inherited — and he
keeps *patriai*, families, rather than the *ethnē*, nations, that Paul will use, which is the
Septuagint's word at 12:3 and is closer to Genesis 10's own vocabulary.

**Paul quotes it as the gospel, in advance.** *"The Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the
Gentiles by faith, preached the Good News beforehand to Abraham, saying, 'In you all the nations will
be blessed'"* (Galatians 3:8). This is a composite citation — *in you* from 12:3, *all the nations*
from 18:18 or 22:18 — and it is doing something considerable. Paul's claim is not that Genesis 12:3
contains a prediction that happens to have come true; it is that the clause **is** the gospel,
spoken to Abraham, and that the mission to the Gentiles is therefore not an expansion of the
promise but the promise itself arriving. Everything in Galatians 3 follows from that: the blessing
comes to the nations *"in Christ Jesus"* (3:14) because the offspring through whom it comes is read
as singular (3:16), and it comes *by faith* because Abraham had nothing else when it was given him.

**And the argument runs against a natural reading of election.** The obvious way to read a God who
picks one family out of seventy nations is as a narrowing. Genesis 12:3 states the opposite in the
same breath as the choosing, and Isaiah (42:6; 49:6), the psalms of the king (72:17), Peter, and Paul
all read it that way. See [[Call and Election]] and [[The Nations]].

## Significance

**It is the sentence that makes Genesis 12 the answer to Genesis 11 rather than a retreat from it.**
The primeval history ends with humanity divided into families and scattered; the patriarchal history
opens by naming those same families as the point of the whole exercise. Without this clause, the call
of Abram is the beginning of one nation's story. With it, the call is the beginning of one nation's
story *told as the mechanism for everybody else's*.

**It is also the Old Testament's own answer to the question the New Testament fights about.** The
Gentile mission of Acts and Galatians did not need a new revelation to justify itself; it needed a
reading of a clause that had been sitting in the fourth verse of the Abraham cycle the whole time.
Paul's word for that is *proeuēngelisato* — the Scripture *preached the gospel beforehand* — and
Genesis 12:3 is what he means by it.

**The unresolved grammar is worth keeping, not smoothing.** The wiki records the passive as the
reading the Septuagint, Acts, and Galatians all work from, and the reflexive as the reading the
hithpaels at 22:18 and 26:4 push toward, and adopts neither as certain. That the New Testament's
most far-reaching argument about the nations rests on a Hebrew stem that can go two ways is a fact
about the text, and stating it costs the argument less than pretending otherwise would.
