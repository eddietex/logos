# Better Than Seven Sons - Ruth 4.15 and 1 Samuel 1.8

## Analysis

Two consolations of a childless woman measure a person against a number of sons, in the same
construction: an adjective *ṭôḇ*, *good, better*, *lāḵ*, *to you*, and the comparative *min* with a
number and *bānîm* (WLC).

| Verse | Speaker | WEB | Hebrew |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ruth 4:15 | the women of Bethlehem, to Naomi | *your daughter-in-law, who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons* | *ṭôḇâ lāḵ miššiḇʿâ bānîm* |
| 1 Samuel 1:8 | Elkanah, to Hannah | *Am I not better to you than ten sons?* | *ṭôḇ lāḵ mēʿăśārâ bānîm* |

**The difference is who is speaking, and whether it consoles.** Elkanah speaks of himself, to a wife who is
weeping and not eating because *Yahweh had shut up her womb* (1 Samuel 1:5–7). His question
does not stop her weeping; she goes up and prays for a son (1:9–11, by reference). The women of Bethlehem
speak of a third person, Ruth, to Naomi, and they say it after the son is born, not instead of him. In Ruth
the comparison is praise of the daughter-in-law; in 1 Samuel it is a husband's attempt to stand in for the
child.

**Seven.** Hannah's song has *the barren has borne seven* (1 Samuel 2:5; *ʿăqārâ yālᵊḏâ šiḇʿâ*, WLC),
though Hannah herself bears Samuel and then *three sons and two daughters* (2:21), six children in all,
not seven. Seven sons is a full house in
both. Naomi had argued on the road that she had no more sons in her womb (Ruth 1:11–12) and lost the two
she had (1:5); the women's *seven* is set against that two.

**Sequence.** In the English canon Ruth stands directly before 1 Samuel, so the reader meets the
women's *seven sons* one chapter before Elkanah's *ten sons*. Neither text cites the other; the parallel is
in the construction, and the wiki makes no claim of dependence.

### 1 Samuel 1 now ingested

Read in place, 1:8 is a string of four questions, *why do you weep? Why don't you eat? Why is your heart
grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?*, and the narrative answers none of them: the next verse
has Hannah rise and go to pray (1:9). The comparison sits between two notices that *Yahweh had shut up her
womb* (1:5, 6) and her vow (1:11), so the chapter itself sets Elkanah's *ten sons* against the one son she
asks for. See [[Hannah Asks Yahweh for a Son]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 1:5–11; Hebrew WLC (OpenScriptures morphhb), 2026-11-17.

### 1 Samuel 2:5 now ingested

In the song, *seven* belongs to the barren woman, not to sons compared with a person: *the barren has borne
seven. She who has many children languishes* (2:5). The pairing is a reversal, the empty filled and the
full emptied, and it sits among other reversals of strength, food, and life (2:4–6). Read after Elkanah's
*ten sons* (1:8), the song does not answer his question either; it moves from Hannah's house to the whole
world. See [[The Song of Hannah]]. Source: stored WEB 1 Samuel 1:8; 2:4–6.

## Significance

- Both books end or begin with a woman's emptiness filled by a son whose line leads to David: Obed is
  David's grandfather (Ruth 4:17), and Hannah's son Samuel will anoint him (1 Samuel 16, not yet ingested).
- The comparison shows what each speaker values: the women put a foreign daughter-in-law's love above a
  full house of sons; Elkanah puts himself there, and the narrative does not agree with him.
- See also [[The Barren Matriarchs]] and [[Barrenness]].
