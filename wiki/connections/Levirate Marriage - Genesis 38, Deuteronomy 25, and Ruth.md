## Analysis

### Deuteronomy 25 now ingested

The law's vocabulary is narrow. The verb *yibbēm*, *perform the brother-in-law's duty*, occurs in
Genesis–Deuteronomy only at Genesis 38:8 and Deuteronomy 25:5, 7; the nouns *yābām* and *yǝbēmet* only
in Deuteronomy 25:5–9 (WLC lemma search). The condition is stated tightly: *"If brothers dwell
together, and one of them dies, and has no son"* (5). The purpose is the name: *"that his name not be
blotted out of Israel"* (6), with the verb of 25:19, *"blot out the memory of Amalek"*. The brother may
refuse (*lōʾ yaḥpōṣ*, 7); the widow goes to the elders and speaks first; she loosens his shoe and spits
in his face, and says, *"So shall it be done to the man who does not build up his brother's house"*
(9). The law does not mention Leviticus 18:16 or 20:21, and the tension recorded below stands.

**A quotation corrected.** This page earlier gave 25:10 as *"sandal removed"* and the table spoke of a
*sandal*. The WEB has *"shoe"* in Deuteronomy 25:9–10 and Ruth 4:7–8; the Hebrew *naʿal* is the same
word the WEB renders *"sandal"* at Exodus 3:5. Both are now *shoe*, matching the WEB.

Source: stored WEB Genesis 38:8; Exodus 3:5; Deuteronomy 25:5–10; Ruth 4:7–8 at bible-api.com
(`translation=web`), 2026-11-09; WLC (morphhb).

### Leviticus 20 now ingested

Leviticus 20:21 now explicitly attaches childlessness to taking a brother’s wife. Genesis 38:8
commands Onan to raise offspring for his deceased brother. The tension remains: Leviticus does not
specify here whether the brother is living or dead. Its sanction is not stated as a communal
execution order.

Source: stored WEB Leviticus 20 and the stored chapters cited. Existing broader
reception material below is retained, not independently reverified in this pass.

### Leviticus 18 now ingested

Leviticus 18:16 states the brother’s-wife prohibition without an explicit widowhood exception. The tension with Judah’s command in Genesis 38:8 remains. An exception drawn from Deuteronomy 25 is an interpretive comparison, not an exception stated in this verse.

Source: stored WEB Leviticus 18; Genesis and Exodus for the comparisons.

**Genesis 38 is the Bible's first levirate marriage and it happens four books before the law that
governs it.** Judah issues the instruction as though it needs no explanation — ***yabbēm***, a verb
built on ***yābām***, *the brother-in-law*, **and this is its first appearance in Scripture.** Nobody
asks what he means. **The institution is therefore older than the Torah's version of it**, and the
chapter is the evidence.

### What the three texts actually require

| | **Genesis 38** | **Deuteronomy 25:5–10** | **Ruth** |
|---|---|---|---|
| who owes the duty | **a brother; then, in the event, the father-in-law** | *"brothers dwell together"* and one dies **without a son** | a ***gōʾēl***, a redeemer-kinsman; the nearer one, then Boaz |
| the purpose | *"raise up offspring for your brother"* | *"that his name not be blotted out of Israel"* | *"to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance"* (4:5, 10) |
| the child's status | counted to the dead brother | *"shall succeed in the name of his brother"* | counted to Mahlon (4:10), though 4:17 calls him Naomi's |
| land involved | **none mentioned** | none mentioned | **central** — the field must be bought with the widow |
| refusal | **Onan performs it in form and voids it**; **Judah simply withholds** | a public ceremony: elders, a pulled-off shoe, spitting, and a name of contempt | the nearer redeemer declines **openly, at the gate, with the shoe** |
| the widow's remedy | **none** | **she initiates** — *"his brother's wife shall come to him in the presence of the elders"* | Naomi and Ruth engineer it; Boaz completes it |
| how she gets her son | **deception at a roadside** | **due process** | **negotiation at a threshing floor and a gate** |

**The most important row is the last three.** *Deuteronomy gives the widow standing*: she brings the
refusing brother before the elders herself, and the penalty is public and permanent — *"his name shall
be called in Israel, 'The house of him who had his shoe removed.'"* **Ruth 4 shows that machinery
working**, in daylight, with ten elders and a witnessed transfer.

**Genesis 38 is what the same situation looks like with none of it.** [[Tamar]] is sent back to her
father's house but not released; she cannot remarry, she cannot compel performance, and there is no
forum in which to raise the matter. **The chapter's whole plot is the absence of the remedy
Deuteronomy would later supply**, and the wiki reads the veil at [[Enaim]] in that light: **not a
substitute for the law, but a substitute for the absence of one.** See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

### The clash with Leviticus

**Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 forbid exactly what Deuteronomy 25 commands**: *"You shall not uncover the
nakedness of your brother's wife; it is your brother's nakedness"*, and *"if a man takes his brother's
wife, it is an impurity … they shall be childless."* **The penalty Leviticus names is the very
condition the levirate exists to cure.**

**Three ways the tension is handled, and the wiki adopts none:**

1. **Deuteronomy 25 is a stated exception** to a general rule — the case of a brother dead without a
   son, which Leviticus is not addressing.
2. **The two are from different legal traditions** and were never reconciled by their compilers.
3. **Leviticus is about a living brother's wife** and the two texts never actually overlap.

**Genesis, which predates both, reports the duty as ordinary and offers no comment at all.** Per the
schema, the wiki sets the three texts beside each other and flags the contradiction rather than
harmonising it.

### Ruth, and the text's own comparison

**The book of Ruth makes the connection itself, so this is not the wiki drawing a line.** When the
elders of Bethlehem bless Boaz's marriage they say: *"Let your house be like the house of Perez, whom
Tamar bore to Judah"* (Ruth 4:12). **Two childless widows, two men who had to be brought to perform an
obligation, one town, and one genealogy** — and the genealogy that closes Ruth begins at [[Perez]], the
child of Genesis 38.

**The differences matter as much as the parallel.** **Ruth's levirate has become a redemption**, fused
with the *gōʾēl* institution and with land: Boaz buys a field and acquires a widow in one transaction
(4:9–10), which Deuteronomy 25 says nothing about. **And Ruth does openly, with Naomi's coaching and
Boaz's cooperation, what Tamar had to do by veil and pledge.** See
[[Tamar in the Genealogies - Ruth 4, 1 Chronicles 2, and Matthew 1]] and [[The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood]].

### The Sadducees

**The one place the New Testament handles the law, it is being used as a trap.** The Sadducees, who
*"say that there is no resurrection"*, cite Deuteronomy 25 and construct a woman married to seven
brothers in succession: *"in the resurrection therefore whose wife will she be of the seven?"*
(Matthew 22:28). **The argument only works if the levirate's purpose — continuing a man's name through
his brother's child — is the only kind of continuation there is.** Jesus answers that they know
*"neither the Scriptures, nor the power of God"*, and that the resurrected *"are like God's angels in
heaven"* — **and then argues the resurrection itself from the Torah they were quoting**, out of Exodus
3:6. See [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].

### Ruth 1:1–5 now ingested

Ruth begins by producing the levirate case and then making it unanswerable. A man dies with two married
sons (1:3); then both sons die with no child reported (1:5). In Genesis 38 Judah had a third son to give;
in Deuteronomy 25:5 the duty applies *If brothers dwell together*. Here there is no surviving
brother at all, and no father to beget one, which is the point Naomi will make at 1:11–13 (not yet
ingested). The law's purpose, *that his name not be blotted out of Israel* (Deuteronomy 25:6), is at
stake from the fifth verse, and Ruth 4:10 will answer it in nearly the same terms: *that the name of the
dead may not be cut off*. See [[Famine and Death in Moab]]. Source: stored WEB Ruth 1:3–5, 11–13; 4:10;
Genesis 38:11; Deuteronomy 25:5–6.

### Ruth 1:6–18 now ingested

Naomi makes the argument the previous section anticipated. *Do I still have sons in my womb, that they may
be your husbands?* (1:11). She takes the custom further than Genesis 38 did: Judah told Tamar to wait for
Shelah to grow up (Genesis 38:11); Naomi asks whether her daughters-in-law would wait for sons not yet
conceived, and answers no (1:12–13). Her word for Orpah is *yəḇimtēḵ*, *your sister-in-law* (1:15, WLC),
the noun *yəḇēmeṯ* that Deuteronomy 25:7, 9 uses for the widow of the levirate law; in the WLC files read
(Genesis, Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, Ruth, 1 Samuel) it occurs only in those three verses. On Naomi's
reasoning the levirate road is closed, and the book reopens it through a more distant kinsman (2:20;
4:5, 10). See [[Ruth Clings to Naomi]]. Source: stored WEB Ruth 1:11–15; Genesis 38:11; Deuteronomy 25:7, 9; Hebrew WLC,
2026-11-17.

## Significance

**The institution exists because a name and a portion were the only forms of survival the earliest
texts could offer.** Deuteronomy states it flatly — *"that his name not be blotted out of Israel"* —
and **the practice is therefore an artefact of a theology with no developed afterlife.** That is why
the Sadducees could think it a decisive argument, and why the answer they get is not about levirate
law at all. See [[Life and Death]] and [[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

**And the line of David runs through the two cases where it nearly failed.** Genesis 38's levirate was
refused by one son, withheld from another, and finally discharged by the father-in-law under duress.
Ruth's was declined by the nearer redeemer at the gate. **Both times the duty is performed by the man
who was not first in line**, and both times the child is in the genealogy at Matthew 1. **Scripture
keeps the messy provenance on the record**, which is the observation the wiki takes from the thread as
a whole.

### Judges 21:17 — a tribe not blotted out

The elders' reason for finding wives for Benjamin borrows the levirate law's purpose clause. Deuteronomy
25:6 has *that his name not be blotted out of Israel*, *wəlōʾ-yimmāḥeh šəmô miyyiśrāʾēl*; Judges 21:17 has
*that a tribe not be blotted out from Israel*, *wəlōʾ-yimmāḥeh šēḇeṭ miyyiśrāʾēl* (WLC). In the WLC files of
Genesis through 1 Samuel searched, *māḥâ* (lemma 4229 a) is joined to *miyyiśrāʾēl* only in
these two verses. Whether
Judges echoes the law deliberately the text does not say; the wording is shared. The levirate keeps a dead
brother's name by a wife the family provides; Judges 21 keeps a brother tribe by wives the family takes. See
[[Wives for Benjamin]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 25:6; Judges 21:17; Hebrew WLC, 2026-11-17.

### Ruth 3:1–18 now ingested

Ruth's request at the threshing floor, *spread the corner of your garment over your servant; for you are a
near kinsman* (3:9), is where the levirate idea and the *gōʾēl* first meet in one sentence. Boaz is not a
brother, so Deuteronomy 25:5's *her husband's brother shall go in to her* does not bind him; Ruth appeals to
the redeemer's duty instead, and Boaz treats it as a duty with an order of precedence, *there is a kinsman
nearer than I* (3:12). Tamar's widowhood garments came off and a veil went on (Genesis 38:14); Ruth washes,
anoints herself, and dresses (3:3), and gives her name (3:9). Both women go to a man by their own act; Judah
does not know who she is, and Boaz asks and is told. See [[Ruth at the Threshing Floor]] and
[[At Night with a Man Who Had Drunk - Genesis 19.30-38 and Ruth 3]]. Source: stored WEB Ruth 3:1–18; Genesis
38:14–16; Deuteronomy 25:5; Hebrew WLC, 2026-11-17.
