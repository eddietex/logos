# Levirate Marriage - Genesis 38, Deuteronomy 25, and Ruth

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 38:8–11** — Judah to Onan: *"perform the duty of a husband's brother to her, and raise up
  offspring for your brother"* — [[Judah and Tamar]]
- **Deuteronomy 25:5–10** — the law, with the refusal ceremony attached
- **Leviticus 18:16; 20:21** — *"you shall not uncover the nakedness of your brother's wife"*, with
  childlessness as the penalty
- **Ruth 1:11–13; 3:1–13; 4:1–12** — Naomi's argument, the threshing floor, and the redemption at the
  gate
- **Matthew 22:23–33 · Mark 12:18–27 · Luke 20:27–40** — the Sadducees' seven brothers
- **Genesis 46:12; Numbers 26:19–22; 1 Chronicles 2:3–5** — the resulting clans

## Analysis

**Genesis 38 is the Bible's first levirate marriage and it happens four books before the law that
governs it.** Judah issues the instruction as though it needs no explanation — ***yabbēm***, a verb
built on ***yābām***, *the brother-in-law*, **and this is its first appearance in Scripture.** Nobody
asks what he means. **The institution is therefore older than the Torah's version of it**, and the
chapter is the evidence.

### What the three texts actually require

| | **Genesis 38** | **Deuteronomy 25:5–10** | **Ruth** |
|---|---|---|---|
| who owes the duty | **a brother; then, in the event, the father-in-law** | *"brothers dwell together"* and one dies **without a son** | a ***gōʾēl***, a redeemer-kinsman; the nearer one, then Boaz |
| the purpose | *"raise up offspring for your brother"* | *"that his name not be blotted out of Israel"* | *"to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance"* (4:5, 10) |
| the child's status | counted to the dead brother | *"shall succeed in the name of his brother"* | counted to Mahlon (4:10), though 4:17 calls him Naomi's |
| land involved | **none mentioned** | none mentioned | **central** — the field must be bought with the widow |
| refusal | **Onan performs it in form and voids it**; **Judah simply withholds** | a public ceremony: elders, a pulled-off sandal, spitting, and a name of contempt | the nearer redeemer declines **openly, at the gate, with the sandal** |
| the widow's remedy | **none** | **she initiates** — *"his brother's wife shall come to him in the presence of the elders"* | Naomi and Ruth engineer it; Boaz completes it |
| how she gets her son | **deception at a roadside** | **due process** | **negotiation at a threshing floor and a gate** |

**The most important row is the last three.** *Deuteronomy gives the widow standing*: she brings the
refusing brother before the elders herself, and the penalty is public and permanent — *"his name shall
be called in Israel, 'The house of him who had his sandal removed.'"* **Ruth 4 shows that machinery
working**, in daylight, with ten elders and a witnessed transfer.

**Genesis 38 is what the same situation looks like with none of it.** [[Tamar]] is sent back to her
father's house but not released; she cannot remarry, she cannot compel performance, and there is no
forum in which to raise the matter. **The chapter's whole plot is the absence of the remedy
Deuteronomy would later supply**, and the wiki reads the veil at [[Enaim]] in that light: **not a
substitute for the law, but a substitute for the absence of one.** See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

### The clash with Leviticus

**Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 forbid exactly what Deuteronomy 25 commands**: *"You shall not uncover the
nakedness of your brother's wife; it is your brother's nakedness"*, and *"if a man takes his brother's
wife, it is an impurity … they shall be childless."* **The penalty Leviticus names is the very
condition the levirate exists to cure.**

**Three ways the tension is handled, and the wiki adopts none:**

1. **Deuteronomy 25 is a stated exception** to a general rule — the case of a brother dead without a
   son, which Leviticus is not addressing.
2. **The two are from different legal traditions** and were never reconciled by their compilers.
3. **Leviticus is about a living brother's wife** and the two texts never actually overlap.

**Genesis, which predates both, reports the duty as ordinary and offers no comment at all.** Per the
schema, the wiki sets the three texts beside each other and flags the contradiction rather than
harmonising it.

### Ruth, and the text's own comparison

**The book of Ruth makes the connection itself, so this is not the wiki drawing a line.** When the
elders of Bethlehem bless Boaz's marriage they say: *"Let your house be like the house of Perez, whom
Tamar bore to Judah"* (Ruth 4:12). **Two childless widows, two men who had to be brought to perform an
obligation, one town, and one genealogy** — and the genealogy that closes Ruth begins at [[Perez]], the
child of Genesis 38.

**The differences matter as much as the parallel.** **Ruth's levirate has become a redemption**, fused
with the *gōʾēl* institution and with land: Boaz buys a field and acquires a widow in one transaction
(4:9–10), which Deuteronomy 25 says nothing about. **And Ruth does openly, with Naomi's coaching and
Boaz's cooperation, what Tamar had to do by veil and pledge.** See
[[Tamar in the Genealogies - Ruth 4, 1 Chronicles 2, and Matthew 1]] and [[The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood]].

### The Sadducees

**The one place the New Testament handles the law, it is being used as a trap.** The Sadducees, who
*"say that there is no resurrection"*, cite Deuteronomy 25 and construct a woman married to seven
brothers in succession: *"in the resurrection therefore whose wife will she be of the seven?"*
(Matthew 22:28). **The argument only works if the levirate's purpose — continuing a man's name through
his brother's child — is the only kind of continuation there is.** Jesus answers that they know
*"neither the Scriptures, nor the power of God"*, and that the resurrected *"are like God's angels in
heaven"* — **and then argues the resurrection itself from the Torah they were quoting**, out of Exodus
3:6. See [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].

## Significance

**The institution exists because a name and a portion were the only forms of survival the earliest
texts could offer.** Deuteronomy states it flatly — *"that his name not be blotted out of Israel"* —
and **the practice is therefore an artefact of a theology with no developed afterlife.** That is why
the Sadducees could think it a decisive argument, and why the answer they get is not about levirate
law at all. See [[Life and Death]] and [[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

**And the line of David runs through the two cases where it nearly failed.** Genesis 38's levirate was
refused by one son, withheld from another, and finally discharged by the father-in-law under duress.
Ruth's was declined by the nearer redeemer at the gate. **Both times the duty is performed by the man
who was not first in line**, and both times the child is in the genealogy at Matthew 1. **Scripture
keeps the messy provenance on the record**, which is the observation the wiki takes from the thread as
a whole.
