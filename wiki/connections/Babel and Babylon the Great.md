# Babel and Babylon the Great

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 10:10** — *"The beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land
  of Shinar."* See [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the city and the tower, the confused language, the scattering, the name. See
  [[The Tower of Babel]].
- **Isaiah 13:1–22; 14:3–23** — the oracle against Babylon, and the taunt over its king.
- **Jeremiah 50–51** — the longest oracle against any nation in the Bible, and 51:53 on Babylon
  mounting up to the sky.
- **Daniel 1:1–2; 4:28–37** — the vessels carried to Shinar, and *"Is not this great Babylon, which I
  have built…?"*
- **Psalm 137** — *"By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down."*
- **Revelation 14:8; 16:19; 17:1–18; 18:1–24** — Babylon the great, fallen in one hour.
- **1 Peter 5:13** — greetings from *"she who is in Babylon"*.

## Analysis

**One Hebrew word runs the whole distance.** *Bābel* is what Genesis 10:10 and 11:9 say, and it is
what every one of the roughly two hundred and eighty later occurrences says that English versions
render *Babylon*. The convention of translating the two Genesis passages as *Babel* and everything
afterwards as *Babylon* is an artefact of English Bibles, and it conceals the single most important
fact about the connection: **the tower and the empire have the same name because they are the same
name.** No later writer needs to make an allusion. They only need to say the word.

**The arc has three stages, and Genesis supplies two of them.**

**1. A city in a list (10:10).** Babel enters Scripture without comment, as the first of four cities
in [[Shinar]] belonging to [[Nimrod]] — *rēʾšît mamlaktô*, the beginning of his kingdom, and the first
occurrence of *mamlākâ* in the Bible. Nothing is said against it. See [[Kingship and Empire]].

**2. A city with a meaning (11:9).** Twenty-four verses later the same name is given an etymology, and
the etymology is an insult. Akkadian *bāb-ilim* means **gate of the god**, which is what Babylon's own
people understood their city's name to say and what the ziggurat *Etemenanki* was built to embody.
Genesis derives it instead from *bālal*, **to confuse**. The gate of the god becomes *babble*. That
single pun is the seed of everything the rest of the canon does with the place, and it is done before
Babylon has conquered anybody.

**3. A city that is an idea.** From the exile onward Babylon is both a specific empire — the one that
burned the temple, deported Judah, and set the terms of the Old Testament's last centuries — and the
standing name for human power organized against God. Isaiah and Jeremiah spend some seventy chapters
between them on its fall. And by Revelation the name has come loose from the Euphrates entirely: John
writes it of a city on seven hills, and 1 Peter 5:13 appears to use it of Rome as a matter of course,
without explanation, which suggests the usage was already conventional among Christians.

**The specific echoes of Genesis 11 in the later texts are precise, not vague.**

- **Building to the sky.** Jeremiah 51:53 — *"Though Babylon should mount up to the sky, and though
  she should fortify the height of her strength, yet destroyers will come to her from me."* This is
  Genesis 11:4's clause turned into a concession in an oracle of doom.
- **The self-made name.** Daniel 4:30 has Nebuchadnezzar on his roof: *"Is not this great Babylon,
  which I have built for the royal dwelling place, by the might of my power and for the glory of my
  majesty?"* It is Genesis 11:4 spoken by one man, and the humiliation follows within the verse. See
  [[A Name for Ourselves and a Great Name]].
- **The boast in the heart.** Revelation 18:7 — Babylon says *"I sit a queen, and am no widow, and
  will in no way see mourning"*, and Isaiah 47:8 had already put nearly the same sentence in her
  mouth. See [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]].
- **The unfinished work.** *"They stopped building the city"* (11:8) is the shape every later oracle
  gives Babylon's end: a great construction abandoned. Isaiah 13:19–22 leaves it to wild animals;
  Jeremiah 51:26 says no stone will be taken from it for a corner; Revelation 18:21 has it thrown down
  like a millstone into the sea and *"found no more at all"*.
- **The scattering.** Genesis has the builders dispersed out of Babel; Revelation 18:4 has God's people
  called out of her — *"Come out of her, my people"* — which Jeremiah 51:6, 45 had said first. The
  movement in both directions is out.

**What is not a connection, and should be said plainly.** The tower is not mentioned again anywhere in
Scripture. No prophet cites Genesis 11, no New Testament writer alludes to it, and the *migdāl* of
11:4 appears in no later text. Everything above works through the **city** and its **name**, not
through the building. This matters because popular treatments of the connection are usually pictures
of a tower, and the biblical thread is about a place people live in. See the note on
[[The Tower of Babel]] that the passage's own subject is the city.

**And one more asymmetry.** Genesis 11 ends with a city abandoned, and the canon ends with a city
descending — *"the holy city, New Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God"* (Revelation 21:2).
The two cities are set against each other across Revelation 17–22 deliberately: one is built up from a
plain and comes to nothing, one comes down from God as a gift. Genesis 11:5's direction of travel,
God coming down to a city, is the direction the last vision keeps — but there is nothing to inspect
and nothing to stop. See [[City and Civilization]] and
[[The Tree of Life - Eden and the New Jerusalem]].

## Significance

The Bible's most sustained image of human power is founded in nine verses that contain no king, no
army, no empire, and no named person. That is the force of the connection: Babylon does not become a
symbol because of what it did to Judah in 587 BC — the symbol is already fixed in Genesis, in a story
about brick-makers who wanted to stay together and be somebody.

It also fixes what the symbol means. Babylon in Scripture is not principally a byword for idolatry,
or cruelty, or wealth, though it acquires all three. It is the name for a human project that
organizes everything it has toward its own permanence and does not consult God, and its standing
literary form is a construction site with the work stopped.

Genesis's own counter-image to it arrives seventeen chapters later, and it is the reverse in every
term: a structure nobody built, seen by a man asleep, whose top reaches heaven and which he calls
*the gate of heaven* — the title the city on the plain had claimed and been refused. See
[[Babel and the Gate of Heaven]].
