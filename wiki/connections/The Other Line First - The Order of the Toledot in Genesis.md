# The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 4:17–24 / 4:25–26** — Cain's descendants for eight verses, then Seth's
- **Genesis 10:2–20 / 10:21–31** — Japheth and Ham, then Shem
- **Genesis 25:12–18 / 25:19** — *"the generations of Ishmael"*, then *"the generations of Isaac"*
- **Genesis 36:1–43 / 37:2** — a whole chapter of *"the generations of Esau"*, then *"the generations
  of Jacob"*
- Genesis 11:10, 27 — the two *toledot* of the transitional pericope, and the one Genesis never gives
- Genesis 2:4; 5:1; 6:9 — the other four occurrences of the formula

## Analysis

**Genesis has a habit and it never breaks it: the line that is not the subject is dealt with first,
in full, and then closed.**

| the other line | length | the chosen line picks up at | its own register at |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cain** — city, crafts, a boast about seventy-sevenfold vengeance | 8 verses | **Seth**, 4:25 | 5:1, *"the book of the generations of Adam"* |
| **Japheth and Ham** — the sea peoples, Egypt, Canaan, Nimrod's empire | 19 verses | **Shem**, 10:21 | 11:10, *"the history of the generations of Shem"* |
| **Ishmael** — twelve princes, a lifespan, a territory | 7 verses | **Isaac**, 25:19 | 25:19, the formula itself |
| **Esau** — chiefs, kings, and a full national register | 43 verses | **Jacob**, 37:2 | 37:2, the formula itself |

**Four times, and in the same order every time.** The book never opens the chosen line first and then
doubles back. In the first two cases the passed-over branch is given its space inside a shared
register and the chosen branch's own *toledot* heading follows a chapter later; from Genesis 25 on,
the two branches get separate headings back to back.

### What the order actually accomplishes

**It clears the stage.** Genesis is a single-threaded narrative and it cannot follow two families. The
*toledot* of the other branch is how the book discharges an obligation — these people are real, they
are numerous, they are related to us, here is who they are — and then never returns to them. **After
25:18 Ishmael appears in Genesis exactly twice more**, both times as a father-in-law (28:9) or as the
tribe that buys Joseph (37:25–28). After chapter 36, Esau appears not at all.

**It also refuses to make the other line villainous.** This is the part most easily missed. Cain's
descendants invent metallurgy, music, and the city; Ham's produce the first empire; Ishmael's produce
twelve princes with villages and encampments and a territory four hundred miles wide; Esau's produce
*"the kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the children of Israel"*
(36:31). **Every one of these lists is a list of achievement.** Genesis grants them everything except
the covenant, and offers no explanation for the difference. See [[Call and Election]] and
[[The Nations]].

### The asymmetry that runs the other way

**The line that is passed over multiplies easily, and the line that is chosen cannot have children.**

Nahor's wife bears eight sons with no difficulty reported. Ishmael has twelve princes. Esau has a
national register by chapter 36. **And the covenant line is barren in every generation**: Sarah until
ninety, Rebekah for twenty years (25:21), Rachel until she is not.
[[Twelve Sons - Nahor, Ishmael, and Jacob]] makes the same observation about the number twelve;
**the *toledot* ordering makes it about narrative structure.** Genesis puts the fertile branch's genealogy on the page and then turns to a
couple who cannot conceive. See [[Barrenness]].

### The one *toledot* Genesis never gives

**There is no *"these are the generations of Abraham"*.** The Abraham cycle runs from 11:27 to 25:11
and it is headed *"the generations of Terah"* — a man who set out for Canaan and stopped in Haran.
Ishmael gets one at 25:12; Isaac gets one at 25:19; Esau and Jacob get theirs. **The founding figure
of Israel is filed under his father's name**, which is consistent on the formula's own logic — a
*toledot* introduces a man's descendants, and Abraham's descendants are the rest of the book — but is
worth naming. [[From Shem to Abram]] records the same observation from the other end.

### Two brothers at one grave, twice

The pattern has a second, quieter symmetry. **Each of the two Genesis burials where a passed-over
brother returns happens immediately before or after his line's genealogy.**

- Isaac and Ishmael bury Abraham at 25:9; Ishmael's *toledot* follows three verses later, and closes
  him out.
- Esau and Jacob bury Isaac at 35:29; Esau's *toledot* follows immediately, filling all of chapter 36,
  and closes him out.

**In both cases the estranged brother is brought back for the funeral, given his register, and
released from the book.** Genesis never says a word about how either reconciliation came about. See
[[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] and
[[Two Sons Nearly Lost - The Sending of Ishmael and the Binding of Isaac]].

## Significance

**The order is the book's way of showing what election is not.**

If the covenant were about strength, numbers, priority of birth, or civilisational achievement, the
*toledot* order would be an embarrassment — the passed-over lines have more of all four, and Genesis
puts their credentials on the page first, at length, without a word of disparagement. **The reader
finishes Ishmael's seven verses knowing that he has twelve princes, a territory, an exactly kept
promise, and his father's own death formula, and then turns the page to a sixty-year-old man whose
wife cannot conceive.**

Paul reads Genesis this way at Romans 9:6–13, taking Ishmael and Esau as the book's own two
demonstrations that descent and precedence decide nothing. **That is a reading of the pattern, not the
pattern itself** — what Genesis does is arrange the material and stay silent. See
[[Two Lines from Adam - Cain's Genealogy and Seth's]] and
[[Seventy Nations - Genesis 10 in Deuteronomy 32 and Luke 10]].
