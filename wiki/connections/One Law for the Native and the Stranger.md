# One Law for the Native and the Stranger

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 12:19** — leaven in the house during the seven days: *"that soul shall be cut off from the
  congregation of Israel, whether he is a foreigner, or one who is born in the land"* — the first
  *ʾezraḥ*, *native*, in Scripture (WLC) — [[The Passover]]
- **Exodus 12:48–49** — *"he shall be as one who is born in the land: but no uncircumcised person shall eat
  of it. One law shall be to him who is born at home, and to the stranger who lives as a foreigner among
  you"* — [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]]
- **Leviticus 24:22** — *"You shall have one kind of law for the foreigner as well as the native-born; for I
  am Yahweh your God"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Numbers 9:14** — the Passover again: *"You shall have one statute, both for the foreigner, and for him
  who is born in the land"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Numbers 15:15–16** — the offerings: *"there shall be one statute for you and for the stranger who lives
  as a foreigner, a statute forever throughout your generations. As you are, so shall the foreigner be
  before Yahweh. One law and one ordinance shall be for you, and for the stranger who lives as a foreigner
  with you"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Numbers 15:29** — unwitting sin: *"You shall have one law for him who does anything unwittingly, for
  him who is native-born among the children of Israel, and for the stranger who lives as a foreigner among
  them"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 17:12–13, 27** — the household rule the Passover law takes over: the bought slave and the
  house-born alike circumcised — [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]]

(WEB throughout; verses outside `raw/` fetched from bible-api.com, `translation=web`.)

## Analysis

**The phrase is a formula, and it begins at the Passover.** Exodus 12:49's ***tôrâ ʾaḥat***, *one law*, is
the first singular *tôrâ* in Scripture (Genesis has only the plural, *"my laws"*, 26:5; WLC lemma
search). The same claim returns four times in the Torah's later law, with the noun varying (WLC):

| Text | Hebrew | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| Exodus 12:49 | *tôrâ ʾaḥat* | the Passover |
| Leviticus 24:22 | *mišpaṭ ʾeḥād* | the law of injury and blasphemy |
| Numbers 9:14 | *ḥuqqâ ʾaḥat* | the Passover |
| Numbers 15:15–16 | *ḥuqqâ ʾaḥat*; *tôrâ ʾaḥat ûmišpāṭ ʾeḥād* | offerings |
| Numbers 15:29 | *tôrâ ʾaḥat* | unwitting sin |

**Its first appearance comes before Israel has land, a sanctuary, or a law code.** It is spoken in Egypt
(Exodus 12:43 follows the departure in the narrative but belongs to the same night), and it distinguishes
a *native of the land* — *ʾezraḥ hāʾāreṣ* — in a land none of them owns. **The category is drawn in
advance of the thing it depends on.**

**It is an equality with a condition.** The stranger who wants to keep the Passover is *"as one who is
born in the land"* only once *"all his males"* are circumcised (12:48), in the words of Genesis 17:10.
**The law is not that anyone may eat; it is that the circumcised stranger eats on the same terms as the
native.** The same chapter excludes the *ben-nēkār*, the *tôšāb*, and the *śākîr* outright (12:43, 45).
Numbers 9:14 keeps the admission and the single statute, and does not repeat the circumcision clause in
that verse; the wiki does not infer from the silence that the condition was dropped.

**The principle cuts both ways.** At 12:19 the stranger shares the native's *penalty*: leaven in the house
means being *"cut off"*. At 12:48 he shares the native's *privilege*. Leviticus 24:22 applies the single law
to injury and blasphemy, where the stranger is protected and punished alike, and Numbers 15:15 states the
principle in theological terms: *"As you are, so shall the foreigner be before Yahweh."*

**And it sits beside the ethic of the stranger.** The commands not to oppress the *gēr*, *"for you were
aliens in the land of Egypt"* (Exodus 22:21; 23:9), are traced on
[[A Foreigner in a Foreign Land - Gershom and the Law of the Stranger]]. That page is about how Israel is
to treat the stranger; this one is about whether the stranger stands under the same law. **The two
threads start in the same place**: the *gēr* of Genesis 15:13, which Israel itself was.

## Significance

- **The first *tôrâ* in Scripture is a rule of inclusion.** The word that will name the whole Torah is
  first used to say one law covers the native and the circumcised stranger.
- **Membership in Israel's central rite is defined by circumcision, not birth.** Genesis 17 had already
  made the bought slave part of the circumcised house; Exodus 12 extends the same terms to the free
  stranger who asks.
- **The formula is repeated wherever the law turns to the stranger** — feasts, offerings, sin, and
  injury — so that a reader of the Torah meets it as a standing principle rather than a one-off rule.
