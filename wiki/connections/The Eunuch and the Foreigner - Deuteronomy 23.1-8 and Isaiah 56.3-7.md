# The Eunuch and the Foreigner - Deuteronomy 23.1-8 and Isaiah 56.3-7

## Passages involved

- **Deuteronomy 23:1–8** — [[The Assembly and the Holy Camp]]: *"He who is emasculated by crushing or
  cutting shall not enter into Yahweh's assembly"* (1); *"An Ammonite or a Moabite shall not enter into
  Yahweh's assembly; even to the tenth generation"* (3); the Edomite and the Egyptian admitted in the
  third generation (7–8). Stored WEB.
- **Leviticus 21:20–23** — [[The Holiness of the Priests]]: the priest *"who has damaged testicles"* may not
  *"come near to the altar"*, but *"He shall eat the bread of his God"*. Stored WEB.
- **Isaiah 56:3–7** *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — *"Let no foreigner, who has joined
  himself to Yahweh, speak, saying, 'Yahweh will surely separate me from his people.' Do not let the eunuch

  name better than of sons and of daughters. I will give them an everlasting name, that will not be cut
  off"* (5); *"for my house will be called a house of prayer for all peoples"* (7).
- **Nehemiah 13:1–3** *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — Deuteronomy 23:3–5 read aloud,

- **Lamentations 1:10** *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — *"the nations have entered into

- **Acts 8:27–28** *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — *"a man of Ethiopia, a eunuch of great

## Analysis

**Two excluded figures, answered by name.** Deuteronomy 23:1–8 shuts out of *"Yahweh's assembly"* a man
injured in his genitals and, among the nations, the Ammonite and the Moabite. Isaiah 56:3–7 takes up the
same two figures — the eunuch and the foreigner — and gives each a place in Yahweh's *"house"*. Neither text
quotes the other, and Isaiah does not name Deuteronomy. The pairing, and three verbal contacts, are what
connect them.

**The vocabulary.** The contacts are in the Hebrew (WLC):

| Isaiah 56 | Deuteronomy 23 |
|---|---|
| *ʾăšer lōʾ yikkārēt*, *"that will not be cut off"* (5) | *kǝrût šopkâ*, *cut* in the organ (WLC 23:2, WEB 23:1) — the same verb, *kārat* |
| *yād wāšēm*, *"a memorial and a name"* (5) | *yād*, the *"place"* outside the camp (WLC 23:13, WEB 23:12) |
| *ben-hannēkār*, *"foreigner"* (3) | *nokrî*, *"foreigner"*, at 23:20 (WEB), the next pericope — the same root, not the same word |

The first is the closest. Deuteronomy excludes the man who has been *cut*; Isaiah gives the eunuch a name
that *will not be cut off*. Whether Isaiah plays on the law deliberately is a reading, not something the
text says; *kārat* is a common verb, and *yikkārēt*, *"be cut off"*, is also the ordinary idiom for a line
or name dying out. The *yād* of Deuteronomy 23:12 is almost certainly unconnected; it is listed because the
word recurs, not because it carries the argument. Isaiah's word for *eunuch*, *sārîs*, is not the word of
Deuteronomy 23:1, which describes the injury and does not name a class.

**What Isaiah sets as the condition.** Not birth or body but conduct: the eunuchs *"who keep my Sabbaths,
and choose the things that please me, and hold fast to my covenant"* (4); the foreigners *"who join
themselves to Yahweh, to serve him, and to love Yahweh's name"* (6). Deuteronomy's exclusions are by
category, and some are permanent — *"even to the tenth generation … forever"* (23:3). Isaiah's admissions are
by allegiance.

**The eunuch's complaint.** *"Behold, I am a dry tree"* (Isaiah 56:3). The complaint is not about the
assembly but about descendants: a man with no children has no one to carry his name. Isaiah's answer is *"a
name better than of sons and of daughters"* (5). Leviticus 21 had already distinguished the altar from the
table — the injured priest serves at neither altar nor veil but eats the holy food. Isaiah goes further than
either law, to *"my house and … my walls"*.

**After the exile, both readings in force.** Nehemiah 13:1–3 has Deuteronomy 23:3–5 read out and enforced
as written. Lamentations 1:10 treats the foreign nations' entry into the sanctuary as the violation of a
command, *"that they should not enter into your assembly"*. Isaiah 56 promises that foreigners' offerings
*"will be accepted on my altar"* (7). The canon holds both, and neither the prophet nor Nehemiah mentions
the other. How the post-exilic community related the two is a historical question this page does not try
to settle.

**Acts 8.** The Ethiopian, *"a eunuch of great authority"*, has *"come to Jerusalem to worship"* (8:27) and is
*"reading the prophet Isaiah"* (8:28) when Philip meets him. Luke does not say whether he was admitted to the temple courts or
what Deuteronomy 23:1 meant for him, and does not cite Isaiah 56. That the episode fulfils Isaiah 56:3–5 is
a common Christian reading, reported here and not adopted.

## Significance

Deuteronomy 23 draws the edge of the assembly; Isaiah 56 names two kinds of people outside it and brings them
in, on terms of covenant loyalty. Read together they show the canon arguing with itself about who belongs
to Yahweh's people, and the argument is not concluded inside the Hebrew Bible. See [[The Nations]],
[[Holiness]], and [[Moab and Ammon - The Two Nations Born in the Cave]], where Ruth the Moabitess is the
narrative counterpart of the same tension.
