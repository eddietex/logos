# The Dog Returns to His Vomit - Proverbs 26.11 and 2 Peter 2.22

## Passages involved

- [[Answer a Fool]] — Proverbs 26:11: *"As a dog that returns to his vomit, so is a fool who repeats
  his folly."*
- 2 Peter 2:22 — *"But it has happened to them according to the true proverb, 'The dog turns to his
  own vomit again,' and 'the sow that has washed to wallowing in the mire.'"* Verified WEB, not yet
  ingested.
- [[The Glory of Kings]] — Proverbs 25:16, honey eaten to excess and vomited, with the same root.

## Analysis

**A proverb about repetition.** In Proverbs 26:11 the comparison is between two returns. The dog goes
back to what it has just brought up, and the fool goes back to his folly. *Returns* is *šāḇ*, from
*šûḇ*, the verb of turning and returning. *Repeats* is *šôneh*, *doing it a second time*. The picture
is not of a fool who is ignorant but of one who has already been sick of his folly once and goes back
to it anyway. It is one of eleven sayings on the fool in 26:1–12, and it follows straight on the
saying about a proverb in a fool's mouth (26:9). (Hebrew from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the
Masorah*, read 2026-10-26.)

**2 Peter quotes it, and labels it.** 2 Peter 2:22 introduces it as *"the true proverb"* and pairs it
with a second saying, the washed sow returning to the mud. The verse closes a passage about people
who had escaped *"the defilement of the world"* and then were entangled in it again (2:20–21, not
quoted here). The proverb is applied to a return after a cleansing: the dog to its vomit, the washed
sow to the mire.

**What changes.** In Proverbs the subject is the *kəsîl*, the fool, and the point is incorrigibility:
the next verse says there is more hope even for him than for the man wise in his own eyes (26:12). In
2 Peter the subject is someone who once knew *"the way of righteousness"*, and the point is
apostasy. The image is the same and the moral is close, but 2 Peter applies it to a more serious case.

**Wording.** The WEB of 2 Peter, *"The dog turns to his own vomit again"*, is not word for word the
WEB of Proverbs 26:11, and it leaves out the fool. 2 Peter calls it a proverb rather than Scripture.
The second saying, about the sow, does not come from Proverbs, and the wiki does not identify its
source. The Greek of 2 Peter and the Septuagint of Proverbs 26:11 were not checked in this pass, so
the wiki makes no claim about which Greek form, if any, 2 Peter follows.

**Within Proverbs.** The same root for vomiting appears a chapter earlier: *"Eat as much as is
sufficient for you, lest you eat too much, and vomit it"* (25:16; *wahăqēʾṯô*, against 26:11's
*qēʾô*). There vomiting is the result of a good thing taken to excess. Here it is folly itself, which
the fool returns to.

## Significance

The saying is one of the few lines of Proverbs that the New Testament quotes, and it carries
Proverbs' portrait of [[Folly]] — folly as something chosen again after its effects are known — into
the New Testament's warnings about falling away. See also
[[Wise in His Own Eyes - Proverbs 3.7, 26.12, Isaiah 5.21, and Romans 12.16]].
