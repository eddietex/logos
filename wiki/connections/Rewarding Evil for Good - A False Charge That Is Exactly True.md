# Rewarding Evil for Good — A False Charge That Is Exactly True

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 44:4** — [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]]; *"Why have you rewarded evil for good?"* —
  ***lāmmâ šillamtem rāʿâ taḥat ṭôbâ***, **the phrase's first occurrence in Scripture**
- **Genesis 37:3–4, 18–28** — [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]]; the coat, the errand, and the pit
- **1 Samuel 24:17; 25:21** — David and Saul in the cave; David on Nabal: *"he has returned me evil
  for good"*
- **Psalm 35:12; Psalm 38:20; Psalm 109:4–5** — the lament formula
- **Proverbs 17:13** — *"Whoever rewards evil for good, evil will not depart from his house"*
- **Jeremiah 18:20** — *"Should evil be recompensed for good?"*
- **Romans 12:17, 21; 1 Thessalonians 5:15; 1 Peter 3:9** — the New Testament's inversion

## Analysis

### The idiom

***Šillēm rāʿâ taḥat ṭôbâ*** — *to repay evil in place of good* — **is built on *šālēm*, the verb of
completing a payment, from the root of *šālôm*.** It is **accounting language**: a debt is settled,
and the settlement is the wrong currency. The preposition ***taḥat*** — *in place of, instead of* —
**is the same one Genesis uses for the ram offered *instead of* [[Isaac]]** (22:13) **and for
[[Judah]] offering to stay *instead of the boy*** (44:33). **The chapter that opens with the idiom
closes with the preposition used properly.**

**Genesis 44:4 is its first appearance in the Bible**, and after it the phrase belongs almost entirely
to the Psalms and to David.

### The charge is false

**Nobody stole the cup.** [[Joseph]] wrote the sentence and gave it to [[Joseph's Steward]] to deliver
on the road (44:4–6), **and the men it is delivered to have done nothing whatever to him in Egypt.**
On the contrary: they returned money they were not asked to return, and they say so — *"the money,
which we found in our sacks' mouths, we brought again to you out of the land of Canaan"* (44:8).
**Their defence against the accusation of ingratitude is an act of scrupulous honesty performed in
response to a plant.**

### The charge is exact

**And it is the truest sentence anyone speaks in the chapter.**

| | the good | the evil returned |
|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 37** | a father's love, a coat, and a boy sent out to ask after their welfare (37:14) | a pit, a sale for twenty pieces, and a coat returned in blood |
| **Genesis 44** | grain in a famine, returned silver, a feast, and [[Simeon]] released | *"you have done evil in so doing"* — **a theft that never happened** |

***"Go now, see whether it is well with your brothers"*** — Jacob's errand at 37:14 sends Joseph to
ask after their *šālôm*. **The idiom at 44:4 is built on the same root.** **He went out to ask whether they were at
peace and came back as a slave**, and the verb for what they did with that is the verb for settling an
account. **Genesis does not connect them.** It simply uses a phrase about repayment in the mouth of a
servant who does not know what is being repaid.

**So the accusation is a lie in every particular and a correct verdict in substance**, and the only
person in the scene who can see both halves is the one who wrote it and is not there. **This is the
chapter's method** — the same doubleness as *"God has found out the iniquity of your servants"*
(44:16), where the brothers confess to something nobody charged. See
[[Truly We Are Guilty - The Brothers' Confession and the Gap in Genesis 37]] and
[[Guilt and Conscience]].

### Where the phrase goes

**Everywhere else in the Old Testament it is the cry of someone genuinely wronged, and usually of
David.**

- **1 Samuel 24:17** — **Saul says it about himself**, and it is the one time in Scripture the phrase
  is spoken in the other direction: *"You are more righteous than I; for you have done good to me,
  whereas I have done evil to you."* **The construction is [[Judah]]'s *ṣādəqâ mimmennî* at 38:26**,
  and the wiki notes the coincidence without pressing it.
- **1 Samuel 25:21** — David, on the point of killing Nabal's household: *"Surely in vain I have kept
  all that this fellow has in the wilderness … he has returned me evil for good."* **Abigail stops
  him**, which makes it another case of a third party defusing a sentence already spoken.
- **Psalm 35:12; 38:20; 109:4–5** — the lament's standing complaint, always in the voice of the
  wronged and always as grounds for asking God to act.
- **Proverbs 17:13** — *"Whoever rewards evil for good, evil will not depart from his house."* **The
  only occurrence that states a consequence**, and it is generational — **which is the Joseph cycle's
  whole shape**: the evil does not depart from the house of the men who did it for twenty-two years,
  and it returns to them as a silver cup.
- **Jeremiah 18:20** — *"Should evil be recompensed for good? For they have dug a pit for my soul."*
  **A pit**, in the only prophetic use of the idiom, **from a prophet who is thrown into one (38:6).**

### The inversion

**The New Testament keeps the vocabulary and reverses the direction.** *"Repay no one evil for evil"*
(Romans 12:17); *"see that no one returns evil for evil to anyone"* (1 Thessalonians 5:15); *"not
rendering evil for evil, or insult for insult; but instead blessing"* (1 Peter 3:9); and Romans 12:21,
*"overcome evil with good."*

**The Old Testament formula complains that good was answered with evil. The New Testament command is
to answer evil with good.** **Genesis 44 sits between them and belongs to neither**: the accusation
is false, the men are guilty of the thing it names, **and the man wronged is in the process of
arranging for them to be fed** (45:11; 50:21). **The accusation he writes is the last cruelty of a
kindness he has already decided on.**

## Significance

**The idiom's first appearance in Scripture is a false accusation**, and that is worth stating
plainly, because the rest of the canon uses it as the paradigm of a real grievance. **Genesis
introduces the Bible's standard formula for ingratitude by putting it in the mouth of a man who has
planted the evidence** — and by making it, in the only sense that matters, accurate.

**What the chapter is doing with it is testing whether the men will repay evil for good a second
time.** They are offered the terms: one brother enslaved, ten walking free, **a court's word that they
are blameless**, and a road to Canaan (44:17). **Taking it would be 37:28 again.** *"Then they tore
their clothes, and each man loaded his donkey, and returned to the city"* (44:13) — **and Proverbs
17:13's evil departs from the house, in the one place Scripture shows it happening.**
