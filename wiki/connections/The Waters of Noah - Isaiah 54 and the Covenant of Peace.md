# The Waters of Noah - Isaiah 54 and the Covenant of Peace

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 8:21–22** — the resolve spoken in God's heart, and the guarantee of seed time and
  harvest *"while the earth remains"* — [[The Waters Recede]]
- **Genesis 9:8–17** — the covenant established with Noah, his offspring, and every living creature:
  *"There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth"* — [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- **Isaiah 54:9–10** — *"For this is like the waters of Noah to me; for as I have sworn that the
  waters of Noah will no more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you,
  nor rebuke you… my covenant of peace will not be removed"*
- **Isaiah 55:3** — *"an everlasting covenant with you, even the sure mercies of David"*
- **Jeremiah 31:35–36; 33:20–21, 25–26** — the fixed order of sun, moon, day, and night as the
  measure of a covenant that cannot be broken: *"If you can break my covenant of the day and my
  covenant of the night…"*
- **Ezekiel 34:25; 37:26** — *"a covenant of peace"*, an *"everlasting covenant"*, and a land where
  the wild animals are no longer a danger
- **Hosea 2:18** — a covenant made *"with the animals of the field, and with the birds of the sky,
  and with the creeping things of the ground"*, with the bow broken out of the land
- **Isaiah 24:5** — the earth polluted because its inhabitants *"have transgressed the laws,
  violated the statutes, and broken the everlasting covenant"*
- **Romans 8:19–22, 38–39** — creation subjected in hope, and the list of things that cannot
  separate

## Analysis

**Isaiah 54:9 is the only place in the Hebrew Bible outside Genesis that names the flood as a
covenant precedent**, and the use it makes of it is exact. The prophet is addressing a people newly
out of exile, and the pastoral problem is obvious: the God who is promising them a future is the God
who destroyed their city. Why should this promise hold when the last one apparently did not?

The answer is an argument from an oath already on the books.

> “For this is like the waters of Noah to me; for as I have sworn that the waters of Noah will no
> more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you, nor rebuke you.
> *(Isaiah 54:9)*

Three moves are made in one sentence.

1. **The exile is classified as a flood.** Not softened and not denied — a catastrophe of the same
   order, God's own act, deserved. Verses 7–8 have just used the vocabulary of inundation: *"For a
   small moment I have forsaken you… In overflowing wrath I hid my face from you for a moment"*. The
   *"waters of Noah"* line then makes the comparison explicit.
2. **The flood is remembered as something that ended and cannot recur.** Which means the terms of
   the Noahic covenant are being read exactly as Genesis 9:11 states them — a permanent limit, no
   conditions, no expiry.
3. **The permanence transfers.** *As I have sworn… so I have sworn.* The strength of the new promise
   is derived from the strength of the old one, and the old one's strength is that nobody had to do
   anything to earn it.

**Isaiah also notices how the Noahic covenant is guaranteed, and copies the technique.** Genesis
attaches its promise to the weather and the calendar: the sign appears in the cloud when the rain
does (9:14), and the seasons themselves are underwritten — *"seed time and harvest, and cold and
heat, and summer and winter, and day and night will not cease"* (8:22). Isaiah 54:10 attaches his to
geology, and then goes one better than the anchor: *"For the mountains may depart, and the hills be
removed; but my loving kindness will not depart from you"*. The promise is guaranteed by the created
order and then declared to outlast it. Jeremiah runs the same argument twice with day and night
(31:35–36; 33:20–26), and both prophets are doing what Genesis 9 taught them — fixing an unbreakable
promise to something no human being can interfere with.

**And Isaiah renames it.** The Noahic covenant is not called a covenant of peace in Genesis; it is
called an everlasting covenant, *bərît ʿôlām* (9:16). Isaiah 54:10 calls his *bərît šəlômî*, *my
covenant of peace*, and Ezekiel takes the phrase up twice. At 34:25 he does so with a detail that
points straight back at Genesis 9's animal parties: *"I will make with them a covenant of peace, and
will cause evil animals to cease out of the land."* At 37:26 the covenant of peace is also *"an
everlasting covenant"*, joining Isaiah's phrase to Genesis's. Hosea 2:18 makes the debt plainest of
all, making the animals parties to a covenant and breaking *"the bow, the sword, and the battle"*
out of the land in the same verse — with the same noun, *qešet*, that Genesis 9:13 hangs in the
cloud (see [[The Bow in the Cloud]]).

The prophets who develop the covenant of peace all reach for the two features peculiar to the Noahic
covenant and to no other: **creation-wide scope**, extending past a people to the animals and the
land, and **a promise about what God will not do**, requiring nothing of the other party.

**The one text that cuts against this, flagged rather than resolved.** Isaiah 24:5 says the earth is
polluted because its inhabitants *"have transgressed the laws, violated the statutes, and broken the
everlasting covenant"* — and the *bərît ʿôlām* of Genesis 9:16 is the only everlasting covenant
humanity as such was ever party to. If the Noahic covenant can be broken by human beings, Isaiah
54's argument loses its foundation. Three responses are available and none is decisive:

- The phrase may mean the **Sinai covenant**, described in universal terms because the passage is
  apocalyptic and its horizon is the whole earth.
- It may mean the **created order** itself, the standing arrangement of Genesis 1 rather than a
  covenant document.
- It may mean **Genesis 9 in its human aspect** — the obligations of 9:1–7, blood not eaten and
  blood required — while leaving God's undertaking in 9:8–17 untouched.

The third reading fits the pericope's own two-part structure exactly: humanity is under commands in
the first speech and under no obligation whatever in the second, and only the first is the kind of
thing a person can break. It is attractive for that reason and cannot be established from Isaiah 24
alone. The tension is worth recording either way, since the whole force of Isaiah 54:9 depends on a
promise nobody can void.

## Significance

The Noahic covenant is easy to file as a curiosity of the primeval history — the rainbow, the
animals, a promise about weather — and Isaiah 54 is the evidence that Israel did not file it that
way. When the prophets needed a model for a promise able to survive the worst thing God had done to
his people, they went to the worst thing God had done to the world, and used the oath he swore
afterwards as the pattern.

What they took from it is a specific shape: the promise is unilateral, it is unconditional, its
guarantee is fixed to the created order, and its content is a limit God places on his own anger.
Every one of those features is in Genesis 9 and none of them is in Sinai. It is also the reason this
covenant, alone among them, has no sign the human party carries — see [[The Bow in the Cloud]] on a
sign given to remind the one who swore.

When the New Testament says that nothing *"will be able to separate us from God's love which is in
Christ Jesus our Lord"* (Romans 8:39), it stands at the end of a line running back through Ezekiel's
covenant of peace and Isaiah's waters of Noah to a bow hung in a cloud over eight people and a boat.
Romans 8:19–22 keeps the other half of the shape as well: the creation that was party to the Noahic
covenant is still in view, still *"subjected to vanity… in hope"*, and still waiting.
