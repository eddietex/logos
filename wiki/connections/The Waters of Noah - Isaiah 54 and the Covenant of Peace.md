# The Waters of Noah - Isaiah 54 and the Covenant of Peace

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:8–17 — the covenant established with Noah, his offspring, and every living creature;
  *"There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Genesis 8:21–22 — the resolve that precedes it, and the guarantee of seed time and harvest
  ([[The Waters Recede]])
- Isaiah 54:9–10 — *"For this is like the waters of Noah to me; for as I have sworn that the waters
  of Noah will no more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you nor
  rebuke you. For the mountains may depart, and the hills be removed, but my loving kindness will
  not depart from you, and my covenant of peace will not be removed"*
- Isaiah 55:3 — *"an everlasting covenant with you, even the sure mercies of David"*
- Jeremiah 31:35–36; 33:19–26 — the fixed order of sun, moon, day, and night as the measure of a
  covenant that cannot be broken: *"If you can break my covenant of the day, and my covenant of the
  night…"*
- Ezekiel 34:25; 37:26 — *"a covenant of peace"*, an *"everlasting covenant"*, and a land where the
  wild animals are no longer a danger
- Hosea 2:18–20 — a covenant made *"with the animals of the field, and with the birds of the sky,
  and with the creeping things of the ground"*, and the bow and sword broken out of the land
- Isaiah 24:5 — *"they have transgressed the laws, violated the statutes, and broken the everlasting
  covenant"*
- Romans 8:19–22, 38–39 — creation subjected in hope, and the list of things that cannot separate

## Analysis

**Isaiah 54:9 is the only place in the Hebrew Bible outside Genesis that names the flood as a
covenant precedent**, and the way it uses it is exact. The prophet is addressing a people just out
of exile, and the pastoral problem is obvious: the God who promises them a future is the God who
just destroyed their city. Why should the promise hold when the last one apparently did not?

The answer is an argument from an existing oath.

> **9** “For this is like the waters of Noah to me; for as I have sworn that the waters of Noah will
> no more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you nor rebuke you.

Three moves are made in one sentence.

1. **The exile is classified as a flood.** Not softened, not denied — a catastrophe of the same
   order, God's own act, deserved. Isaiah 54:7–8 has just said *"For a small moment I have forsaken
   you… In overflowing wrath I hid my face from you for a moment"*, using the vocabulary of
   inundation, and the *"waters of Noah"* line makes the analogy explicit.
2. **The flood is remembered as a thing that has ended and cannot recur.** Which means the terms of
   the Noahic covenant are being read exactly as Genesis 9:11 states them: a permanent limit, no
   conditions, no expiry.
3. **The permanence transfers.** *As I have sworn… so I have sworn.* The strength of the new promise
   is derived from the strength of the old one, and the old one's strength is that nobody had to do
   anything to earn it.

**Isaiah also notices that the Noahic covenant is sworn on something in the created order, and
copies the technique.** Genesis anchors its promise to the weather: the sign appears in the cloud
when the rain does, and the seasons themselves are guaranteed (8:22). Isaiah 54:10 anchors his to
geology — *"the mountains may depart, and the hills be removed, but my loving kindness will not
depart from you"* — and then goes one better than the anchor: the covenant outlasts the thing it is
compared to. Jeremiah runs the same argument twice with day and night (31:35–36; 33:20–26), and
both prophets are doing what Genesis 9 taught them, which is to attach an unbreakable promise to
something no human being can interfere with.

**And Isaiah renames it.** The Noahic covenant is not called a *covenant of peace* in Genesis; it
is called an *everlasting covenant* (9:16). Isaiah 54:10 calls his *bərît šəlômî*, *my covenant of
peace*, and Ezekiel picks the phrase up twice (34:25; 37:26) — in 34:25 with a detail that points
straight back at Genesis 9's animal parties: *"I will make with them a covenant of peace, and will
cause evil animals to cease out of the land."* Hosea 2:18 makes the debt explicit by including the
animals as parties and breaking the *bow* out of the land in the same verse. The prophets who
develop the covenant of peace all reach for the two features that are peculiar to the Noahic
covenant and to no other: creation-wide scope, and a promise about what God will not do.

**The one text that appears to cut against this.** Isaiah 24:5 says the earth is defiled because its
inhabitants *"have broken the everlasting covenant"* — and the *bərît ʿôlām* of Genesis 9:16 is the
only everlasting covenant humanity as such was ever party to. If the Noahic covenant can be broken,
Isaiah 54's argument collapses. Three responses are available, and none is certain. The phrase may
refer to the Sinai covenant, described in universal terms in an apocalyptic passage. It may refer to
the created order as a whole. Or it may refer to Genesis 9 in its *human* aspect — the obligations
of 9:1–7, the blood laws — while leaving God's undertaking of 9:8–17 untouched, which would fit the
pericope's own two-part structure exactly: humanity is under commands in the first speech and under
no obligation at all in the second, and only the first is the kind of thing a person can break.
That last reading is attractive and cannot be proved from Isaiah 24 alone. It is worth flagging
that the tension exists, since the whole force of Isaiah 54:9 depends on a promise nobody can void.

## Significance

The Noahic covenant is easy to file as a curiosity of the primeval history — the rainbow, the
animals, the promise about weather — and Isaiah 54 is the proof that Israel did not file it that
way. When the prophets needed a model for a promise that could survive the worst thing God had ever
done to his people, they went to the worst thing God had ever done to the world, and used the oath
he swore afterwards as the pattern.

What they took from it is a specific shape. The promise is unilateral, it is unconditional, its
guarantee is fixed to the created order, and its content is a limit God puts on his own anger. Every
one of those features is in Genesis 9 and none of them is in Sinai. When the New Testament wants to
say that nothing *"will be able to separate us from the love of God"* (Romans 8:39), it is standing
at the end of a line that runs back through Ezekiel's covenant of peace and Isaiah's waters of Noah
to a bow hung in a cloud over eight people and a boat — see [[The Bow in the Cloud]].
