## Analysis

Proverbs 14:29 commends a temperament:

> He who is slow to anger has great understanding, but he who has a quick temper displays folly.

In the WEB, *slow to anger* is the phrase of Yahweh's self-proclamation on Sinai, given to Moses after
the golden calf:

> Yahweh passed by before him, and proclaimed, "Yahweh! Yahweh, a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth,

The book of Proverbs uses the phrase three more times: the slow to anger *"appeases strife"* (15:18),
is *"better than the mighty"* (16:32), and is made so by discretion, whose glory is *"to overlook an
offense"* (19:11). Proverbs 14:17 gives the opposite: *"He who is quick to become angry will commit
folly."*

**The parallel is verbal in English and has not been verified in Hebrew here.** The WEB renders both
passages with the same phrase. This page does not assert that the Hebrew expressions are identical,
and Proverbs does not cite Exodus or mention the proclamation. What can be said from the English is
that the quality Proverbs calls *great understanding* in a man is, in Exodus, part of how Yahweh
names himself. Whether the sage intended the echo is not recoverable.

**What it adds to each.** Read beside Exodus 34, the wise man's patience is a likeness of God's;
read beside Proverbs, God's patience is wisdom and not weakness. Exodus 34:6–7 also holds that
patience together with the refusal to clear the guilty, so *slow to anger* is not *never angry* in
either text — Proverbs 14:35 has a king's wrath fall rightly on the servant *"who causes shame"*.
See also [[Merciful and Gracious - Exodus 34, Numbers 14, and Jonah 4]].

Sources: Proverbs 14:17, 29, 35; 15:18; 16:32; 19:11; Exodus 34:6–7, checked in stored WEB.

### Proverbs 19 in context

Proverbs 19:11 locates delayed anger in discretion and calls overlooking an offense a person's
glory. Verse 19 supplies a contrasting pattern: a hot-tempered man incurs a penalty and repeatedly
needs rescue. The glory is therefore tied to restraint rather than successful retaliation. The
comparison with Exodus 34:6 remains an English thematic comparison; no Hebrew identity is newly
asserted. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19:11, 19; Exodus 34:6–7.

### Proverbs 22:24–25 in context

*"Don't befriend a hot-tempered man, and don't associate with one who harbors anger: lest you learn his
ways, and ensnare your soul"* (22:24–25). The sayings on this page praise the slow temper; this command
adds that the quick one is contagious. See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:24–25; no new
source-language claim.

### Anger governed, and a city spared — Proverbs 29

Verse 11 contrasts the fool’s unrestrained anger with the wise person’s self-control; verse 8
carries the benefit into a city, and verse 22 makes wrath a source of further sin. These develop
the patience commended in 14:29 and 15:18. The comparison with Exodus 34:6 remains thematic; no
Hebrew identity is asserted for chapter 29.

See [[Where There Is No Vision]]. Sources: stored WEB Proverbs 29 and the cited local comparisons; no new
source-language claim.

## Significance

[[Strife and Peacemaking]] and [[Wisdom]] meet in the control of anger, and [[Compassion and Mercy]]
connects the human virtue to the divine attribute. The connection gives Proverbs' moral
psychology a theological anchor without claiming that the book makes it explicit.

### Appeasing strife — Proverbs 15:18

[[A Gentle Answer]] brings the first of the later uses into the wiki: *"A wrathful man stirs up
contention, but one who is slow to anger appeases strife"* (15:18). The chapter's first line had said the
same of speech — *"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger"* (15:1) — so the
slow to anger is the person from whom the gentle answer comes. The divine attribute of Exodus 34:6 is,
in this chapter, a peacemaker's. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15:1, 18; Exodus 34:6.

### Better than the mighty — Proverbs 16:32

[[The Plans of the Heart]] ranks the virtue against war: *"One who is slow to anger is better than the
mighty; one who rules his spirit, than he who takes a city"* (16:32). The attribute Exodus 34:6 gives
Yahweh is here set above the warrior's, and the parallel line names it as rule over one's own spirit.
In the same chapter Yahweh's *ḥesed we'ĕmet*, the next words of Exodus 34:6, is the means of atonement
(16:6; Hebrew from Sefaria). Source: stored WEB Proverbs 16:6, 32; Exodus 34:6.
