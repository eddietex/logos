# His Blood Is Required - Darash and the Reckoning for Blood

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 9:5–6** — the Noahic charter — [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- **Genesis 42:22** — [[Reuben]] quotes it at his brothers —
  [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]]
- Genesis 4:10 — the blood that *cries* from the ground — [[Cain and Abel]]
- 2 Samuel 4:11 — David on the murderers of Ishbosheth
- Ezekiel 3:18, 20; 33:6, 8 — the watchman
- Luke 11:50–51 — *"that the blood of all the prophets … may be required of this generation"*

## Analysis

### The formula

***Dāraš*** means *to seek out, to enquire after, to demand an accounting for.* Paired with ***dām***,
blood, it becomes **a technical idiom: to treat a death as an open case.**

**Genesis 9:5 states it as a divine undertaking, and states it three times in one verse:**

> **5** I will surely **require** your life's blood. At the hand of every animal I will **require** it.
> At the hand of man, even at the hand of every man's brother, I will **require** the life of man.
> **6** Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image.
> (9:5–6)

**Three subjects are named as liable — the animal, the man, and the brother** — and the escalation ends
on the last of the three. **The Noahic charter singles out fratricide**, and Genesis has already
supplied the case it is legislating against: **[[Cain]]**, whose brother's blood *"cries to me from the
ground"* (4:10). See [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]] and [[Do Not Cover My Blood]].

**The grammatical point of *dāraš* is that the accounting is God's.** Blood does not simply cry out
into the air; **someone goes looking for it.** The verb is the same one used of *enquiring of Yahweh*
(25:22) — **a death is a question God asks.**

### Reuben's use of it — Genesis 42:22

> **22** Reuben answered them, saying, "Didn't I tell you, saying, 'Don't sin against the child,' and
> you wouldn't listen? Therefore also, behold, **his blood is required**." (42:22)

***Dāmô nidrāš*** — **the niphal, the passive: *his blood is being sought.*** Where 9:5 has God as the
active subject three times, **Reuben uses the form that does not name who is doing the seeking**, which
is the idiom's usual later shape and which makes the demand feel like a standing condition of the world
rather than an act of anyone in particular.

**Three things make the quotation remarkable.**

1. **He is applying the covenant with Noah to his own family, and it fits exactly.** Genesis 9:5 says
   *"at the hand of every man's brother"*; **Reuben is a man's brother, standing in a group of them.**
   He has found the one clause of the Noahic charter written for his exact situation.
2. **He is factually wrong.** Joseph is not dead. **The blood being required is blood that was never
   shed** — the brothers sold him precisely so that they would not have to shed it, on Judah's argument
   that there is no profit in concealing blood (37:26).
3. **He is standing six feet from the man whose death he is invoking**, who is listening through an
   interpreter and understands every word.

**And the quotation of himself is not accurate either.** *"Don't sin against the child"* is not what
37:22 records. **What he said was *"Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit … lay no hand on him"*** —
the words of a man amending a murder into an abandonment, and the narrator had to add the explanation
that he intended to come back. **Twenty-two years on, Reuben has upgraded his own speech into a moral
stand, and he does it in the middle of the first genuine confession any of them has made.** See
[[Guilt and Conscience]].

**The irony the chapter builds is total: he invokes a legal formula for unavenged blood, in front of
the man whose blood it is, to avoid joining a confession, while misquoting the objection he is claiming
credit for.**

### The idiom afterwards

| text | who requires | of whom |
|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 9:5** | God, three times | animal, man, **brother** |
| **Genesis 42:22** | *(passive — unstated)* | the brothers |
| **2 Samuel 4:11** | David, as king | the assassins of Ishbosheth |
| **Ezekiel 33:6** | God | **the watchman who did not warn** |
| **Luke 11:50–51** | God | *"this generation"* |

**The line of development runs from the killer to the bystander.**

- **2 Samuel 4:11** — *"shall I not now require his blood from your hand?"* — **puts the king in God's
  role as the one who seeks**, and executes men who expected a reward.
- **Ezekiel 3:18 and 33:6 make the decisive move.** *"His blood I will require at the watchman's
  hand"* — **the liability now attaches to a man who killed nobody and failed to speak.** The one who
  saw and did not act is answerable.
- **That is Genesis 42:21 in legal form**, and the brothers reach it on their own without the
  vocabulary: *"we saw the distress of his soul … and we wouldn't listen."* **The thing they confess is
  exactly the watchman's offence.**
- **Luke 11:50–51** — *"that the blood of all the prophets, which was shed from the foundation of the
  world, may be required of this generation; **from the blood of Abel** to the blood of Zachariah"* —
  **closes the arc by naming Genesis's first case and applying the accounting to a generation that
  committed none of the murders.**

### The one death the formula is not applied to

**Genesis never applies *dāraš* + *dām* to Joseph's sale, because Joseph does not die.** The formula
enters the Joseph story only as a mistake — Reuben's — and **the cycle's actual accounting is settled
with a different vocabulary entirely**: *ʾāšēm*, liability (42:21), and *ḥāšab*, intention (50:20).

**Genesis lets the blood-formula be quoted in error, by the wrong man, about a living person, and then
resolves the story without it.** The book that opens with blood crying from the ground **ends with a
brother who was nearly killed telling his brothers not to be afraid** (50:19–21).

## Significance

**The idiom is Scripture's insistence that a death does not simply pass.** *Dāraš* makes God the party
who comes looking, which is why the formula is so frequently cited in cases where no human court will
ever act — Abel's, the prophets', Ishbosheth's, and the ones the watchman failed to warn.

**Reuben's misuse of it in Genesis 42 is the wiki's clearest example of a character quoting Scripture
correctly and applying it wrongly.** The clause fits his family, his generation and his crime; it is
simply not true that his brother is dead, and **he is saying it to defend himself against a confession
rather than to make one.** Genesis prints the quotation without comment, in the same breath as the
confession it is deflecting, and lets the reader hold both.

**And Ezekiel's extension of the formula to the watchman is the reason Genesis 42:21 reads as it does.**
The brothers do not confess to murder — they did not commit one. **They confess to having watched.**
*"We saw the distress of his soul … and we wouldn't listen"* is the offence Ezekiel will make a capital
matter a thousand years later, and the brothers name it against themselves, unprompted, in a foreign
jail.
