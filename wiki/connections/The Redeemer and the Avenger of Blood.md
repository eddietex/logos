# The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood

## Passages involved

- **Job 16:18–19** — *"Earth, don't cover my blood. Let my cry have no place to rest. Even now,
  behold, my witness is in heaven."* —
  [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]]
- **Job 19:25** — *"But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives. In the end, he will stand upon the
  earth."* — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Leviticus 25:25** — the *gōʾēl* redeeming land a poor relative has had to sell
- **Leviticus 25:47–49** — the *gōʾēl* redeeming a relative who has sold himself to a resident alien
- **Numbers 35:12, 19–27; Deuteronomy 19:6, 12; Joshua 20:1–9** — the *gōʾēl haddām*, the avenger of
  blood, and the cities of refuge that limit him
- **Ruth 3:9–4:10** — Boaz as *gōʾēl*: land redeemed and a widow married *"to raise up the name of
  the dead on his inheritance"*
- **Proverbs 23:10–11** — *"Don't move the ancient boundary stone. Don't encroach on the fields of
  the fatherless, for their Defender is strong. He will plead their cause against you."*
- **Exodus 6:6** — *"I will redeem you with an outstretched arm"*, the exodus as an act of *gāʾal*
- **Isaiah 41:14; 43:14; 44:6, 24; 47:4; 54:5; 59:20** — *"your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel"*,
  the title's settled prophetic use
- **Psalm 19:14; 78:35; 103:4** — God as *gōʾēl* in the psalms
- **Jeremiah 50:34** — *"Their Redeemer is strong. Yahweh of Armies is his name. He will thoroughly
  plead their cause."*
- **Genesis 4:10** — *"The voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the ground"* —
  [[Cain and Abel]], where God does the office himself
- **Revelation 6:9–10** — *"How long, Master … until you judge and avenge our blood?"*

## Analysis

**The word is family law, not courtroom law.** A *gōʾēl* — the participle of *gāʾal*, to redeem or
reclaim — is the nearest able kinsman of a man in trouble, and Israelite law lays four duties on
him. He **buys back** ancestral land a relative has been forced to sell (Leviticus 25:25). He
**buys out** a relative who has sold himself into slavery (Leviticus 25:47–49). He **marries** a
childless widow so the dead man's name is not lost from his inheritance (Ruth 4:5, 10). And he
**hunts down** the killer of a relative (Numbers 35:19).

The four look unrelated in English and are one thing in Hebrew. In each case a member of a family
has suffered a loss he cannot himself repair — because he is poor, enslaved, or dead — and the
obligation to repair it falls on the nearest relative who can. The *gōʾēl* is not a benefactor. He
is a man on whom a duty rests by blood, and his defining feature is that he **shows up**.

**Job uses the word in the place where it is least available to him.** Job 19 spends verses 13–20
proving that he has no family left: brothers put far off, relatives gone, familiar friends
estranged, servants who will not answer, a wife who cannot bear his breath, and children dead since
chapter 1. He then says, five verses later, *"I know that my Redeemer lives."* The claim is made in
the exact gap the preceding verses have opened. The office requires a living kinsman; Job has
established that he has none; and the assertion is that one exists anyway.

**The blood-avenger sense is the one that binds Job 19 to Job 16.** Three chapters earlier Job
addressed the ground and forbade it to absorb his blood — *"Earth, don't cover my blood"* — which
is an appeal to the law that Genesis 4:10 states and Numbers 35 codifies: blood left uncovered goes
on crying, and someone is obliged to answer it. See [[Do Not Cover My Blood]] and
[[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]]. The officer whose job it is to answer that cry is the *gōʾēl
haddām*. Job asks for the cry not to be silenced in chapter 16 and names the officer in chapter 19,
and the two verses should be read as one movement.

What makes it strange, and worth stating precisely: **Job is not dead.** The avenger of blood acts
for a man who has been killed. Job is speaking, and he is asking for the office to be performed
while he can still watch it. The most economical account of that oddity is that Job now regards
himself as effectively dead — socially erased, physically wasted, his testimony inadmissible — and
is calling for the officer the dead are entitled to. His wish two verses earlier that his words be
cut into rock *"forever"* is the wish of a man preparing a case to be argued after him.

**Against whom is the redemption sought?** This is where the thread becomes uncomfortable, and it
should not be smoothed. In every other biblical use, the *gōʾēl* acts against a third party — a
creditor, a slaveholder, a murderer, a man who moved a boundary stone. In Job 19 the party who has
stripped him, besieged him, driven off his family, and *touched* him is God (19:6–13, 21). If the
Redeemer is a third party, Job is asking a kinsman to prosecute God. If the Redeemer is God — the
majority reading, and the one every other biblical occurrence of the title supports — then Job is
appealing from God to God, exactly as he did at 17:3 when he asked God to post bond with God. The
book does not adjudicate, and [[The Witness in Heaven]] sets out the three readings in full.

## Significance

**The title's later career is where the thread pays.** By the time of Isaiah 40–55, *gōʾēl* has
become one of God's standard names — *"your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel"* — and it carries the
family logic with it: God acts for Israel because of a relationship, not because Israel has earned
it, and the acts named are buying back, bringing home, and taking up a case. Exodus 6:6 had already
used the verb of the exodus. Proverbs 23:11 uses it of the fatherless, whose Defender is *strong*
because they have no other. Every one of these is Job's situation generalised: someone who cannot
act for himself, and a relative under obligation who can.

**The Christian reading, and its limit.** *Redeemer* passes into New Testament vocabulary through
the Greek *lytroō* and *apolytrōsis* — a buying back, with a price — and Job 19:25 has been read as
a confession of Christ since the patristic period, fixed in English ears by the King James Version
and by Handel. The reading is not arbitrary: the New Testament's redeemer is a kinsman (Hebrews
2:11–17 argues at length that he had to share flesh and blood to do the office), he acts for those
who cannot act for themselves, and he is alive. What it must not do is quietly convert Job's
sentence into a creed. Job says he knows a Redeemer lives; he does not say who, he says nothing
about atonement, and the emphasis in his mouth falls on **vindication** — the clearing of a name —
rather than on the forgiveness of sins. The office he wants performed is the one that takes up an
unanswered case, and 42:7 is where it is performed, by God, on God's own initiative, without
anyone having been named.

**And the office is never abolished, only relocated.** Genesis 4:10 has God take up Abel's blood
himself, before there is any law of the *gōʾēl*. Numbers 35 hands the duty to a relative and
immediately fences it with cities of refuge, because a private avenger is dangerous. Revelation
6:9–10 has the martyrs asking how long before their blood is avenged, and the answer is that it is
God's to do. The line from Abel's blood through Job's uncovered blood to the souls under the altar
is one thread: a cry that has not been answered, and the claim that someone is obliged to answer
it.
