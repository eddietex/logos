# Bought, Sacked, or Taken by the Sword - How Jacob Got Shechem

## Passages involved

- Genesis 33:18–20 — *"He bought the parcel of ground where he had spread his tent, at the hand of
  the children of Hamor, Shechem's father, for one hundred pieces of money."*
- Genesis 34:25–30 — his sons sack the city; Jacob disowns the act on grounds of safety
- Genesis 48:21–22 — *"I have given to you one portion above your brothers, which I took out of the
  hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow."*
- Joshua 24:32 — Joseph's bones buried *"in the parcel of ground which Jacob bought of the sons of
  Hamor the father of Shechem for a hundred pieces of money"*
- John 4:5 — *"the parcel of ground that Jacob gave to his son, Joseph"*

## Analysis

**Genesis says three different things about how Jacob came to hold ground at Shechem, and retracts
none of them.**

| text | the claim | who says it |
|---|---|---|
| **33:19** | he **bought** a parcel for a hundred pieces of money | the narrator |
| **34:27–30** | his **sons sacked** the city, and he repudiated it as a danger to the household | the narrator, then Jacob |
| **48:22** | he **took** the portion *"with my sword and with my bow"* | Jacob, on his deathbed |

**The third is the odd one, and it is the only one in Jacob's own mouth.** Genesis never narrates
Jacob fighting anybody, at Shechem or anywhere else. **The one battle in his vicinity is the one he
disowns** — and at 34:30 he is explicit that he had no force to fight with: *"I am few in number.
They will gather themselves together against me and strike me."* The Hebrew of 48:22 makes it
sharper still: ***šĕkem ʾaḥad***, *one shoulder* or *one portion*, is also simply *Shechem*, so the
line reads equally as *"I have given you Shechem, one above your brothers"*. **The pun is almost
certainly intended, and it is the only place in Genesis where Jacob claims a conquest.**

**Four readings stand, and the wiki adopts none of them.**

1. **Jacob is claiming his sons' conquest after all**, in old age, having condemned it at the time —
   the reading that costs the least textually and the most morally, since it makes 34:30 a complaint
   about method rather than about the act.
2. **The verse is figurative**: *sword and bow* as a formula for *acquired and held*, of ground he
   paid for and then had to keep. Comfortable, and unsupported by any other use of the phrase in
   Genesis.
3. **A tradition Genesis does not narrate**, of an Amorite war for the Shechem district, known to the
   text's audience and preserved only in this line. The *Testament of Judah* in the
   Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs tells exactly such a story at length, which shows the reading
   is old but not that the story is.
4. **Two independent traditions about the same ground**, a purchase tradition and a conquest
   tradition, standing side by side in the book without being harmonized — the reading that requires
   the least of the verse and the most of the book's composition.

**The rest of the Bible follows the purchase, not the sword.** Joshua 24:32 buries Joseph's bones in
the parcel *"which Jacob bought of the sons of Hamor… for a hundred pieces of money"*, quoting 33:19
almost word for word and giving the land three owners in sequence — bought by Jacob, willed to
Joseph, and finally *"the inheritance of the children of Joseph"*. **John 4:5 keeps the same line**,
placing the well outside Sychar on *"the parcel of ground that Jacob gave to his son, Joseph"*.
**Nothing outside Genesis 48:22 remembers a sword at Shechem at all.**

**And Genesis 34 itself is careful about what the brothers took.** They take flocks, herds, donkeys,
wealth, wives and children (34:28–29) — **an inventory of movables. The chapter never says they took
the city or held the ground.** The family leaves for Bethel in the next chapter and does not come
back.

## Significance

**This is the wiki's clearest case of a contradiction the text leaves standing**, and the practice
`AGENTS.md` requires is to flag it rather than resolve it: **both claims are recorded, with the
sources that support each, and no harmonization is adopted.**

It also sets a limit on how much weight Jacob's deathbed can bear as a reading of Genesis 34. **The
same deathbed that curses Simeon and Levi's anger (49:5–7) claims, one chapter earlier, the spoil of
the act that anger produced** — or claims something else entirely, which the text does not let us
decide. See [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]],
