## Analysis

### The allotment’s closing verdict

Joshua 21:43–45 now gives the retrospective counterpart: the land sworn to the fathers is given and inhabited, rest is given, and every good word comes to pass. The fathers are not named in these verses; Deuteronomy 9:5 supplies Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Joshua does not here repeat the denial of merit, so that link is a comparison across passages, not a quotation or an additional assertion in this summary. Source: stored WEB Joshua 21:43–45 and Deuteronomy 9:4–6.

Genesis 15:16–18 joins the future return, the Amorites’ iniquity, and the covenant land gift.
Deuteronomy 9:5 again places judgment beside the ancestral oath, now naming Abraham, Isaac,
and Jacob. This is a thematic correspondence, not a claim that Deuteronomy quotes Genesis verbatim.

Within Moses’ speech the denials accumulate: numerical greatness does not explain election
(7:7–8); productive strength itself is given by Yahweh (8:17–18); righteousness does not explain
possession (9:4–6). The people must neither despair before stronger enemies nor turn their
success into evidence of moral superiority.

**The tension with 6:25 remains explicit.** That verse calls obedience righteousness for Israel;
9:4–6 denies that righteousness is the cause of receiving the land and calls Israel stubborn.
They address different questions—what conduct is right and why this people receives the land.
The latter does not make obedience irrelevant, and the former does not make the land earned.

Sources: stored WEB Genesis 15:16–18 and Deuteronomy 6:25, 7:7–8, 8:17–18, 9:1–6.
These comparisons are paraphrases; no source-language equivalence is asserted.

**Rebellion supplies the evidence.** Deuteronomy 9:7–29 follows the denial of merit with
the calf and other remembered provocations. Moses’ intercession asks Yahweh to remember
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and to spare his redeemed inheritance (26–29). The argument moves
from the ancestral oath explaining possession (9:5) to ancestral remembrance supporting
preservation (9:27). Neither appeal claims innocence for Israel.

Source for this extension: stored WEB Deuteronomy 9:1–29, checked 2026-11-06.

## Significance

[[Covenant]], [[Righteousness]], and [[Favor and Grace]] meet in a gift that does not acquit
its recipients. The claim is about Israel’s possession of this land; using it in a wider theology
of grace requires a further argument rather than treating national possession and individual
salvation as interchangeable.
