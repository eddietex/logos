# Don't Let Your Anger Be Kindled - Genesis 18.30-32 and Judges 6.39

## Passages involved

- [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] — **Genesis 18:30, 32** — *Oh don't let the Lord be
  angry, and I will speak*; *Oh don't let the Lord be angry, and I will speak just once more* (stored
  WEB)
- [[The Fleece]] — **Judges 6:39** — *Don't let your anger be kindled against me, and I will speak but
  this once* (stored WEB)
- Also **Genesis 18:3** and **Judges 6:17** — *if now I have found favor in your sight*, the opening of
  both men's first request (stored WEB). See [[The Call of Gideon]].

## Analysis

The Hebrew, all WLC:

| Verse | Hebrew | WEB |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 18:30 | *ʾal-nāʾ yiḥar laʾdōnāy waʾădabbērâ* | *Oh don't let the Lord be angry, and I will speak* |
| Genesis 18:32 | *ʾal-nāʾ yiḥar laʾdōnāy waʾădabbərâ ʾak-happaʿam* | *… and I will speak just once more* |
| Judges 6:39 | *ʾal-yiḥar ʾappəkā bî waʾădabbərâ ʾak happaʿam* | *Don't let your anger be kindled against me, and I will speak but this once* |

Gideon's sentence has the same four elements as Abraham's last one. There is a negated *yiḥar*, *let it
not burn*, then *waʾădabbərâ*, *and let me speak*, then *ʾak happaʿam*, *only this time*. Abraham speaks
of God in the third person, *the Lord*, and leaves the subject of *burn* unnamed. Gideon speaks to God
directly and names it, *your anger* (*ʾappəkā*), *against me* (*bî*). WEB translates the shared words
differently in the two books, *just once more* and *but this once*, so the match is not visible in
English.

The match is not limited to one verse. Each man's first request opens with the same courtesy clause,
*ʾim-nāʾ māṣāʾtî ḥēn bəʿênêkā*, *if now I have found favor in your sight* (Genesis 18:3; Judges 6:17,
WLC). In Genesis it is the welcome to the visitors. In Judges it introduces Gideon's first sign request.
The phrase is a common formula of polite request elsewhere too, so it supports the comparison less
than the rare *ʾak happaʿam* sentence does.

**What each man asks for.** Abraham has been bargaining down the number of righteous needed to spare
Sodom, fifty to ten, and *just once more* is his last step. He asks God to be merciful to a city.
Gideon has had one test answered, and *but this once* introduces a second, the reverse of the first,
*let me make a trial*, *ʾănassê* (Judges 6:39, WLC). He asks God to prove himself to Gideon. Both men
know they may be going too far, and both say so before they ask.

**What God does.** In both stories God gives what is asked and does not rebuke. Yahweh answers
Abraham, *I will not destroy it for the ten's sake* (Genesis 18:32), and *God did so that night* for
Gideon (Judges 6:40). Neither text says God was angry. The fear of anger is in the speaker, not in the
narrative.

## Significance

- **A formula of bold prayer.** In both texts the man asks for more than he thinks he should, and asks
  permission for it first. Whether Judges is consciously echoing Genesis or both draw on a common way of
  speaking to a superior cannot be settled from the two texts. The wiki records the verbal match and
  does not claim an allusion.
- **The comparison helps on the fleece.** One common reading of the fleece treats it as a fault. Read
  beside Abraham, Gideon's request has the same form as one of the boldest intercessions in Genesis,
  and Abraham's is not treated as a fault. The difference is the object. Abraham pleads for others,
  and Gideon asks for assurance for himself. See [[The Fleece]] for both readings.
- **The phrase and its other uses.** In the WLC of Genesis through Deuteronomy and Judges, *ʾak
  happaʿam*, *only this time*, occurs four times (searched for *ʾak* immediately before *happaʿam*).
  Abraham (Genesis 18:32) and Gideon (Judges 6:39) use it with *let me speak*. Pharaoh uses it to Moses,
  *please forgive my sin again*, WEB's *again* for *ʾak happaʿam* (Exodus 10:17; [[Locusts and Darkness]]).
  Samson uses it in his last prayer, *strengthen me, please, only this once* (Judges 16:28, not yet
  ingested). All four are requests to be granted one more time. Only Genesis 18 and Judges 6 also have
  *let it not burn* and *let me speak*, so the wiki pairs those two. Whether the phrase occurs elsewhere
  in the Hebrew Bible was not checked.
