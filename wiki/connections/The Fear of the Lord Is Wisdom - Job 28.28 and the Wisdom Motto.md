# The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto

## Passages involved

- **Job 28:28** — *"To man he said, 'Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart from evil
  is understanding.'"* The last verse of [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]].
- **Job 1:1, 8; 2:3** — Job *"blameless and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from
  evil"*: the narrator's description, twice repeated by God. [[Job of Uz]],
  [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]], [[The Second Test]].
- **Proverbs 1:7** — *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge; but the foolish despise wisdom
  and instruction."* The motto at the head of Proverbs. Not yet ingested.
- **Proverbs 9:10** — *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of wisdom. The knowledge of the Holy One
  is understanding."* The same motto closing the book's first section. Not yet ingested.
- **Proverbs 15:33** — *"The fear of Yahweh teaches wisdom."* Not yet ingested.
- **Psalm 111:10** — *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of wisdom. All those who do his work have a
  good understanding."* Not yet ingested.
- **Ecclesiastes 12:13** — *"This is the end of the matter. All has been heard. Fear God, and keep his
  commandments; for this is the whole duty of man."* Not yet ingested.
- **Isaiah 11:2** — the Spirit resting on the shoot of Jesse, *"the spirit of wisdom and
  understanding … the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of Yahweh."* Not yet ingested.
- **Isaiah 33:6** — *"The fear of Yahweh is your treasure."* Not yet ingested.

## Analysis

**One sentence, used as a motto by four books.** Proverbs puts it at the head of the collection and
again at the end of its prologue (1:7; 9:10), a Psalm closes with it (111:10), Ecclesiastes ends on it
(12:13), and Job 28 ends on it. Each places it at a boundary — a beginning or a conclusion — and each
uses it slightly differently.

**Proverbs says *beginning*; Job says *is*.** Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10 make the fear of Yahweh the
*rēʾšît* or *təḥillâ* of wisdom: its first principle, the starting point of a course of study that goes
on to the ant, the sluggard and the adulteress. Job 28:28 has no such word. *Yirʾat ʾădōnāy hîʾ
ḥokmâ* — *the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom*. In Proverbs the fear of God opens the way into
wisdom; in Job, coming after a poem that has established that wisdom proper is God's alone and
cannot be found or bought, it is the whole of what a human being receives. **Proverbs offers a
curriculum. Job 28 offers a substitute for one** — and the difference is the book of Job's argument
with the tradition in a single grammatical change.

**Job 28:28 is Job 1:1 made into a definition.** The narrator's *yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm wəsār mērāʿ* —
*fearing God and turning from evil* — becomes *yirʾat ʾădōnāy … wəsûr mērāʿ*. The participles become
nouns and the description of one man becomes a statement about *ʾādām*, mankind. **The book thus
tells its reader, before God speaks, that Job had wisdom on the only terms available to a human
being** — and had it in chapter 1, before the catastrophes, while the friends who claim wisdom by
inheritance (8:8; 15:18) never show either half of it toward him. God's verdict at 42:7 is the same
judgment from the other side.

**What the motto does not do.** It does not explain anything. Job had the fear of God and turning from
evil, and lost his children, his property and his health. The friends' doctrine said those two things
guarantee prosperity; the motto, in Job's book, guarantees only that a man is wise — that he stands in
the right relation to a God whose reasons he cannot see. **Ecclesiastes reaches the same place from the
other direction**: after finding every other pursuit to be vapour, it concludes *"Fear God, and keep his
commandments"* (12:13), offered not as the key to the world but as what remains when the world has not
been explained.

**Isaiah makes it a quality of the king.** Isaiah 11:2 gives the coming ruler *"the spirit of knowledge
and of the fear of Yahweh"* — the fear of God not only as the starting point of a student but as an
endowment of the Spirit, which later Christian reading attaches to Christ. Isaiah 33:6 calls it a
*treasure*, which is the vocabulary of Job 28:15–19 turned round: the thing no gold can buy is itself
the treasure. See [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]].

## Significance

The phrase is the hinge between the wisdom books and the rest of the Old Testament, because it puts
Israel's covenant God — *Yahweh* in Proverbs and the Psalm, *ʾădōnāy* in Job — at the root of a
tradition that otherwise argues from observation and shares its material with Egypt and Edom. In Job,
a book set entirely outside Israel, it appears once, at the end of the hymn, in the one divine title the
dialogue uses nowhere else. **The tradition's motto is put in God's own mouth and addressed to every
human being**, and the human being it describes is the man the tradition's spokesmen have just spent
twenty-five chapters condemning. See [[The Fear of God]] and [[Wisdom]].
