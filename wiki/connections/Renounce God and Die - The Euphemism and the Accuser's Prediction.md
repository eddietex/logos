# Renounce God and Die - The Euphemism and the Accuser's Prediction

## Passages involved

- **Job 1:11** — *"But stretch out your hand now, and touch all that he has, and he will renounce
  you to your face."* — [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]]
- **Job 2:5** — *"But stretch out your hand now, and touch his bone and his flesh, and he will
  renounce you to your face."* — [[The Second Test]]
- **Job 2:9** — *"Then his wife said to him, 'Do you still maintain your integrity? Renounce God,
  and die.'"*
- **Job 1:5** — *"It may be that my sons have sinned, and renounced God in their hearts"* —
  [[Job of Uz]]
- **Job 1:21** — *"Blessed be Yahweh's name."*
- **Genesis 3:1–6, 17** — the serpent's question, the woman's giving, and *"because you have
  listened to your wife's voice"* — [[The Fall]]
- **1 Kings 21:10, 13** — the two false witnesses against Naboth: *"You blasphemed God and the
  king"*, the same euphemism
- **Job 2:3** — *"He still maintains his integrity"*

## Analysis

**One verb, *bārak*, *to bless*, appears five times in the prologue of Job, and three of them mean
the opposite.** At 1:5 Job fears his sons have *bārak*-ed God in their hearts; at 1:11 and 2:5 the
accuser predicts Job will *bārak* God to his face; at 2:9 his wife says *bārēk ʾĕlōhîm*. In all four
the sense required is *curse*, and WEB renders it *renounce*. Only 1:21 — *"Blessed be Yahweh's
name"* — uses the word to mean what it says.

**The usual explanation is scribal reverence**: an ancient reluctance to write *curse* and *God* in
the same clause, so the antonym is substituted and the reader is trusted to invert it. The same
substitution appears at 1 Kings 21:10 and 13, where Jezebel's suborned witnesses accuse Naboth of
having *blessed* God and the king, in a trial that ends in his execution for blasphemy. **Whatever
its origin, the effect in Job is a deliberate literary instrument**: the prologue's single most
loaded word is a word whose meaning inverts depending on who is speaking, and the book uses that
instability three times before chapter 3.

**Which sets up the thing this page exists for.** The accuser makes one prediction and makes it
twice, in identical words: *ûbērak ʿal-pānêkā* — **he will *bārak* you to your face.** He never
sees it tested. He leaves at 2:7.

**And two verses later, the words are spoken.** *Bārēk ʾĕlōhîm wāmūt.* **The imperative of the
accuser's verb, in the mouth of the only person left who loves Job**, addressed to the man himself.
The prediction is not fulfilled — Job does not say it — but it is **delivered**, put into the air
where it can be accepted or refused, by someone with standing to be listened to. The accuser
proposed to reduce Job to those words by taking everything; what actually produces them is not the
taking but another person's grief.

**The wife's sentence is built out of two quotations, and this is the second.** The first is God's:
*"Do you still maintain your integrity?"* is 2:3's *"He still maintains his integrity"* with the
pronoun turned. **Between them, her nine Hebrew words contain the two most important things said in
the heavenly scene she was not present at, quoted back accurately by someone who cannot have heard
them.** Nothing in the text explains this, and it is the strongest single argument that the author
intends her as more than a bystander.

**The Eve parallel, and its limits.** The tradition reads her against Genesis 3 almost
automatically — a woman's word standing between a man and God, and Augustine's *diaboli adiutrix*
is the compressed form of it. The structural echo is real: in both scenes **a question about God
precedes a proposal about God**, and in both the proposal comes to the man from the person closest
to him rather than from the adversary directly. See [[The Serpent]] and [[Eve]].

**But the differences run against the reading at nearly every point.**

| | Genesis 3 | Job 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Who speaks first | the serpent, to the woman | the wife, to the man, unprompted in the text |
| What is offered | a gain — *you will be like God* | a release — *and die* |
| The adversary's involvement | present, and speaking | gone four verses earlier, and never shown with her |
| The man's response | takes and eats | refuses, and answers in the plural |
| God's verdict on her | stated, at 3:16 | **never given**; 42:7 rebukes the friends by name and says nothing about her |

**The last row is the heaviest.** The book of Job ends by naming who spoke wrongly about God, and it
names three men. If the author had meant her as the accuser's instrument, the epilogue that settles
every other account had an obvious place to say so.

**And she offers no gain.** The serpent's proposal is an advancement; hers is an ending. A wife
telling a husband with an incurable disease that he may stop holding on is doing something the
Bible has no category for and that readers have supplied a category for out of Genesis 3. **The
Hebrew supports the hostile reading through its vocabulary and the sympathetic reading through its
narrative, and closes neither.** See [[Job's Wife]].

**Job's refusal is not a refusal of her.** He convicts the sentence — *"you speak as one of the
foolish women would speak"*, putting the weight on the speech — and then answers in the **first
person plural**: *shall **we** receive good… and shall **we** not receive evil*. Contrast Genesis
3:12 and 3:17, where Adam's response to the same structural situation is to hand the woman to God as
the explanation, and God's sentence on him opens *"because you have listened to your wife's
voice"*. **Job listens and declines. Adam does not listen; he obeys and then blames.**

## Significance

- **It is where the accuser's case is finally spoken aloud on earth**, by someone who has never heard
  of him, after he has left the book — and the wiki treats that as the point of the arrangement
  rather than a coincidence.
- **It completes the prologue's pattern of one word meaning two things.** *Bārak* has now been used
  to mean *curse* three times and *bless* twice inside two chapters, and 1:21's genuine blessing sits
  in the middle of them.
- **It is the hinge on which Job's wife is judged**, and the wiki's position is that the vocabulary
  is genuinely incriminating, the narrative is genuinely exculpating, and the book leaves it open.
- **It marks the difference between the two great temptation scenes in the Hebrew Bible.** One is
  answered by eating; the other by a question about what a person may reasonably expect from God.
  See [[The Second Test]] and [[Shall We Receive Good and Not Evil - Weal and Woe from One Hand]].
