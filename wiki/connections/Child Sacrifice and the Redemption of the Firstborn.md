# Child Sacrifice and the Redemption of the Firstborn

## Passages involved

- [[The Covenant Renewed]] — Exodus 34:1–28.

- **Genesis 22:1–19** — God asks for a son as a burnt offering and stops the knife. See
  [[The Binding of Isaac]].
- **Exodus 4:22–23** — *"Israel is my son, my firstborn … Let my son go, that he may serve me …
  Behold, I will kill your son, your firstborn."* — [[Moses Returns to Egypt]]
- **Exodus 13:2, 11–15; 22:29–30; 34:19–20** — every firstborn is Yahweh's; every firstborn son is
  redeemed — [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]]
- **Numbers 3:11–13, 40–51; 18:15–16** — the Levites taken instead of the firstborn, and the
  five-shekel redemption price
- **Leviticus 18:21; 20:2–5; Deuteronomy 12:31; 18:10** — the prohibition, attached to Molech and to
  *"passing through the fire"*
- **Judges 11:29–40** — Jephthah's vow, and the daughter nobody stops
- **2 Kings 3:26–27; 16:3; 21:6; 23:10** — kings of Moab, Judah, and Israel who do it
- **Jeremiah 7:31; 19:5; 32:35** — *"which I didn't command, nor did it come into my mind"*
- **Ezekiel 20:25–26** — *"I gave them statutes that were not good"*
- **Micah 6:6–8** — *"Shall I give my firstborn for my disobedience?"*

## Analysis

**Genesis 22 asks for the one thing the rest of the Bible says most vehemently that God does not
want. That is the problem, and it does not go away by being restated gently.**

The law's language is not measured. Child sacrifice is *"an abomination to Yahweh"* (Deuteronomy
12:31); it is what the nations do *"to their gods"*; it is grounds for stoning (Leviticus 20:2). And
Jeremiah says three separate times, of the fire-altars in the Valley of Hinnom, that it is a thing
*"which I didn't command, nor did it come into my mind"* (7:31; 19:5; 32:35) — a denial so emphatic
that it reads as an answer to someone who had claimed otherwise.

### The claim being answered

**Israel had a law that could be misread into exactly this.** *"All the firstborn are mine"* (Numbers
3:13). *"You shall give the firstborn of your sons to me"* (Exodus 22:29). Taken alone, that sentence
is a demand for children, and Exodus 22:29 states it without the qualifier, going straight on to the
cattle and sheep. See
[[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]].

**The qualifier is always supplied elsewhere, and always in the same form.** Exodus 13:13: *"you
shall redeem all the firstborn of man among your sons"*; 34:20: *"You shall redeem all the firstborn
of your sons"* — *pādâ*, to buy back. Numbers 18:16 puts a price
on it: five shekels, at a month old. Numbers 3 substitutes an entire tribe for an entire generation
of firstborn sons, and counts the surplus. **The law claims every firstborn son and then, without
exception, hands him back for an animal or a payment.**

So the Bible holds two things at once: **the son belongs to God, and the son is not to be killed.**
Genesis 22 is the narrative form of exactly that pair — the claim made in its most absolute terms,
and then satisfied by a ram.

### Ezekiel's answer, which is the strangest verse in the thread

> **25** Moreover also I gave them statutes that were not good, and ordinances in which they should
> not live. **26** I polluted them in their own gifts, in that they caused to pass through the fire
> all that opens the womb… *(Ezekiel 20:25–26)*

**Ezekiel does not say the practice was never commanded; he says it was commanded as a punishment.**
This is the boldest thing anyone in Scripture says about the subject, and it is plainly in tension
with Jeremiah's *"nor did it come into my mind"*. The standard readings are that Ezekiel means the
misreading of the firstborn law was itself the judgment; that he speaks of God giving people over to
what they wanted (the Romans 1 pattern); or that he means the statutes of the nations, permitted to
stand. **The wiki records the tension between Jeremiah 7:31 and Ezekiel 20:25–26 as a real one
between two prophets, not as an appearance to be dissolved.**

### The narrative cases, and what the text does with each

| passage | who | outcome | narrator's verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 22 | Abraham | **stopped**; a ram *instead of his son* | the act of a man who fears God |
| Judges 11:29–40 | Jephthah | **carried out** | none — the text reports and does not judge |
| 2 Kings 3:26–27 | the king of Moab | carried out, on the wall | *"there was great wrath against Israel"*, unexplained |
| 2 Kings 16:3; 21:6 | Ahaz, Manasseh | carried out | *"like the abominations of the nations"* |

**The comparison that matters is Jephthah.** The same elements are present — a vow, a child, a
knife — and there is no voice from the sky. Nobody intervenes, the daughter asks for two months and
gets them, and Judges records the result as an annual custom. **Genesis 22 is the story in which the
angel comes; Judges 11 is the story in which he does not**, and Scripture keeps both without
comparing them.

### What Genesis 22 is doing, on the three main readings

1. **Polemic.** The chapter exists to stage the demand and abolish it: the ancient world's most
   extreme act of devotion is demanded, credited to Abraham in full, and then refused in favour of an
   animal — after which the law makes the animal permanent. On this reading the chapter is the
   founding text of the redemption of the firstborn, and its point is 22:13.
2. **Test.** The demand is not about the practice at all; it is about whether Abraham will hand back
   the promise. The son is the currency because he is what Abraham has, and the reason the command is
   monstrous is that a test made of anything less would prove nothing. See [[Testing]].
3. **Both, historically layered.** A tradition about a sanctuary and a substituted animal, retold as
   a story about faith, in a culture that was moving away from a practice its neighbours kept.

**None of the three makes the command comfortable, and the wiki does not choose among them.** What
can be said flatly is that the Bible's own trajectory runs one direction only: from a claim on the
firstborn, through redemption by substitute, to an unqualified prohibition — and that Micah puts the
whole question and its answer in three verses.

> **7** Will Yahweh be pleased with thousands of rams? With tens of thousands of rivers of oil? Shall
> I give my firstborn for my disobedience? The fruit of my body for the sin of my soul? **8** He has
> shown you, O man, what is good. What does Yahweh require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy,
> and to walk humbly with your God? *(Micah 6:7–8)*

### Where the firstborn claim starts — Exodus 4:22–23

**The law of the firstborn rests on a sentence spoken before the plagues begin.** God names Israel his
firstborn son and puts Pharaoh's firstborn in the balance against it: release mine, or I take yours.
**When the night comes, Israel's firstborn are spared and Egypt's are not, and the redemption law
follows directly from it** — *"Yahweh killed all the firstborn in the land of Egypt … Therefore I
sacrifice to Yahweh all that opens the womb, being males; but all the firstborn of my sons I redeem"*
(13:15). **Every redeemed firstborn in Israel is a reminder of a firstborn that was not.** Three verses
after the announcement, at the lodging place, Moses' own son is cut and Yahweh *"let him alone"*
(4:24–26). See [[Israel My Firstborn - Exodus 4.22, Hosea 11.1, and Matthew 2.15]].

### The firstborn law at its first giving — Exodus 13

**Exodus 13 is where the firstborn law enters Scripture, and the redemption clause comes with it.** God's
own words are only *"Sanctify to me all the firstborn … It is mine"* (13:2); Moses' instruction gives the
redemption at once — *"you shall redeem all the firstborn of man among your sons"* (13:13) — and the reason
in the son's catechism: *"Yahweh killed all the firstborn in the land of Egypt … Therefore I sacrifice to
Yahweh all that opens the womb, being males; but all the firstborn of my sons I redeem"* (13:15). **So the
claim without the clause at 22:29 comes after a giving that already included it.** The tension recorded
above stands; the order of the text is now part of it. See [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]].

**The verb is the one the prohibition uses.** *"You shall set apart to Yahweh all that opens the womb"*
(13:12) is ***wəhaʿăbartā***, the hiphil of *ʿābar*, *cause to pass over* — the same verb as Leviticus
18:21's *ləhaʿăbîr*, *to make pass over* to Molech, and Deuteronomy 18:10's *maʿăbîr … bāʾēš*, *who makes
… pass through the fire* (WLC). **The law that claims the firstborn and the law that forbids child
sacrifice share a verb.** Whether that is why the redemption clause is never omitted where the verb is
used, or simply the ordinary verb for handing over, the text does not say.

**The donkey shows the logic.** The firstborn donkey cannot be offered; it is redeemed with a *śeh*, a lamb,
or its neck is broken (*ʿārap*; 13:13). **The animal has a stated alternative to redemption; the son has
none** — the sentence turns to *"all the firstborn of man"* with no *if*. And the lamb that stands in for the
donkey is the word Isaac used: *"where is the lamb (haśśeh)?"* (Genesis 22:7; WLC).

**Exodus 22:29–30 is now ingested, and the omission stands as written.** The verse sits in a unit
that also forbids sacrifice to any god but Yahweh (22:20), so the same passage that omits the
redemption clause makes the worship this page describes a capital offence. The animal firstborn is
given *"on the eighth day"* (22:30); the text does not say when the son is given, or whether he is
redeemed. **The wiki still records 22:29 and 13:13 side by side without harmonizing them.** See
[[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] and [[The Eighth Day - Circumcision and the Firstborn Given to God]].

### The renewal in its narrative setting — Exodus 34

[[The Covenant Renewed]] again states redemption of every firstborn son (34:20), after the ownership claim
(34:19). The neck-breaking alternative is supplied for the donkey, not the son. **This also
qualifies the earlier summary about an animal or payment:** Exodus 34 assigns a lamb to the
donkey and leaves the son's means of redemption unspecified. It does not itself prescribe an
animal substitute for a human son. The clause absent at 22:29 is explicit here, and the
difference between the formulations remains recorded.

## Significance

- **It is the reason Genesis 22 cannot be read as a story about how far devotion should go.** The
  law, the prophets, and the historical books all treat the act it stops as the definitive mark of
  the nations Israel is not to imitate. **The chapter's ending is the chapter's doctrine.**
- **It is where the wiki's rule about contradictions gets its hardest case.** Exodus 22:29 and Exodus
  13:13; Jeremiah 7:31 and Ezekiel 20:25–26. Both pairs are stated here, with the passages that
  support each, and neither is smoothed. See [[The Ram Instead of the Son - Substitution at Moriah]].
- **And it puts Jephthah's daughter in the wiki's field of view early.** When Judges is ingested,
  this page is the one that has been waiting for it: the vow that is kept, in a book with no voice
  from the sky in it.
