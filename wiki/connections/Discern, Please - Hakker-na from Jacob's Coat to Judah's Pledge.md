# Discern, Please - Hakker-na from Jacob's Coat to Judah's Pledge

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 37:31–33** — the brothers dip Joseph's coat in goat's blood and hand it to their father:
  *"Examine it, now (*hakker-nāʾ*), whether it is your son's coat or not"* — [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]]
- **Genesis 38:25–26** — Tamar sends Judah his own pledge: *"Please discern (*hakker-nāʾ*) whose are
  these — the signet, and the cords, and the staff"* — [[Judah and Tamar]]
- **Genesis 42:7–8** — Joseph *recognises* his brothers and *"made himself strange to them"*; *"they
  didn't recognise him"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 27:23** — Isaac *"didn't recognise him, because his hands were hairy"* —
  [[The Stolen Blessing]]
- **Genesis 31:32** — Jacob to Laban about the teraphim: *"discern what is yours with me, and take
  it"* — [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]]

## Analysis

**The exact phrase *hakker-nāʾ* occurs twice in Genesis, one chapter apart, and the man who says it
the first time is the man it is said to the second.**

| | **Genesis 37:32** | **Genesis 38:25** |
|---|---|---|
| who speaks | **Judah and his brothers** | **Tamar** |
| who receives | **Jacob**, their father | **Judah**, her father-in-law |
| the object | a ***kətōnet***, a coat | a ***ḥōtām***, ***pətîl***, ***maṭṭeh*** — signet, cord, staff |
| whose identity it carries | **Joseph's** | **Judah's own** |
| the blood on it | a goat's | — |
| what the goat does | is killed to fake the evidence | **is promised and never delivered** |
| who knows the truth | the speakers | the speaker |
| what the receiver concludes | *"an evil animal has devoured him"* — **false** | *"she is more righteous than I"* — **true** |
| what it costs | twenty-two years of mourning | **a confession** |

**Both times the phrase does the work a lie would otherwise have to do.** The brothers tell Jacob no
falsehood at all — they say *"we have found this"* and then **hand him a question**, and he supplies
the sentence they had drafted for him at 37:20, word for word and unprompted. **Tamar likewise asserts
nothing** beyond *"by the man whose these are, I am with child"*, and lets three objects say the rest.
**In the first case a question is used to install a false conclusion; in the second, to force a true
one.**

**And both times the receiver is holding a piece of a man's identity in his hands.** A garment and a
seal are the two things in the ancient Near East that stand for a person in his absence — **which is
why Genesis 37 can kill Joseph with a coat and Genesis 38 can convict Judah with a signet.** The coat
was taken off its owner by force; **the signet was handed over voluntarily, for one night, as security
for a goat.**

### The verb

***Nākar*** — *to recognise*, *to discern*, *to acknowledge* — **runs the whole Joseph cycle, and it
is nearly always about failing.** [[Isaac]] *"didn't recognise"* Jacob (27:23) and blessed the wrong
son. [[Jacob]] *"recognised"* the coat (37:33) and reasoned correctly from real evidence to a false
conclusion. **[[Judah]] recognises the pledge (38:26) and gets it right — the only one of the three who
does.** And [[Joseph]] will recognise his brothers while they do not recognise him (42:7–8), which is
the cycle's last and longest working of the verb.

**The pattern is worth stating exactly, because it is easy to overstate.** Genesis does not moralise
about recognition; it simply keeps handing characters objects and letting the reader watch what they
conclude. **Three of the four get it wrong, and the one who gets it right is the one being convicted.**

### Why Genesis 38 is where it is

**The chapter is an interruption, and this pairing is the best internal evidence that it is a placed
one.** Joseph is sold in 37:36 and bought in 39:1; **nothing in Genesis 38 depends on either verse**,
and neither Joseph nor Jacob is named in it. **Two standard explanations exist:**

1. **An independent Judah tradition, inserted here because this is where Judah leaves the brothers.**
   The chapter has its own genealogical interest, its own geography, and no contact with the Joseph
   plot — which is what a separately transmitted unit looks like.
2. **A deliberate foil.** Genesis 38 and 39 are consecutive chapters about a son of Jacob and a sexual
   proposition, and they run opposite ways: **Judah solicits and is caught; Joseph is solicited and
   refuses.** A garment is used as false evidence against the innocent party in both (the coat at
   37:32, Joseph's garment at 39:13–18), and the phrase *"and it happened about this time"* brackets
   the pair.

**The wiki adopts neither explanation and notes that they are not exclusive.** What it does adopt is
the observation that the two occurrences of *hakker-nāʾ* are not distributed at random: **the only two
in the book sit ninety verses apart, in the mouths of the deceiver and of the woman who deceives him,
about the two objects that stand for a man.**

## Significance

**Genesis draws no line between the two verses.** It never says *as Judah did to his father, so was it
done to him*; there is no narratorial comment, no divine speech, and no character who notices.
**The pairing is entirely structural, and it is the clearest instance in the book of the technique the
wiki has flagged repeatedly**: the Hebrew Bible's narrative ethics run by juxtaposition rather than by
ruling. See [[Dinah and Tamar - Two Rapes and Two Avenging Brothers]] for the same technique across
two books.

**And what the second *hakker-nāʾ* produces is the thing the first one prevented.** The coat's question
bought the brothers twenty-two years of a father's mourning and their own silence inside it. **The
pledge's question buys three Hebrew words** — ***ṣādəqâ mimmennî*** — **and they are the first
unqualified admission of fault by anyone in Genesis.** Adam blames the woman; Cain asks a question;
Abraham explains the sister arrangement twice; Jacob, caught with Laban's daughters and Laban's flocks,
makes a speech about his own hard usage. **Judah is handed evidence, recognises it, and states the
verdict against himself without bargaining.** See [[Righteousness]] and [[Deception]].

**It is also the moment the succession becomes intelligible.** Genesis has been moving the birthright
toward Judah without announcing it — *"his brothers listened to him"* at 37:27, which the book never
says of [[Reuben]] — and the man who will make the longest speech in Genesis offering himself as a
slave in Benjamin's place (44:18–34) **is first shown learning, on a road in the Shephelah, what it
costs to be handed your own seal.** See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] and [[Judah]].
