# A Place to Flee - Exodus 21 and Numbers 35

## Passages involved

- [[Refuge for the Unwitting Killer]] — Deuteronomy 19:1–13; three cities in the land, three more if the border
  grows, and the murderer handed to the avenger.

- [[Three Cities Beyond the Jordan]] — Deuteronomy 4:41–43; the three eastern cities designated.

- [[The Levitical Cities and the Cities of Refuge]] — Numbers 35:1–34.

- [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]] — Proverbs 28:17.

- [[Laws of Violence and Injury]] — Exodus 21:12–14, 29–30: refuge for unintentional killing, no altar protection
  for planned murder, and a possible ransom for the negligent bull owner.
- Numbers 35:9–34 — cities of refuge, adjudication, witnesses, and prohibited ransoms; verified
  WEB, now ingested from the stored raw text.

## Analysis

### The eastern cities named — Deuteronomy 4:41–43

Moses sets apart Bezer for the Reubenites, Ramoth in Gilead for the Gadites, and Golan
in Bashan for the Manassites. Numbers 35:14 had required three eastern cities without
naming them. Deuteronomy supplies the names and repeats the protective purpose for a
killer who acted unwittingly and had no previous hatred. Exodus 21:13’s place of flight
thus has specific locations in the narrative.

Three here and six in Numbers are not conflicting totals: this notice concerns the
eastern half. Its silence about judgment, the high priest’s death, and the avenger does
not cancel Numbers 35:24–28. Neither passage here narrates an actual asylum case or
settles the date when the full system began operating.

### Numbers 35 now ingested

The whole chapter now places the six refuges within forty-eight Levite cities (1–8), apportioned from larger and smaller inheritances. The death of the anointed high priest ends confinement (25–28); no reason for that timing is stated. Verse 33’s blood-pollution rule must be read with the explicit rescue of the unwitting killer, not as an order to execute every person who has caused a death.

Source: stored WEB Numbers 35; comparisons use stored Exodus 21, Leviticus 25, and Numbers 18.


Exodus 21:13 promises a place to which the unintentional killer may flee. Verse 14 denies even the
altar to a person who schemes to kill a neighbor. It names neither a city nor the procedure for
deciding the difference. Numbers 35:11–15 specifies six cities and makes refuge available to
Israelites, strangers, and resident foreigners. The congregation is to judge between the killer
and the avenger (35:24–25); one witness cannot establish a capital case (35:30).

Refuge is protection with conditions, not a declaration that nothing happened. The person judged
to have killed without hostility must remain until the high priest's death, and leaving the
boundary exposes him to the avenger (35:25–28). This develops the distinction Exodus already
makes between deliberate and unintentional killing; it does not turn every killer into the same
kind of offender.

**The ransom provisions must also stay distinct.** Exodus 21:29–30 permits an imposed ransom for
the owner who failed to restrain a bull known to be dangerous. Numbers 35:31 forbids ransom for a
murderer, and 35:32 separately forbids payment for an early return from refuge. Thus it is too
broad to say that the Torah refuses ransom in only one case. The cases have different grounds and
outcomes; money is not a universal means of canceling liability.

### Proverbs 28 in context

Proverbs describes the person tormented by life blood as a fugitive until death, without support.
Its brief saying gives no account of intent, witnesses, or trial. Exodus 21:12–14 expressly
distinguishes an unintentional killing from a planned one. Reading Proverbs as an instruction to
deny all refuge would contradict the protection Exodus grants; the proverb should not erase the
law’s distinctions. This addition compares the stored WEB of Proverbs and Exodus only.

### Deuteronomy 19:1–13 now ingested

Deuteronomy's own refuge law repeats 4:42's definition — *"kills his neighbor unawares, and didn't hate
him in time past"* (19:4) — and adds an example, the ax head that slips from the handle (5). Its
concerns are distance and the land: three cities *"in the middle of your land"*, the land divided in
three and the way prepared (2–3), because *"the way is long"* and the avenger's heart is hot (6).

It differs from Numbers 35 in what it leaves out and what it adds. It does not use *"city of refuge"*
(WLC *miqlāṭ* is absent from Deuteronomy; morphhb), names no congregation and no high priest, and
gives the murderer's fate to *"the elders of his city"* (12). Joshua 20:4 has the fugitive state his case
to *"the elders of that city"*, the city of refuge, and 20:6 keeps Numbers' congregation and high priest
(WEB, checked at bible-api.com). Each text names a different body; none describes how they fit together.

**Six cities or nine.** With the eastern three of 4:41–43, the three western cities of 19:2 make the six
of Numbers 35:13–14 and of Joshua 20:7–8, which names Kedesh, Shechem, and Kiriath Arba west of the
Jordan and Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan east of it. 19:8–9 then adds *"three cities more … besides these
three"* if the border is enlarged *"as he has sworn to your fathers"*. Read as nine, the extra three
were conditional and never appointed; read as six, the *"three more"* are the eastern cities, which sits
awkwardly with 19:8–9's future tense after 4:41–43. The wiki keeps both readings.

## Significance

Refuge and punishment both require distinctions that an unrestricted cycle of retaliation would
ignore. See [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]], [[Judgment]], and [[Redemption]]. The two passages do
not supply evidence for identifying a modern refuge site or reconstructing all historical practice.
