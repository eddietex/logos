# Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 11:27–32** — Terah takes Abram, Sarai, and Lot out of Ur of the Chaldees *"to go into
  the land of Canaan"*, stops at Haran, and dies there at 205 — [[From Shem to Abram]]
- **Genesis 12:1–5** — *"Now Yahweh said to Abram, 'Leave your country…'"*, and Abram leaves Haran at
  seventy-five — [[The Call of Abram]]
- **Genesis 15:7** — *"I am Yahweh who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldees, to give you this land"*
  *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 24:4, 10; 31:53** — Haran as Abraham's *"country"* and *"relatives"*, and *"the God of
  Nahor"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Joshua 24:2–3** — *"Your fathers … even Terah … served other gods. I took your father Abraham
  from beyond the River"*
- **Nehemiah 9:7** — *"you are Yahweh, the God who chose Abram, and brought him out of Ur of the
  Chaldees"*
- **Acts 7:2–4** — Stephen: God appeared to Abraham *"in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran"*, and
  he left Haran *"when his father was dead"*
- **Hebrews 11:8** — *"he went out, not knowing where he went"*

## Analysis

Genesis tells the story of Abraham's departure twice, in two consecutive paragraphs, with a different
man in charge each time — and four later texts retell it in ways that do not all fit.

### The two departures in Genesis

**11:31 has Terah as the subject.** *"Terah took Abram his son, Lot the son of Haran, his son's son,
and Sarai his daughter-in-law … They went from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of Canaan."*
No command, no vision, no divine speech; God does not appear anywhere in Genesis 11:10–32. Abram is
an object — *Terah took Abram* — and the destination named is the one Abram will later be promised.
Then they stop.

**12:1 has Yahweh as the subject.** *"Leave your country, and your relatives, and your father's
house, and go to the land that I will show you."* Three ablatives, and each one is smaller and
harder than the last. And this time the destination is *not* named: **"the land that I will show
you"**, where 11:31 had said plainly *"to go into the land of Canaan."* The journey with the known
destination stopped halfway; the journey with the unknown one arrives. Hebrews 11:8 is reading
exactly this: *"he went out, not knowing where he went."*

Read together, the two paragraphs make the call a resumption rather than a beginning. Somebody in
this family was already going to Canaan. What Genesis 12 adds is not the direction but the speaker.

**And the second journey completes what the first could not.** *"They went to go into the land of
Canaan. They entered into the land of Canaan"* (12:5) writes the clause 11:31 stopped one short of,
using the same verb of intention and then supplying the arrival. Abram goes on to cross the country
from Shechem to the Negev. The one asymmetry the two paragraphs never resolve is that the man who
arrives is given no ground in it, and the man who did not arrive is buried in a city he owned no
more of.

### The chronology that does not work

The numbers are given in three verses and they conflict with Acts.

- Terah is seventy when he begins fathering (11:26).
- Terah dies at two hundred five (11:32).
- Abram leaves Haran at seventy-five (12:4).

If Abram was born in Terah's seventieth year, he leaves Haran when his father is 145 — and Terah
then lives another sixty years. But **Acts 7:4** says Abraham left Haran *"when his father was
dead."*

Three ways out, and only the first is widely held.

1. **Abram is not the firstborn.** 11:26 gives the age at which Terah *began* fathering — exactly as
   5:32 does for Noah — and Abram, born when Terah was 130 (205 − 75), is listed first because he is
   the son the book follows. This makes [[Haran (son of Terah)]] the eldest, which the surrounding
   text supports: his daughter [[Milcah]] is old enough to marry, and his son [[Lot]] travels as an
   adult. It is the resolution the rabbis, the church fathers, and most modern commentators adopt.
   Its cost is the tidy 292-year interval from the flood to Abram's birth, which becomes 352.
2. **Stephen is following a different text or tradition.** The Samaritan Pentateuch gives Terah a
   lifespan of 145, not 205 — which makes Acts 7:4 exactly right on the plain reading of 11:26.
   Stephen's speech contains several details that track the Samaritan text or Second Temple
   retellings rather than the Masoretic Genesis, so this is not an ad-hoc suggestion; it does mean
   accepting that Acts and the Hebrew Genesis disagree about a number.
3. **"When his father was dead" is theological rather than chronological** — Terah dead to the story
   rather than dead in fact. This is a reading of convenience and has little to commend it.

The wiki reports the first as standard, records the second as the strongest textual explanation, and
notes that the conflict is real and is not resolved by any reading that respects both numbers as
they stand.

### Where the call happened

**Genesis puts it in Haran.** 12:1 follows 11:32 with no change of scene, and 12:4 has Abram
departing *from Haran*.

**Stephen puts it in Mesopotamia, before Haran.** *"The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham
when he was in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran, and said to him, 'Get out of your land and away
from your relatives, and come into a land which I will show you.' Then he came out of the land of the
Chaldaeans and lived in Haran"* (Acts 7:2–4). On Stephen's telling the call came in Ur and the move
to Haran was already obedience — which makes Terah's journey in 11:31 a following of his son rather
than a leading of him.

Stephen is not inventing this. **Genesis 15:7** has Yahweh identify himself as the one *"who brought
you out of Ur of the Chaldees"* — not out of Haran — and **Nehemiah 9:7** says the same. Both texts
treat Ur, not Haran, as the place Abram was taken from. So the Old Testament itself contains the
seed of Stephen's reading, and Genesis 12 read on its own contains the other. A common harmonization
posits two calls, or one call in Ur repeated in Haran; nothing in the text requires it and nothing
excludes it.

### What they were called out of

Genesis says nothing about the family's religion. **Joshua 24:2–3 does**, in the middle of the
covenant renewal at Shechem, and it is blunt:

> Your fathers lived of old time beyond the River, even Terah, the father of Abraham and the father
> of Nahor. They served other gods. I took your father Abraham from beyond the River, and led him
> throughout all the land of Canaan.

That single verse changes the register of the whole story. The call is not the promotion of a devout
man but an extraction. It is also Israel's own account of its origins, delivered by Joshua to the
assembled tribes as the ground of the demand *"put away the gods which your fathers served"* (24:14)
— which is to say, the nation is told at the outset that it has no ancestral claim on Yahweh at all.

Two further data points, and neither says as much as it is often made to say.

- **Genesis 31:53.** Laban's oath: *"The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their
  father, judge between us."* Jacob answers by swearing *"by the fear of his father Isaac"*, which is
  usually read as a pointed refusal to accept the equation. Whether the verse names one God under
  three descriptions or two family deities side by side is genuinely disputed, and it is the closest
  the Bible comes to showing what the branch that stayed in Mesopotamia worshipped.
- **The moon-god.** Both [[Ur of the Chaldees]] — on the southern identification — and
  [[Haran (city)]] were principal cult centres of **Sin/Nanna**. That the family lives in exactly the
  two great sanctuaries of one deity is striking, and it is an inference, not a text: Joshua's charge
  is generic, no biblical writer names Sin, and the northern-Ur proposal would change one half of the
  coincidence anyway. Later tradition was less restrained — the *Book of Jubilees* and *Genesis
  Rabbah* make Terah an idol-maker and give Abram the story of smashing his father's stock, which is
  a homily on Joshua 24:2 rather than an exegesis of Genesis.

## Significance

**The primeval history ends on a failed journey so that the patriarchal history can begin on a
successful one.** Terah sets out for Canaan under his own power, with the destination known, and gets
halfway. Abram is sent, without being told where, and arrives. The two paragraphs are next to each
other, and the difference between them is a voice.

**And the departure is the first act of the promise, not a preliminary to it.** The Bible keeps
naming Ur as the thing God brought this family out of — Genesis 15:7, Nehemiah 9:7, Joshua 24:3, Acts
7:2–4 — in the same grammatical form it will use of the exodus from Egypt: *I brought you out*.
Israel's founding memory has two extractions in it, and this is the older one.
