# By the Life of Pharaoh - Oaths Sworn by a King

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 42:15, 16** — Joseph swears twice — [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]]
- Genesis 24:2–9; 47:29–31 — the patriarchal oath, sworn with a hand under the thigh —
  [[Put Your Hand Under My Thigh]] and [[Joseph and the Famine]]. **[[Joseph]] swore by the king's
  life; his father makes him swear by a gesture that has nothing to do with Egypt at all**
- 1 Samuel 17:55; 20:3; 25:26; 2 Samuel 11:11; 15:21 — the formula in the Israelite monarchy
- Matthew 5:34–37 — *"don't swear at all"*

## Analysis

### The formula

An oath in the Hebrew Bible normally invokes the one who cannot lie and cannot die:

> ***ḥay-YHWH*** — ***"as Yahweh lives."***

**It is the standard form, used well over thirty times**, and its logic is that the oath's guarantor
outlasts the oath. **To swear by something is to place it as forfeit**, which is why Genesis 22:16 has
God swearing *"by myself"* — there being nothing above him to swear by. See
[[I Have Sworn by Myself - The Oath at Moriah]].

**Genesis 42:15–16 puts a king in that slot:**

> **15** By this you shall be tested. ***By the life of Pharaoh***, you shall not go out from here,
> unless your youngest brother comes here… **16** …or else ***by the life of Pharaoh*** surely you are
> spies.

***Ḥê parʿōh.*** **This is the only place in Scripture where anyone swears by the life of an Egyptian
king**, and Joseph does it twice in two verses.

### Why it is the chapter's most Egyptian sentence

**The oath is a costume.** Genesis 41 dressed Joseph in linen, a signet, a gold chain and a chariot,
gave him an Egyptian name and an Egyptian wife, and shaved his head. **Chapter 42 adds the last item:
an Egyptian court idiom, sworn in front of the ten men who would recognise a Hebrew oath instantly.**

**The contrast with the same man three chapters earlier is exact.** Standing before Pharaoh himself at
41:16, Joseph said ***"It isn't in me. God will give Pharaoh an answer of peace"*** — **he declined
credit and named God in the presence of the king.** Here, in the presence of his own family, **he names
the king and does not mention God until verse 18.** The one place he does mention God — *"for I fear
God"* (42:18) — is the sentence in which he softens the terms, **and the Hebrew reader is meant to hear
that the two formulas do not belong to the same man.**

**The oath also does what nothing else in the chapter can do: it makes the disguise audible.** The
brothers cannot see through a costume, and the interpreter hides his voice. **But a Hebrew who heard
*"as Yahweh lives"* would have known.** Joseph reaches for the one oath that guarantees they will not.

### Swearing by a king in Israel

**The idiom is not confined to Egypt — Israel picked it up, and Israelite usage always pairs it with
the divine form rather than replacing it.**

| text | formula | speaker |
|---|---|---|
| **1 Samuel 17:55** | *"as your soul lives, O king"* | Abner to Saul |
| **1 Samuel 20:3** | *"as Yahweh lives, and as your soul lives"* | David to Jonathan |
| **1 Samuel 25:26** | *"as Yahweh lives, and as your soul lives"* | Abigail to David |
| **2 Samuel 11:11** | *"as you live, and as your soul lives"* | **Uriah to David** |
| **2 Samuel 15:21** | *"as Yahweh lives, and as my lord the king lives"* | Ittai to David |

**The doubled form is the telling one.** *"As Yahweh lives, and as your soul lives"* keeps the king
inside the oath **without letting him displace God from it** — and Genesis 42 is the one occurrence
with the divine half removed entirely.

**Uriah's oath at 2 Samuel 11:11 is the sharpest of them.** He swears by David's life, to David, that
he will not go home to his wife — **and the man he is swearing to has already slept with her and is
arranging his death.** Like Joseph's, it is an oath made to a person whose real position the swearer
does not know, and **in both cases the reader knows and the swearer does not.** The Bible uses the
king-oath twice for dramatic irony and both instances are among its best-made scenes.

### What Joseph is actually swearing to

**Both oaths in Genesis 42 guarantee statements that are not true.**

- **42:15** — *"you shall not go out from here, unless your youngest brother comes here."* **Nine of
  them go out three verses later** (42:19), without Benjamin.
- **42:16** — *"or else by the life of Pharaoh surely you are spies."* **He knows they are not spies.**

**The most solemn form of speech available to him is used twice, in two verses, for two falsehoods** —
by a man who is at that moment concealing his identity from his own family, and who in the next chapter
will have a steward lie on his behalf about the money (43:23) and will plant a cup in a sack (44:2).
**And at 44:18 the king returns as a yardstick rather than an oath** — [[Judah]] opens the longest
speech in Genesis with *"for you are even as Pharaoh"*, ***kî kāmôkā kəparʿōh***, **naming the power
he is addressing instead of swearing by it.** See [[The Silver Cup and Judah's Plea]].
**Genesis records the whole of it and passes no judgment**, exactly as it passed none on the deceptions
of [[Jacob]], [[Rebekah]], [[Laban]] and [[Tamar]] before it. See [[Deception]] and [[Oaths and Vows]].

### Matthew 5 reads the practice out

> *"Don't swear at all: neither by heaven … nor by the earth … nor by Jerusalem … Let your 'Yes' be
> 'Yes' and your 'No' be 'No'."* (Matthew 5:34–37)

**The rabbinic distinction the Sermon on the Mount is dismantling is precisely the one Joseph is
exploiting** — that an oath's binding force depends on what is named in it. **Genesis 42 is the earliest
narrative instance of a man choosing his oath's guarantor for effect**, and Matthew 5's answer is to
abolish the choice.

## Significance

**The oath is the smallest and most complete piece of evidence for how thoroughly Joseph has become an
Egyptian, and for how deliberate the concealment is.**

Every other element of the disguise in Genesis 42 was given to him by someone else: **the office, the
name, the wife and the clothes are Pharaoh's doing in chapter 41.** The interpreter is a court
functionary. **The oath is the one item Joseph supplies himself**, and it is the one that would have
been unmistakable to a Hebrew ear. He chooses it.

**And it marks the distance the cycle still has to travel.** The man who swears by Pharaoh's life in
42:15 is the same man who will say *"I am Joseph, your brother"* in 45:4 and *"God meant it for good"*
in 50:20. **The chapter in which he sounds least like himself is the chapter in which his brothers
finally say what they did** — and he hears it, in Hebrew, through a translator he did not need, having
just sworn by a foreign king.
