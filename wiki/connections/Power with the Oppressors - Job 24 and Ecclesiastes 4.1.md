# Power with the Oppressors - Job 24 and Ecclesiastes 4.1

## Passages involved

- [[The King and the Limits of Power]] — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9.

- [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]] — Ecclesiastes 5:8–20.

- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 24:1–12.
- [[The Tears of the Oppressed]] — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3.
- [[From Dust to Dust]] — Ecclesiastes 3:16–17.

## Analysis

Job 24 moves from a question about God's times to displaced boundaries, seized animals,
the fatherless and widows exploited, naked workers, and hungry harvesters. Verse 12 leaves
the wounded crying in the city without the intervention Job seeks. Ecclesiastes 4:1 gives
no comparable catalogue of crimes. It compresses the situation into tears, absent comfort,
and power belonging to the oppressors.

The relationship is thematic: both speakers observe other people's suffering and resist a
simple inference that the world's present distribution of security reflects justice. Job's
speaker gives concrete cases; the Preacher evaluates being alive to see such evil and
prefers the dead and the never-existent (4:2–3). Job 24 does not end its opening observation
with that same ranking.

**Tension retained.** Ecclesiastes 3:17 affirms that God will judge, immediately before
4:1's uncomforted victims. Job 24:1 asks why those who know God do not see his days. Neither
passage provides a schedule that resolves the discrepancy. The comparison establishes
neither literary dependence nor a shared Hebrew expression.

Sources: immutable stored WEB Job 24:1–12; Ecclesiastes 3:16–17; 4:1–3.

### Officials above officials — Ecclesiastes 5:8–9

[[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]] returns to oppression, now explicitly of the
poor, and to justice and righteousness violently taken away. Official supervision is
layered: one official is eyed by a higher one, with further officials above them (8).
The WEB does not say that this arrangement has restored justice. It can be read as
part of the explanation of entrenched oppression; an assurance of effective redress
would go beyond what is narrated. Verse 9 connects even the king to the field's profit.
No dynasty or administrative period is identified. As in Job 24 and Ecclesiastes 4:1,
recognizing a system does not justify the harm it contains. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 3:16–17; 4:1–3; 5:8–9; Job 24:1–12.

### Dominion that harms — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

Ecclesiastes 8:9 returns to observed power: one person rules another to his hurt. The WEB
pronoun does not expressly identify whether the hurt falls on the ruled person or returns
to the ruler. Harm to the subject fits the victims of 4:1, but the ambiguity should not be
converted into a promise of the ruler's downfall. The observation now follows counsel to
keep the king's command (8:2) and an affirmation of safety (8:5). **The tension is explicit:**
practical counsel before power does not erase its capacity to harm. No victim's guilt is
specified and no rescue is narrated. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:1; 5:8–9; 8:1–9.

See [[The King and the Limits of Power]].

## Significance

[[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] are subjects whose injuries must be heard, not evidence
from which their guilt can be inferred. [[Suffering]] here has human perpetrators, while
[[Judgment]] remains a question or affirmation whose fulfillment is not shown in these
observations. See also [[Retribution]] and [[Compassion and Mercy]].
