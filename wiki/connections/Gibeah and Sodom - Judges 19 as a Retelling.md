# Gibeah and Sodom - Judges 19 as a Retelling

## Passages involved

- Genesis 19:1–11 — two visitors, a host at the gate, a town at the door, a daughter offered
- Judges 19:11–30 — the Levite at Gibeah: the same scene, in Israel, with no angels in it
- Judges 20–21 — the war it causes, and the near-extinction of Benjamin
- Hosea 9:9; 10:9 — *"the days of Gibeah"*, the prophets' shorthand for it

## Analysis

**Judges 19 is Genesis 19 told again, and the correspondence is not a matter of theme. It is a
matter of wording.** The wiki records it as one of the most sustained deliberate re-uses of a
Genesis narrative anywhere in the Old Testament.

| | Genesis 19 | Judges 19 |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival | *"came to Sodom at evening"* (19:1) | the sun goes down as they reach Gibeah |
| The host | Lot, a *gēr* in the town, sitting in the gate | an old man from the Ephraim hills, himself a sojourner in Gibeah, coming in from the field |
| Nobody else | — | *"no man took them into his house to lodge"* |
| The offer | *"come into your servant's house, stay all night"* (19:2) | *"only don't sleep in the street"* |
| The refusal | *"No, but we will stay in the street all night"* (19:2) | they had already sat down in the square |
| The mob | *"the men of the city, the men of Sodom, surrounded the house"* (19:4) | *"certain wicked fellows … surrounded the house, beating at the door"* |
| The demand | *"Bring them out to us, that we may have sex with them"* (19:5) | *"Bring out the man who came into your house, that we may have sex with him"* |
| The host's plea | *"Please, my brothers, don't act so wickedly"* (19:7) | *"No, my brothers, please don't act so wickedly"* |
| The substitute | *"I have two virgin daughters … do to them what seems good to you"* (19:8) | *"here is my virgin daughter and his concubine … do with them what seems good to you"* |
| What happens | angels pull the host inside and blind the crowd | **nothing intervenes** |

**The last row is the connection's whole point.** Genesis 19 is a scene of appalling danger that is
interrupted: the offer of the daughters is refused by the mob, the guests turn out to be able to
defend themselves, the blindness falls, and nobody in the house is harmed. Judges 19 runs the same
scene with the supernatural removed, and it goes to the end. The concubine is put outside, abused
through the night, and found at dawn with her hands on the threshold. Then she is cut into twelve
pieces and sent through Israel, and the tribes destroy Benjamin.

### What the retelling is for

Judges is arguing, and it is arguing with Genesis's own vocabulary. **Its refrain is *"In those days
there was no king in Israel; every man did that which was right in his own eyes"*** (17:6; 18:1;
19:1; 21:25), and it frames the Gibeah story with two of the four occurrences. The claim the
narrative makes by its wording is blunt: **a town in Benjamin behaved exactly as Sodom did**, and
the reader who knows Genesis 19 is expected to hear it in the third sentence, long before the outcome.

Two details sharpen it further.

- **The host in both stories is an outsider.** Lot is a resident alien the town throws the charge at
  (*"This one fellow came in to live as a foreigner"*, 19:9); the old man of Gibeah is from Ephraim
  and *"lived in Gibeah"* as a sojourner among Benjaminites. In both, the only person who keeps the
  guest obligation is the one who is not from there.
- **Judges 19 has no [[Abraham]].** Genesis 19's structure is a mob, a rescue, and an intercessor
  who was arguing for the place the day before. Judges supplies the mob and nothing else, and the
  consequence is not fire from heaven but a civil war that Israel conducts on itself.

### Where it goes afterwards

Hosea reaches for it twice — *"They have deeply corrupted themselves, as in the days of Gibeah"*
(9:9), *"You have sinned from the days of Gibeah"* (10:9) — using Gibeah the way Isaiah and Jeremiah
use Sodom, as the fixed name for a moral floor. The wiki notes the further irony that the tradition
does not press: **Gibeah is Saul's town** (1 Samuel 10:26; 11:4), and the first king of the Israel
that had no king comes from the place Judges chose to make its case about not having one.

## Significance

For reading Genesis 19, the Judges parallel does two things.

**It settles that the scene was understood in Israel as a hospitality atrocity and a sexual one at
once**, because Judges reproduces both elements and cannot separate them: the crime is committed
against a guest, and it is committed by rape. That is one datum in the disputed question of what
Sodom's sin was, which [[Sodom]] sets out in full; it does not settle the question, but it shows how
an Israelite writer close to the tradition read the chapter.

**And it makes visible how much of Genesis 19 is rescue.** Read alone, Genesis 19 is a chapter about
a city destroyed. Read against Judges 19, it is also a chapter in which the guests could defend
themselves and the daughters were not taken — and the difference between the two nights is entirely
the presence of the two visitors. See [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] and
[[Lot's Daughters]].
