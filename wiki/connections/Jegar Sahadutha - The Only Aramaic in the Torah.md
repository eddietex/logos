# Jegar Sahadutha - The Only Aramaic in the Torah

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 31:47** — *"Laban called it Jegar Sahadutha, but Jacob called it Galeed"* —
  [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]]
- **Genesis 31:20, 24** — *Laban the Syrian*, ***hāʾărammî***, twice in one chapter
- **Deuteronomy 26:5** — *"A Syrian ready to perish was my father"*
- **2 Kings 18:26** — the officials at the wall: *"speak to your servants in the Syrian language, for
  we understand it; and don't speak to us in the Jews' language"*
- **Ezra 4:8–6:18; 7:12–26; Jeremiah 10:11; Daniel 2:4–7:28** — the Old Testament's other Aramaic

## Analysis

**Two words, and they are the only Aramaic in the Pentateuch.**

***Yəgar śāhădûtāʾ*** and ***galʿēd*** mean the same thing — ***heap of witness*** — and Genesis
prints both without translating either. **The verse is doing something no other verse in the Torah
does: it lets a character speak a language that is not Hebrew, on the page, and expects the reader to
notice that the other man answered in a different one.**

**The book has been preparing this for four chapters.** Laban is *the Aramean*, ***hāʾărammî***, at
25:20, 28:5, 31:20 and 31:24 — **a label Genesis attaches to nobody else** — and the reader has been
told twice in this chapter alone. **At 31:47 the label finally becomes audible.** See [[Laban]] and
[[The Nations]].

**What the naming scene establishes is a boundary of three kinds at once**, all in the same handful
of verses:

- **a physical boundary** — *"I will not pass over this heap to you, and… you will not pass over this
  heap and this pillar to me, for harm"* (31:52);
- **a religious boundary** — *"the God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor"* offered as a joint formula,
  and Jacob swearing instead by ***the fear of his father Isaac*** (31:53);
- **and a linguistic boundary**, in the two names for one pile of rocks.

**Terah's family left Ur together and split at Haran** (11:31–32). **Four generations later the two
halves need a translator.** Genesis makes the point by doing nothing at all — no comment, no gloss,
no *which being interpreted is* — **and lets the reader hear it.** See [[Language and Speech]] and
[[Aram Naharaim]].

**The historical question the verse raises is not one the wiki settles.** Aramaic spread across the
Near East late — it is the diplomatic language by the eighth century (2 Kings 18:26) and the imperial
one under Persia, which is when the Bible's other Aramaic is written. **Whether a second-millennium
Laban would have spoken it is exactly the sort of question the text does not answer**, and the
standing readings (an authentic old tradition; a narrator supplying the language his own audience
associates with Arameans; a gloss) **are all arguments from outside the verse.** The wiki records the
question and takes no position.

## Significance

**The Old Testament contains Aramaic in four places and three of them are exilic or post-exilic** —
Daniel's court narratives and visions, Ezra's Persian correspondence, and one defiant sentence
Jeremiah tells the exiles to say to idolaters in their own language (Jeremiah 10:11). **They are all
the language of empire, addressed outward.**

**Genesis 31:47 is the exception, and it is domestic.** Two words, in a family argument, about a pile
of stones. **The Torah's one non-Hebrew utterance is a man naming a boundary marker between himself
and his son-in-law.**

**It also gives the wiki its clearest instance of a technique Genesis uses everywhere**: the book
makes its point by withholding the comment. It does not say *the family had grown apart*; it prints
two names. It does not say *Laban's religion was not Abraham's*; it prints a plural verb (31:53). **It
does not say the two branches were now separate peoples — it lets Deuteronomy 26:5 say it, in one
line, to be recited over a basket of fruit**: *"A Syrian ready to perish was my father."*
