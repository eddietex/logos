# Women Among the Flocks - Rachel, Reuel's Daughters, and Song of Solomon 1.7-8

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 29:6, 9** — [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]]: *"See, Rachel, his daughter, is coming with
  the sheep"*; *"Rachel came with her father's sheep, for she kept them."*
- **Exodus 2:16–17** — [[Moses Flees to Midian]]: *"Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters.
  They came and drew water, and filled the troughs to water their father's flock. The shepherds
  came and drove them away; but Moses stood up and helped them, and watered their flock."*
- **Song of Solomon 1:7–8** — [[Dark and Lovely]]: *"Tell me, you whom my soul loves, where you
  graze your flock, where you rest them at noon; For why should I be as one who is veiled beside
  the flocks of your companions?"* and *"Graze your young goats beside the shepherds' tents."*
- Compare [[The Betrothal at the Well]], where the scenes in Genesis 29 and Exodus 2 belong to a
  larger type-scene.

## Analysis

**Three passages show a woman, or women, working with flocks among male shepherds.** In
each, the meeting with men at the pasture carries both possibility and risk.

| | Genesis 29 | Exodus 2 | Song 1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| the woman's work | Rachel *kept* her father's sheep | seven daughters water their father's flock | she grazes *young goats* |
| other men at the flocks | shepherds waiting at the covered well | shepherds who *drove them away* | the beloved's *companions* and the *shepherds' tents* |
| the time | *the middle of the day* (29:7) | not given | *at noon* (1:7) |
| what the woman seeks | nothing; she is met | water, and is hindered | the beloved himself |

**The time of day is worth noting but not overreading.** Jacob objects that it is *"still the
middle of the day"* (Genesis 29:7). In the Song the woman asks where the beloved rests his flock
*"at noon"* (1:7). The two texts share the pastoral routine of a midday halt. That is a shared
setting, not evidence that one text alludes to the other.

**What differs is who moves.** In Genesis 29 and Exodus 2, a man arrives from far away and acts
for the woman at the well: Jacob rolls away the stone, and Moses drives off the shepherds. In the
Song nobody arrives. The woman asks the question herself, and she does not want to wait among
other men's flocks *"as one who is veiled"*. The meaning of that line is debated; see
[[Dark and Lovely]] and [[The Attire of a Prostitute - Genesis 38 and Proverbs 7.10]]. Whichever
reading is right, she wants to avoid a compromised position among the companions. That is
the hazard Exodus 2 shows in action, where the shepherds drive the women off.

**The reply sends her back to the flocks.** *"Follow the tracks of the sheep. Graze your young
goats beside the shepherds' tents"* (1:8). It is not a rescue. It locates her among the
flocks in her own right, with her own young goats. In Genesis 29 and Exodus 2, by contrast,
the scene at the flocks is the start of a betrothal.

## Significance

Shepherding in these texts is ordinary family work, and women do it. Rachel and Reuel's daughters
work for their fathers. The woman of the Song has been set to work by her brothers (1:6) and grazes
her own young goats. The pasture is where lovers can meet, as in Genesis 29 and Exodus 2. It is also
where a woman can be harassed, as in Exodus 2, and where she can be misjudged, if the veiled line
means that. The Song keeps the setting but gives the woman the initiative that the earlier scenes
give the arriving man. See [[Work]], [[Love]], and [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]].
