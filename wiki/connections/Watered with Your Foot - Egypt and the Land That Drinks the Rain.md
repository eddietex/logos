# Watered with Your Foot - Egypt and the Land That Drinks the Rain

- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — Genesis 13:10.
- [[The Rebellion of Korah]] — Numbers 16:12–14.
- **Leviticus 26:4, 19** — rain in season, and a sky like iron.
- **Deuteronomy 28:12, 23–24** — verified in the stored WEB; 28:12 now ingested, 23–24 still ahead.

## Analysis

Deuteronomy 11:10–11 sets two ways of watering land side by side. In Egypt "you sowed your
seed, and watered it with your foot, as a garden of herbs". The land ahead "drinks water from
the rain of the sky". The text does not explain "with your foot". It is often read as
foot-worked irrigation, but that is an inference, not something the verse says. The contrast
is about dependence, not richness: Yahweh's eyes are on the rain-fed land all year (12), and
the rain arrives or fails according to Israel's loyalty (13–17).

Egypt has been the standard of fertility before. Lot sees the plain of the Jordan as
"well-watered everywhere … like the garden of Yahweh, like the land of Egypt"
(Genesis 13:10), and chooses it. Dathan and Abiram go further. They call Egypt itself "a land
flowing with milk and honey" and accuse Moses of failing to bring them into one
(Numbers 16:13–14). Deuteronomy 11 names those same two men (6). Three verses later it gives
"milk and honey" back to the promised land (9) and says plainly that this land "isn’t like the
land of Egypt" (10). The passage does not say it is answering Numbers 16. The pairing is the
wiki's observation, drawn from the shared names and phrase.

The conditional rain has a history in the stored text. Leviticus 26:4 promises "your rains in
their season" for obedience, and 26:19 threatens a sky "like iron". Deuteronomy 11:14, 17 gives
the same pair as the early and late rain and a shut sky. Deuteronomy 28:12 and 23–24 expand
both sides: Yahweh's "good treasure in the sky" for blessing, and a sky of brass and rain of
"powder and dust" for curse. Its blessing section (28:1–14) is now ingested; the curses remain ahead. Later narratives of
withheld rain, such as Elijah's drought in 1 Kings 17–18, are named here only by reference.
They are not quoted, because those books are not yet stored.

### Blessings now ingested — Deuteronomy 28:1–14

Verse 12 now brings the earlier rain promise into the blessing list: Yahweh opens his good treasure in the sky, gives seasonal rain, and blesses work. Deuteronomy 28:23–24 remains the un-ingested curse counterpart; its text is available in the stored WEB.

Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 28:1–14 and the comparison verses cited above.

## Significance

The land's goodness is defined by its exposure to the sky, and so to its giver. A land that
cannot be watered by human effort makes obedience a matter of the harvest. It also overturns
the rebels' nostalgia for Egypt.
