# Cain and Abel in the New Testament

## Passages involved

- [[Cain and Abel]] — Genesis 4:1–16
- Matthew 23:35 · Luke 11:51 · Hebrews 11:4 · Hebrews 12:24 · 1 John 3:12 · Jude 11 *(not yet
  ingested)*

## Analysis

Genesis 4 says remarkably little about either brother. Abel gets no words and four facts; Cain
gets a warning, a murder, a sentence, and a sign. The New Testament refers back to them five
times, and every reference adds something the passage does not state — which makes this a good
place to see how the New Testament reads Genesis, and how much of what "the story of Cain and
Abel means" was supplied later.

**Abel the righteous — Matthew 23:35 / Luke 11:51.** Jesus, pronouncing woes on the scribes and
Pharisees, dates the whole record of martyrdom *"from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of
Zachariah son of Barachiah, whom you killed between the sanctuary and the altar"*. The pairing is
a canonical bracket: Abel dies in Genesis, the first book of the Hebrew Bible, and the Zechariah
of 2 Chronicles 24:20–22 dies in the last book of the Jewish ordering — *"from Abel to
Zechariah"* is the Hebrew canon's equivalent of "from beginning to end". Genesis never calls Abel
righteous; this is the verse that does, and it does it in passing, as something assumed.

**Abel the first believer — Hebrews 11:4.** *"By faith Abel offered to God a more excellent
sacrifice than Cain, through which he had testimony given to him that he was righteous, God
testifying with respect to his gifts; and through it he, being dead, still speaks."* This is the
opening entry in the roll of faith, and it supplies the reason Genesis withholds: the difference
between the two offerings was faith, and the *testimony* that Abel was righteous is God's regard
in 4:4 read as a verdict on the man. Note what Hebrews does with 4:10 in its last clause — the
blood that cried out becomes a man who *still speaks*, and what he speaks is not now an
accusation but a witness.

**Abel's blood outspoken — Hebrews 12:24.** The letter's climax lists what believers have come to,
ending: *"to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling that speaks
better than that of Abel."* The comparison assumes the reader hears 4:10 — blood with a voice,
addressed to God, from the ground. Abel's blood asks for justice on the one who shed it; the blood
of the new covenant asks for something else, and Hebrews says only that it speaks **better**. The
verse is the hinge of the classical typology: the first innocent blood in Scripture and the last,
compared by what they say rather than by what they cost.

**Cain the archetype — 1 John 3:12.** *"Unlike Cain, who was of the evil one and killed his
brother. Why did he kill him? Because his works were evil, and his brother's righteous."* John is
arguing that love of the brothers is the mark of life, and he takes Cain as the negative
definition of it — the first man to have a brother and the first to fail him. Two additions to
Genesis are visible: the causal explanation of the murder (the difference between the two men's
works, which Genesis leaves as the difference between two offerings), and *"of the evil one"*,
which reads Cain's line back to the serpent Genesis 3 never identified with anyone. Compare the
same move on [[The Fall]].

**Cain the apostate — Jude 11.** *"Woe to them! For they went in the way of Cain, and ran riotously
in the error of Balaam for hire, and perished in Kadesh's rebellion."* Cain heads a triad of
figures who knew better and went anyway — which is a fair use of 4:6–7, the one thing in the
passage that makes Cain culpable beyond the act: he was warned, plainly, and told the thing could
be ruled.

**What the New Testament does not use.** None of the five references mentions the mark, the sign,
the sevenfold vengeance, or God's protection of Cain — the passage's strangest and most merciful
material. The Genesis Cain is warned before he acts and guarded after it; the New Testament Cain
is a type of the wicked. Both are readings of the same sixteen verses, and the wiki keeps the
Genesis portrait in view on [[Cain]] rather than letting the archetype replace it.

## Significance

This is the clearest early case of a pattern that will recur for the whole canon: the New
Testament supplies motive, verdict, and category for Genesis narratives that offer events without
them. Abel is *righteous* and offered *by faith*; Cain is *of the evil one*. Neither claim is in
Genesis 4, and neither contradicts it — Genesis simply declines to say, and Hebrews, John, and
Jesus say. Whether that is drawing out what the passage implies or reading a later theology back
into it is the same open question the wiki keeps flagged at Genesis 3:15 and at the identity of
the serpent, and it is worth answering the same way: record both, and mark which is which.

The other thing worth carrying forward is Hebrews 12:24's comparison, because it makes the
Bible's first murder the first term in a series about **what blood says**. Genesis 4:10 (blood
crying from the ground), Genesis 9:5–6 (blood required), Leviticus 17:11 (life in the blood),
Deuteronomy 21:1–9 (a land polluted by blood), and Hebrews 12:24 are one strand. It now has a
page of its own — [[Blood]] — and Genesis 9's two rulings on it are set out at
[[The Covenant with Noah]] and traced at [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]] and
[[The Blood Is the Life]].

That strand now has a middle term. Job 16:18 — *"Earth, don't cover my blood. Let my cry have
no place to rest"* — takes Genesis 4's conception and turns it into a petition, from a man not yet
dead who expects to die uncleared. See [[Do Not Cover My Blood]] and
[[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]].
