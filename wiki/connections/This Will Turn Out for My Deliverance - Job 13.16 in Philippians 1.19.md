# This Will Turn Out for My Deliverance - Job 13.16 in Philippians 1.19

## Passages involved

- **Job 13:16** — *"This also shall be my salvation, that a godless man shall not come before
  him."* Septuagint: *kai touto moi apobēsetai eis sōtērian, ou gar enantion autou dolos
  eiseleusetai* — *and this will turn out for me for salvation, for deceit shall not come in before
  him.* — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Philippians 1:19–20** — *"For I know that this will turn out to my salvation (touto moi
  apobēsetai eis sōtērian), through your supplication and the supply of the Spirit of Jesus Christ,
  according to my earnest expectation and hope, that I will in no way be disappointed, but with all
  boldness, as always, now also Christ will be magnified in my body, whether by life, or by death."*
- **Job 13:15, 18** — *"I will maintain my ways before him"*; *"I know that I am righteous."*
- **Job 19:25** — *"But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives."* —
  [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Genesis 49:18** — *"I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh"*: *yəšûʿâ*, the noun of Job 13:16.
  See [[I Have Waited for Your Salvation - The First Yeshuah in Scripture]]

## Analysis

**Five Greek words, in the same order.** *Touto moi apobēsetai eis sōtērian* stands in the
Septuagint of Job 13:16 and in Philippians 1:19, word for word. Paul gives no
quotation formula, and the phrase is short enough that it is sometimes treated as a coincidence; but
the verb *apobainō*, *to turn out*, is rare in Paul (only here and Luke 21:13 in the New Testament,
of a matter *turning out* as testimony), and the conjunction of all five words is not a commonplace.
Most commentators on Philippians accept that Paul is echoing Job.

**The situations match closely.** Job is a righteous man accused, who has resolved to state his case
before God even if it kills him (*"Behold, he will kill me"*, 13:15), and who finds in that
resolution a ground of hope: a godless man would not dare come before God, so his readiness to do so
will be his *salvation* — his vindication in the trial. Paul is in prison awaiting a verdict that may
be death (*"whether by life, or by death"*, 1:20), surrounded by rival preachers who mean to
*"add affliction to my chains"* (1:17), and he says that *this* — the whole situation — will turn
out for his *sōtēria*. In both cases the word means **vindication before the court that matters**,
not simply release, because both men are explicit that they may die.

**And both connect it to not being put to shame.** Paul continues: *"according to my earnest
expectation and hope, that I will in no way be disappointed (aischynthēsomai, put to shame)"*
(1:20). Job's speech had been an extended answer to the friends' insistence that his suffering
proved him shameful, and its next verses are *"I have set my cause in order. I know that I am
righteous"* (13:18). Paul borrows the voice of the innocent sufferer appealing past his accusers to
God, and applies it to himself — with the difference that where Job's speech ended in *"so you
destroy the hope of man"* (14:19), Paul's *"hope"* (*elpis*, 1:20) is attached to Christ being
magnified *"whether by life, or by death."*

## Significance

- One of the very few places the New Testament quotes the speeches of Job's friends or of Job; the
  other clear one is 1 Corinthians 3:19's quotation of Eliphaz (Job 5:13), and Romans 11:35 quotes
  God's speech (41:11). See
  [[He Takes the Wise in Their Craftiness - Job 5.13 in 1 Corinthians 3.19]]. **Paul quotes the
  friends once and Job once, and he quotes the friend for a true saying about God and Job for his own
  situation.**
- Paul's echo reads Job 13:16 as a statement of confidence, which supports the sense of the verse in
  the Septuagint and the Masoretic text alike, whichever reading of 13:15 one takes. See
  [[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]].
