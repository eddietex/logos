# Passing Between the Pieces

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 15:9–18** — five animals cut in half and laid opposite each other; *"a smoking furnace,
  and a flaming torch passed between these pieces"*, and *"In that day Yahweh made a covenant with
  Abram."* See [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].
- **Jeremiah 34:8–22** — the only passage in Scripture that explains the ceremony: Judah's princes
  *"cut the calf in two and passed between its parts"*, break the covenant they cut, and are told
  what the rite means.
- **Genesis 6:18; 9:8–17** — the Noahic covenant, *established* (*hēqîm*) rather than *cut*, with no
  ceremony and no ritual at all. See [[The Covenant with Noah]].
- **Genesis 14:13** — *bərît*'s first appearance in the Abraham cycle, as a treaty between human
  beings. See [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].
- **Genesis 17:1–14** — the same covenant re-given with a sign in flesh and, for the first time, a
  demand on the human party.
- **Exodus 24:3–8** — Sinai sealed with blood thrown on an altar and on a people who have first said
  *"All that Yahweh has spoken we will do."*
- **Hebrews 6:13–18; 9:15–22** — God swearing by himself, and *diathēkē* as a will that requires a
  death.

## Analysis

Hebrew does not *make* a covenant. It **cuts** one — *kārat bərît* — and Genesis 15:18 is the idiom's
first occurrence in the Bible, in the one chapter that shows the reader why the verb is *cut*.

### What the ceremony means

Jeremiah 34 is the key, and it is the only place in Scripture that spells the rite out. Zedekiah's
Judah had covenanted to free its Hebrew slaves and then re-enslaved them; God's sentence names the
ceremony they used:

> *"I will give the men who have transgressed my covenant, who have not performed the words of the
> covenant which they made before me, when they cut the calf in two and passed between its parts …
> I will even give them into the hand of their enemies … and their dead bodies shall be food for the
> birds of the sky."*

The logic is exact. The parties walk the corridor between the halves and thereby say: *may I be made
like this animal if I break my word.* The rite is a **self-curse**, enacted rather than spoken, and
Jeremiah's judgment is simply the curse being executed — down to the birds, which is a detail Genesis
15:11 has already put on the ground.

The same idiom appears in second-millennium Near Eastern treaty texts outside the Bible, where a
vassal is made to watch an animal dismembered and told *this is not the head of a ram, this is your
head*. Genesis 15 is not inventing a symbol; it is using the region's standard instrument for a
binding oath.

### Who walks

And then it does the one thing no treaty in the ancient world does.

*"When the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram"* (15:12). *"When the sun went down, and it
was dark, behold, a smoking furnace, and a flaming torch passed between these pieces"* (15:17).

**Abram is unconscious for his own covenant.** He gathers the animals, cuts them, and drives the
vultures off — and then God puts him under and walks the corridor alone. The party who invokes the
self-curse is the party who cannot be made like a divided animal, and the party who could be is
asleep. Genesis stages a bilateral rite and performs it unilaterally.

| | The rite as Jeremiah 34 describes it | Genesis 15 |
|---|---|---|
| Who cuts the animals | the covenanting parties | Abram |
| Who passes between | both parties | God only |
| Who is bound | both | God |
| What the human party undertakes | stated obligations | nothing |
| Sanction for breach | *"their dead bodies … food for the birds"* | unstated, and unstateable |

### Where it sits in the sequence

Genesis has three covenant scenes before this one and none of them is cut. Noah's is *established*
(*hēqîm*, 6:18; 9:9) with no ceremony and a sign God keeps for himself; the Abram–Amorite alliance at
14:13 is a human treaty named by the noun alone. Genesis 15 is the first time the standard idiom, the
standard rite, and God as a party all arrive together — and the first thing the arrangement does with
its standard form is break it, by sending one walker.

Genesis 17 then adds what 15 conspicuously lacks: a sign borne by the human party, and a stipulation
(*"Walk before me, and be blameless"*). Exodus 24 adds the rest — obligations read aloud, a people
agreeing to them, and blood applied to both altar and congregation. The Abrahamic covenant acquires
conditions over the following chapters; it does not begin with any.

### What later readers built on it

**Paul.** Galatians 3:15–18: a *diathēkē* ratified beforehand cannot be annulled or added to by a law
that arrives four hundred thirty years later. The argument depends on the covenant of Genesis 15
having no human party bound to anything, which is exactly what 15:12 and 15:17 report.

**Hebrews.** 6:13–18 makes the point about the oath — *"when God made a promise to Abraham, since he
could swear by no one greater, he swore by himself"* — and calls it *"two immutable things"*, the
promise and the oath, given so that heirs might have strong encouragement. 9:16–17 then plays on
*diathēkē* meaning both *covenant* and *will*: a will takes effect only at a death. Whether the
epistle has Genesis 15's animals in view there is disputed; what is not disputed is that the whole
letter reads God's unilateral oath to Abraham as the pattern.

**Christian typology** has often read the smoking furnace and flaming torch — two objects, one
corridor — as God taking on himself the curse the human party deserves, and pointed to the cross.
This is a legitimate development of the passage's own logic and is not what Genesis 15 says; the
wiki records it as reception rather than as exegesis. What Genesis says is that God swore, alone, on
a sanction he named for nobody.

## Significance

- **It explains an idiom that appears three hundred times in the Old Testament.** After Genesis 15 a
  reader knows why *kārat bərît* is *cut a covenant* and what the cutting was for.
- **It is the strongest textual ground for calling the Abrahamic covenant unconditional.** Not an
  inference from theology but a report of who was awake.
- **And it puts the sanction in an impossible place.** A self-curse only means something if the
  swearer can suffer it. Genesis 15:17 has God accept a form of oath whose penalty cannot apply to
  him, which is either a piece of accommodated language or the most serious thing in the chapter, and
  the New Testament writers who use the passage all take the second view.
