# Two Creation Accounts — Genesis 1 and Genesis 2

## Passages involved

- [[The Creation of the World]] — Genesis 1:1–2:3
- [[The Garden of Eden]] — Genesis 2:4–25

## Analysis

Genesis opens twice. The second account is not a continuation of the first — it restarts from a
pre-creation state (2:5, no plants, no rain, no man) and tells the making of humanity again, in a
different order, a different idiom, and with a different name for God.

| | Genesis 1:1–2:3 | Genesis 2:4–25 |
|---|---|---|
| Divine name | *Elohim*, ~35× | *YHWH Elohim*, ~11× here, ~20× through ch. 3 |
| Problem to solve | formless, empty, dark, **watery** | dry — no rain, no one to till |
| Order of making | vegetation (day 3) → animals (days 5–6) → humanity (day 6) | man → garden and trees → animals → woman |
| Humanity | "male and female", created together, last of all | the man first and alone, the woman last, in two separate acts |
| Verb of creation | *bārāʾ* (create), *ʾāmar* (say) | *yāṣar* (form, as a potter), *nāṭaʿ* (plant), *bānâ* (build) |
| God portrayed as | sovereign speaking from outside | potter, gardener, surgeon, matchmaker, walking in the garden (3:8) |
| Register | measured, repetitive, liturgical | narrative, earthy, full of wordplay |
| Scale | the cosmos, six days | one garden, one man, one day |
| Ends with | rest, blessing, holiness | a marriage, and nakedness without shame |

### The three main ways of reading this

**1. Two sources joined.** The classic documentary analysis assigns chapter 1 to the Priestly
writer (the *Elohim* name, the ordered structure, the interest in blessing, holiness, and the
sabbath) and 2:4–25 to the Yahwist (the *YHWH* name, anthropomorphic God, folk etymologies,
earthy narrative). The seam is at 2:4, where the *toledot* formula sits and where the divine name
changes mid-verse. This explains the differences well and says nothing about whether they can be
read as one thing.

**2. Two perspectives, complementary.** A wide shot followed by a close-up: chapter 1 answers
*what* was made and in what relation, chapter 2 answers *what it was for* — a specific man, a
specific place, a specific vocation and relationship. On this reading 2:19's *"Out of the ground
Yahweh God formed every animal"* is not a claim that the animals came after the man in time but a
recapitulation ("had formed"), which is how some translations render it. The reading is
harmonizing but not violent; the two accounts are not doing the same job.

**3. Deliberate juxtaposition.** Whoever assembled Genesis had both texts and put them adjacent
without smoothing them, and the pairing does work that neither could alone: humanity as the
crown of an ordered cosmos *and* as dust with breath in it; God as transcendent speaker *and* as
the one with his hands in the mud; a mandate to subdue the earth *and* a mandate to keep a garden.
The tension is a feature of the canonical text, and the same editorial willingness to hold two
versions side by side recurs across the Bible (two flood strands, two accounts of Saul's rise,
four Gospels).

### What both agree on

Whatever their origin, the two accounts do not disagree about anything load-bearing: one God,
with no rival and no struggle; a creation that is his and is good; humanity as the point at which
the account slows down and becomes personal; male and female both from God's hand; and a
relationship between humanity and the ground that is definitional. The divergences are of
sequence and idiom, not of doctrine.

## Significance

This is the first place a reader of the Bible has to decide what kind of book they are holding —
whether apparent seams are problems to solve, evidence to analyse, or a compositional method to
learn to read. The decision made here tends to be made again at every later doublet.

It also matters for how [[Adam]] is read. Chapter 1's humanity is a category (male and female,
plural, "let *them* have dominion"); chapter 2's is an individual with a job, a command, and a
wife. Paul's use of Adam as a corporate figure (Romans 5, 1 Corinthians 15) depends on both being
true at once.
