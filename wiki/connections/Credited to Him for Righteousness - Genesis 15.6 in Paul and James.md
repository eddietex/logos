# Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 15:6** — *"He believed in Yahweh, who credited it to him for righteousness."* See
  [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].
- **Psalm 106:30–31** — of Phinehas: *"Then Phinehas stood up and executed judgment, so the plague
  was stopped. That was credited to him for righteousness, for all generations forever more."* The
  same formula, for a deed.
- **Nehemiah 9:7–8** — *"you found his heart faithful before you, and made a covenant with him"*: the
  post-exilic prayer's own reading of Genesis 15, which grounds the covenant in Abraham's
  faithfulness.
- **1 Maccabees 2:52** — *"Was not Abraham found faithful in temptation, and it was reckoned to him
  for righteousness?"* Second-century BC, and it reads 15:6 through Genesis 22.
- **Romans 4:3, 9, 22–24** — 15:6 quoted three times, with the chronology of Genesis 17 as the
  argument.
- **Galatians 3:6–9** — 15:6 quoted, and *"those who are of faith are children of Abraham."*
- **James 2:21–24** — 15:6 quoted, and *"you see that a man is justified by works, and not only by
  faith."*
- **Hebrews 11:8–12, 17–19** — Abraham's faith narrated across the whole cycle, with the binding as
  its climax and no use of the crediting formula at all.

## Analysis

Nine Hebrew words in Genesis 15:6 — *wə-heʾĕmin ba-YHWH wa-yaḥšəbehā llô ṣədāqâ* — carry more
theological weight than any other sentence in the Torah, and the reason they can carry so much is
that they are underdetermined. The verse says a man trusted, and that the trust was entered in a
ledger as righteousness. It does not say what righteousness is, whether trust is a work, whether the
crediting is a legal fiction or a real assessment, or what happens to a man who stops trusting. Every
later reader supplies the missing terms, and they supply different ones.

### The Old Testament is already arguing with itself

The identical formula — *wattēḥāšeb lô li-ṣədāqâ* — occurs once more in the Hebrew Bible, at Psalm
106:31, and what is credited there is a spear. Phinehas sees an Israelite man bring a Midianite woman
into the camp, runs both of them through, and stops a plague (Numbers 25:6–13); the psalm reckons
that to him as righteousness *"for all generations forever more"*, and Numbers itself gives him a
covenant of perpetual priesthood for it.

So the Hebrew Bible contains both readings. One man is credited for believing a promise about stars;
another is credited for an act of lethal zeal. Nothing in the vocabulary distinguishes them. The
argument the New Testament conducts is not an argument the New Testament started.

### What the Septuagint changed

The Greek renders 15:6 as *episteusen de Abram tō theō, kai elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn* — and
turns the active *he credited it to him* into the **passive** *it was reckoned to him*. This settles
in Greek an ambiguity the Hebrew leaves open (the Hebrew has no explicit subjects, so *he credited*
could in principle be Abram crediting God), and it hands Paul a verb with no agent named, which is
exactly the form a doctrine of imputation needs. Every New Testament quotation of the verse uses the
Septuagint's passive. *Logizomai*, like *ḥāšab*, is a bookkeeping word — Paul uses it eleven times in
Romans 4 alone, and at 4:4 he presses the metaphor to its edge: *"Now to him who works, the reward is
not counted as grace, but as something owed."*

### Paul's argument is about a sequence, not a definition

Romans 4 does not primarily argue about what *faith* means. It argues about **when** Genesis 15:6
happens.

| Paul's move | What in Genesis it uses |
|---|---|
| Abraham was justified by faith, not works | 15:6 stands before any command in the cycle |
| Therefore he cannot boast | he had done nothing in the chapter but complain and believe |
| He was credited **before** circumcision | Genesis 17 is two chapters later — Romans 4:9–12 |
| Therefore he is father of the uncircumcised too | 15:6's priority makes the promise reach the nations |
| The promise came **before** the law | Galatians 3:17: four hundred thirty years before Sinai |

Take away the chapter order and the argument collapses. Paul is reading Genesis as a narrative
sequence with legal consequences, and the sequence — promise, then faith credited, then sign, then
law — is Genesis's own.

Galatians 3 adds one thing Romans does not: it reads Genesis 12:3 and 15:6 together, so that faith
is the mechanism by which *"all the families of the earth"* get in. See
[[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]].

### James is answering a slogan, not Paul

James 2:21–24 quotes the same verse and concludes the opposite: *"Was not Abraham our father
justified by works, in that he offered up Isaac his son on the altar? … and the Scripture was
fulfilled which says, 'Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him as righteousness.'"*

Three observations keep this from being a flat contradiction.

**James is reading 15:6 through Genesis 22, and he is not the first.** 1 Maccabees 2:52 does exactly
that, roughly two centuries earlier, in a book Paul's opponents would have known. The reading James
defends was the standing Jewish reading of the verse; he is not improvising a counter-Paul.

**The word *works* does not mean the same thing in the two writers.** Paul's *erga nomou* are the
boundary-marking observances of Torah — circumcision above all, which is the specific case Romans 4
is built on. James's *erga* are acts of obedience and mercy, and the example that frames his chapter
is feeding a hungry person (2:15–16). They are arguing about different objects with the same noun.

**And *fulfilled* is doing real work in James 2:23.** He does not say the binding of Isaac earned the
verdict; he says the verdict of 15:6 was **fulfilled** — brought to its intended completion — by what
happened at Moriah fifty years or so later. That is a claim about the relation between a declaration
and a life, not a competing account of how the declaration was obtained.

What remains genuinely in tension is emphasis rather than logic: Paul's Abraham is credited at the
moment of the stars, James's Abraham is vindicated on the mountain, and Genesis narrates both without
telling the reader which one is the point.

### The one thing Genesis does not say

It does not say Abram was righteous. It says the belief was **credited as** righteousness — and then
the next four chapters show him taking Hagar (16), laughing at the promise (17:17), and running the
wife-sister ruse a second time (20). Genesis 15:6 is not a character reference, and the wiki reads the
sequel as the strongest internal evidence for Paul's reading rather than against it: whatever
standing the verse confers, it survives conduct that would not have earned it.

## Significance

- **Genesis 15:6 is the verse the Reformation is about.** *Ḥāšab* / *logizomai* — reckoned, credited,
  imputed — is the vocabulary of the sixteenth century's central dispute, and the exegetical question
  (does the crediting recognize something in Abraham, or confer something on him?) is a question the
  Hebrew genuinely does not answer.
- **The New Testament contains an argument, not a system, and both sides of it are canonical.** Any
  reading that has to make James wrong, or has to make Paul mean less than he says, is doing
  something to the canon rather than reading it. Both are quoting a verse that the Hebrew Bible had
  already applied in both directions.
- **And the chronology matters more than the definition.** Paul's decisive move is not a theory of
  faith; it is the observation that Genesis 15 comes before Genesis 17, and that Genesis 12 comes
  before Exodus 20. See [[Covenant]] and [[Faith]].
