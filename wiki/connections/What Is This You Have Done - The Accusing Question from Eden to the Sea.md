# What Is This You Have Done - The Accusing Question from Eden to the Sea

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 3:13** — God to the woman: *"What have you done?"* — [[The Fall]]
- **Genesis 12:18** — a Pharaoh to Abram: *"What is this that you have done to me? Why didn't you tell me
  that she was your wife?"* — [[Abram in Egypt]]
- **Genesis 26:10** — Abimelech to Isaac: *"What is this you have done to us?"* —
  [[Isaac in Gerar]]
- **Genesis 29:25** — Jacob to Laban: *"What is this you have done to me? … Why then have you deceived
  me?"* — [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]]
- **Genesis 42:28** — the brothers, finding the silver: *"What is this that God has done to us?"* —
  [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]]
- **Exodus 14:5** — Pharaoh and his servants: *"What is this we have done, that we have let Israel go from
  serving us?"* — [[The Crossing of the Sea]]
- **Exodus 14:11** — the people to Moses: *"Why have you treated us this way, to bring us out of Egypt?"* —
  [[The Crossing of the Sea]]

## Analysis

**The Hebrew is one string: *mah-zzōʾt* (*what is this*) followed by *ʿāśâ* (*do*).** In Genesis through
Joshua it occurs at exactly these seven verses (WLC search for the sequence). The WEB renders it four ways —
*"What have you done?"*, *"What is this that you have done …?"*, *"What is this we have done?"*, *"Why have
you treated us this way?"* — so the English hides a formula the Hebrew keeps.

| | Who asks | Of whom | About |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 3:13 | God | the woman | the fruit |
| Genesis 12:18 | Pharaoh | Abram | the wife called a sister |
| Genesis 26:10 | Abimelech | Isaac | the wife called a sister |
| Genesis 29:25 | Jacob | Laban | the sister given as wife |
| Genesis 42:28 | the brothers | God | the silver in the sack |
| Exodus 14:5 | Pharaoh and his servants | themselves | the slaves let go |
| Exodus 14:11 | Israel | Moses | the slaves brought out |

**In Genesis it is an accusation of deception.** God's question opens the first trial; the two Gentile
kings use it of a patriarch who lied about his wife; Jacob, the deceiver, uses it of the man who deceived
him, with *rāmâ*, *deceive*, in the same breath. **In 42:28 it turns toward God** — the brothers, who sold
Joseph for silver, find silver they did not pay for and ask what *God* has done. **Each time the asker has
been put in a position he did not choose.**

**At the sea it is asked twice in one chapter, from both sides.** Egypt asks it of itself — the only time in
the sequence the subject is *we* — and the deed it regrets is a release. Israel asks it of Moses six verses
later, and the deed it resents is the same release seen from the other side. **The two parties in the
exodus use the question of Genesis's deceived kings and husbands, each blaming the release on someone,
before the sea answers both.**

**The Pharaoh echo is the closest.** Genesis 12:18's *mah-zzōʾt ʿāśîtā llî*, *what is this you have done to
me*, is a Pharaoh's word to the first Hebrew in Egypt; Exodus 14:11's *mah-zzōʾt ʿāśîtā llānû* is the
Hebrews' word to the man who brought them out of it — **the identical form appears at 26:10**, Abimelech's
complaint (WLC). [[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]] reads Abram's descent as a
first exodus; **this formula is one of the threads that ties the two scenes**, though the text never
says so.

## Significance

- **It records the question the Bible keeps putting in the mouths of people overtaken by consequences**, and
  the wiki tracks it by its Hebrew rather than its English. See [[Guilt and Conscience]] and
  [[Deception]].
- **It shows the exodus told with the vocabulary of Genesis's household quarrels.** Pharaoh's regret and
  Israel's complaint are given the same words as a cheated bridegroom and a deceived king.
- **Later uses are not yet ingested**, and no claim is made about them here.
