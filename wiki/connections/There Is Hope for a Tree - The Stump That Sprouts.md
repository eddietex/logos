# There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts

## Passages involved

- **Job 14:7–12** — *"For there is hope for a tree, If it is cut down, that it will sprout again,
  that the tender branch of it will not cease. Though its root grows old in the earth, and its stock
  dies in the ground, yet through the scent of water it will bud, and sprout boughs like a plant. But
  man dies, and is laid low… so man lies down and doesn't rise."* — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Job 14:14** — *"If a man dies, shall he live again? All the days of my warfare would I wait,
  until my release should come."*
- **Job 19:10** — *"My hope he has plucked up like a tree."* — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Isaiah 6:13** — *"As a terebinth, and as an oak, whose stump remains when they are cut down, so
  the holy seed is its stump."*
- **Isaiah 11:1** — *"A shoot will come out of the stock of Jesse, and a branch out of his roots
  will bear fruit."*
- **Isaiah 40:24** — of princes: *"Their stock has scarcely taken root in the ground. He merely
  blows on them, and they wither."*
- **Isaiah 53:2** — *"For he grew up before him as a tender plant, and as a root out of dry
  ground."*
- **Daniel 4:15, 23, 26** — Nebuchadnezzar's tree felled, *"Nevertheless leave the stump of its roots
  in the earth"*, and *"your kingdom shall be sure to you, after that you will have known that the
  heavens do rule."*
- **Ezekiel 17:22–24** — *"I will also take some of the lofty top of the cedar… I, Yahweh, have
  brought down the high tree, have exalted the low tree, have dried up the green tree, and have made
  the dry tree flourish."*
- **Job 8:11–19** — Bildad's papyrus, which withers without water — see
  [[The Papyrus Without Water - Bildad's Plants, Psalm 1, and Jeremiah 17]]

## Analysis

**Job's tree is the one image in his speeches that offers hope, and he offers it in order to deny
it to himself.** A felled tree — the olive, the sycamore, the terebinth — throws up new shoots from
the stump. *"Through the scent of water"* (*rêaḥ mayim*) is enough: the stump needs only the
nearness of water, not the marsh Bildad's papyrus required (8:11). Then the comparison: *"But man
dies."* The tree's hope is real, and it is what a man does not have.

**The vocabulary links it to the prophets' stump.** *Gezaʿ*, *stock* or *stump* (14:8), is a rare
word: it occurs in Job 14:8, Isaiah 11:1 (*"the stock of Jesse"*), and Isaiah 40:24, and nowhere else.
*Yaḥălîp*, *it will sprout again* (14:7), is from *ḥālap*, *to pass on* or *to be renewed*, and
reappears in 14:14 as *ḥălîpātî*, *my release* — the renewal the tree has and Job waits for. And
*yônaqtô*, *its tender shoot* (14:7), is the word behind Isaiah 53:2's *yônēq*, *tender plant*.

**In the prophets the stump becomes a promise.** Isaiah 6:13 ends the prophet's commission, which is
a sentence of devastation, with a stump: *"the holy seed is its stump."* Isaiah 11:1 makes the stump
of Jesse — the felled house of David — the root of the coming king, and Isaiah 53:2 has the Servant
grow up *"as a tender plant, and as a root out of dry ground"*, without even the scent of water.
Daniel 4 leaves Nebuchadnezzar's stump in the ground *"bound with a band of iron and brass"* until he
learns that heaven rules, and then restores the kingdom. Ezekiel 17 has God making *"the dry tree
flourish"*. **Every one of these is the national or royal form of the hope Job grants the tree and
withholds from the man.** What the prophets claim for Israel, the house of David, and even a
Babylonian king — that a felled thing can sprout again — Job cannot yet claim for an individual
human being.

**Job's own speeches turn the image twice more.** At 14:13–15 he imagines, as a wish, the human
version: hidden in Sheol, then remembered, then called. At 19:10 he says God has *"plucked up"* his
hope *like a tree* — not felled it, which would leave a stump, but uprooted it. And at 29:19 he
remembers being a tree with *"my root… spread out to the waters"*. The epilogue's restoration
(42:10–17) is the stump sprouting: a second family and a doubled estate, out of a man cut to the
ground.

## Significance

- Job 14:7–12 is the closest the Old Testament comes to stating the resurrection hope as an image
  and then refusing it. See [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].
- The stump connects Job's private complaint to Isaiah's messianic oracle by a word shared only
  between them, and the Servant's *tender plant* by another.
- With [[The Papyrus Without Water - Bildad's Plants, Psalm 1, and Jeremiah 17]], it completes the
  dialogue's chain of plant images: the friends' plants die, Job's stump could live.
