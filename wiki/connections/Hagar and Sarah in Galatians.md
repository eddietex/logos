# Hagar and Sarah in Galatians

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 16:1–16** — Hagar given, conceiving, afflicted, and promised offspring beyond counting.
- **Genesis 17:15–21; 18:9–15; 21:1–7** — Sarah renamed, promised, laughing, and bearing Isaac at
  ninety.
- **Genesis 21:8–14** — *"Cast out this servant and her son! For the son of this servant will not be
  heir with my son, Isaac."* See [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].
- **Galatians 4:21–31** — *"Abraham had two sons, one by the servant, and one by the free woman …
  These things contain an allegory, for these are two covenants."*
- **Isaiah 54:1** — *"Rejoice, you barren who don't bear … for the children of the desolate are more
  than the children of her who has a husband"*, quoted at Galatians 4:27.
- **Romans 9:6–9** — *"they are not all Israel who are of Israel … the children of the promise are
  counted as offspring."*

## Analysis

Galatians 4:21–31 is the only place in the New Testament that mentions Hagar, and it is not a reading
of her story. It is an argument about circumcision that borrows her name.

**Paul's scheme.** Two sons of Abraham; one *"born according to the flesh"* by a slave, one *"through
the promise"* by a free woman. He then maps the pair onto two covenants:

| | Hagar's column | Sarah's column |
|---|---|---|
| the son | Ishmael, *born according to the flesh* | Isaac, *born through promise* |
| the covenant | Sinai | the promise to Abraham |
| the city | *"the present Jerusalem … in bondage with her children"* | *"the Jerusalem that is above … our mother"* |
| the status | slavery | freedom |
| the outcome | *"cast out the servant and her son"* (Genesis 21:10, quoted) | *"heirs according to promise"* |

**Why this is startling.** Every Jewish reader of Paul's letter would have placed themselves in the
Sarah column by descent and the Galatian gentiles in the Hagar column. Paul reverses it. The people
insisting on circumcision and Torah — the ones with the pedigree — are assigned to Hagar and Sinai;
the uncircumcised gentile believers are assigned to Sarah and the promise. He is using the
genealogical argument against genealogy, which is the same move as Romans 9:6–9 and the same one John
the Baptist makes at Matthew 3:9.

**The Isaiah quotation is the hinge.** Galatians 4:27 cites Isaiah 54:1, addressed to exiled Zion as a
barren woman promised more children than the married one. Paul reads Sarah's barrenness through
Isaiah's: the promise runs through the womb that cannot produce, so that the offspring can never be
credited to the flesh. That is a legitimate reading of Genesis's own pattern (see [[Barrenness]]) and
it is the theological engine of the whole passage.

### The textual crux at 4:25

The manuscripts differ on the opening of Galatians 4:25. Some read *"for this Hagar is Mount Sinai in
Arabia"*; others omit *Hagar* and read *"for Sinai is a mountain in Arabia"*. The reading with the
name has generated a long search for an Arabic word *hajar*, stone, or for a place-name near Sinai, on
the theory that Paul is punning. Nothing is secure, and the argument does not depend on it: what Paul
needs is that Sinai is outside the land, in the country where Ishmael's descendants live.

### What Paul is not saying

Four things this page states plainly, because the passage has been made to carry all of them.

- **It is not exegesis of Genesis 16, and Paul says so.** *Hatina estin allēgoroumena* — *these things
  are spoken allegorically*. He is announcing a figural reading, not claiming that Genesis meant this.
- **It is not a verdict on Hagar as a person.** Genesis has God address her by name, give her the
  offspring promise in its own covenant formula, and hear her affliction; Galatians has her as one
  half of a diagram. The two are doing different things, and letting the diagram overwrite the woman
  is how most later Christian reading of Hagar went wrong.
- **It is not a judgment on Judaism or on the Sinai covenant as such.** Paul's target is a specific
  demand that gentile believers be circumcised, and elsewhere in the same letter and in Romans 7:12 he
  calls the law holy. Reading 4:24–25 as a repudiation of Jews is a use the letter does not license
  and the church's history has repeatedly made anyway.
- **It is not an instruction about anyone's household.** The quotation of *"cast out the servant and
  her son"* (4:30) is an application to teachers Paul thinks should be excluded from the Galatian
  churches, not a comment on the ethics of Genesis 21.

### The one place the allegory touches the story

Galatians 4:29 says the son born according to the flesh *"persecuted"* the son born according to the
Spirit — which is Paul's reading of the single Hebrew participle at Genesis 21:9, *məṣaḥēq*, rendered
*"mocking"* in the WEB. The word is from the root behind *Isaac* (*yiṣḥāq*, *he laughs*), and it can
mean playing, laughing, or mocking; Genesis does not say what Ishmael actually did, and the entire
weight of the expulsion rests on it. Paul takes the harshest available sense. Rabbinic tradition
supplies still harsher ones; the Septuagint reads *"playing with Isaac her son"*. The text underneath
all of them is one ambiguous word.

## Significance

- **It is the reason Hagar has a place in Christian theology at all**, and the reason that place was
  for centuries a negative one. Recovering the Genesis 16 figure — the woman God finds, names, and
  hears — from the Galatians 4 figure is one of the more consequential re-readings of the last century
  of Old Testament scholarship, and it does not require rejecting Paul; it requires noticing that he
  told his readers he was allegorizing.
- **It shows how the New Testament handles the non-covenant lines.** Ishmael, like Esau at Romans
  9:13, is used as a category rather than described. Genesis is consistently more generous to both of
  them than the arguments built on them are.
- **It joins the wiki's other Genesis-15-and-16 threads.** Paul's *"heirs according to promise"* runs
  straight back through Genesis 15:6 (see
  [[Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James]]) and Genesis 12:3 (see
  [[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]]); Galatians 3 and Galatians 4
  are one continuous argument, and Genesis 15, 16, and 21 are its three exhibits.
