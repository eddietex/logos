# Let Us Go Down - The Divine Plural in Genesis

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 1:26** — *"Let us make man in our image, after our likeness."* See
  [[The Creation of the World]].
- **Genesis 3:22** — *"Behold, the man has become like one of us, knowing good and evil."* See
  [[The Fall]].
- **Genesis 11:7** — *"Come, let's go down, and there confuse their language."* See
  [[The Tower of Babel]].
- **Genesis 6:2; Job 1:6; 2:1** — *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, the sons of God, presenting themselves before
  Yahweh. See [[The Sons of God]] and [[Job of Uz]].
- **Genesis 18:17, 21** — *"Will I hide from Abraham what I do…?"* and *"I will go down now, and
  see"*: the deliberation and the descent both repeated in the **singular**, one chapter into the
  Abraham cycle. See [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Isaiah 6:8** — *"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"*
- **1 Kings 22:19–22; Psalm 82:1; Daniel 7:9–10** — the council seen, seated, and consulted.

## Analysis

**God speaks of himself in the plural exactly three times in Genesis, and all three are in the
primeval history.** They are 1:26, 3:22, and 11:7, and they share more than the grammar: each is a
divine deliberation immediately before a decisive act, and each concerns the boundary between what is
human and what is not. Humanity is made in the divine image; humanity has become *like one of us* in
knowing good and evil; humanity has become capable of anything it schemes. The plural shows up at
precisely the moments when that boundary is at issue, and nowhere else in the book.

**The surrounding grammar is singular every time.** Genesis 1:27 answers 1:26 with *"God created man
in his own image"* — singular verb, singular pronoun, in the very next clause. At 11:5 and 11:8
*Yahweh* comes down and *Yahweh* scatters, both singular, framing the plural of 11:7 on either side.
Whatever the plural is doing, Hebrew narrative does not treat it as a plurality of actors.

**The four readings, and what each costs.**

1. **Plural of majesty or deliberation.** The speaker uses *we* the way a monarch or a person
   thinking aloud does. This is the commonest modern account and the simplest, and it has the merit
   of explaining why the following verbs go straight back to singular. Against it: a true plural of
   majesty is hard to document elsewhere in classical Hebrew for verbs, as opposed to nouns like
   *ʾĕlōhîm*, and 3:22's *"like one of us"* — *kəʾaḥad mimmennû*, *like one from among us* — is a
   partitive, which does not fit a self-reference well at all.

2. **The divine council.** God addresses the heavenly beings who attend him. The Bible has such a
   court and describes it repeatedly: Micaiah sees Yahweh with *"all the army of heaven standing by
   him on his right hand and on his left"* and hears him ask who will entice Ahab (1 Kings 22:19–22);
   Isaiah hears *"who will go for us?"* (6:8); Psalm 82 has God standing in the divine assembly; Job
   opens with the sons of God presenting themselves. This reading takes *"like one of us"* at face
   value and explains the partitive. Against it: nothing in Genesis 1 makes creatures co-creators, and
   1:27 immediately says God alone created.

3. **The Trinity.** The classical Christian reading from the second century onward, taking the plural
   as an intra-divine address. Against it as *exegesis*: no Old Testament writer and no New Testament
   writer cites these verses that way — John 1:1–3 grounds the Word's role in creation without
   appealing to 1:26 — and the reading was unavailable to the text's first audience. Most careful
   Christian commentators now hold it as a theological reading made in the light of the whole canon
   rather than as the sense of the Hebrew, which is a distinction worth keeping. See
   [[In the Beginning - Genesis 1 and John 1]].

4. **A relic of polytheistic idiom.** The forms survive from an older Canaanite pantheon and no longer
   mean what they once did. This accounts for the strangeness historically and leaves the text's own
   theology — resolutely one God, acting alone in every verb — doing the work.

**Genesis 11:7 has something the other two do not: an answer.** *Hābâ nērədâ*, *Come, let us go
down*, repeats the builders' own opening word. They said *hābâ*, come, twice — *"Come, let's make
bricks"* (11:3) and *"Come, let's build ourselves a city"* (11:4) — and God's deliberation begins with
the same imperative and the same cohortative. The plural here is not only a theological curiosity but
a piece of literary mimicry: the divine council is made to answer the human council in its own
idiom, and the two *let us* speeches are set against each other as rival deliberative bodies.

**The direction of travel is the other joke.** The builders' tower has its head in the heavens, and
the response from heaven is *let us go down*. It is the third and last time Genesis uses the plural,
and it is spent on a descent.

### And then it stops — Genesis 18:17, 21

**The plural does not survive into the Abraham cycle, but the soliloquy does.** Genesis 18:17 is the
book's fourth divine deliberation-before-acting and its first in the singular: *"Will I hide from
Abraham what I do?"* Four verses later comes the descent, and it is 11:5's *"Yahweh came down to
see"* almost word for word — *"I will go down now, and see whether their deeds are as bad as the
reports which have come to me"* (18:21) — again singular, and this time about a city on the plain
rather than a city in Shinar.

The shape survives intact and the grammar changes. And the chapter that drops the plural is the one
chapter in Genesis where God's *appearance* is unmistakably threefold — *"three men stood opposite
him"* (18:2), with reading 3 above resting on this scene more heavily than on 1:26 ever since
Ambrose's *tres vidit, unum adoravit*. The wiki notes the irony and draws nothing from it: the
grammatical plural and the visual three have no textual relation, and Genesis 18 does not use the
first person plural once. See [[Entertaining Angels Unawares]].

## Significance

The wiki records all four readings and adopts none, for the reason the passages themselves suggest:
the plural is never explained, never repeated after Genesis 11, and never cited by any later biblical
writer. Whatever it is, Scripture does not build on it — which is itself an argument against making
too much rest on it in either direction.

What the thread does establish is a shape. At each of the three moments the divine speech is
deliberative, first-person plural, and about a limit; and at each the narrative immediately reports a
singular God acting. God consults, in some sense, and then acts alone. The last of the three
consultations is about a city on a plain in Shinar, and it ends the primeval history.

And then the form outlives the grammar. Genesis 18:17 deliberates aloud in the singular before
acting on another city, and 18:21 repeats 11:5's descent to see — so the pattern the plural belonged
to is not a feature of the primeval history at all. Only the *we* is.
