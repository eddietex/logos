# The Unloved Wife and the Servant Heir - Proverbs 30.23, Leah, and Hagar

## Passages involved

- **Proverbs 30:21–23** — [[Four Things]]: the earth trembles *"for an unloved woman when she is
  married; and a servant who is heir to her mistress"* (23).
- **Genesis 29:31** — [[The Birth of Jacob's Children]]: *"Yahweh saw that Leah was hated, and he
  opened her womb, but Rachel was barren."*
- **Genesis 16:1–6** — [[Hagar and Ishmael]]: Sarai gives her servant Hagar to Abram, and when Hagar
  conceives, *"her mistress was despised in her eyes"* (16:4).

## Analysis

**Two shared words.** In the Hebrew of Proverbs 30:23, the unloved woman is *śənûʾāh* and the
servant girl inherits *gəḇirtāh*, *her mistress*. Genesis 29:31 says Yahweh saw that Leah was
*śənûʾāh* — the WEB translates it *"hated"* in Genesis and *"unloved"* in Proverbs. Genesis 16:4 says
Hagar's mistress, *gəḇirtāh*, was despised in her eyes. All three verses were read in Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah* on 2026-10-27. Both words are ordinary vocabulary, so the shared
wording does not show that the proverb was written with either story in mind; it shows that
Proverbs names the same household tensions Genesis narrates.

**The proverb fears what Genesis tells.** Proverbs 30:21–23 lists four reversals the earth cannot
bear, and the last two are domestic. A servant girl who displaces her mistress is the trouble of
Genesis 16 in one line: Hagar's pregnancy changes how she sees Sarai, Sarai blames Abram, and the
household breaks (16:4–6). Hagar does not literally inherit Sarai's estate, so the parallel is to the
reversal of standing, not to an inheritance.

**Genesis takes the unloved woman's side.** In Proverbs the unloved woman's marriage is
destabilizing. In Genesis the unloved wife is the one Yahweh sees: he *"opened her womb"*, and at
her first two sons Leah says that Yahweh *"has looked at my affliction"* and *"has heard that I am
hated"* (29:32–33). Hagar too is seen by God in the wilderness after
Sarai dealt harshly with her (16:6–13). **This is an explicit tension of perspective, not a
contradiction to be resolved.** The proverb speaks from the household's need for stable order; the
narratives show God attending to the person the order treats as the problem. Neither cancels the
other, and the wiki records both.

## Significance

The connection joins Proverbs' observation to Genesis' stories of [[Marriage]], [[Barrenness]],
and [[Slavery and Servitude]] in the patriarchal households. It also explains why the saying
should not be read as contempt for unloved wives or servant women: Scripture has already told
stories in which God sides with them. See [[Leah]], [[Hagar]], [[Sarah]], and
[[The Two Flights of Hagar]].
