# None Shall Make You Afraid - The Covenant Blessing of Security

## Passages involved

- [[Do Not Withhold Good]] — Proverbs 3:21–26; retained wisdom, secure walking, sweet sleep,
  and Yahweh as confidence.

- **Leviticus 26:6** — *"I will give peace in the land, and you will lie down, and no one will make
  you afraid"* — *wəʾên maḥărîd*, the blessing of the covenant
- **Job 11:19** — Zophar: *"Also you shall lie down, and no one shall make you afraid"* —
  [[Zophar's First Speech]]
- **Job 5:22–24** — Eliphaz: *"you shall not be afraid of the animals of the earth… the animals of
  the field shall be at peace with you"* — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]
- **Deuteronomy 28:26** — the curse inverted: carcasses for the birds, *"and there shall be no one
  to frighten them away"*
- **Isaiah 17:2; Zephaniah 3:13** — flocks that *"lie down (rābəṣû), and no one will make them
  afraid"*
- **Micah 4:4** — *"they will every man sit under his vine and under his fig tree. No one will make
  them afraid"*
- **Jeremiah 30:10; 46:27; Ezekiel 34:25–28** — the restored people, *"none will make him afraid"*;
  the covenant of peace and the wild animals banished
- **Psalm 4:8; Proverbs 3:24** — *"In peace I will both lay myself down and sleep"*; *"When you lie
  down, you will not be afraid"*

## Analysis

**It is a formula.** *Wəʾên maḥărîd*, *and there is none to cause trembling*, appears in a cluster of
texts that all picture the same scene: a people or a flock lying down at night, in the open, with no
one coming. Its home is the blessings of the covenant at Leviticus 26:6, and from there the prophets
use it as the shape of restoration after exile — the vine and the fig tree of Micah 4:4, the
regathered flock of Zephaniah 3:13, the covenant of peace of Ezekiel 34:25–28, which also banishes
the wild animals. Its negative is Deuteronomy 28:26, where the formula describes corpses that nobody
is left to drive the birds from. **Security, in this language, is not wealth; it is sleep.**

**Zophar quotes it to a man outside the covenant.** Job is from [[Uz]], not Israel; none of the
friends uses the name *Yahweh*; the book takes pains to set its whole debate in the international
wisdom world. And yet Zophar's promise at 11:19 is the covenant's own sentence, with the covenant's
verb for lying down — *rābaṣ*, used of flocks — and its formula for the absence of fear. **Whether
the poet meant it as an echo or drew both from a common stock, the effect is that the friends offer
Job Israel's blessing on Israel's terms**: put away iniquity, and the land will let you sleep.
Eliphaz had made the same offer at 5:22–24 in its animal form, where the beasts of the field are at
peace with the righteous man — the same pairing Ezekiel 34:25 and Hosea 2:18 make.

**Job's problem is that he has already had the night the formula excludes.** Chapter 3 ends *"I am
not at ease, neither am I quiet, neither have I rest; but trouble comes"* (3:26), and chapter 7
*"When I lie down, I say, 'When shall I arise?'… you scare me with dreams, and terrify me through
visions"* (7:4, 14). **The one who makes him afraid, in Job's account, is God.** Zophar's promise
answers that without seeing it: the condition for no longer being terrified is to be reconciled with
the one doing the terrifying. That is close to true, and the whirlwind will bear it out in a form
Zophar would not recognise.

### Proverbs now in context — 3:21–35

The sleep promise of 3:24 belongs to an instruction to retain wisdom and discretion
(3:21–22), grounded in Yahweh's protection (3:26). It is followed by duties toward a
neighbor who lives securely beside the hearer (3:27–30). Security received must not
become security betrayed. See [[Do Not Withhold Good]].

**Tension retained:** Job 7:3–4, 13–14 describes unrest and terrifying dreams in the
life of the man introduced as upright (Job 1:1). Proverbs' promise cannot be made an
exceptionless test of the sleeper's moral character. This addition compares the
stored WEB wording; it does not claim that Proverbs uses the Hebrew formula discussed
above. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3 and Job 1, 7, checked 2026-10-25.

## Significance

- It shows the friends' promises drawing directly on covenant blessing, and so shows what kind of
  theology Job is refusing: not a private superstition but the shape of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy
  28 applied to one man.
- It ties [[Hope]] and [[The Fear of God]] to a concrete image the prophets keep — the night without
  an alarm — and that the New Testament takes up in its own way (*"Don't be afraid, little flock"*,
  Luke 12:32).
- Within Job, it frames a reversal: the man promised that *"many shall court your favor"* (11:19) in
  the same verse is the man whose prayer the friends will need at 42:8.
