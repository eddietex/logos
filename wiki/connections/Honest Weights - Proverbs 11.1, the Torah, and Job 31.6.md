# Honest Weights - Proverbs 11.1, the Torah, and Job 31.6

## Passages involved

- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:1, 3, 20.
- [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] — Job 31:5–8.
- Leviticus 19:35–36 and Deuteronomy 25:13–16 — WEB verified; books not yet ingested.
- Proverbs 20:10, 23 — stored WEB; not yet ingested.

## Analysis

**The marketplace is accountable to Yahweh.** Proverbs 11:1 condemns a false balance and delights in
accurate weights. Verses 3 and 20 place that practical example beside integrity and blameless ways.
A balance can be dishonest even when the person using it offers no spoken lie: the instrument makes
the deception appear objective.

Leviticus 19:35–36 requires justice in measurements of length, weight, and quantity, grounding just
balances and measures in the identity of the God who brought Israel from Egypt. Deuteronomy 25:13–16
forbids maintaining differing weights and measures and calls unjust practice an abomination to
Yahweh. Proverbs shares that moral evaluation in a compressed saying; Proverbs 20:10, 23 repeats it.
This is a thematic correspondence, not evidence here for a particular direction of borrowing.

**Job turns the commercial instrument into a plea for judgment.** After denying falsehood and
deceit, Job asks to be weighed in an even balance so that God may know his integrity (31:5–6). The
accurate standard praised in commerce becomes an image for a fair assessment of the sufferer. This
is especially pointed beside Proverbs 11:3: integrity is supposed to guide, yet the upright man
needs his integrity recognized amid ruin. Job 2:3 makes clear that the ruin cannot itself prove
dishonesty.

## Significance

Fair dealing and fair judgment belong together. The comparison prevents treating integrity as merely
inward and prevents treating a sufferer’s losses as an accurate balance of that person’s worth. See
[[Integrity]], [[Deception]], [[Theft and Restitution]], and [[Law and Obedience]].
