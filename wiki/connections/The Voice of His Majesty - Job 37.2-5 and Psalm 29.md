# The Voice of His Majesty - Job 37.2-5 and Psalm 29

## Passages involved

- **Job 37:2–5** — Elihu: *"Hear, oh, hear the noise of his voice, the sound that goes out of his mouth.
  He sends it out under the whole sky, and his lightning to the ends of the earth. After it a voice roars.
  He thunders with the voice of his majesty. He doesn't hold back anything when his voice is heard. God
  thunders marvelously with his voice. He does great things, which we can't comprehend."* —
  [[Elihu on God's Greatness]]
- **Psalm 29:3–5, 7–9** — *"Yahweh's voice is on the waters. The God of glory thunders, even Yahweh on
  many waters. Yahweh's voice is powerful. Yahweh's voice is full of majesty. Yahweh's voice breaks the
  cedars"*; *"Yahweh's voice strikes with flashes of lightning"*; *"In his temple everything says,
  'Glory!'"*
- **Psalm 29:10** — *"Yahweh sat enthroned at the Flood. Yes, Yahweh sits as King forever."*
- **Job 26:14** — Job: *"How small a whisper do we hear of him! But the thunder of his power who can
  understand?"* — [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]]
- **Job 40:9** — God: *"Or do you have an arm like God? Can you thunder with a voice
  like him?"* — [[Behemoth and Leviathan]]
- **Exodus 19:16, 19** — Sinai: *"there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud on the mountain"*;
  *"Moses spoke, and God answered him by a voice."* Not yet ingested.
- **John 12:28–29** — *"Then there came a voice out of the sky … The multitude therefore, who stood by
  and heard it, said that it had thundered."* Not yet ingested.

## Analysis

**One word, two meanings.** Hebrew *qôl* is both *voice* and *sound*, and in the plural, *qōlôt*, it is
the ordinary word for *thunder* (as at Exodus 19:16). So the thunder-as-voice of Job 37 and Psalm 29 is
not a metaphor laid on top of the language; it is already in the language. Elihu says *qôl* five times in
four verses (37:2–5); Psalm 29 says *qôl yhwh*, *Yahweh's voice*, seven times in nine verses. Both move
across the same storm — waters, lightning, the ends of the earth — and both arrive at *majesty*: *"He
thunders with the voice of his majesty"* (*qôl gəʾônô*, Job 37:4); *"Yahweh's voice is full of
majesty"* (*behādār*, Psalm 29:4). The words for majesty differ; the image does not.

**What the two do with it.** Psalm 29 is worship. It calls the *"sons of the mighty"* (*bənê ʾēlîm*, often read as the heavenly beings) to give glory
(29:1–2), shows
the storm breaking cedars and shaking the wilderness, and ends in the temple, where *"everything says,
'Glory!'"* (29:9), and on the throne, *"Yahweh sat enthroned at the Flood"* (29:10). The storm proves God
king, and the people rest in it: the psalm closes, *"Yahweh will bless his people with peace"* (29:11). Elihu's use is argument. The voice
in the thunder is the voice no man can answer — *"He does great things, which we can't comprehend"*
(37:5) — and it leads to his conclusion that no one should ask to speak with God: *"Will it be told him
that I would speak? Or should a man wish that he were swallowed up?"* (37:20).

**Job had said it first, and the other way.** *"How small a whisper do we hear of him! But the thunder of
his power who can understand?"* (26:14). For Job the thunder was what no one hears; what reaches a man is
a whisper. Elihu replies, *Hear, oh, hear* (37:2). And God settles it in his own terms: *"Can you thunder
with a voice like him?"* (40:9) — the thunder as the voice Job does not have, asked as a question to the
man who wanted to speak with God as an equal (9:32–35).

**And the voice speaks.** The surprise of Job 38:1 is that the voice in the storm turns out to have words.
*"Then Yahweh answered Job out of the whirlwind"* — not thunder, but sentences, questions, a speech
addressed to one man. Sinai is the same: thunder and lightning, and then *"God answered him by a voice"*
(Exodus 19:19). In the Gospel the order is reversed: a voice out of the sky speaks words, and the crowd
hears only thunder (John 12:28–29).

## Significance

- It places Elihu's hymn inside Israel's liturgy: the storm-psalm tradition of Psalm 29 (and of Psalm 18:7–15
  and 77:17–18), not a private image.
- It shows the same image used for opposite ends: for worship and rest in Psalm 29, for silence before God
  in Elihu, and in God's own speech for both — *"Can you thunder?"* is a rebuke, and it is also an answer.
- It sharpens the turn at 38:1. Elihu says the voice in the thunder cannot be answered; the voice in the
  whirlwind asks to be.
- Some scholars read Psalm 29 as an adaptation of a Canaanite hymn to the storm god Baal-Hadad, whose voice
  is the thunder; the wiki records this and does not build on it. See [[Language and Speech]],
  [[Creation]], [[Kingship and Empire]], and
  [[From Elihu's Storm to the Whirlwind - Job 36.27-37.24 and 38.1]].
