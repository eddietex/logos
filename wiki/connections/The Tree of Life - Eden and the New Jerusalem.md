## Analysis

The tree of life links Genesis 2–3 with Revelation 2 and 22, while Proverbs develops the
image repeatedly within instruction. Its narrative arc is planting, loss of access, and
restoration; its proverbial uses describe life-giving wisdom, conduct, desire, and speech.
These are related uses of an image, not one continuous report of access to Eden's tree.

**Genesis 2:9** puts it *"in the middle of the garden"*, alongside the tree of the knowledge of
good and evil. It is never forbidden. The prohibition of 2:16–17 names only the other tree, which
means the man and woman were free to eat from the tree of life for as long as they were in the
garden — a detail easy to miss, and one that changes the tone of the expulsion.

**Genesis 3:22–24** is the reversal, and it gives the reason for the exile explicitly: *"lest he
reach out his hand, and also take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever."* Cherubim and a
flaming sword are stationed at the east of the garden — not to keep them from the tree of
knowledge, which they have already eaten from, but from the tree of life. Endless life in a
fallen condition is treated as a fate to be prevented, which reframes the expulsion as at least
partly mercy.

Now that [[The Fall]] is ingested, two details in that verse are worth holding onto. First, God's
sentence **breaks off unfinished** — *"and eat, and live forever—"* — and the expulsion completes
it as an action rather than a word; the guarding of the tree *is* the rest of the sentence.
Second, the tree of knowledge is never mentioned again after 3:11. Only the tree of life is
guarded, only the tree of life has a sequel, and the whole canon's return route runs past the
cherubim posted here.

**The cherubim come back in Exodus 25, over the ark** (25:18–22), and the next object described is a
gold lampstand with branches, buds, and almond blossoms (25:31–36). Many readers take the lampstand as
a stylized tree, inside a sanctuary guarded by cherubim, and so as an echo of Eden. Exodus does not
call it a tree. The wiki records the reading as a reading; see
[[The Ark, the Table, and the Lampstand]] and [[Cherubim]].

**Revelation 2:7** reopens it as a promise: *"To him who overcomes I will give to eat from the
tree of life, which is in the Paradise of God."* Note *paradeisos*, the Septuagint's word for the
garden of Genesis 2:8.

**Revelation 22:1–2, 14, 19** restores it in full — a river flowing from the throne, the tree on
both sides of it, twelve kinds of fruit, and leaves *"for the healing of the nations"*. The
guarded gates are open (22:14), and access to the tree is now the reward rather than the danger.

**The pattern, and its one shift.** What is restored is not simply what was lost:

| Genesis 2–3 | Revelation 21–22 |
|---|---|
| a garden | a city, with the garden inside it |
| one river dividing into four | one river from the throne |
| the tree in the middle of the garden | the tree on both sides of the river, bearing twelve crops |
| access barred by cherubim and a sword | gates never shut; access is the promise |
| a man and a woman | the nations, healed by its leaves |
| God walking in the garden | *"the throne of God and of the Lamb will be in it"* |

The end is not a return to Eden but Eden urbanized and multiplied. Between the two ends,
Proverbs keeps the phrase alive as metaphor — wisdom is a tree of life to those who take hold of
her (3:18), and so are the fruit of the righteous, fulfilled desire, and a gentle tongue (11:30,
13:12, 15:4).

**The cherubim also appear on the dividing veil.** Exodus 26:1, 31 prescribes their figures on
the inner curtains and veil, and 26:33 puts the ark behind that separation. The lampstand remains
outside the veil (26:35). An Eden reading therefore involves the whole sanctuary's imagery,
not a claim that a lampstand-tree stands behind cherubim exactly as Genesis' tree does. Exodus
never identifies the lampstand as the tree of life or calls the textile figures guards.
See [[The Tabernacle Curtains and Frames]]; the architectural details qualify the typology rather than prove it.

### Wisdom grasped as a tree — Proverbs 3:18

[[Wisdom's Worth and the Founding of the Earth]] says, in the stored WEB: *"She is a
tree of life to those who lay hold of her. Happy is everyone who retains her."* The image
joins finding wisdom (3:13) to holding it through time (3:18). Genesis 3:22–24 narrates
a barred tree and the prevention of endless life; Proverbs praises an accessible way of
living. **The difference must remain:** Proverbs does not announce the removal of the
cherubim or promise that the wise will never die.

Later sayings within the same stored book call the fruit of the righteous a tree of life
(11:30), compare fulfilled longing to one (13:12), and apply the image to a gentle tongue
(15:4). These uses support reading 3:18 as life-giving metaphor, while leaving its
resonance with Eden visible.

Source for this addition: Genesis 2:9; 3:22–24 and Proverbs 3:13–20; 11:30; 13:12; 15:4
checked in the stored WEB, 2026-10-25. No new claim about original-language wording is made.

### Righteous fruit that gives life — Proverbs 11:30

[[A False Balance]] now brings the next tree-of-life saying into the wiki: the fruit of the
righteous gives life, and the WEB follows with “He who is wise wins souls.” The image moves from
Wisdom herself in 3:18 to the effect of righteous conduct. Read among sayings about speech, mercy,
counsel, and generosity, it commends life-giving influence without identifying an evangelistic
program or announcing literal immortality. The underlying Hebrew and alternative translations of the
second clause are not adjudicated here. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 3:18; 11:9–30.

## Significance

This is the clearest single instance of the Bible's beginning-and-end symmetry, and the reason
Christian readers speak of redemption as restoration rather than replacement. It also sets up the
tree as an image the New Testament can use in a third way: Acts 5:30, 10:39, 13:29, Galatians
3:13, and 1 Peter 2:24 all call the cross a *tree* (*xylon*) — the same word Revelation 22:2 uses
for the tree of life — so that the way back to the tree runs through one.

Revisit for Revelation 2 and 22, and for the *xylon* passages in Acts, Galatians, and 1 Peter.
See also [[Curse]] — Revelation 22:3, *"there will be no curse any more"*, stands in the same
breath as the tree's restoration, undoing Genesis 3:14–19 in the same verses that undo 3:24.
