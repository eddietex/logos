# The Two Birthdays in the Bible - Pharaoh's Feast and Herod's

*This page records a coincidence, not a thread. Neither text alludes to the other, no New Testament
writer draws the comparison, and the wiki argues nothing from it. It exists because the observation is
striking enough that a reader will make it, and should find that the wiki has already checked it.*

## Passages involved

- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:20–22: *"On the third day, which was Pharaoh's birthday,
  he made a feast for all his servants"*
- Mark 6:21–28 — Herod's birthday, *"a supper for his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of
  Galilee"*, and the head of John the Baptizer on a platter
- Matthew 14:6–11 — the same episode
- [[Job of Uz]] — Job 1:4: the sons' feasts *"in the house of each one on his birthday"*, and whether
  that is what the Hebrew says
- Job 3:1–3 — Job cursing *"his day"*, where the sense is unambiguous

## Analysis

**Scripture records exactly two birthday celebrations, and at both of them somebody loses his head.**

The Hebrew Bible's is Genesis 40:20 — ***yôm hulledet ʾet-parʿōh***, *the day of Pharaoh's being born* —
**and it is the only unambiguous birthday observance in the Old Testament.** The New Testament's is
Herod Antipas's at Mark 6:21, ***genesia***, a word that in Greek usage could mean either a birthday
feast or a commemoration of a death.

**The third candidate does not survive inspection.** Job 1:4 has Job's sons feasting *"in the house of
each one on his birthday"* in the WEB, **but the Hebrew is simply *yômô*, *his day***, and the reading
is a translator's decision; the wiki's own passage page sets out the alternative — a rotation of seven
brothers through seven days of the week — and adopts neither. See [[Job of Uz]]. **Whatever Job 1:4 is,
nothing happens at it.**

**So the comparison is between two, and it is closer than it has any right to be.**

| | **Genesis 40:20–22** | **Mark 6:21–28** |
|---|---|---|
| the host | Pharaoh | Herod Antipas |
| the guests | *"all his servants"* | *"his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee"* |
| who is in custody | two royal officers, offence unstated | John the Baptizer, arrested for naming a marriage unlawful |
| what the feast produces | one man restored, one man executed | one man executed |
| the method | *"lift up your head from off you"* | *"brought his head on a platter"* |
| the verdict's basis | not given | a rash oath before guests, which the king *"didn't want to refuse"* |
| who saw it coming | a prisoner who read a dream | nobody |

**Both kings kill a prisoner at a party, and in both the killing is incidental to the occasion.** That is
the whole of the resemblance and it is enough to be worth writing down, because **the Bible's attitude to
birthdays is otherwise complete silence**: no patriarch's is recorded, no king of Israel's, none of the
festivals is one, and the nativity narratives give no date. **The two times Scripture describes the
custom, it is a foreign court doing it, and a man dies.**

**It is easy to over-read and the wiki does not.** Mark's Greek carries no allusion to Genesis 40; the
vocabulary is unrelated, and **Mark's interest is in Herod's weakness before his own oath**, which has no
counterpart in Pharaoh, who is not weak and swears nothing. **Genesis 40:20's feast is not even the point
of its own sentence** — the feast is a subordinate clause, and the main verb is *lifted up the head*, the
muster at which two men are counted back among the staff before the verse splits them. See
[[Lift Up Your Head - One Idiom and Two Verdicts]].

**What the pairing is good for is negative.** A reader who notices that the Bible's only two birthday
parties both end badly sometimes concludes that Scripture disapproves of birthdays — an old argument,
and one that has been made seriously. **Two narrated instances, both in foreign royal courts, with no
comment attached to either, will not carry it.** The texts do not praise or condemn the custom; they
report a court doing what courts did. **The wiki records the pattern and declines the inference.**

## Significance

Its use is as a check on a particular way of reading. **The coincidence is real, memorable, and means
nothing** — and a wiki that files it honestly under *coincidence* is more useful than one that either
omits it (leaving the next reader to rediscover it and assume it is significant) or dresses it up as
typology.

It also fixes a small fact worth having on file: **Genesis 40:20 is the Old Testament's only certain
birthday**, and the Hebrew phrase for it is a hophal infinitive that occurs nowhere else.
