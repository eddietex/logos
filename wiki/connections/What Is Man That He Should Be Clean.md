# What Is Man That He Should Be Clean

## Passages involved

- **Job 4:17–19** — Eliphaz, reporting his night vision: *"Shall mortal man be more just than God?
  Shall a man be more pure than his Maker? Behold, he puts no trust in his servants. He charges his
  angels with error. How much more, those who dwell in houses of clay…"*
- **Job 15:14–16** — Eliphaz, second speech: *"What is man, that he should be clean? What is he who
  is born of a woman, that he should be righteous? … how much less one who is abominable and
  corrupt, a man who drinks iniquity like water!"* — [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]]
- **Job 25:4–6** — Bildad, third speech, the friends' last words: *"How then can man be just with
  God? Or how can he who is born of a woman be clean? … how much less man, who is a worm, the son of
  man, who is a worm!"*
- **Job 9:2; 14:4** — Job saying much the same thing himself
- **Genesis 6:5** — *"every imagination of the thoughts of man's heart was continually only evil"* —
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]
- **Psalm 8:4** — *"What is man, that you think of him?"* — the same question, opposite key
- **Psalm 130:3; 143:2; Proverbs 20:9; Ecclesiastes 7:20; 1 Kings 8:46** — the canon's other
  statements that no one is clean
- **Romans 3:10–12, 23** — Paul's catena, and *"all have sinned"*
- **Job 42:7** — *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right"*

## Analysis

Three times in Job, a friend argues from the same premise in the same shape: an a-fortiori running
downward from heaven. *God does not trust his angels / his holy ones; the heavens are not clean in
his sight; **how much less** a human being.* The three statements escalate.

| | Speaker | The heavenly term | The human term |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4:17–19** | Eliphaz | *"he puts no trust in his servants … charges his angels with error"* | *"those who dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust"* |
| **15:14–16** | Eliphaz | *"he puts no trust in his holy ones … the heavens are not clean in his sight"* | *"abominable and corrupt, a man who drinks iniquity like water"* |
| **25:4–6** | Bildad | *"even the moon has no brightness, and the stars are not pure in his sight"* | *"man, who is a worm, the son of man, who is a worm"* |

The heavenly term moves outward — servants, then angels, then the holy ones and the heavens
themselves, then the moon and stars — and the human term moves downward, from a dweller in a clay
house to a drinker of iniquity to a worm. Eliphaz's first version at least grants that the problem
is creaturely fragility (*"houses of clay"*); by Bildad's version the problem is that the human
being is disgusting. The friends' anthropology degrades as their patience does.

**And most of what they say is true.** This is the difficulty, and the reason this connection page
exists.

- That no human being is clean before God is asserted by Genesis 6:5, 1 Kings 8:46, Psalm 130:3 and
  143:2, Proverbs 20:9, Ecclesiastes 7:20, and Romans 3:10–23.
- **Job says it himself.** *"Truly I know that it is so, but how can man be just with God?"*
  (9:2); *"Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one."* (14:4). He is not disputing the
  premise.
- Paul quotes Eliphaz as scripture at 1 Corinthians 3:19 (from Job 5:13), which settles that the
  friends' speeches are not simply a body of error.

**So what is wrong with it?** Three things, and it is worth being exact, because the popular
summary — *the friends were wrong, so ignore what they said* — is not what the book supports.

**(1) The inference is invalid.** From *no one is perfectly clean* nothing follows about whether a
given calamity is a given man's due. Universal guilt cannot ground a particular verdict, because it
does not discriminate — that is precisely what makes it universal. The friends use it to close the
question of Job's case, and it cannot close any case.

**(2) The premise is deployed to defeat evidence rather than to explain it.** Job's protest is met
not by weighing what he says but by reclassifying it: *"Your own mouth condemns you"* (15:6). Once
universal depravity is available as a response to any denial, the argument has become unfalsifiable
and pastorally lethal. See [[Retribution]].

**(3) The verdict falls on the portrait of God, not on the anthropology.** Job 42:7 says Eliphaz did
not speak rightly *of God* — *ʾēlay*, concerning me. What the argument produces is a God whose
dealings can be read backwards off a ledger, who has no purposes he does not disclose, and who
cannot be wronged. Job, who accused that God of hunting him like a beast, is the one God points to instead: *"as my
servant Job has"*.

The comparison with **Psalm 8:4** is the cleanest illustration of the point. *"What is man, that you
think of him? What is the son of man, that you care for him?"* is the same question in the same
words, asked in wonder, and it arrives at *"you have crowned him with glory and honor"*. The
sentence is not the problem. The use is.

## Significance

- **It is the wiki's standing example of a true doctrine used as a false diagnosis**, and the
  distinction generalizes well beyond Job. The friends do not need to be heretics to do enormous
  damage; they need only to apply a universal to a particular that it cannot decide, and to keep
  applying it harder when contradicted.
- **It complicates any doctrine of Scripture that reads every sentence as an assertion of the
  text.** Job 15:14–16 is in the Bible; it is not necessarily what the Bible teaches, and 42:7 says
  so about the man who spoke it. What is inspired is the book's report of the argument, including
  its verdict on the argument. Meanwhile 1 Corinthians 3:19 shows the New Testament happily quoting
  a rebuked speaker for a true proposition. Both facts are needed to read the book properly.
- **It gives the doctrine of universal sinfulness its correct home.** The doctrine is well attested
  and Genesis 6:5 states it more strongly than Eliphaz does, in the narrator's voice rather than a
  disputant's. What Job establishes is what the doctrine *cannot* be made to do: it cannot be
  reversed into an explanation of any particular person's suffering. Luke 13:1–5 and John 9:1–3 make
  the same refusal explicit.
- **The 15:15 clause has a second life.** *"He puts no trust in his holy ones. Yes, the heavens are
  not clean in his sight"*, with 4:18's *"he charges his angels with error"*, is one of the Hebrew
  Bible's few statements about fallibility among heavenly beings, and is regularly cited alongside
  the material on [[The Sons of God and the Angels That Sinned]]. Note who is speaking, and that
  nothing in the book confirms it.
