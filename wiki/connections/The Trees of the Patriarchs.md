# The Trees of the Patriarchs

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 12:6** — *"Abram passed through the land to the place of Shechem, to the oak of Moreh. At
  that time, Canaanites were in the land."* See [[The Call of Abram]].
- **Genesis 13:18** — *"Abram moved his tent, and came and lived by the oaks of Mamre, which are in
  Hebron, and built an altar there to Yahweh."* See [[Abram and Lot Separate]].
- **Genesis 14:13; 18:1** — the same oaks, as an address and as the site of the visit. See
  [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] and
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Genesis 21:33** — *"Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and called there on the name of
  Yahweh, the Everlasting God."* See [[The Treaty at Beersheba]].
- **Genesis 35:4, 8** *(not yet ingested)* — the foreign gods buried *"under the oak which was by
  Shechem"*, and Deborah buried *"below Bethel under the oak"* at *Allon Bacuth*, the oak of weeping.
- **Joshua 24:26** — Joshua sets a great stone *"under the oak that was by the sanctuary of Yahweh"*
  at Shechem, at the covenant renewal.
- **Judges 6:11, 19; 9:6, 37** — the oak at Ophrah; the oak of the pillar at Shechem where Abimelech
  is made king; the *oak of the diviners*.
- **Deuteronomy 16:21** — *"You shall not plant for yourself an Asherah of any kind of tree beside
  Yahweh's altar which you shall make for yourself."*
- **Hosea 4:13; Isaiah 1:29; 57:5; Ezekiel 6:13** — the prophets on worship *"under every green
  tree"*.

## Analysis

### Genesis is full of trees, and every one of them is a place where something is decided

The patriarchs do not build shrines. They mark ground, and the marks are **stones, altars, and
trees** — and in Genesis the trees come first. Abram's very first stop in the land is not a town but a
tree: *"to the place of Shechem, to the oak of Moreh"* (12:6), where he receives the promise and
builds his first altar. He then lives at the oaks of Mamre for the whole middle of his story; the
three visitors find him there; and the one tree he *plants* is the last thing he does before Moriah.

**The Hebrew words are not settled.** *ʾĒlôn* / *ʾēlâ* (Moreh, Mamre) is rendered *oak* by the WEB
and *terebinth* by many others; the terebinth (*Pistacia*) is the likelier botanical identification
for a solitary landmark tree in that country, and the ambiguity runs through every English Bible.
*ʾĒšel* at 21:33 is traditionally the **tamarisk**, a salt-tolerant desert tree that suits Beersheba
exactly; the Septuagint read the consonants differently and gave *a field*. And the words for oak
(*ʾallôn*, *ʾēlâ*, *ʾēlôn*) share consonants with *ʾēl*, God, which is almost certainly coincidence
and has never stopped anyone noticing it.

### The one tree that is planted

**Every other patriarchal tree is found. Abraham's tamarisk is put in the ground.**

That is the whole force of 21:33. Moreh and Mamre are landmarks he arrives at; the oak at Shechem is
already there when Jacob buries the household gods under it and still there when Joshua sets his
stone beneath it four hundred years later. A planted tree is different in kind: it is an act with a
horizon longer than the planter, performed by a man who at that moment holds **no land at all** and
has just signed a treaty acknowledging that the ground belongs to somebody else. Two chapters later
he will pay four hundred shekels for a cave, and a grave will be the only real property he ever owns.

**And the tree and the name go together.** *"planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and called there
on the name of Yahweh, the Everlasting God"* — *ʾĒl ʿÔlām*, the God whose duration runs past the
horizon, invoked beside the slowest-growing thing available. See
[[The Everlasting God - El Olam in Genesis and Isaiah]] and [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].

### The problem Deuteronomy has with all of this

> *"You shall not plant for yourself an Asherah of any kind of tree beside Yahweh's altar which you
> shall make for yourself."* — Deuteronomy 16:21

**Read flatly, that verse forbids what Genesis 21:33 records without a flicker of unease.** Nor is it
the only one: the patriarchs sacrifice at open-air sites all over the land, set up standing stones
(28:18, 35:14) which Deuteronomy 16:22 also prohibits, build altars wherever they stop, and worship
at named trees — a cult that Deuteronomy's centralizing law would recognize immediately and
outlaw comprehensively. The prophets then denounce the descendant of that same practice in the
harshest terms available: *"they sacrifice on the tops of the mountains, and burn incense on the
hills, under oaks and poplars and terebinths"* (Hosea 4:13).

**Three ways this is handled, and the wiki adopts none of them.**

1. **Historical.** The patriarchal narratives preserve a genuinely older stratum of Israelite
   religion, from before centralization, in which sacred trees and standing stones were unremarkable.
   Deuteronomy legislates against a practice Genesis simply reports, and the two books are separated
   by centuries of development. This is the standard critical account and it explains the silence
   very economically.
2. **Dispensational-within-the-canon.** The law given at Sinai does not apply to men who lived before
   it, so nothing Abraham does is a violation of anything, and Genesis has no reason to comment. This
   is the traditional harmonization, and it is how the same books handle Jacob marrying two sisters
   (Leviticus 18:18) and Abraham marrying his half-sister (Leviticus 18:9).
3. **Distinguishing the object.** What Deuteronomy forbids is an *ăšērâ* — a cult object of a
   goddess, planted *beside Yahweh's altar* as a rival — not a tree as such; Abraham's tamarisk is
   shade and a landmark, and no altar is even mentioned at 21:33. The difficulty is that this
   requires the reader to supply a distinction the text does not draw, at a verse where a tree, a
   place, and the invocation of the divine name occur in a single clause.

**What is not in doubt is that Genesis is untroubled.** It reports the planting the way it reports
the pitching of a tent, and the wiki records the tension rather than dissolving it — the same
treatment it gives the patriarchal altars, pillars, and marriages.

### Trees as the place where things are buried, sworn, and decided

Once the pattern is visible it runs through the historical books:

- **Buried under a tree.** The foreign gods and earrings at Shechem (35:4); Deborah the nurse below
  Bethel at *Allon Bacuth*, the **oak of weeping** (35:8); and, at the far end, the bones of Saul and
  his sons under the tamarisk at Jabesh (1 Samuel 31:13) — the Bible's third and last *ʾēšel*.
- **Sworn under a tree.** Joshua's covenant stone under the oak at the sanctuary at Shechem, with the
  stone itself made a witness (Joshua 24:26–27) — the same evidentiary logic as Abraham's seven ewe
  lambs at the same kind of site. See [[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].
- **Ruled from under a tree.** Abimelech son of Gideon crowned *"by the oak of the pillar that was in
  Shechem"* (Judges 9:6); Deborah the judge under her palm (Judges 4:5); Saul with his spear under
  the tamarisk at Gibeah (1 Samuel 22:6).

**Shechem's tree collects most of it** — Abram's first altar, Jacob's buried gods, Joshua's covenant,
Abimelech's coronation — which is why the wiki treats that site separately. See
[[The Altar at Shechem and the Covenant at Shechem]].

## Significance

- **The patriarchs' religion is a religion of marked places**, and trees mark them more durably than
  altars do. An altar of unhewn stones is a heap; an oak is visible from a distance and lasts for
  centuries, which is why the same three or four trees keep reappearing across a thousand years of
  narrative.
- **Abraham's tamarisk is the one act of planting in the patriarchal narratives**, and it is done at
  the moment his legal claim to the ground is weakest. It says, without a word of theology, what
  Genesis 21:33's divine name says out loud.
- **And the practice Genesis records is the practice the law and the prophets later condemn.** The
  wiki keeps that unresolved. It is one of the clearest places where the Pentateuch's narrative and
  its legislation describe two different worlds, and neither book shows any awareness of the other's
  discomfort.

## The one patriarch who cuts them — Genesis 30:37

**Every other tree in this thread is stood under, camped beside, planted, or buried at. Jacob's are
cut and peeled.**

> **30:37** Jacob took to himself rods of fresh **poplar**, **almond**, **plane tree**, peeled white
> streaks in them, and made the white appear which was in the rods.

***Libneh***, ***lûz***, ***ʿermôn*** — **three species named in one verse, which is more botanical
detail than the whole rest of Genesis contains**, and **none of them is a cult site, an altar, a
grave, or an address.** They are raw material. **The book that will not say what Abraham's oak at
Mamre was for describes exactly what Jacob did to three trees and why**, and there is no religion in
it anywhere. See [[Jacob's Flocks]].

**Two of the names still carry the thread's memory.** ***Libneh*** is built on *l-b-n*, *white*, and
so is ***Lābān*** — the wordplay of the pericope. ***Lûz***, the almond, **is the name Bethel went by
before Jacob renamed it** (28:19), which puts a tree's name on the one place in this thread the
patriarch met God rather than camped. **Genesis supplies both and glosses neither.**
