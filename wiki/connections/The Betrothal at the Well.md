# The Betrothal at the Well

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 24:10–61** — the servant and [[Rebekah]] outside the city of Nahor — [[A Wife for Isaac]]
- **Genesis 29:1–20** — Jacob and Rachel at the well in the east *(not yet ingested)*
- **Exodus 2:15–21** — Moses and Zipporah at the well in Midian
- **John 4:1–42** — Jesus and the Samaritan woman at Jacob's well
- Compare **1 Samuel 9:11–13** — Saul meets young women drawing water, and the scene declines to
  become this one
- Compare **Ruth 2:8–9** — the elements redistributed: the water is already drawn, and the man offers
  it to the woman

## Analysis

Three passages in the Torah tell the same story with the same moving parts, in the same order. It is
the clearest **type-scene** in the Hebrew Bible — a fixed narrative pattern an audience expects, whose
meaning lies in what each telling does differently — and the New Testament uses it once more, on a
reader who is assumed to know it.

**The fixed sequence:**

| element | Genesis 24 | Genesis 29 | Exodus 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| the hero is far from home | the servant, sent by Abraham | Jacob, fleeing Esau | Moses, fleeing Pharaoh |
| he arrives at a well | *the well of water* outside the city of Nahor | *a well in the field*, three flocks lying by it | *the well* in Midian |
| a girl or girls come to draw | Rebekah, *before he had finished speaking* | Rachel, *while he was yet speaking with them* | the seven daughters of the priest of Midian |
| water is drawn — and the drawing is the deed | **she** waters ten camels | **he** rolls the stone away and waters her flock | **he** drives off the shepherds and waters their flock |
| the girl runs home with the news | *The young lady ran, and told her mother's house* | *She ran and told her father* | *they came to Reuel their father* |
| the man is brought in and fed | Laban: *Come in, you blessed of Yahweh* | Laban again: *Surely you are my bone and my flesh* | Reuel: *call him, that he may eat bread* |
| a betrothal follows | Rebekah goes; Isaac marries her | fourteen years, and two wives | *Moses was content to dwell with the man* |

**The variations are where the meaning is.**

**Genesis 24 is the only one in which the woman does the work.** In the other two the hero performs a
feat of strength or nerve at the well — Jacob single-handedly rolls off a stone that normally waits
for all the flocks to gather, Moses drives off a gang of shepherds — and the woman receives it. Here
the traveller asks for a drink and **Rebekah waters ten camels**, which is the heaviest labour anybody
performs in any version of the scene. **The type-scene's usual test of the man is turned into a test
of the woman**, and she is the one who passes it.

**Genesis 24 is also the only one in which the bridegroom is not there.** Isaac is four hundred miles
away, and the man at the well is a proxy who cannot marry anybody. **The pattern's protagonist has
been replaced by a servant**, which is of a piece with everything else Genesis does with Isaac: named
before conception, bound without speaking, married by errand. See [[Isaac]].

**Genesis 29 is the same well, the same family, and the same house — and it goes wrong.** Jacob
arrives at a well in the same country and is met by [[Laban]], who welcomed the servant twenty-odd
years earlier with *"Come in, you blessed of Yahweh"* and now says *"Surely you are my bone and my
flesh"* — and then charges him seven years. **The scene that produced Rebekah in one day produces
Rachel in fourteen**, and a substituted bride in between. Genesis is running the same pattern on the
same location to show a different man getting a different result from it.

**Exodus 2 moves it out of the family.** Moses' bride is a Midianite priest's daughter, no kinship is
involved, and the endogamy that drives Genesis 24 is simply absent. What the scene establishes there
is that the deliverer of Israel is, at this point in his life, a man who intervenes for strangers at
a watering trough — the third time in one chapter he steps into somebody else's fight.

### John 4

**The Gospel puts Jesus at a well in the middle of the day and lets the pattern do the work.** He is
*"wearied with his journey"* at *"Jacob's well"* (4:6); a woman comes to draw; there is a
conversation about drawing water; she leaves her water jar, **runs to the town**, and tells them; the
townspeople come out and press him to stay, *"and he stayed there two days"* (4:40).

Every beat is there — the journey, the well, the woman with the jar, the request for a drink, the
running with news, the hospitality, the staying. **And the betrothal does not happen.** The woman has
had five husbands and is living with a sixth (4:18), the conversation is about worship rather than
marriage, and what she brings back to the town is not a bridegroom for herself. A reader who knows
Genesis 24 and Exodus 2 arrives at this well expecting a wedding and gets a confession of the
Messiah instead.

The Gospel writer's fondness for nuptial imagery is not incidental: John the Baptist has already
called himself *"the friend of the bridegroom"* two chapters earlier (3:29), which is precisely the
office [[Abraham's Servant]] holds in Genesis 24.

## Significance

**The type-scene is evidence about how the Bible's narrators worked**, and it is one of the few
literary observations about the Hebrew Bible that can be checked rather than argued: the elements
recur, in order, across three books, with one instance in a fourth.

Two things follow for reading. **First, the differences are deliberate.** When a pattern is this
fixed, an omission is a statement — the absent bridegroom in Genesis 24, the absent kinship in Exodus
2, the absent wedding in John 4. **Second, the wells are not incidental.** The Bible's marriages,
like its covenants, keep happening at water in a country where water is scarce and public, and the
place where a household's daughters must go daily is the one place a stranger can meet them.

The pattern's founding instance is also the one where the theology is thickest: the servant asks God
for a sign at the well, gets it before he has finished asking, and the narrator never confirms that
God did anything. See [[Prayer]] and [[Marriage]].
