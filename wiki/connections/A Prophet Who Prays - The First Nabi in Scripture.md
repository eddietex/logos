# A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 20:7** — *"For he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you will live"* — the first
  occurrence of *nābîʾ* in the Bible, and the first of *hitpallēl*, to pray —
  [[Abraham and Abimelech]]
- **Genesis 20:17** — *"Abraham prayed to God. God healed Abimelech, and his wife, and his female
  servants"* — the prescription carried out
- **Genesis 18:22–33** — the same man doing the same thing without the word being used —
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]]
- **Exodus 7:1** — *"I have made you as God to Pharaoh; and Aaron your brother shall be your
  prophet"* — **the second *nābîʾ*, and the first defined by speech** —
  [[Aaron's Staff Before Pharaoh]]
- **Exodus 8:8–13, 28–31** — Pharaoh: *"Entreat Yahweh"*; *"Pray for me"* — and *"Yahweh did according
  to the word of Moses"*: **Moses praying for a foreign king, as Abraham did** — [[Frogs, Lice, and Flies]]
- **Exodus 9:27–33** — *"Pray to Yahweh"* a third time, after *"I have sinned"*; Moses *"spread abroad
  his hands to Yahweh"*, knowing Pharaoh does *"not yet fear"* — [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]]
- **Exodus 32:11–14, 30–32** — Moses at the golden calf: *"Yahweh relented from the evil which he
  said he would do to his people"*
- **Numbers 12:6–8; 14:13–20** — prophecy defined by dream and vision, with Moses as the exception,
  and Moses turning a sentence a second time
- **1 Samuel 12:19, 23** — *"far be it from me that I should sin against Yahweh in ceasing to pray
  for you"*
- **Jeremiah 7:16; 11:14; 14:11; 15:1** — the prophet forbidden to intercede, and told that even
  Moses and Samuel would not be heard
- **Amos 7:1–6** — *"Lord Yahweh, forgive, I beg you!" … Yahweh relented concerning this*
- **Ezekiel 22:30** — *"I sought for a man among them who would build up the wall and stand in the
  gap before me for the land … but I found no one"*
- **Job 42:7–9** ([[The Restoration of Job]]) — *"my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him"* — the same arrangement
  at the far end of the canon's other book about who may stand between
- **Hebrews 7:25; Romans 8:34; 1 John 2:1** — the office continued in one person who *"always lives
  to make intercession"*

## Analysis

**The Bible introduces the word *prophet* in a sentence that says nothing about predicting
anything.**

> **7** Now therefore, restore the man’s wife. For he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you
> will live.

*Nābîʾ* has never appeared before Genesis 20:7. Neither has *hitpallēl*, the ordinary Hebrew verb for
praying, which occurs here and then at 20:17 when Abraham actually does it. **Both words enter
Scripture in the same speech, and the second explains the first**: what makes Abraham a *nābîʾ*, in
the only definition Genesis ever gives, is that his prayer will keep a foreign king alive.

That is not the definition most readers arrive with, and it is not the definition the word carries
in ordinary English. It is, however, the definition the rest of the Old Testament keeps using.

### The office as the canon actually uses it

Set the great prophetic scenes beside each other and the intercession is not a sideline.

| | the prophet | what he does |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 20:7, 17 | Abraham | prays for the king he deceived; the household is healed |
| Exodus 32:11–14 | Moses | argues God out of destroying Israel, on God's own reputation |
| Exodus 32:30–32 | Moses | offers to be blotted out of the book instead |
| Numbers 14:13–20 | Moses | again, after the spies: *"I have pardoned according to your word"* |
| 1 Samuel 12:23 | Samuel | calls *not* praying for the people a sin against Yahweh |
| Amos 7:1–6 | Amos | asks twice, and twice *"Yahweh relented"* |
| Jeremiah 15:1 | — | *"Though Moses and Samuel stood before me"* — the two named as the standard |
| Ezekiel 22:30 | nobody | God looks for someone to stand in the gap and does not find one |

**The clearest proof that intercession is the job is that Jeremiah is forbidden to do it.** *"Don't
pray for this people, neither lift up a cry or prayer for them, neither make intercession to me"*
(7:16, and again at 11:14 and 14:11). A prohibition on that scale, repeated three times, only makes
sense against an assumption that the prophet would otherwise be praying — and the sentence at 15:1
names Moses and Samuel as the two who would ordinarily have prevailed. **The withdrawal of the
prophet's prayer is itself the judgment.** Ezekiel 22:30 says the same thing from the other end: the
absence of an intercessor is what leaves the land with nothing between it and the sentence.

### What Genesis 20 adds that the later scenes do not have

Two things, and both are strange.

**The intercessor is the guilty party.** Moses prays for a people who made a calf; Samuel prays for a
people who demanded a king; Amos prays for a nation under judgment. Abraham prays for a man whose
household he endangered by lying to him — and God's speech makes the prayer a condition of the
king's survival before Abraham has admitted anything or been rebuked by anybody. **The office is
conferred, not earned, and it is conferred in the middle of the failure.**

**And the beneficiary is an outsider.** The first prayer in the Bible is offered by an Israelite
ancestor for a Canaanite king, on God's instruction, and it works. Whatever else the promise of
12:3 means — *"All the families of the earth will be blessed through you"* — this is its first
concrete instance, and it takes the form of a healed household in Gerar. See
[[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]].

### Job, which does it again from the far side

**The one other place in the Old Testament where God tells offenders that a named individual's prayer
is their remedy is the end of Job**, and the resemblance is close enough to be worth setting out:

> **Genesis 20:7** — *"he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you will live."*
>
> **Job 42:8** — *"my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him, that I not deal with you
> according to your folly."*

In both, God speaks to the party who is in the wrong, names the person they wronged as the one whose
prayer he will accept, and makes their standing depend on it. In both, the man so named has just
spent the narrative saying things about God that a reader would not have predicted this ending from.
And in both the prayer is offered and the sentence lifted without further comment. The books are
about entirely different questions, and they answer the question *who can stand between* the same
way. See [[Mediation and Advocacy]] and [[The Witness in Heaven]].

### The second *nābîʾ*: a mouth, not an intercessor

**The word's second occurrence defines it differently.** In Genesis, Exodus, and Job *nābîʾ* occurs
only at Genesis 20:7 and Exodus 7:1 (WLC lemma search). **At 7:1 Aaron is Moses' *nābîʾ* because he
will speak what Moses gives him**, as Moses is *"as God"* to Pharaoh — the arrangement of 4:16,
where Aaron was to be Moses' *"mouth"*. **Genesis 20:7 makes the prophet someone who speaks *to*
God for others; Exodus 7:1 makes him someone who speaks *for* another to others.** The canon keeps
both directions, and Moses, who does both, is the figure in whom they meet (Exodus 32:11–14;
Deuteronomy 18:18, not yet ingested). See [[Aaron's Staff Before Pharaoh]].

**And Exodus 8 brings the two directions together within a chapter of 7:1.** The man who is *"as
God"* to Pharaoh, with a *nābîʾ* of his own, is asked by the same Pharaoh to *"Entreat Yahweh"* (8:8)
and *"Pray for me"* (8:28) — and does, and *"Yahweh did according to the word of Moses"* (8:13, 31).
**It is Genesis 20 again: a prophet of Israel praying for the king of the land Israel is sojourning in,
for relief from a plague God sent on that king's house.** The verbs differ — Abraham *hitpallēl*,
Moses *ʿātar* and *ṣāʿaq* (WLC) — and the outcomes differ: Abimelech returned Sarah; Pharaoh hardens
his heart. See [[Frogs, Lice, and Flies]].

**At the hail it happens a third time, and with less hope.** Pharaoh confesses, *"I have sinned this
time"*, and asks again (*haʿtîrû*, 9:28). Moses agrees, and says to his face, *"I know that you don't yet
fear Yahweh God"* (9:30). **The intercessor does not wait for the king to be sincere**; he prays so that
*"you may know that the earth is Yahweh's"* (9:29), spreading his palms (*pāraś kappayim*) outside the
city, and the storm stops (9:33). See [[Livestock, Boils, and Hail]].

### The New Testament's move

The New Testament does not drop the category; it consolidates it. Hebrews 7:25 keeps the function and
changes the tense — *"he is able also to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through
him, seeing that he lives forever to make intercession for them"* — and Romans 8:34 and 1 John 2:1
say it in their own idiom. The office Genesis 20:7 opens with a prophet praying for a king closes
with a priest who does nothing else.

**What is worth noticing is that the New Testament does not therefore retire the practice.** James
5:16–18 argues from Elijah to ordinary prayer for one another, and 1 Timothy 2:1 makes intercession
*"for all men, for kings and all who are in high places"* the first thing on the list — which is,
almost exactly, what Abraham is told to do at Genesis 20:7.

## Significance

The Bible defines its own central religious office at the first opportunity, and defines it by
prayer for somebody else. That is a different centre of gravity from the one the word *prophet*
usually carries: not a man who knows what is coming, but a man whose asking is heard.

It also puts a particular kind of pressure on the Abraham cycle. He is called *nābîʾ* in the chapter
where he behaves worst, on the strength of a prayer he has not yet prayed, for a man who has just
told him what he did *"ought not to be done"*. Genesis is not interested in making the title a
reward. **The prophet's usefulness in this book has nothing to do with his record**, which is the
same point [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]] makes about the promise: the arrangement holds up its bearer
rather than the other way round.
