## Analysis

**Most of the sentence collection is antithetical: the righteous prosper, the wicked fall.** Read alone,
that form invites the inference Job's friends drew — that circumstances show standing. **The *better than*
saying is the collection's built-in correction.** It can only be spoken in a world where the righteous man
has *little* and the wicked man has *great treasure*, and it says so outright. Proverbs 15:16 grants the
little and the great treasure both, and then ranks them: the little *with the fear of Yahweh* is better.

**The form ranks goods; it does not deny them.** A dinner of herbs is not called better than a fattened
calf. A dinner of herbs *where love is* is better than a fattened calf *with hatred* (15:17). Feasting is
good, and a house full of it is good, until it comes *with strife* (17:1). The sayings do not teach that
poverty is holy or wealth corrupt — the same chapter puts *"much treasure"* in *"the house of the
righteous"* (15:6). They teach that when the goods come apart, as they do, the fear of Yahweh, love,
quietness, righteousness, and integrity outrank possessions.

**Proverbs 15 holds both forms in one chapter.** Verse 6 is antithetical and says the righteous house is
rich; verses 16–17 are comparative and say a righteous house may be poor. The chapter does not reconcile
them, because they are not in conflict: one is the ordinary run of things, the other the ranking that
holds when the ordinary run fails. The page for [[Retribution]] has followed that distinction since Job.

**The form returns through the collection.** Chapter 16 uses it of righteousness against unjust revenue
(16:8), of the lowly against the proud (16:19), and of self-rule against conquest (16:32); 17:1 opens the
next chapter with it; and two sayings ten chapters apart (19:1; 28:6) put *the poor who walks in his
integrity* above the perverse, the second explicitly above the perverse *rich*. The prologue had already
used the comparative of wisdom itself — better than silver and gold (3:14; 8:11, 19).

**Read beside Job.** Job, *"blameless and upright"* (Job 1:1), is the case the better-than sayings
describe: the man with integrity and, for most of the book, nothing else. His wife asks, *"Do you still
maintain your integrity?"* (Job 2:9); 19:1 and 28:6 say that integrity with poverty is the better
portion. The sayings do not promise that it will be restored, as Job's was.

Sources: Proverbs 3:14; 8:11, 19; 15:6, 16–17; 16:8, 19, 32; 17:1; 19:1; 28:6; Job 1:1; 2:9, checked in
stored WEB. No claim is made about the Hebrew form of the comparative.

## Significance

The better-than form is the strongest textual evidence that Proverbs itself does not read its promises as
guarantees, and so the strongest support for the wiki's practice of reading the sentence collection
alongside Job rather than against it. It connects [[Wealth and Possessions]], [[The Fear of God]],
[[Integrity]], and [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]].

### Three in one chapter — Proverbs 16:8, 19, 32

[[The Plans of the Heart]] brings three more into the wiki. *"Better is a little with righteousness, than
great revenues with injustice"* (16:8) repeats 15:16's concession with *righteousness* in the place of
*the fear of Yahweh*. *"It is better to be of a lowly spirit with the poor, than to divide the plunder
with the proud"* (16:19) sets humility-with-poverty against the gang's plunder of 1:13. And *"One who is
slow to anger is better than the mighty; one who rules his spirit, than he who takes a city"* (16:32)
moves the form from goods to power: self-rule ranks above conquest. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 1:13;
15:16; 16:8, 19, 32.
