## Analysis

**Most of the sentence collection is antithetical: the righteous prosper, the wicked fall.** Read alone,
that form invites the inference Job's friends drew — that circumstances show standing. **The *better than*
saying is the collection's built-in correction.** It can only be spoken in a world where the righteous man
has *little* and the wicked man has *great treasure*, and it says so outright. Proverbs 15:16 grants the
little and the great treasure both, and then ranks them: the little *with the fear of Yahweh* is better.

**The form ranks goods; it does not deny them.** A dinner of herbs is not called better than a fattened
calf. A dinner of herbs *where love is* is better than a fattened calf *with hatred* (15:17). Feasting is
good, and a house full of it is good, until it comes *with strife* (17:1). The sayings do not teach that
poverty is holy or wealth corrupt — the same chapter puts *"much treasure"* in *"the house of the
righteous"* (15:6). They teach that when the goods come apart, as they do, the fear of Yahweh, love,
quietness, righteousness, and integrity outrank possessions.

**Proverbs 15 holds both forms in one chapter.** Verse 6 is antithetical and says the righteous house is
rich; verses 16–17 are comparative and say a righteous house may be poor. The chapter does not reconcile
them, because they are not in conflict: one is the ordinary run of things, the other the ranking that
holds when the ordinary run fails. The page for [[Retribution]] has followed that distinction since Job.

**The form returns through the collection.** Chapter 16 uses it of righteousness against unjust revenue
(16:8), of the lowly against the proud (16:19), and of self-rule against conquest (16:32); 17:1 opens the
next chapter with it; and two sayings ten chapters apart (19:1; 28:6) put *the poor who walks in his
integrity* above the perverse, the second explicitly above the perverse *rich*. The prologue had already
used the comparative of wisdom itself — better than silver and gold (3:14; 8:11, 19).

**Read beside Job.** Job, *"blameless and upright"* (Job 1:1), is the case the better-than sayings
describe: the man with integrity and, for most of the book, nothing else. His wife asks, *"Do you still
maintain your integrity?"* (Job 2:9); 19:1 and 28:6 say that integrity with poverty is the better
portion. The sayings do not promise that it will be restored, as Job's was.

### Proverbs 19 in context

Proverbs 19:1 names the poor person's integrity and the opposing figure's crooked lips and folly; it
does not explicitly call that figure rich. Verse 22 independently ranks the poor man above the liar.
Poverty is not praised for itself: character supplies the ranking. The chapter's report of social
abandonment (4, 7) and its call to pity the poor (17) show why this distinction matters. Source:
stored WEB Proverbs 19:1, 4, 7, 17, 22; 28:6.

### Proverbs 21 in context

Proverbs 21 adds two better-than sayings about the household: a housetop corner (9) or a desert (19)
is better than living with a contentious woman. See
[[The Contentious Woman - Proverbs 19.13, 21.9, 19, 25.24, and 27.15]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; no new source-language claim.

### Proverbs 22 in context

Proverbs 22:1 ranks a good name above *"great riches"* and loving favor above *"silver and gold"*.
Unlike the poverty-with-integrity sayings, it does not set a poor man against a rich one; it ranks
reputation and goodwill above wealth, and three verses later (22:4) promises wealth itself to
humility and the fear of Yahweh. The comparison is of goods, not a rejection of riches.

See [[A Good Name]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:1, 4; no new source-language claim.

### Proverbs 27:5, 10 — rebuke and nearness

Chapter 27 has two better-than sayings, and both rank relationships rather than possessions. *"Better is
open rebuke than hidden love"* (5) puts the uncovered (*məḡullāh*) rebuke above a love kept hidden
(*məsuttāreṯ*); *"better is a neighbor who is near than a distant brother"* (10) puts nearness above
kinship in the day of disaster. Like the earlier sayings, they admit a cost — the sting of rebuke, the
brother not turned to — and judge it worth paying. See [[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Hebrew from
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-26.

### Proverbs 28 in context

The comparison now explicitly identifies the morally compromised person as rich. Proverbs 19:1 spoke
of perverse lips and folly; 28:6 speaks of perverse ways and riches. Poverty and integrity can
therefore coexist within Proverbs itself. Verse 18’s safety promise remains alongside that
concession and Job’s undeserved ruin (Job 2:3, 7).

See [[The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 28; comparisons with
Proverbs, Exodus, and Job use the stored WEB. No new source-language claim.

## Significance

The better-than form is the strongest textual evidence that Proverbs itself does not read its promises as
guarantees, and so the strongest support for the wiki's practice of reading the sentence collection
alongside Job rather than against it. It connects [[Wealth and Possessions]], [[The Fear of God]],
[[Integrity]], and [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]].

### Three in one chapter — Proverbs 16:8, 19, 32

[[The Plans of the Heart]] brings three more into the wiki. *"Better is a little with righteousness, than
great revenues with injustice"* (16:8) repeats 15:16's concession with *righteousness* in the place of
*the fear of Yahweh*. *"It is better to be of a lowly spirit with the poor, than to divide the plunder
with the proud"* (16:19) sets humility-with-poverty against the gang's plunder of 1:13. And *"One who is
slow to anger is better than the mighty; one who rules his spirit, than he who takes a city"* (16:32)
moves the form from goods to power: self-rule ranks above conquest. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 1:13;
15:16; 16:8, 19, 32.

[[A Dry Morsel with Quietness]] adds the fourth on a poor table. *"Better is a dry morsel with
quietness, than a house full of feasting with strife"* (17:1) is closest to 15:17's *"dinner of herbs,
where love is"*, and its *feasting* is *zibḥê-rîb*, *sacrifices of strife* — the sacrificial meal of a
house that is quarrelling over it (Hebrew from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*). The
comparison is again between a little with peace and plenty without it; the saying does not say the
dry morsel is the reward of the quiet. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15:17; 17:1.

### Read beside Ecclesiastes 4:6, 9

The form crosses into Ecclesiastes. *"Better is a handful, with quietness, than two handfuls with
labor and chasing after wind"* (4:6) is closest to Proverbs 17:1's *"dry morsel with quietness"*, and
like the Proverbs sayings it concedes the larger amount before ranking it lower. What it sets against
the two handfuls is not the fear of Yahweh, love, or righteousness, as in 15:16–17 and 16:8, but the
Preacher's own refrain, *"chasing after wind"*. Its neighbor at 4:5 rules out the empty hands of the
fool, so the handful is a portion actually worked for. Three verses later the form ranks people rather
than goods: *"Two are better than one"* (4:9). See [[Two Are Better Than One]]. Source: stored WEB
Proverbs 15:16–17; 16:8; 17:1; Ecclesiastes 4:5–6, 9.

### Ecclesiastes 4:13 — the poor youth and the king

The Preacher's third *better* in one chapter ranks people by wisdom: *"Better is a poor and wise
youth than an old and foolish king"* (4:13). It is closest to Proverbs 19:1 and 28:6, where *the
poor who walks in his integrity* is better than the perverse fool or the perverse rich. Like them it
concedes the advantage — age, a crown — before ranking it lower. What it adds is the reason the
king is foolish: he *"doesn’t know how to receive admonition any more."* Unlike the Proverbs sayings,
the comparison is then put to the test of time, and 4:16 judges even the wise youth's popularity
vanity. See [[The Youth and the Old King]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19:1; 28:6; Ecclesiastes
4:13–16.
