# Gentleness Before the Ruler - Proverbs 15.1, 25.15, and Ecclesiastes 10.4

## Passages involved

- [[A Gentle Answer]] — Proverbs 15:1.
- [[The Glory of Kings]] — Proverbs 25:15.
- [[The King and the Limits of Power]] — Ecclesiastes 8:3.
- [[The Forgotten Wise Man]] — Ecclesiastes 9:17; 10:4.

## Analysis

Three sayings commend soft speech or patient bearing in the face of anger or power.
*"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger"* (Proverbs 15:1). *"By
patience a ruler is persuaded. A soft tongue breaks the bone"* (Proverbs 25:15). *"If the
spirit of the ruler rises up against you, don’t leave your place; for gentleness lays great
offenses to rest"* (Ecclesiastes 10:4).

The settings differ. Proverbs 15:1 is general — any wrath, any answer. Proverbs 25:15 sits in
the collection of royal and court sayings and concerns persuading a ruler. Ecclesiastes 10:4
addresses the subordinate whom the ruler's anger has already reached, and adds an instruction
the Proverbs do not give: hold your place. Ecclesiastes 8:3 gives related counsel, *"Don’t be
hasty to go out of his presence."* Whether *place* in 10:4 means a literal post at court or
one's composure is left open on the passage page.

Ecclesiastes 9:17, immediately before, supplies the same preference in another form: *"The
words of the wise heard in quiet are better than the cry of him who rules among fools."*

**What the sayings claim, and what they do not.** None promises that gentleness always
succeeds, and none forbids a hard word in every case — Proverbs 27:5 ranks *"open rebuke"*
above hidden love ([[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]). Ecclesiastes 10:4 gives the ground (*great offenses* laid to rest) without promising the
outcome, consistent with the book's refusal to guarantee results from wisdom (9:11). No
literary dependence is asserted; the shared English vocabulary of gentleness and softness
belongs to the WEB and is not a claim about identical Hebrew terms.

## Significance

[[Strife and Peacemaking]], [[Language and Speech]], and [[Kingship and Empire]] converge on
the subordinate's position before power. The wise response to anger is restraint of speech
and steadiness of place, not flight and not answering heat with heat.
