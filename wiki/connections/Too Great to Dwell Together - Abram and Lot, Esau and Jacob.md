# Too Great to Dwell Together - Abram and Lot, Esau and Jacob

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 13:5–12** — *"The land was not able to bear them, that they might live together; for their
  substance was great, so that they could not live together."* Abram gives Lot the choice; Lot takes
  the plain of the Jordan — [[Abram and Lot Separate]]
- **Genesis 36:6–8** — *"For their substance was too great for them to dwell together, and the land of
  their travels couldn't bear them because of their livestock. Esau lived in the hill country of
  Seir."* — [[The Descendants of Esau]]
- **Genesis 25:5–6** — Abraham gives gifts to the sons of the concubines and sends them east, *"while
  he yet lived"* — [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]]
- **Genesis 21:8–14** — Hagar and Ishmael sent away, over a quarrel rather than over pasture —
  [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]]
- **Deuteronomy 2:4–5** — *"I have given Mount Seir to Esau for a possession"*

## Analysis

**Genesis 36:7 is Genesis 13:6 with the names changed.**

| | **Genesis 13:6** | **Genesis 36:7** |
|---|---|---|
| the cause | *for their substance was great* | *for their substance was too great* |
| the consequence | *so that they could not live together* | *for them to dwell together* |
| the land | *the land was not able to bear them* | *the land of their travels couldn't bear them* |
| the reason | flocks, herds, and tents on both sides | *because of their livestock* |

**Same two clauses, same causal order, same vocabulary.** ***Rəkûš***, *substance*, is the word in
both; ***yāḵōl*** and ***nāśāʾ***, *able* and *bear*, are the verbs in both; and in both the result is
that two relatives part and one of them leaves the land of promise. **The likeness is close enough to
read as deliberate quotation**, and Genesis makes no comment on it either time.

### What the repetition is for

**It is the book's polite way of clearing the stage**, and it is remarkably consistent about the
manner.

Genesis separates four pairs of relatives, and **not one of the separations is a quarrel between the
principals**:

| who leaves | the stated cause | who chooses |
|---|---|---|
| **Lot** (13) | too much livestock for one pasture | Lot, given first refusal |
| **Ishmael** (21) | Sarah's demand, *"the thing was very grievous in Abraham's sight"* | Sarah, ratified by God |
| **Keturah's sons** (25) | none given; gifts, and *eastward, to the east country* | Abraham |
| **Esau** (36) | too much livestock for one pasture | Esau, unprompted |

**The first and last are the same story told twice, at the two ends of the book.** In both, prosperity
rather than enmity is what makes the household too big for the country; in both, the man who leaves is
the one the covenant is not running through; and in both, Genesis takes care to say that nobody was
wronged. See [[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]] and [[Call and Election]].

### The one difference, and it is the whole difference

**At 13:8–9 Abram speaks**, and what he says is the most generous sentence in the chapter: *"Please,
let there be no strife between you and me … If you go to the left hand, then I will go to the right."*
He is the elder, the caller, and the holder of the promise, and **he hands the choice to his nephew**,
who takes the well-watered plain and ends up in Sodom.

**At 36:6 nobody says anything at all.** Esau *"took his wives, his sons, his daughters, and all the
members of his household … and went into a land away from his brother Jacob."* **No offer, no choice,
no negotiation, no farewell** — the verb does the whole work. **The brother who was cheated twice
removes himself without being asked**, one chapter after the two of them buried their father, and the
narrator does not pause over it.

**This is of a piece with everything Genesis 33 did.** Esau ran, embraced, wept, refused the staged
gift once, took it when pressed, offered his company twice, was declined twice, and went back to Seir
exactly as he said he would — while Jacob's stated plan to follow him there is never shown carried
out. See [[The Reconciliation with Esau]] and [[Esau]]. **The last thing Genesis records him doing is
making room.**

### Prosperity as the engine

**Both passages are about wealth, and neither treats it as a problem in itself.** The land failing to
*bear* two households is a symptom of blessing, not of scarcity — the same blessing 13:2 records of
Abram (*"very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold"*) and 30:43 records of Jacob (*"the man
increased exceedingly"*). **Genesis's recurring difficulty is not famine but success**, and the
recurring remedy is distance. See [[Wealth and Possessions]] and [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

**The one case where the remedy is applied before the trouble starts is Esau's**, which is why the
verse reads as a summary rather than as a scene.

### Where each one ends up

**The choices are not symmetrical in their outcomes, and Genesis is unsentimental about it.**

- **Lot chooses the best land in sight** (13:10, *"like the garden of Yahweh, like the land of
  Egypt"*) and it is destroyed in six chapters, leaving him in a cave above Zoar with two daughters
  and no future but [[Moab (person)]] and [[Ben Ammi]].
- **Esau takes the hill country of Seir**, which is dry, defensible, and his — and Deuteronomy 2:5
  makes it a permanent grant in the same voice that grants Canaan: *"I have given Mount Seir to Esau
  for a possession."* **He is the only man in Genesis who separates from the covenant line and gains
  a country by it.** See [[Edom]] and [[Land and Sojourning]].

**And the brother who stays does not get a country.** Genesis 37:1, the verse after the register ends:
*"Jacob lived in the land of his father's travels, in the land of Canaan"* — ***məgûrê***, the land of
his **sojournings**. **Esau possesses; Jacob sojourns.** The contrast is drawn in consecutive verses,
and the book leaves it there.

## Significance

- **It shows Genesis reusing its own sentences.** The verbal overlap between 13:6 and 36:7 is one of
  the clearest cases in the book of a later passage written in the language of an earlier one, and it
  costs nothing to notice: the reader who remembers Abram and Lot reads Esau's departure as the
  generous act it is.
- **It puts the separations in a set.** Four times a relative leaves, four times without a fight
  between the men involved, and each time the book gets down to one thread again.
- **And it is the last kind word Genesis says about Esau.** It says it in a genealogy, without
  adjectives, by giving his departure the wording it had given Abraham's.
