# Who Provides for the Raven - Job 38.41, Psalm 147.9, and Luke 12.24

## Passages involved

- **Job 38:39–41** — *"Can you hunt the prey for the lioness, or satisfy the appetite of the young lions …?
  Who provides for the raven his prey, when his young ones cry to God, and wander for lack of food?"* —
  [[Out of the Whirlwind]]
- **Job 10:16** — *"If my head is held high, you hunt me like a lion."* — [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]]
- **Job 19:7; 30:20** — *"I cry for help, but there is no justice"*; *"I cry to you, and you do not answer
  me"* — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] and [[Now They Mock Me]]
- **Psalm 104:21, 27–28** — *"The young lions roar after their prey, and seek their food from God … These
  all wait for you, that you may give them their food in due season. You give to them; they gather. You open
  your hand; they are satisfied with good."*
- **Psalm 147:9** — *"He provides food for the livestock, and for the young ravens when they call."*
- **Matthew 6:26** — *"See the birds of the sky, that they don't sow, neither do they reap, nor gather into
  barns. Your heavenly Father feeds them. Aren't you of much more value than they?"*
- **Luke 12:24** — *"Consider the ravens: they don't sow, they don't reap, they have no warehouse or barn,
  and God feeds them. How much more valuable are you than birds!"*
- **Luke 12:6–7** — the sparrows, *"Not one of them is forgotten by God"*

## Analysis

**The same picture, said three ways.** In Job it is a question; in the Psalms, praise; in the Gospels, an
argument. The content does not change: animals that cannot provide for themselves are provided for, and
the provider is God.

**In Job the question is aimed, and its target is Job's own complaint.** The raven's young *"cry to God"* —
*yəšawwəʿû*, the verb of Job's *ʾăšawwaʿ* at 19:7 and 30:20, the cry he said went unanswered. The lion is
the animal Job compared God to (10:16), and now God hunts *for* it. Neither point is drawn out. God does not
say *and you, too*; the speech never applies its creatures to Job. It lets him hear that the cry of a
fledgling is heard, and leaves the inference to him — which is why it can be read either as reassurance or
as a reminder of how small his complaint is in the scale of what God attends to. Both readings have
defenders; the text supports the fact of the feeding and does not settle the tone.

**The psalms make the same observation the ground of praise.** Psalm 104 sets the lions and the creatures of
the sea together, all waiting on the open hand; Psalm 147:9 names the young ravens *"when they call"* — the
call, again, of the young. Neither psalm argues from the animals to people. They praise God for the
animals' sake.

**Jesus completes the argument the whirlwind withholds.** *"Consider the ravens … God feeds them. How much
more valuable are you than birds!"* (Luke 12:24). Matthew has *"the birds of the sky"*; Luke names the
raven, Job's bird and the psalm's. The *how much more* is precisely the step Job 38 does not take, and the
setting explains why: Jesus is speaking against anxiety about food and clothing to people he is sending
out, where God in Job is answering a man who has accused him. The same image serves both, but only in the
Gospel is it turned into a promise.

**A note on the raven.** It was unclean food (Leviticus 11:15, by reference) and the first bird out of the
ark, which *"went back and forth"* and did not return (Genesis 8:7; see [[The Waters Recede]]). That the
creature God is said to feed is a scavenger and an unclean one is part of the point in every passage: the
provision is not a reward.

## Significance

- It shows one of the whirlwind's images passing into the Bible's ordinary language of providence, and
  then into the teaching of Jesus — the clearest case of a line from Job 38–39 becoming common property.
- It is a measure of what the whirlwind leaves unsaid. Job is shown the fed raven and is not told he will
  be fed; the epilogue does that in prose (42:10–17).
