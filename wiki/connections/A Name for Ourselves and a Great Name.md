# A Name for Ourselves and a Great Name

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 6:4** — *"the mighty men who were of old, men of renown"* — *ʾanšê haššēm*, literally
  **men of the name**. See [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- **Genesis 11:4** — *"let's make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of
  the whole earth."* See [[The Tower of Babel]].
- **Genesis 12:2** — *"I will make of you a great nation. I will bless you and make your name great.
  You will be a blessing."* — [[The Call of Abram]]
- **2 Samuel 7:9** — *"I will make you a great name, like the name of the great ones who are in the
  earth"*, said to David.
- **Isaiah 56:5** — to the eunuchs, *"an everlasting name that will not be cut off"*.
- **Philippians 2:6–11** — one who did not consider equality with God something to be grasped, and is
  given *"the name which is above every name"*.

## Analysis

**Three verses in Genesis use the same noun, and the difference between them is entirely
grammatical.** *Šēm* — name, fame, reputation, the thing that outlasts you — occurs at 6:4, 11:4, and
12:2 in three consecutive movements of the book, and each time the question is who the subject of the
verb is.

- At **6:4** the *ʾanšê haššēm* have it. Nobody is said to give it or make it; they simply are the
  men of the name, and the next verse is *"Yahweh saw that the wickedness of man was great in the
  earth."* The reputation is stated and the world is drowned.
- At **11:4** a people set out to manufacture it: *naʿăśeh-lānû šēm*, **let us make for ourselves a
  name**. First-person plural, reflexive, with the purpose clause attached — *lest we be scattered*.
  They are scattered, and the name they get is [[Babel]], which the narrator glosses as *confusion*.
- At **12:2** God says *waʾăgaddəlâ šəmekā*, **and I will make your name great**, to one man who has
  been told to leave his country, his relatives, and his father's house. He is given no city, no
  tower, and no plan.

**Three verses separate the last two.** Genesis 11:9 ends the primeval history with a name taken; the
call of Abram begins the patriarchal history with a name given. Nothing in the book's design is more
economical than that, and it is easy to miss because the genealogy of
[[From Shem to Abram]] sits between them — twenty-three verses in which nobody speaks, God is not
named, and the family that will be given a name loses its lifespan, its firstborn, its fertility,
and its destination.

**What changes is not the desire but the source.** Genesis does not treat wanting a name as the
problem — it promises one, twice over, to Abram and to David, and Isaiah extends the promise to the
people least able to leave descendants. A name that lasts is a good the Bible is willing to hand out.
The distinction it draws is between a name seized and a name conferred, and the vocabulary is
identical on both sides of it.

**The purpose clauses are the tell.** Babel builds *"lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of the
whole earth"*; Abram is told *"You will be a blessing… All the families of the earth will be blessed
through you."* One project is defensive and closed — its aim is that nothing leave the plain of
Shinar. The other is centrifugal by definition, and it uses [[The Table of Nations]]'s own word,
*mišpəḥōt*, families, for what it is aimed at. The builders wanted to stay together and were
scattered; Abram is sent out and becomes the means by which the scattered are blessed.

**The New Testament closes it with a third grammar.** Philippians 2:6–11 has one who *"didn't
consider equality with God a thing to be grasped"* but emptied himself, and it is *therefore* that
God *"highly exalted him, and gave to him the name which is above every name."* Grasping and
receiving are set as alternatives in the same sentence, and the movement is down before up — the
exact inverse of a tower. It is worth noting that the Greek is not quoting Genesis and makes no
allusion to Babel; the connection is thematic, and it works because the Bible's whole treatment of
*šēm* has been building the alternative since chapter 6.

**One further irony belongs here.** *Šēm* is also the name of Noah's eldest son — [[Shem]], the word
itself. His line is the one Genesis follows, and it is his genealogy that occupies the verses between
Babel's name and Abram's. The book that ends the primeval history with a failed attempt to make a
name resumes by tracing the line of a man who *is* one.

## Significance

The connection sets the terms for everything Genesis does after chapter 11. The primeval history's
last human act is an attempt at self-establishment, and it fails in the terms it set for itself: the
builders get neither the name nor the unity they wanted. Three verses later God begins the same
project from the other end, unasked, with a childless man of seventy-five, and states the outcome as
a promise rather than a plan.

It also supplies the standing biblical answer to the ambition itself, which is not that greatness is
wrong but that it is not something you can build for yourself. *"What do you have that you didn't
receive?"* (1 Corinthians 4:7) is the same argument in one line, and Philippians 2 is the same
argument as a hymn about someone who went the other way down.
