## Analysis

**Proverbs warns about pleasure from outside.** Proverbs 14:13 says, *"Even in laughter the heart
may be sorrowful, and mirth may end in heaviness."* Proverbs 21:17 says, *"He who loves pleasure
shall be a poor man. He who loves wine and oil shall not be rich."* Proverbs 20:1 adds that
*"Whoever is led astray by"* wine and beer *"is not wise."* Each is a general observation about
what pleasure does to an ordinary person: it hides sorrow, it impoverishes, it misleads.

**Ecclesiastes tests it from inside.** The Preacher sets out to *"test you with mirth"* (2:1), and
two of the Proverbs warnings are removed by the conditions of his experiment. He cheers his flesh
with wine *"my heart yet guiding me with wisdom"* (2:3), so he is not led astray in the sense of
Proverbs 20:1; and he has the treasure of kings (2:8), so the love of pleasure does not make him
poor, as Proverbs 21:17 warns. His result agrees with Proverbs 14:13 more than with either of the
others: the trouble with mirth is not what it costs but what it fails to produce. *"I said of
laughter, 'It is foolishness'; and of mirth, 'What does it accomplish?'"* (2:2); and at the end,
*"there was no profit under the sun"* (2:11).

**Where they differ.** Proverbs ties pleasure to loss — poverty, heaviness, folly. Ecclesiastes
2:10 does not: *"my heart rejoiced because of all my labor, and this was my portion."* The joy was
real. What it lacked was lasting gain. Proverbs offers a reason to avoid the love of pleasure;
Ecclesiastes offers a reason not to expect anything more from it than it gives. These are
different claims, and neither page should be read as correcting the other.

**Ecclesiastes against itself.** The book's own later verdicts pull both ways. 7:4 sets *"the
heart of fools"* in *"the house of mirth"*; 8:15 says *"Then I commended mirth."* 2:24, 3:13, and
5:18–19 call eating, drinking, and enjoying good in one's labor a portion and *"the gift of God"*
(3:13). The trial of 2:1–11 does not mention God, and its verdict is about pleasure pursued as a
project. Whether the later commendations answer it — enjoyment received as a gift rather than
sought as profit — is a common reading, but it is an interpretation; the text sets the statements
side by side without stating how they relate. The remaining units will be connected here as they are
ingested.

**The first commendation is now ingested.** [[The Labor Left to Another]] (Ecclesiastes
2:18–26) locates the affirmation inside despair over a legacy and a restless heart. Eating,
drinking, and enjoying good in labor are from God's hand (2:24), and joy is given with wisdom
and knowledge (2:26). The shift is not from a failed experiment to a guarantee of lasting
profit. Enjoyment is acknowledged within the unresolved problem, and the closing vanity
verdict remains. Reading this as received gift rather than secured gain is an interpretation
of the juxtaposition, not an explicit distinction stated by the text. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 2:18–26.

### Enjoyment within the times — Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

[[A Time for Every Purpose]] now supplies the second ingested commendation. Weeping and
laughter, mourning and dancing each have a time (3:4); rejoicing and doing good are commended
(12), and eating, drinking, and enjoying good in labor are God's gift (13). The question of
profit (9), the burden (10), and limited understanding (11) remain in the same unit. The
affirmation is therefore not a denial of grief or a guarantee that pleasure makes the whole
work intelligible. It strengthens the reading of enjoyment as a present gift without
establishing that the earlier trial achieved lasting gain. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes
3:1–15; 2:10–11, 24–26.

### A portion before an unseen future — Ecclesiastes 3:22

[[From Dust to Dust]] again commends rejoicing, now after observed injustice and the shared
death of humans and animals. One's works are the setting and one's portion is the limit;
the concluding question asks who can show a person what will follow. Unlike 3:13, this
verse does not expressly repeat the gift formula. It continues enjoyment within unresolved
limits rather than providing the lasting gain denied in 2:11. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes
2:10–11; 3:13, 16–22.

### Enjoyment beside tears — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

[[The Tears of the Oppressed]] interrupts any reading of 3:22 as a description of everyone's
present experience. The observer now sees victims without comfort and prefers the dead and
the one who has never seen evil to the living. Enjoyment commended and oppression lamented
are both claims of this speaker. The text does not offer a formula dissolving the tension,
and receiving enjoyment cannot be made a reason to ignore the victims. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 3:22–4:3.

### The power to enjoy wealth — Ecclesiastes 5:18–20

[[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]] adds a distinction within divine giving: God
has given riches and wealth and has also given power to eat of it and rejoice in labor
(19). Joy occupies the recipient's heart during given days (18, 20). The unit has already
pictured harmful wealth and eating in darkness (13–17), so the affirmation does not
describe every wealthy person's experience. The now-ingested 6:2 in
[[Riches Without Enjoyment]] expressly describes wealth from God without power to enjoy it.
The gift reading of enjoyment is strengthened, while the tension with suffering and unsatisfied desire
remains. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20; 6:1–2.

### The power withheld — Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

[[Riches Without Enjoyment]] now brings the anticipated contrast into the ingested wiki.
God gives wealth but no power to eat of it (2); many children and years cannot make up
for good unexperienced (3–6). This differs from the solitary worker's self-deprivation
in 4:8 and supplies no stated sin as explanation. The enjoyed gift of 5:19 and its absence
in 6:2 remain separate cases, both attributed to God. See
[[Wealth Given and Enjoyment Withheld - Proverbs 10.22 and Ecclesiastes 5.19-6.2]].
Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:8; 5:18–20; 6:1–9.

### Mirth after reversed outcomes — Ecclesiastes 8:14–17

The commendation anticipated above now has its passage page. After reporting righteous
and wicked people receiving outcomes at odds with their conduct (14), the Preacher
commends mirth, eating, drinking, and joy as a companion in labor during God's given
days (15). The inability to find out God's work follows (16–17). Enjoyment thus occurs
inside the unresolved inquiry, without repairing injustice or making the whole knowable.

**Explicit tension:** mirth judged vanity in 2:1–2, the house of mourning preferred in
7:2–4, and mirth commended in 8:15 all remain. Distinguishing enjoyment received in life
from pleasure pursued as lasting profit is a plausible reading, not an explicit formula
that cancels the different verdicts. Sources: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:1–11; 7:1–4;
8:10–17. No Hebrew lexical identity is asserted.

## Significance

The connection keeps [[Joy and Enjoyment]] from collapsing into a single verdict. Proverbs warns
about pleasure's costs, Ecclesiastes 2 records its limit even when it costs nothing, and later
Ecclesiastes commends it as a gift. All three remain. See also [[Vanity]], [[Folly]], and
[[Wealth and Possessions]].
