# The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out

## Passages involved

- **Job 18:5–6** — *"Yes, the light of the wicked shall be put out, The spark of his fire shall not
  shine. The light shall be dark in his tent. His lamp above him shall be put out."* —
  [[Bildad's Second Speech]]
- **Proverbs 13:9** — *"The light of the righteous shines brightly, but the lamp of the wicked is
  snuffed out."*
- **Proverbs 20:20** — *"Whoever curses his father or his mother, his lamp shall be put out in
  obscurity of darkness."*
- **Proverbs 24:20** — *"for there will be no reward to the evil man. The lamp of the wicked shall
  be snuffed out."*
- **Job 21:17** — Job's reply: *"How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out, that their
  calamity comes on them, that God distributes sorrows in his anger?"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Job 29:3** — Job on his own past: *"when his lamp shone on my head, and by his light I walked
  through darkness."* *(not yet ingested)*
- **2 Samuel 21:17** — David's men swear he will not go out to battle again, *"that you don't quench
  the lamp of Israel."*
- **1 Kings 11:36; 15:4; 2 Kings 8:19** — *"that David my servant may have a lamp always before me
  in Jerusalem"*, the dynastic promise stated three times as a lamp not extinguished.
- **Psalm 132:17** — *"I have ordained a lamp for my anointed."*
- **Revelation 18:23** — of Babylon: *"The light of a lamp will shine no more at all in you."*

## Analysis

The lamp is a household image before it is a moral one. An oil lamp left burning through the night
in a tent or a house meant the household was alive, occupied, and continuing; a dark house at night
meant it was empty or its people were dead. From that the figure takes on two extensions at once —
the **life** of the man and the **continuity of his line** — and biblical usage moves freely between
them. When 1 Kings 11:36 promises David a *lamp* in Jerusalem it means a descendant on the throne.
When Proverbs 20:20 says the lamp of the man who curses his parents *"shall be put out"* it means
he will have no such descendant.

[[Bildad the Shuhite]] uses both senses at once and adds a third. Job 18:5–6 puts out the light of
the wicked, the spark of his fire, the light *in his tent*, and the lamp *above him* — four
extinguishings in two verses, moving from the man to the fire on his hearth to the interior of his
dwelling to the lamp hanging over his head. And then verse 19 finishes the thought: *"He shall have
neither son nor grandson among his people."* The line is out because the lamp is out.

**The proverb Bildad is quoting is genuine, and that is the difficulty.** Proverbs 13:9, 20:20, and
24:20 are canonical, and they say what he says. He is not inventing a doctrine to torment a
neighbour; he is applying the wisdom tradition's own sentence to the case in front of him. What
makes his use of it an offence is the direction of the inference. Proverbs states a general
tendency and offers it as instruction for how to live. Bildad runs it backwards — this man's lamp
has gone out, therefore he is wicked — and a proverb read backwards is a verdict.

**Job answers with a count.** At 21:17 he takes the exact phrase and turns it into a question about
frequency: *"How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out…"* The implied answer is *not
often enough to infer anything*, and the rest of chapter 21 supplies the evidence — the wicked
growing old, their children established, their houses safe, their herds calving without loss, and
their funerals well attended. Job's method there is observational, and it is the correct answer to
a claim about how the world generally goes. See [[The Prosperity of the Wicked]].

**And there is a further irony in Job's own use of the image.** At 29:3 he remembers the days when
God's *"lamp shone on my head"* — the same lamp, hanging over the same head as 18:6's *"his lamp
above him"*, but the light is God's and it is a gift. Bildad's figure and Job's are the same
picture. The difference is that Bildad reads the darkness as a sentence and Job reads it as a
loss.

## Significance

The thread is a small, clean example of the problem the book of Job exists to pose, and it is
useful precisely because the material is so ordinary. Nothing in Proverbs 13:9 is wrong. Nothing in
Job 18:5–6 misquotes it. What the two passages do differently is everything: one is a description
of how life tends to go, offered to a reader deciding how to live, and the other is a diagnosis
delivered to a man whose lamp has already gone out. The same sentence, moved fifteen degrees, stops
being wisdom and becomes an accusation.

This is worth holding when reading the friends anywhere. Their orthodoxy is not the problem, and
God's rebuke at 42:7 is not a rebuke of Proverbs. The doctrine and its abuse are separated by a
question of direction and of who is being addressed, and nothing in the sentence itself marks the
difference — which is why the book had to be forty-two chapters long.

The dynastic use in Samuel and Kings then shows the figure's positive half doing real theological
work: the promise to David is that *the lamp will not be put out*, and 2 Kings 8:19 says God
withheld judgment on Judah *"for David his servant's sake, as he promised him to give to him a lamp
for his children always."* Read against Job 18, the promise is a guarantee against precisely
Bildad's sentence — a line that will not be ended — and it is given not because the recipients
deserve it but because it was promised. Revelation 18:23 closes the figure by putting out the lamp
of a city rather than a man.
