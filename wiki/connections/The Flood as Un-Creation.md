# The Flood as Un-Creation

## Passages involved

- Genesis 1:1–2:3 — [[The Creation of the World]]
- Genesis 6:9–7:24 — [[Noah and the Flood]]
- Genesis 8:1–22 — the wind over the waters, dry land reappearing, and the resolve at 8:21 —
  [[The Waters Recede]]
- Genesis 9:1–7 — the blessing of 1:28 reissued, with changes ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Psalm 104:5–9 — the waters set a boundary they may not pass
- Jeremiah 4:23–26 — *"I saw the earth, and, behold, it was waste and void"*: the same reversal used
  of a coming judgment on Judah
- 2 Peter 3:5–7 — the world *"overflowed with water"*, and the present heavens reserved for fire

## Analysis

**The flood is narrated in the vocabulary of Genesis 1, run backwards.** This is not an
interpretation imposed on the text; the correspondences are lexical, and they come in order.

| Genesis 1 | Undone at |
|---|---|
| *"darkness was on the surface of the deep"* (*təhôm*), 1:2 | *"all the fountains of the great deep"* (*təhôm rabbâ*) burst open, 7:11 |
| An expanse divides the waters from the waters — day two, 1:6–8 | The deep opens below and *"the sky's windows"* above, 7:11: the barrier breached from both sides at once |
| The waters gathered, dry land appears — day three, 1:9–10 | *"All the high mountains that were under the whole sky were covered"*, 7:19; *"all on the dry land … died"*, 7:22 |
| Birds, sea creatures, livestock, creeping things, humanity — days five and six, 1:20–27 | *"birds, livestock, animals, every creeping thing … and every man"*, 7:21 — the same roll, for erasure |
| *"Be fruitful and multiply"*, 1:22, 1:28 | The blessing goes unmentioned through the whole account, and is not renewed until 9:1 |
| *"God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good"*, 1:31 | *"God saw the earth, and saw that it was corrupt"*, 6:12 — same verb of inspection, inverted verdict |

The order matters. Day three is undone before day two is fully undone — the mountains are covered
after the fountains open — but the two barriers that made the world habitable, the separation of
waters above from waters below and the separation of sea from land, are the two that fail. What is
left at 7:24 is a dark, water-covered earth with the breath of life gone out of it, which is a
description of Genesis 1:2.

**And then the re-creation runs forwards.** Genesis 8:1–2 is the account's answer, and it uses the
same words in the same order: *"God made a wind to pass over the earth"* — *rûaḥ*, the word of 1:2,
where *"God's Spirit was hovering over the surface of the waters"*. Then the fountains and the
windows are stopped, the waters recede, the mountaintops appear, dry land emerges, the creatures
come out *"that they may breed abundantly in the earth, and be fruitful, and multiply"* (8:17), and
in 9:1 the blessing of 1:28 is given again to a man and his sons. Genesis is not telling the story of
a disaster with a happy ending. It is telling the story of the world being made twice.

**The theological claim this carries.** Two, in fact, and they pull in different directions, which
is why the structure is worth noticing rather than merely admiring.

The first is that **creation is held, not self-sustaining.** The waters above and below did not go
away on day two; they were *divided*, and the account of the flood is what happens when the division
is withdrawn. Psalm 104:9 makes the same point positively — *"You have set a boundary that they may
not pass over, that they don't turn again to cover the earth"* — and the boundary is described as
something God set and maintains, not as a property of the water.

The second is that **the second world is not the first one.** Genesis 9 reissues the blessing with
alterations that are easy to read past: the dominion verbs *kābaš* and *rādâ* are simply gone, and
the animals now live in *"the fear of you and the dread of you"* (9:2), *"delivered into your
hand"*; flesh is given as food (9:3), which 1:29's vegetarian grant did not; and a law of blood is
instituted (9:4–6). The flood does not restore Eden.
It restarts the world on harder terms, and Genesis 8:21 says why in the same breath as the promise —
*"the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth"* — which is the verdict of 6:5 restated as
the reason for **never doing this again**. The same fact grounds the destruction and the promise not
to repeat it.

**Jeremiah reads it exactly this way.** Jeremiah 4:23 is the only other place in the Hebrew Bible
where *tōhû wābōhû* — *waste and void*, the phrase of Genesis 1:2 — appears, and Jeremiah uses it of
the judgment coming on Judah: *"I saw the earth, and, behold, it was waste and void; and the heavens,
and they had no light … the fruitful field was a wilderness, and all its cities were broken down."*
A prophet describing an invasion reaches for the vocabulary of Genesis 1 and un-creates the land
verse by verse. Whoever wrote that had read the flood account the way this page reads it.

## Significance

**It settles what kind of judgment the flood is.** Not a punishment scaled to an offence, and not a
natural catastrophe theologically interpreted after the fact — a withdrawal. The account's language
says that God's response to a world that had *corrupted* itself (*šāḥat*, 6:11–12) was to let it be
*ruined* (*šāḥat*, 6:13, 17), and the mechanism is the un-making of the distinctions that made it a
world. Genesis is claiming that the alternative to creation is not chaos arriving from outside but
order being let go.

**It sets the terms for every later use of the flood.** 2 Peter 3:5–7 makes the argument explicitly
— the world that then was, being *"overflowed with water, perished"*, and the present one is
*"reserved for fire"* — and its force depends entirely on the flood having been an un-creation
rather than a large storm: what happened once to the whole order can happen again to the whole
order. Every New Testament appeal to the days of Noah assumes this reading. See
[[As It Was in the Days of Noah]] and [[The Flood and Baptism]].

**And it explains why the narrative's centre is a memory rather than a rescue.** If the flood is the
undoing of Genesis 1, then nothing in the water can turn it — no ark, no righteousness, no
seaworthiness. What turns it is 8:1, *"God remembered Noah"*, and the wind that passes over the
waters immediately after is the Spirit of 1:2 starting again. The structure puts the whole weight of
the world's survival on a single act of divine recollection, and the un-creation reading is what
makes that placement legible.
