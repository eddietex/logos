# A Steed in Pharaoh's Chariots - Exodus 14-15, 1 Kings 10.28-29, and Song of Solomon 1.9

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 14:7, 9, 23** — [[The Crossing of the Sea]]: *"All the horses and chariots of
  Pharaoh, his horsemen, and his army overtook them"* (14:9).
- **Exodus 15:1, 4, 19** — [[The Song of the Sea]]: *"He has cast Pharaoh's chariots and his
  army into the sea"* (15:4).
- **Deuteronomy 17:16** *(not yet ingested; WEB verified)* — the king *"shall not multiply horses
  to himself, nor cause the people to return to Egypt, to the end that he may multiply horses"*.
- **1 Kings 10:28–29** *(not yet ingested; WEB verified)* — *"The horses which Solomon had were
  brought out of Egypt"*; a chariot cost six hundred shekels of silver and a horse a hundred
  and fifty.
- **Song of Solomon 1:9** — [[Delight and the Unawakened Love]]: *"I have compared you, my
  love, to a steed in Pharaoh's chariots."*

## Analysis

**One image, three uses.** In Exodus, Pharaoh's horses and chariots are the strength of the
enemy, the force that pursues Israel and is thrown into the sea. Deuteronomy turns that memory
into a limit on Israel's own king: he is not to multiply horses or send the people back to
Egypt for them. 1 Kings reports that Solomon's horses came out of Egypt, and that his merchants
traded chariots and horses on to other kings. The Song, a book headed with Solomon's name,
makes Pharaoh's horses a compliment to a woman.

**What the parallel can and cannot say.** The Song does not mention the exodus, Deuteronomy's
law, or Solomon's trade. Nothing in Song 1:9 refers to any of them, and the wiki claims no
allusion. What the texts share is an image: in each of them, Egypt's horses and chariots stand
for strength and splendor. Exodus fears them, Deuteronomy restrains them, 1 Kings counts them as
wealth, and the Song admires them. The comparison shows how far the same image can travel.

**The Song's horse is female.** The Hebrew of Song 1:9 is *ləsusātî*, which contains a feminine
form of *sûs*, horse (Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*). The WEB gives *steed*. Why a mare
in Pharaoh's chariots is a compliment is debated: splendor of harness, which fits the jewelry of
1:10–11, or the stir a mare causes among chariot horses. See the passage page's notes. No claim
is made here about the Hebrew of Exodus, Deuteronomy, or 1 Kings.

**Against Solomon?** Some readers set Song 1:9 beside Deuteronomy 17:16 and 1 Kings 10:28 and
hear irony: the king who multiplied Egyptian horses praised for comparing his love to one. The
text gives no sign of irony, and the wiki does not adopt the reading. It is recorded only
because the three verses do sit side by side.
