# Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 26:12–17** — Isaac reaps a hundredfold, grows very great, is envied, has his wells filled
  in, and is told *"Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we"* — [[Isaac in Gerar]]
- **Exodus 1:7–14** — the children of Israel are fruitful and multiply and fill the land; a new king
  says *"the people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we"*, and sets taskmasters
  over them
- **Genesis 12:10–20** — the first descent, the first enrichment in a foreign country, and the first
  escorted expulsion — [[Abram in Egypt]]
- **Genesis 47:27** — the family in Goshen: *"they got themselves possessions therein, and were
  fruitful, and multiplied exceedingly"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Exodus 12:35–36** — the departure with the Egyptians' silver, gold, and clothing

## Analysis

### The two sentences

> **26:16** Abimelech said to Isaac, “Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we.”

***ʿĀṣamtā mimmennû məʾōd*** — *you have become mighty from us, very.*

> **Exodus 1:9** He said to his people, “Behold, the people of the children of Israel are more and
> mightier than we.”

***Rab wəʿāṣûm mimmennû*** — *many and mighty from us.*

**The same root, the same comparative preposition, the same speaker's position, and the same
conclusion**: a king looks at a foreign household inside his territory, measures it against his own
people, and decides it has become a problem. **The difference is what he does next**, and that
difference is the whole distance between a sojourn and a bondage.

### The shape, side by side

| | **Genesis 26** — Gerar | **Exodus 1** — Egypt |
|---|---|---|
| how they arrived | a famine drove them | a famine drove them |
| what they were told on arrival | *"Live in this land, and I will be with you"* (26:3) | Joseph settled them in the best of the land (47:6) |
| what happens to them | sowed, reaped a hundredfold, *"grew great … very great"* | *"fruitful, and increased abundantly … the land was filled with them"* |
| the reaction | *"The Philistines envied him"* (26:14) | *"They were grieved because of the children of Israel"* (1:12) |
| the king's finding | *"you are much mightier than we"* | *"more and mightier than we"* |
| the measure taken | expulsion; the wells filled in with earth | taskmasters, brick, mortar, and field labour |
| what the family leaves with | flocks, herds, a great household | the Egyptians' silver, gold, and clothing |
| how it ends | a treaty sworn at Beersheba, and peace | ten plagues, and a sea |

**Six of the eight rows correspond**, and the two that do not are the two that matter: **the response,
and the ending.**

### What the parallel is and is not

**It is not a prophecy and Genesis does not present it as one.** Nothing at Gerar is said to
foreshadow Egypt, no character makes the connection, and the two books use the phrase independently
because it is what a frightened ruler says.

**What it is, is a pattern the Torah runs twice and comments on the second time.** Genesis 15:13 had
already told Abram that *"your offspring will live as foreigners in a land that is not theirs, and
will serve them. They will afflict them four hundred years"* — the terms of Exodus stated as a
prediction before either episode. **Genesis 26 is the small version and it resolves peacefully**: the
king expels rather than enslaves, the general goes home without fighting, and the chapter ends with a
feast and an oath. **Exodus 1 is the same fear with the brakes off.** See
[[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]] and
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

### And the vocabulary is the covenant's own

***ʿĀṣûm***, mighty, is not a neutral word in the Torah. It is one of the terms of the promise itself:
*"Abraham will surely become a great and mighty nation"* — *gôy gādôl wəʿāṣûm* — at Genesis 18:18, and
the same pairing runs through Deuteronomy's descriptions of Israel. **So both kings are, without
knowing it, reporting the fulfilment of the promise in the promise's own vocabulary**, and both treat
it as a threat. The narrator of Exodus 1:7 says it outright by stacking the creation blessing's verbs
— *fruitful*, *multiplied*, *filled the land* (Genesis 1:28; 9:1) — on the household a king is about
to enslave.

**In Genesis 26 the same thing happens in miniature**: *"Yahweh blessed him"* (26:12) is the narrator's
explanation for the harvest, and the envy, the filled wells, and the eviction follow in three verses.
**Blessing is what provokes the reaction, in both texts.** See [[Envy]] and [[Blessing]].

### The wells and the bricks

The two measures are worth putting side by side because they are the same instinct at different
scale. **Stopping a well with earth does not transfer an asset; it destroys one**, and it removes the
possibility of a household living there at all — eviction by making the ground uninhabitable.
**Taskmasters do the opposite**: they keep the household and take its labour. The first gets rid of
the problem, the second monetizes it, and Exodus 1:10's *"lest they multiply"* explains why the second
king cannot simply do what the first did. See
[[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].

## Significance

- **The Torah's account of Israel in Egypt has a rehearsal in Genesis, and it is Isaac's.** The
  patriarch the book gives the least to do is the one whose single chapter contains the whole
  mechanism of the exodus in small: famine, sojourn, prosperity, envy, a king's arithmetic, and an
  expulsion.
- **And it is the one time the mechanism stops.** The same fear that produces four hundred years of
  bondage produces, in Genesis 26, a treaty — because the king who says *"you are much mightier than
  we"* is the same king who says *"We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you"* (26:28), and acts on the
  second sentence as well as the first.
- **Genesis 26:2's prohibition is what keeps the two apart.** *"Don't go down into Egypt"* is the only
  prohibition in the patriarchal promises, and the chapter that contains it is the one in which the
  family gets the Egypt experience without going to Egypt. **When Jacob is finally told at the same
  well to go down (46:3), the brakes come off.**

## The middle term — Jacob in Paddan Aram

**The pattern has a third instance between the two, and it is the closest of the three to Exodus.**

[[Jacob's Flocks]] ends ***wayyipṛōṣ hāʾîš məʾōd məʾōd***, *the man burst out exceedingly* (30:43) —
**and Genesis 26:13 had said *"the man became great, and grew more and more until he became very
great."*** **Both verses call the patriarch *the man*, not by name, at the moment of his
enrichment**, and both are immediately followed by the host's resentment: *"the Philistines envied
him"* (26:14), and *"Jacob has taken away all that was our father's"* with a face that is no longer
*"toward him as before"* (31:1–2).

**Where the three cases diverge is what the host then does.**

| | the host's diagnosis | the host's response |
|---|---|---|
| **Isaac in Gerar** | *"We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you"* (26:28) | **expel**, then sue for a treaty |
| **Jacob and Laban** | *"Yahweh has blessed me for your sake"* (30:27) | **detain** — *"stay here"* |
| **Israel in Egypt** | *"the people… are more and mightier than we"* (Exodus 1:9) | **enslave** |

**All three read the situation correctly and none of them is glad of it.** And the middle one is
where the Exodus vocabulary first appears: Jacob's request at 30:25 is ***šalləḥēnî***, *send me
away* — **the verb of Exodus 5:1** — refused by a master who has just admitted that God is prospering
him through the man he is refusing to release. See
[[Blessed for His Sake - Laban's House and Potiphar's]] and
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].
