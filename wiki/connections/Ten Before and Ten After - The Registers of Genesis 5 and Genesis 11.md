# Ten Before and Ten After - The Registers of Genesis 5 and Genesis 11

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 5:1–32** — Adam to Noah, ten generations with ages, remainders, totals, and deaths —
  [[From Adam to Noah]]
- **Genesis 11:10–26** — Shem to Abram, ten generations with ages and remainders, and neither totals
  nor deaths — [[From Shem to Abram]]
- **Genesis 6:3** — *"his days will be one hundred twenty years"*, the sentence the second register's
  numbers carry out
- **1 Chronicles 1:1–4, 24–27** — both lists again, stripped of every number
- **Luke 3:34–38** — both lists again, in reverse, with one extra name

## Analysis

The two registers are the same instrument played twice, and every difference between them is
audible because everything else is identical.

**What is the same.**

- Ten generations, if Adam and Shem are each counted as the first.
- One man per generation — the *linear* form, as against the branching form of
  [[The Table of Nations]] — with the branch that is not being followed either omitted or, as with
  Joktan at 10:26–30, dealt with elsewhere.
- The same two-part entry: *X lived n years and became the father of Y; X lived m years after he
  became the father of Y, and became the father of other sons and daughters.*
- The same ending. *"Noah was five hundred years old, then Noah became the father of Shem, Ham, and
  Japheth"* (5:32). *"Terah lived seventy years, and became the father of Abram, Nahor, and Haran"*
  (11:26). Same construction, a round number, three sons, and the entry deliberately left open — no
  remainder, no total, no death. Both registers stop mid-sentence and hand the reader to a narrative.
- The same trick of listing the followed son first without saying he is the eldest. Shem heads
  Noah's three and Genesis 9:24 and 10:21 both make his seniority doubtful; Abram heads Terah's
  three and the chronology makes his seniority nearly impossible. See the note on Terah's 205 years
  in [[From Shem to Abram]].

**What is different, and it is all in one direction.**

**1. The death formula is gone.** Genesis 5 closes eight of its ten entries with *"and then he
died"*, after stating a total. Genesis 11 states no totals and records no deaths at all — not one of
the nine men in 11:10–25 is said to die. This is the single largest formal difference between the
two chapters and Genesis never comments on it. Genesis 5's refrain was an argument: the sentence of
2:17 proved by attrition, nine hundred years at a time. That argument has been made, and the flood
has ratified it. The second register is not proving that people die; it is delivering a family to
Genesis 12. The only death it records is Terah's, at 11:32, and it is in the narrative section, not
the list.

**2. The numbers halve, twice.** Noah 950, Shem 600 — then Arpachshad 438, Shelah 433, Eber 464 — then
Peleg 239, Reu 239, Serug 230, Nahor 148. Two cliffs, one after Shem and one after Eber, and by the
end a man's whole life is shorter than the *remainder* of most entries in Genesis 5. It keeps falling
after the chapter closes: Terah 205, Abraham 175, Isaac 180, Jacob 147, Joseph 110, Moses 120, and
at last *"the days of our years are seventy"* (Psalm 90:10). The ages at fatherhood collapse with
them — 65 to 187 in the first register, and 29 to 35 in the second once Shem's hundred is past.
Genesis 6:3 announced this in one sentence and then never mentions it again; the second register is
that sentence being executed in a table.

**3. Nothing breaks the form.** Genesis 5 broke its own pattern twice, and both breaks were the point
of the chapter: Enoch, who *"walked with God, and he was not found"*, and Lamech, who spoke the
chapter's only sentence and named his son for relief from the curse. Genesis 11 has no Enoch and no
Lamech. Nine entries, no variation, no speech, no comment, and no God — *Yahweh* and *ʾĕlōhîm* do not
occur anywhere in 11:10–32.

**4. The frame around each is inverted.** Genesis 5 opens by quoting the blessing and the image of
God (5:1–2) and then runs downhill into 6:5, *"every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was
only evil continually."* Genesis 11 opens immediately after judgment — the scattering at Babel, 11:9 —
and runs into 12:1–3, five uses of *bless* in two verses. The first register goes from blessing to
flood; the second goes from scattering to promise. Same machine, opposite gradient.

## Significance

**It tells the reader how to read the second list.** A form that has been established and then
altered says something by the alteration. Genesis 5 taught the reader to hear *"and then he died"*;
Genesis 11 withholds it nine times running. Whether that is meant as relief, as neutrality, or as
nothing at all is not stated — but the reader who has just come through Genesis 5 cannot fail to
notice a genealogy that stops burying people.

**It makes the primeval history symmetrical.** Ten generations from the first man to the flood, ten
from the flood to Abraham, each ending with a man at a round age and three sons, with a narrative
hinge between them. Ten-generation spans are a recognized organizing device, and Matthew 1 does
something comparable with three sets of fourteen — while stating openly (1:17) that it omitted kings
to get the count, which is the clearest evidence in the Bible that ancient genealogies could be
selective by design and not thought inaccurate for it. That bears directly on whether either of these
lists is meant as a gapless father-to-son chain. Against gaps in these two particular lists is the
tight interlocking of the ages, which serves no purpose if the sequence is not continuous; in favour
is the fact that the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and the Hebrew do not agree on the numbers
or, in Genesis 11, even on how many generations there were.

**And it sets up the joke the text never tells.** By the numbers of these two chapters, [[Noah]] is
alive for the first fifty-eight years of Abram's life, [[Shem]] outlives Abraham by thirty-five
years, and [[Eber]] outlives him by sixty-four. The flood survivor and the patriarch are
contemporaries. Genesis supplies every figure required to work this out, never states it, and never
puts any of them in a room together — which is the strongest single indication that the numbers are
doing something other than what a modern reader assumes they are doing.
