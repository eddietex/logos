## Analysis

**God and the ruler, named together.** Exodus 22:28 forbids cursing either in the same breath; in
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read 2026-10-26, numbered 22:27 there) the verse is
*ʾĕlōhîm lōʾ ṯəqallēl wənāśîʾ ḇəʿamməḵā lōʾ ṯāʾōr*, two prohibitions with two different verbs for
cursing. Proverbs 24:21 puts the pairing positively, with one verb governing both objects: *yərāʾ-ʾeṯ-
YHWH bənî wāmeleḵ*, *fear Yahweh, my son, and the king* (same edition). The king is placed after
*"my son"*, almost as an afterthought, which keeps the two from being made equal even while one verb
covers them.

**What the fear is for.** Proverbs gives the reason as danger: *"their calamity will rise suddenly;
the destruction from them both—who knows?"* The rebels' ruin comes from Yahweh and from the king, and
its timing is unknown. The command does not argue that every king is just — Proverbs elsewhere
assumes kings can do wrong (16:12) and keep the wicked in their presence (25:5) — but that rebellion against the order
Yahweh upholds is folly. 21:1, which puts *"the king's heart"* in Yahweh's hand, explains why the two
can be feared together: the king is under Yahweh, not beside him.

**1 Peter 2:17 changes one verb.** *"Fear God. Honor the king."* Peter's sentence has the same two
objects in the same order as Proverbs 24:21, but separates them: God is feared, the king honoured,
just as *"all men"* are. Commentators commonly read this as a deliberate echo of Proverbs 24:21; the
wiki records the similarity of the English and the order, but this pass did not check the Greek of
1 Peter or the Septuagint of Proverbs, and makes no claim about verbal dependence. Romans 13:7 gives
the same distinction in general terms: honour to whom honour, respect to whom respect.

### An oath before God and a human command — Ecclesiastes 8:1–9

Ecclesiastes 8:2 grounds keeping the king's command in the oath to God; verses 3–4 stress
the ruler's ability to act as he pleases. This belongs beside Proverbs 24:21–22's combined
instruction and warning. The oath's terms and occasion are not given in Ecclesiastes.

**Explicit tension:** Exodus 1:16–21, in [[The Oppression in Egypt]], describes the midwives'
refusal of a royal killing order because they feared God, and God's favor toward them.
Ecclesiastes does not supply that scenario or an explicit exception, while its own 8:9
acknowledges harmful human dominion. The passages must not be flattened into an unrestricted
command to carry out every royal demand. Nor does the Exodus case make all rebellion right.

## Significance

The thread shows a single instinct — reverence for God carrying over into restraint toward the
human ruler — expressed three ways: as a ban on cursing (Exodus), as one fear with two objects
(Proverbs), and as two duties kept distinct (1 Peter). The distinction Peter draws is already
implicit in Proverbs' word order and in 21:1. See [[Kingship and Empire]] and [[The Fear of God]].
