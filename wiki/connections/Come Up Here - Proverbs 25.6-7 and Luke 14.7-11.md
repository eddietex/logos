# Come Up Here - Proverbs 25.6-7 and Luke 14.7-11

## Passages involved

- [[The Glory of Kings]] — Proverbs 25:6–7: *"Don't exalt yourself in the presence of the king, or
  claim a place among great men; for it is better that it be said to you, 'Come up here,' than that
  you should be put lower in the presence of the prince, whom your eyes have seen."*
- Luke 14:7–11 — the parable of the wedding guests; verified WEB, not yet ingested.
- Matthew 23:12 — *"Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be
  exalted."* Verified WEB, not yet ingested.
- [[The Glory of Kings]] — Proverbs 25:27, *"nor is it honorable to seek one's own honor"*.

## Analysis

**The same scene, moved from the court to the table.** Proverbs 25:6–7 is advice for a man in the
king's presence: do not take a place among the great, because being called up is better than being
sent down. Luke 14:7–11 sets the scene at a wedding feast. Jesus, watching guests choose *"the best
seats"*, tells them not to sit there, *"since perhaps someone more honorable than you might be invited
by him"*, and the host would say *"Make room for this person"*, and *"Then you would begin, with
shame, to take the lowest place."* Instead: *"go and sit in the lowest place, so that when he who
invited you comes, he may tell you, 'Friend, move up higher.' Then you will be honored in the
presence of all who sit at the table with you."*

The shape is the same in both: a public seating, two possible outcomes, and the one to prefer stated
as *being called up* rather than *being put down*. In both the decisive thing is a word spoken in
front of others — *"Come up here"* in Proverbs, *"Friend, move up higher"* in Luke — and the shame or
honour is witnessed. Proverbs says *"in the presence of the prince"*; Luke, *"in the presence of all
who sit at the table with you."*

**What Luke adds.** Proverbs gives the advice as prudence and states no general rule. Luke closes
with one: *"For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be
exalted"* (14:11), a saying Matthew 23:12 also records in nearly the same words. The passives point
past the host to the one who finally exalts and humbles. Luke also calls the advice a *parable*
(14:7), so the seating at a feast stands for something beyond table manners. The wiki notes the
framing without settling how far it reaches.

**Allusion or shared wisdom.** Luke does not cite Proverbs, and its wording differs from the WEB of
Proverbs 25:7. The resemblance in scene and outcome is close enough that the proverb is commonly
named as the background of the parable; the wiki records it as a thematic parallel rather than a
quotation. The Greek of Luke and any Septuagint form of Proverbs 25:6–7 were not checked in this
pass.

**Within Proverbs.** The chapter returns to the idea at its close: *"nor is it honorable to seek
one's own honor"* (25:27), a line whose Hebrew is difficult (see the passage's Notes). The courtier
who does not *exalt himself* (*tiṯhaddar*, 25:6) and the man who does not *seek his own honour* frame
the chapter's sayings on pride. Proverbs had already said *"Pride goes before destruction"* (16:18)
and *"before honor is humility"* (15:33; 18:12).

## Significance

[[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] is treated in both passages as a social error before it is a moral one:
the self-promoted guest risks a public reversal. Luke makes the reversal a principle of God's dealing.
The link connects the court wisdom of Proverbs to the banquets of the Gospels; see also
[[Come, Eat of My Bread - Proverbs 9, Isaiah 55, and the Gospel Banquets]] and [[Hospitality]].
