# I Will Be With You - The Promise of Presence

## Passages involved

- Genesis 26:3, 24 — to Isaac, in a famine and at Beersheba
- Genesis 28:15 — to Jacob at Bethel: *"I am with you, and will keep you, wherever you go"*
- Genesis 31:3 — to Jacob again, sending him home — [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]]
- Genesis 39:2, 21, 23 — *"Yahweh was with Joseph"*, in a house and in a prison
- Exodus 3:12 — to Moses at the bush
- Joshua 1:5, 9 — *"I will not fail you, nor forsake you"*
- Judges 6:12, 16 — to Gideon in a winepress
- Isaiah 41:10; 43:2 — to the exiles
- Jeremiah 1:8, 19 — to a prophet who says he is too young
- Haggai 1:13; 2:4 — to the builders of the second temple
- Matthew 1:23; 28:20 — *Immanuel*, and *"I am with you always"*
- Hebrews 13:5 — Joshua 1:5 quoted as a reason not to love money

## Analysis

**The formula is not in the Abrahamic promises. It enters Scripture when the promise starts
travelling.**

Abraham is called *into* the land and told to walk it; the one clause of his marriage oath with no
release attached is that Isaac must never leave it (Genesis 24:6, 8). Isaac is forbidden to go down
to Egypt in a famine and is told, in the same breath, *"Live in this land, and I will be with you"*
(26:3). **The presence and the place are still one thing.**

**Jacob is the first bearer of the covenant to leave the country carrying it**, and at Bethel the
promise is rebuilt around that fact. Genesis 28:15 adds four clauses to everything Abraham had
received, and all four are about absence:

- ***Hinnēh ʾānōkî ʿimmāk*** — *behold, I am with you*
- ***ûšmartîkā bəkōl ʾăšer tēlēk*** — *and will keep you, wherever you go*
- ***wahăšibōtîkā ʾel-hāʾădāmâ hazzōʾt*** — *and will bring you again into this land*
- ***lōʾ ʾeʿĕzobkā*** — *I will not leave you*

**Two of the four have no geography in them at all**, and one of them — *wherever you go* — explicitly
cancels it. **The land is still promised; it is no longer the condition of the presence.**

### What the formula does everywhere it appears

**It is always a commissioning, and it always answers an objection.** The pattern is fixed enough to
be listed:

| | the person | the objection | the answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 28:15 | Jacob | leaving the land, hunted | *with you wherever you go*, and a return |
| Exodus 3:11–12 | Moses | *"Who am I?"* | *"Certainly I will be with you"* |
| Joshua 1:5–9 | Joshua | following Moses | *"I will not fail you, nor forsake you"* |
| Judges 6:12–16 | Gideon | *"my family is the poorest"* | *"Surely I will be with you"* |
| Jeremiah 1:6–8 | Jeremiah | *"I am a child"* | *"I am with you to deliver you"* |

**In none of these is the answer an argument.** God does not tell Moses he is capable, or Gideon that
his family is stronger than he thinks. **The objection is conceded and the presence is substituted
for the qualification.** Genesis 28 is the head of that list and the purest case of it, because
Jacob does not even raise an objection — he is asleep.

### The narrator's version

**Genesis 39 turns the promise into a report.** Four times in one chapter the narrator says
***wayhî YHWH ʾet-Yôsēp*** — *Yahweh was with Joseph* — twice in Potiphar's house and twice in the
prison, and both times the sentence is immediately followed by an account of Joseph prospering **in
a place he did not choose to be**. **It is Genesis's own commentary on 28:15**: the phrase that was a
promise to the grandfather is a statement of fact about the great-grandson, made about a slave.

### Immanuel

**Matthew builds his Gospel between two occurrences of it.** The first chapter glosses Isaiah 7:14 —
*"they shall call his name Immanuel, which is, being interpreted, **God with us**"* (1:23) — and the
last sentence of the book is *"behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age"* (28:20).
**The Gospel's first and last words about who Jesus is are the same word**, and the closing promise
is attached, as it always is, to a commission and to a going-out: *"Go therefore and make disciples
of all nations."*

**Hebrews takes the Joshua form of it and puts it to a use nobody would predict.** *"Be free from the
love of money, content with such things as you have, for he has said, 'I will in no way leave you,
neither will I in any way forsake you'"* (13:5). **The presence formula as an argument against
anxiety about possessions** — which lands unexpectedly close to Jacob, whose whole answer to it was
to ask for bread and clothing.

## Significance

**The thread's shape is that God's promises get portable at the moment his people stop being
settled.**

Everything Abraham was given was tied to a country. Jacob is given the country and something else,
because he is about to spend twenty years outside it working for a man who will cheat him ten times.
**What Bethel adds to the covenant is the clause that makes exile survivable**, and it is added
before there is an exile — which is why Isaiah reaches for it when there is one: *"When you pass
through the waters, I will be with you"* (43:2).

**And it costs the recipient nothing and requires nothing.** In every passage listed above the
presence is announced, never earned, and usually announced to someone who has just explained why they
are the wrong person. **Jacob, asleep on a stone with his brother's blessing in his pocket, sets the
standard for that.**
