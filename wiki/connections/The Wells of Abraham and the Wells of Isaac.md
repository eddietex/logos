# The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 21:22–34** — Abimelech and Phicol come to Abraham; a seized well; seven ewe lambs; a
  covenant; the naming of Beersheba. See [[The Treaty at Beersheba]].
- **Genesis 26:12–33** — the Philistines stop up Abraham's wells; Isaac digs them again and restores
  their old names; [[Esek]], [[Sitnah]], [[Rehoboth]]; Abimelech, [[Ahuzzath]], and Phicol come to
  Isaac at Beersheba; an oath; a well named Shibah. See [[Isaac in Gerar]].
- **Genesis 16:7, 14** — the spring in the wilderness on the way to Shur, and *Beer Lahai Roi*, named
  by Hagar. See [[Hagar and Ishmael]].
- **Genesis 21:19** — *"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water"* — eleven verses before
  Abraham's dispute over a different one. See [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].
- **Genesis 24:11–20; 29:1–10; Exodus 2:15–17** — the betrothal well, three times.
- **Numbers 21:16–18** — the Song of the Well: *"Spring up, well! Sing to it."*

## Analysis

### The two treaties, side by side

| | Genesis 21:22–34 | Genesis 26:12–33 |
|---|---|---|
| patriarch | Abraham | Isaac |
| king | Abimelech of Gerar | Abimelech of Gerar |
| officer | Phicol, captain of the army | Phicol, captain of the army |
| third party | — | Ahuzzath, *his friend* |
| who approaches | the king comes to the patriarch | the king comes to the patriarch |
| the king's opening | *"God is with you in all that you do"* | *"We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you"* |
| the grievance | a well seized by the king's servants | wells stopped up; herdsmen contend over three |
| the settlement | sheep and cattle; seven ewe lambs as witness | a feast; an oath in the morning |
| the naming | *Beersheba*, *"because they both swore an oath there"* | *Shibah*, *"Therefore the name of the city is Beersheba to this day"* |

**Six of the nine rows match, including two proper names and a divine-favour formula, and the two
scenes explain the same place name by the same root.** That is more overlap than any other pair of
episodes in Genesis except the wife-sister stories — which involve the same town and the same king,
and which the wiki treats the same way. See [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].

### Three accounts of the overlap, and the wiki adopts none

1. **One tradition, told of two patriarchs.** A single Beersheba etiology — a treaty, a well, an oath
   — attached itself to Abraham in one stream of tradition and to Isaac in another, and the book
   preserves both rather than choosing. This is the standard source-critical reading, and it has the
   strongest single piece of evidence on its side: **Genesis explains the name twice, at 21:31 and
   26:33, and never notices.**
2. **Two real incidents in a family that kept using the same well.** Wells in the Beersheba basin
   were the only fixed capital a pastoralist had; disputes over them recurred by the nature of the
   thing, and a treaty made with one generation routinely had to be renewed with the next. On this
   reading *Abimelech* and *Phicol* are titles or hereditary names, exactly as *Pharaoh* is, and the
   repetition is what treaty practice actually looked like.
3. **A deliberate literary rhyme.** Genesis's method is repetition with variation — two creation
   accounts, two flood sources, two genealogies from Adam, three wife-sister episodes, two Hagar
   flights. On this reading 26 is *supposed* to feel like 21, because Isaac's whole characterization
   is that he re-does his father's life at lower intensity: same famine, same lie, same king, same
   wells, and the text says so outright — *"besides the first famine that was in the days of
   Abraham"* (26:1), and *"He called their names after the names by which his father had called
   them"* (26:18).

**And the ingested chapter adds one row to the table above.** Genesis 26's embassy has **three**
members where Genesis 21's had two — the king, his general, and *Ahuzzath his friend*, which is a
court office rather than a personal remark — and **the settlement is cheaper**: Abraham paid sheep,
cattle, and seven ewe lambs to get his digging on the record, and Isaac pays nothing and sets out no
witness object. He also enters no complaint about the filled wells, which is the one grievance the
delegation's *"we have not touched you"* (26:29) passes over.

**That last verse is the crux, and it cuts both ways.** 26:18 has the book explicitly presenting
Isaac's wells as a re-excavation of Abraham's, with the old names deliberately restored — which is
either the redactional stitch that harmonizes two traditions, or the point of the whole episode.
The wiki records the three readings and does not choose, on the same principle it applies to the
creation accounts.

### What a well is worth

**In this country, water is title.** A well in the Beersheba basin is a shaft to a water table that
exists nowhere else nearby; digging one is months of labour and it is the only reason a household can
stand south of the hills at all. So the two ways of dispossessing a sojourner without touching him
are both attested here: **seizing the well** (21:25) and **filling it in** (26:15, 18). The second is
the more pointed — it destroys the asset rather than taking it, which is not economics but eviction.

Correspondingly, **the customary law that both episodes assume is that labour confers the right.**
Abraham does not claim the well; he claims *"that I have dug this well"* (21:30), and the seven ewe
lambs exist to put the king on record as having accepted the claim. Isaac's servants dig, and dig
again, and the herdsmen of Gerar say *"The water is ours"* (26:20) — the same argument from the other
side.

### The names of the wells

Genesis names five bodies of water in the patriarchal narratives, and **every one of them is named
for something that happened rather than for the water**:

- [[Beer Lahai Roi]] — *the well of the Living One who sees me* (16:14), named by a runaway slave.
- [[Beersheba]] — *well of the oath* / *well of seven* (21:31), named for a swearing.
- [[Esek]] — *ʿēśeq*, **contention**, because the herdsmen contended (26:20).
- [[Sitnah]] — *śiṭnâ*, **hostility**, for the same reason again (26:21) — and the first occurrence in
  the Bible of the noun behind *śāṭān*, the accuser.
- [[Rehoboth]] — *room*, *"For now Yahweh has made room for us"* (26:22).
- **Shibah** — the second Beersheba etiology (26:33), and the reason this wiki keeps it on the
  [[Beersheba]] page rather than giving it one of its own.

**Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth is a sequence with an argument in it**: two wells lost without a fight, and a
third kept — Isaac's method being simply to move rather than to contend, which is the closest thing
to a virtue Genesis credits him with. It is the same method Abraham used with Lot at 13:9. See
[[Strife and Peacemaking]] and [[Abram and Lot Separate]].

### And the wells nobody fights over

The other thing Genesis does with wells is arrange marriages at them — Abraham's servant and Rebekah
(24:11–20), Jacob and Rachel (29:1–10), and, outside Genesis, Moses and the daughters of Reuel
(Exodus 2:15–17). The type-scene is fixed enough that its elements are predictable: a journey, a
well, a woman, water drawn, and news carried home. **The same object that is the flashpoint of every
territorial dispute in the Abraham and Isaac cycles is the site of every betrothal in the book.**

And one well in Genesis is neither: at 21:19, *"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water"* —
no dispute, no name, no dig, and eleven verses before Abraham's lawsuit over one. See
[[Seeing and Being Seen]].

## Significance

- **Two treaties, one place, and one name explained twice.** Whatever the relation between Genesis 21
  and Genesis 26, it is the clearest case in the Abraham material of the book preserving two accounts
  of the same thing without reconciling them — and, unlike the creation accounts, it comes with the
  book's own cross-reference (26:1, 26:18) attached.
- **The dispute is never resolved by force, in either chapter.** A general is present in both scenes
  and the settlement in both is a gift, an oath, and a name. In a book that has already had a war
  (14), a massacre by fire (19), and a death sentence over a marriage (20), the wells are where
  Genesis shows what peace-making actually costs and what it looks like.
- **And the claim being defended is labour, not possession.** Neither patriarch says the land is his.
  Abraham says he dug; Isaac digs again. The promise of the land is not once mentioned in either
  negotiation. See [[Land and Sojourning]].
