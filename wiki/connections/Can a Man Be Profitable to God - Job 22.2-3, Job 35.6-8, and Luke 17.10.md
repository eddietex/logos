# Can a Man Be Profitable to God - Job 22.2-3, Job 35.6-8, and Luke 17.10

## Passages involved

- **Job 1:8–9; 2:3** — *"Have you considered my servant Job? For there is no one like him in the
  earth"*; *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"*; *"He still maintains his integrity"* —
  [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]], [[The Second Test]]
- **Job 7:20** — Job: *"If I have sinned, what do I do to you, you watcher of men?"* —
  [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]
- **Job 21:15** — the wicked, quoted by Job: *"What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? What
  profit should we have, if we pray to him?"* — [[Why Do the Wicked Live]]
- **Job 22:2–4** — Eliphaz: *"Can a man be profitable to God? Surely he who is wise is profitable to
  himself. Is it any pleasure to the Almighty, that you are righteous? Or does it benefit him, that
  you make your ways perfect? Is it for your piety that he reproves you?"* —
  [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]
- **Job 22:21** — *"Acquaint yourself with him"*: the same root, *s-k-n*, as *profitable* in 22:2
- **Job 35:6–8** *(not yet ingested)* — Elihu: *"If you are righteous, what do you give him? Or what
  does he receive from your hand? Your wickedness may hurt a man as you are, and your righteousness
  may profit a son of man."*
- **Job 41:11** *(not yet ingested)* — God: *"Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?"*,
  quoted at **Romans 11:35** *(not yet ingested)*
- **Luke 17:10** *(not yet ingested)* — *"when you have done all the things that are commanded you,
  say, 'We are unworthy servants. We have done our duty.'"*

## Analysis

**The book asks the profit question four times, in four mouths, and each asks it the other way
round.**

- **The accuser asks it of Job** (1:9): does Job's piety profit *Job*? His wager is that religion is
  a transaction on the human side — Job fears God because it pays.
- **Job asks it of his sin** (7:20): what does my sin do to *you*? If it costs God nothing, why does
  God pursue it?
- **The wicked ask it of prayer** (21:15): what profit would *we* have if we prayed? The accuser's
  theory, stated by the people it actually describes.
- **Eliphaz asks it of God** (22:2–3): does Job's righteousness profit *God*? His answer is no — God
  is self-sufficient — and from that premise he concludes that God has no reason to afflict a
  righteous man, so Job must not be one.

Elihu then repeats Eliphaz almost word for word (35:6–8), which is one of several places where the
fourth speaker, whom God does not rebuke, says what the first speaker said.

**Eliphaz's premise is true in one sense and refuted by the book in another.** It is true that God
is not *enriched* by human goodness, and is not put in anyone's debt by it. God says so himself at
41:11 — *"Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?"* — and Paul quotes the line at Romans
11:35 to close his argument that salvation is not owed. Jesus' unprofitable servants in Luke 17:10
make the same point from the servant's side: obedience earns no credit, because it is what was
owed.

But the prologue refutes the inference Eliphaz draws. **God is not indifferent to Job's integrity.**
He boasts of it twice (1:8; 2:3), he stakes the council's question on it, and he allows Job's ruin
precisely in order that it may be shown. The verb Eliphaz uses for *make your ways perfect*,
*tattēm* (22:3), is from the *t-m-m* root of *tām* and *tummâ* — the root of God's own description
of Job. Eliphaz asks whether Job's *tummâ* benefits God, in a book whose first two chapters exist
because it does. And 22:4's *"Is it for your piety that he reproves you?"* — *yirʾâ*, the *fear of
God* that the accuser said was bought — has the one answer Eliphaz considers impossible.

**The distinction the book draws is between obligation and interest.** God is not obligated by
Job's righteousness: he does not owe Job prosperity in return for it, which is why the epilogue's
restoration is gift and not wages, and why 41:11 can say *who has first given to me?* But God is
interested in it: it is what he points to in the council, and what the whole test exists to display.
Eliphaz collapses the two. If God gets nothing from righteousness, he concludes, God's dealings with
the righteous must be pure ledger — and so the ledger must explain Job's ruin. The book's answer is
that God has a stake in Job that is not a debt, and that the suffering came from the stake, not the
ledger.

**And the root plays one more time.** *Yiskān* (22:2), *can a man be of use*, returns in 22:21 as
*hasken*, *acquaint yourself*, *be at home* with him. A man cannot make himself useful to God; he can
make himself familiar with him. Eliphaz intends the pun as the turn from rebuke to invitation, and
the second half of it is the better theology: what God wants from Job, as chapters 38–42 show, is
not payment but presence — *"now my eye sees you"* (42:5).

## Significance

- It names the hinge of the wager. The accuser's charge is that religion is profitable to the
  worshipper; Eliphaz's premise is that it is unprofitable to God; the prologue's premise is that it
  matters to God anyway. All three are on the table before the dialogue begins, and only the reader
  can see all three.
- It explains why Elihu's repetition of 22:2–3 at 35:6–8 is not rebuked: as a statement about
  obligation, it is the same thing God says at 41:11. What is rebuked in Eliphaz is the inference.
- It connects the book to the New Testament's doctrine of grace by the route Paul himself takes —
  Romans 11:35 is Job 41:11 — rather than by analogy.
