# Ha-Satan - The Accuser in Job, Zechariah, and Chronicles

## Passages involved

- **Job 1:6–12; 2:1–7** — *haśśāṭān* among the sons of God; *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* —
  [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] and [[The Second Test]]
- **Job 2:3** — *"you **incited** me against him, to ruin him without cause"*: ***hēsît***, the same
  verb as 1 Chronicles 21:1 and 2 Samuel 24:1 below, **with the accuser as subject and God as
  object** — the one verse in Scripture that puts both halves of the Chronicles/Samuel problem into
  a single sentence
- **Zechariah 3:1–5** — *"He showed me Joshua the high priest standing before Yahweh's angel, and
  Satan standing at his right hand to be his adversary. Yahweh said to Satan, 'Yahweh rebuke you,
  Satan!'"*
- **1 Chronicles 21:1** — *"Satan stood up against Israel, and moved David to number Israel."*
- **2 Samuel 24:1** — the same census: *"Again Yahweh's anger burned against Israel, and he moved
  David against them, saying, 'Go, count Israel and Judah.'"*
- **Numbers 22:22, 32** — *"God's angel placed himself in the way for an adversary against him"*
  (*ləśāṭān*) — [[The Angel of Yahweh]]
- **1 Samuel 29:4** — the Philistine commanders on David: *"lest in the battle he become an
  adversary to us"*
- **1 Kings 5:4; 11:14, 23, 25** — Solomon has no *śāṭān*, and then two of them, both raised up by
  Yahweh
- **Psalm 109:6** — *"Set a wicked man over him. Let an adversary stand at his right hand."*
- **Wisdom of Solomon 2:24; 1 Enoch; Jubilees** — the intertestamental development
- **Luke 22:31–32; 2 Corinthians 12:7; Revelation 12:9–10** — the New Testament figure

## Analysis

Three passages in the Hebrew Bible put a *śāṭān* in a heavenly setting. **They are usually read as
three appearances of one character, and they are better read as three points on a line.**

**The word first.** *Śāṭān* is a common noun meaning *adversary*, *opponent*, *obstructor*, with a
verbal root *śāṭan*, to oppose or accuse. Most of its Old Testament occurrences are entirely
mundane: David is a potential *śāṭān* to the Philistines, Hadad and Rezon are *śāṭān* to Solomon,
and Psalm 109:6 asks for one to be stationed at an enemy's right hand — a courtroom position, since
the accuser in an Israelite trial stood at the defendant's right. **And at Numbers 22:22 the Angel
of Yahweh, sent by God, stands in Balaam's road *ləśāṭān*.** Any account of this word has to
accommodate the fact that its first heavenly bearer in canonical order is God's own messenger doing
God's own errand.

**Job: the office, exercised.** *Haśśāṭān*, with the article, fourteen times in two chapters. He is
a member of the assembly, reports on patrol, raises no accusation of his own accord — **God raises
Job** — and acts only under authorisation with stated limits. His challenge is not that Job has
sinned but that Job's righteousness is bought. He is wrong, he is never punished for being wrong,
and he is never mentioned again after 2:7. **Nothing in Job calls him evil, and nothing in Job makes
him autonomous.** What it does do is give him a voice the court does not silence, and a proposal the
court adopts.

**Zechariah: the office, rebuked.** Again *haśśāṭān*, again with the article, again in a court —
this time an actual trial, of a named defendant, the post-exilic high priest Joshua, standing in
filthy garments. The accuser takes the accuser's position, at the right hand. And this time the
proceeding goes against him before he has said a word: *"Yahweh rebuke you, Satan! Yes, Yahweh who
has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you! Isn't this a burning stick plucked out of the fire?"* The filthy
garments are removed and replaced. **The shift from Job is decisive and easy to miss: in Job the
accuser's question is entertained; in Zechariah the accusation is disallowed because of an election
that precedes it.** He is still an officer of the court, and the judge has stopped treating him as
one.

**Chronicles: the article is gone.** *"Śāṭān stood up against Israel."* No definite article, which
opens the possibility of a proper name — and creates the ambiguity the English versions split over:
*Satan* (KJV, WEB, NIV, ESV) against *an adversary* or *a satan* (NRSV, JPS). The Septuagint reads
*diabolos*, also anarthrous.

**The comparison with 2 Samuel 24:1 is what makes the verse important.** The two accounts narrate one
event, David's census, and they differ in exactly one place:

| | 2 Samuel 24:1 | 1 Chronicles 21:1 |
|---|---|---|
| Who incites David | *"Yahweh's anger… he moved David"* | *"Satan… moved David"* |
| The census | taken, and counted as sin | taken, and counted as sin |
| The punishment | plague; three days; 70,000 dead | plague; three days; 70,000 dead |

Everything else holds. **The Chronicler has taken an act the older text attributes to Yahweh and
assigned it to a *śāṭān*** — which is the clearest evidence in the Hebrew Bible of a developing
reluctance to make God the direct author of an incitement to sin, and the clearest evidence of the
word moving from job title toward name. Whether the Chronicler meant *the* accuser of Job and
Zechariah, a human adversary (a foreign enemy, on one minority reading), or an unnamed heavenly
agent, is not recoverable from the verse. **What is recoverable is the direction of travel.**

**And Job 2:3 sits in the middle of that development, saying both things at once.** God tells the
accuser *"you incited me against him, to ruin him without cause"* — the verb of incitement is the
same root the two census accounts use (*sût*). **Job has the accuser incite God, and God perform the
ruin, in one sentence.** Samuel has God do both. Chronicles has the accuser do both. The three
verses are a nearly complete set of the available options, and the Bible preserves all three without
harmonising them.

**Where it goes next.** The intertestamental literature completes the movement: *Wisdom of Solomon*
2:24 makes the devil's envy the cause of death and, in doing so, identifies him with the serpent of
Genesis 3 for the first time; *1 Enoch* and *Jubilees* supply fallen angels, a chief, and a name.
The New Testament inherits a figure with a will of his own — but keeps the Joban shape visible where
it matters. **Luke 22:31 is Job 1 in miniature**: *"Satan asked to have all of you, that he might
sift you as wheat"* — a request, therefore a permission, therefore no autonomy — *"but I prayed for
you."* What is new there is the advocate, and it is precisely what the court in Job 1 does not have.
See [[The Witness in Heaven]].

Revelation 12:10 then names the office and announces its end: *"the accuser of our brothers has been
thrown down, who accuses them before our God day and night."* **That is a description of the job
held in Job 1 and Zechariah 3, and a statement that the position is being abolished** — which is a
more exact continuity with the Hebrew texts than the usual picture of an eternal rival ever was.

## Significance

The Old Testament has no Satan in the sense most readers bring to it — no rival power, no kingdom,
no independent will, no origin story, and no fall. **What it has is an accuser: a function within
God's own government, which is exercised in Job, disallowed in Zechariah, and possibly named in
Chronicles.** Reading the developed figure back into Job 1 does not merely add detail; it destroys
the scene, because a scene in which God's enemy proposes a test is a completely different scene from
one in which God's prosecutor asks whether disinterested love exists and God declines to answer by
assertion.

It also matters for how the book of Job is used. **The prologue is not an explanation of suffering
by demonic agency.** The accuser is off-stage after 2:7, no character learns of him, God takes the
act as his own at 2:3, and the epilogue calls the whole thing *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought
on him"* (42:11). A wiki reading that lets the accuser absorb the blame would be reading a book the
author declined to write. See [[Satan]], [[The Divine Council]], and
[[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]].
