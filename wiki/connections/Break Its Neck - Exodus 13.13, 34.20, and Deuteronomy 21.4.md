# Break Its Neck - Exodus 13.13, 34.20, and Deuteronomy 21.4

## Passages involved

- [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] — Exodus 13:13; *"Every firstborn of a donkey you shall redeem
  with a lamb; and if you will not redeem it, then you shall break its neck"*.
- [[The Covenant Renewed]] — Exodus 34:20; the same law repeated: *"If you will not redeem it, then you
  shall break its neck."*
- [[An Unsolved Killing and the Broken-Necked Heifer]] — Deuteronomy 21:4, 6; the elders *"break the
  heifer's neck there in the valley"*, and wash their hands over *"the heifer whose neck was broken"*.

## Analysis

**One verb, four times, two laws.** *ʿĀrap*, *break the neck*, is built on *ʿōrep*, *neck*. In the Torah
it occurs only in these verses: *waʿăraptô* at Exodus 13:13 and 34:20, *wǝʿārǝpû* at Deuteronomy 21:4, and
the participle *hāʿărûpâ* at 21:6 (WLC lemma 6202, morphhb, retrieved 2026-11-08).

**In both laws the animal is killed and not offered.** The firstborn donkey belongs to Yahweh like every
firstborn, but it does not part the hoof or chew the cud (compare Leviticus 11:3), and no law provides
for offering it; that is the usual explanation of why it is redeemed, though Exodus does not state it. It
must be redeemed with a lamb, and if it is not, it is killed — not kept, and not sacrificed. The heifer of Deuteronomy 21 is not brought to the place
Yahweh chooses, no blood is collected, and no priest handles it. In both cases breaking the neck is the
way of killing an animal that is removed from use without being sacrificed.

**The differences are as large as the likeness.** The donkey's death is the alternative to redemption:
it is what happens if the owner will not pay the lamb. The heifer's death is the rite itself, after which
the elders ask Yahweh to *kipper* for his people (21:8). The donkey is a male firstborn claimed by
Yahweh; the heifer is a young female of the herd that has never been worked. Nothing in either text
refers to the other.

## Significance

The shared verb shows that Israel's law had a way of killing an animal that was neither butchery nor
sacrifice. Whether the two laws share a meaning beyond that — a death that marks a claim unpaid, or an
animal withdrawn from use — is an inference from the verb, not a statement of either text, and the wiki
records it as such.
