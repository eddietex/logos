# Kings Will Come Out of You

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 17:6, 16** — *"I will make you exceedingly fruitful, and I will make nations of you.
  Kings will come out of you"*, and of Sarah, *"Kings of peoples will come from her."* The Bible's
  first promise of monarchy. See [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].
- **Genesis 35:11** — the same promise transferred to Jacob at Bethel, under the same divine name:
  *"I am God Almighty … and kings will come out of your body."* **The third and last of its three
  Genesis occurrences**, and the only one given to a man who already has the twelve sons the kings
  will descend from. See [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]].
- **Genesis 36:31** — the note that dates the whole thread: *"These are the kings who reigned in the
  land of Edom, before any king reigned over the children of Israel."* — [[The Descendants of Esau]]
- **Genesis 49:10** — *"The scepter will not depart from Judah, nor the ruler's staff from between
  his feet"*: the promise narrowed to one tribe.
- **Numbers 24:17** — Balaam, an outsider: *"A star will come out of Jacob. A scepter will rise out
  of Israel."*
- **Deuteronomy 17:14–20** — the law of the king: permitted, hedged, and required to keep a copy of
  the law beside him.
- **1 Samuel 8:4–22** — Israel asks for a king *"like all the nations"*, and is told the request is a
  rejection of God's own kingship, and is granted it anyway.
- **2 Samuel 7:8–17** — the promise landed on David: a house, a throne, and a kingdom *forever*.
- **Psalm 72:11, 17** — a king before whom *"all kings shall fall down"*, in a psalm that ends by
  quoting the promise to Abraham.
- **Matthew 1:1; Luke 1:32–33; Revelation 21:24** — the genealogy that begins *"the son of David, the
  son of Abraham"*, the throne of David given to a child, and the kings of the earth bringing their
  glory into the new city.

## Analysis

**Genesis 17 is where the Bible first says there will be kings, and it says it as a blessing.**

That is worth stating plainly, because the Old Testament's dominant attitude to monarchy is
suspicious. Judges ends with a refrain about there being no king in Israel; 1 Samuel 8 treats the
demand for one as apostasy; the books of Kings are a long account of the institution failing. And yet
the promise that produces it is spoken by God, unprompted, to a childless ninety-nine-year-old, twice
in one chapter, in the middle of the covenant that founds the nation — and it is repeated to Jacob at
35:11 in almost identical words.

**Note what the promise is not.** Abraham is not told he will be a king or that his descendants
should want one. He is told kings *"will come out of you"* — the same construction as *"he who will
come out of your own body"* at 15:4, which is about offspring. Monarchy enters the Bible as a
prediction about a family's future, not as a political programme, and Genesis attaches no
institutions, no capital, and no laws to it.

### The tension the Bible never quite resolves

| | Genesis 17:6, 16; 35:11 | 1 Samuel 8:4–22 |
|---|---|---|
| Who initiates | God | the elders of Israel |
| Framing | blessing, alongside fruitfulness and nations | *"they have rejected me, that I should not be king over them"* |
| Model | unspecified | *"like all the nations"* |
| Outcome | promised | granted, with a warning about conscription and taxation |

Deuteronomy 17:14–20 sits between them and is the closest thing to a synthesis: a king is permitted
in anticipation of exactly the request 1 Samuel records, and then hedged with four prohibitions —
no multiplying of horses, wives, or silver, and no return to Egypt — plus the requirement that he
write out the law for himself and read it all his life. The office is conceded and then aimed.
The promise of Genesis 17 is what makes the concession possible; the failure of every king to keep
Deuteronomy 17 is what makes the prophets necessary.

### Sarah's half of it

*"Kings of peoples will come from her"* (17:16) is the only time in Scripture a woman is told this
directly, and it is the sharpest sign of what the chapter is doing. Genesis 15 promised offspring
from Abraham's body and said nothing about hers; Genesis 16 filled the gap with a slave. Genesis 17
puts the dynasty in the barren woman, twice, and gives her a new name in the same speech. See
[[Sarah]] and [[Barrenness]].

### Edom collects first

**The promise is made to Abraham at 17:6, repeated to Jacob at 35:11 — and the very next chapter
hands eight kings to the other brother.**

> **31** These are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the
> children of Israel.

**The placement is brutal and almost certainly deliberate.** 35:11's *"kings will come out of your
body"* is the last thing God says to Jacob in the cycle; 36:31 is thirty-one verses later. See
[[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] and [[The Descendants of Esau]].

**It is of a piece with everything the *toledot* order does.** The passed-over line always gets its
credentials on the page first — Cain's crafts, Ham's empire, Ishmael's twelve princes — and here it
gets the institution Israel has been promised and will not have for four hundred years. **Genesis
grants Edom everything except the covenant**, and does not explain the difference. See
[[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]].

**And the clause is a dating formula, which is why it is one of the most discussed verses in the
Torah.** *Before any king reigned over the children of Israel* assumes a reader who knows there were
kings in Israel; written before Saul it is a prediction, written after it is a retrospect, and the
plain sense is the second. It is among the verses Ibn Ezra hinted at in *the secret of the twelve*,
and it became a standard pillar of the case that the Pentateuch reached its final form long after
Moses. **The thread above does not depend on how that question is settled** — the promise and the
irony sit in the text either way.

**Note also what kind of monarchy Edom's is.** Eight kings, no son succeeding a father, eight
different cities. **It is an elective or imposed chieftainship, not a dynasty** — which makes it a
poor model for what 2 Samuel 7 eventually promises David, and a good illustration of why Deuteronomy
17:14–20 has to hedge the office rather than simply grant it.

### The narrowing

Genesis then does what it always does with a promise: it keeps it and narrows it. Kings from
Abraham (17:6) become kings from Jacob (35:11), become the scepter of Judah (49:10), become the house
of David (2 Samuel 7), become one name at the head of a genealogy (Matthew 1:1). Along the way
Genesis 36:31 quietly notes that **Edom had kings first** — Esau's line reaching the promise's content
before Jacob's does, which is the same pattern the book uses for Ishmael's twelve princes at 17:20 and
25:12–18. The non-chosen line gets the blessing early and the covenant line waits.

## Significance

- **Monarchy is promised before it is requested and long before it is criticized.** Any reading of
  1 Samuel 8 as a simple condemnation of kingship has to account for Genesis 17:6, which is God's own
  word and is a blessing.
- **It plants the messianic thread in the Abrahamic covenant rather than only in the Davidic one.**
  Psalm 72 and Matthew 1:1 both reach back past David to Abraham, and 17:6 is why they can.
- **And it is given to two people who have no children.** The Bible's first sentence about kings is
  addressed to a hundred-year-old man and a ninety-year-old woman, which is the chapter's method in
  miniature: every promise in it is made to somebody with no visible means of receiving it.
