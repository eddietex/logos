# The Noahic Commandments and the Council of Jerusalem

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 2:16–17** — the one prohibition given in the garden — [[The Garden of Eden]]
- **Genesis 9:1–7** — the blessing, the diet, the blood prohibition, and the requirement of blood
  for blood — [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- **Leviticus 17–18** — the holiness code's four rules that bind the resident alien as well as the
  Israelite: sacrifice to the goat-demons, blood, carrion and strangled meat, and forbidden unions
- **Acts 15:19–21, 28–29** — the Jerusalem decree: *"abstain from the pollution of idols, from
  sexual immorality, from what is strangled, and from blood"*
- **Acts 21:25** — the decree restated to Paul years later
- **Tosefta *Avodah Zarah* 8:4; *Sanhedrin* 56a–60a** — the rabbinic seven commandments of the sons
  of Noah

## Analysis

**Genesis 9 is the only law in the Hebrew Bible addressed to everyone.** Sinai is given to a nation
that has just been brought out of Egypt; the patriarchal commands are given to households. Genesis
9:1–7 is spoken to eight people who are, at that moment, the entire human race, and through them —
*"you, and… your offspring after you"* — to every person who has ever lived. Whatever else it
contains, it is the natural place for any tradition needing a universal ethic to look, and two
traditions independently went there.

### The rabbinic seven

The *sheva mitzvot b'nei Noach*, the seven commandments of the sons of Noah, are the classical
Jewish account of what is required of non-Jews. The list (Tosefta *Avodah Zarah* 8:4; *Sanhedrin*
56a) is: prohibitions of **idolatry**, **blasphemy**, **murder**, **sexual immorality**, **theft**,
and **eating flesh taken from a living animal**, plus the positive requirement to **establish courts
of justice**.

Only two of the seven are actually stated in Genesis 9. The blood prohibition of 9:4 supplies the
sixth — the Talmud reads *"flesh with its life… you shall not eat"* as forbidding *ever min
ha-chai*, a limb torn from a living creature — and 9:6 supplies the third, with the requirement of
courts read out of *"by man his blood shall be shed"*, since a rule with no procedure implies the
institution that would run it. The rest are derived from Genesis 2:16 by a piece of rabbinic
word-exegesis that reads each element of *"Yahweh God commanded the man"* as a separate heading. The
number seven, in other words, is a construction, and the tradition does not pretend otherwise.

What the construction gets right is the shape of the text. Genesis 9 is legislation without a
legislature: it states two rules, gives a reason for one of them that applies to all humanity (*"for
God made man in his own image"*), and provides no court, no penalty scale, and no covenant people to
administer it. A tradition that wanted to say what God requires of the nations had exactly one place
to start, and it started there. The legal category of the *ger toshav*, the resident alien who keeps
the Noahide laws without converting, is built on the same foundation.

### The four abstentions

Acts 15 has the church facing the same question from the other side: Gentiles are being saved
without circumcision, and nobody knows what, if anything, still binds them. James's proposal,
adopted as the council's letter, is four abstentions — *"from the pollution of idols, from sexual
immorality, from what is strangled, and from blood"* (15:20, and again at 15:29 and 21:25).

**Two of the four are Genesis 9:4.** *"What is strangled"* is meat killed without draining the
blood; *"blood"* is blood itself. The oldest standing dietary rule in Scripture, given before Israel
existed, is the one the first church council kept when it set circumcision and the rest of the
Mosaic law aside.

**Where the four come from is genuinely disputed**, and the wiki notes both cases rather than
choosing:

- **From Noah.** The overlap with the rabbinic seven is real — idolatry, sexual immorality, and
  blood are three of them — and the logic fits the council's question exactly. If Gentile believers
  are not becoming Jews, then what binds them is what binds humanity, which is Noah. This reading
  has the advantage that Genesis 9 is the only pre-Israelite law available.
- **From Leviticus 17–18.** Those two chapters legislate four things and attach each of them
  explicitly to *"the strangers who live as foreigners among you"* as well as to Israelites:
  sacrificing outside the camp (17:1–9), eating blood (17:10–14), eating what dies of itself or is
  torn (17:15–16), and the forbidden unions of chapter 18. The four match Acts 15's four in content
  and in order, and the ground is stated: these are the rules that apply to a Gentile living inside
  Israel's territory. On this reading the decree is not a universal ethic at all but a practical
  rule for mixed congregations — what Gentiles must avoid so that Jewish believers can eat at the
  same table.

The second is the stronger case on the details; the first is the stronger case on the question the
council was actually asked. They are not exclusive, since Leviticus 17:10–14 grounds its own blood
prohibition in the same premise Genesis 9:4 does — *"the life of the flesh is in the blood"* — and
both are reaching back past Sinai for a reason.

**Whichever it is, the blood rule kept going.** It is not a dead letter in the early church:
Tertullian and Minucius Felix both cite Christians' refusal of blood against the charge that they
drank it, the canons of the councils repeat the prohibition for centuries, and the Eastern churches
have never formally set it aside. Western Christianity mostly stopped observing it, generally on the
reading that Acts 15 was a temporary accommodation for the sake of table fellowship rather than a
permanent law — which is, in effect, the Leviticus reading of where the four came from.

## Significance

The thread is a good test of what Genesis 9 is for. Read as narrative, it is the terms on which the
world restarts. Read as law, it is the only text in the Hebrew Bible that legislates for humanity as
such, and both Judaism and the early church went to it — or to something built on the same premise —
when they had to say what is required of people outside the covenant.

It is also a reminder of how the pericope's two halves belong together. The covenant of 9:8–17 asks
nothing of the human party at all; the blessing of 9:1–7 asks two things, and they are the two
things that turn out to be portable. What God binds himself to is unconditional. What he requires is
short, universal, and about blood.
