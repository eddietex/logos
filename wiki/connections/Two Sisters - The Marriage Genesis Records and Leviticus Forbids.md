# Two Sisters - The Marriage Genesis Records and Leviticus Forbids

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 29:23–30** — *"In the evening, he took Leah his daughter, and brought her to him … He
  gave him Rachel his daughter as wife … He went in also to Rachel, and he loved also Rachel more
  than Leah"* — [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]]
- **Leviticus 18:18** — *"You shall not take a wife in addition to her sister, to be a rival, to
  uncover her nakedness, while her sister is yet alive."*
- **Genesis 30:1–24** — the rivalry itself: two sisters, two slaves, and eleven sons
- **Genesis 35:16–20; 49:31** — one sister buried on a road, the other at Machpelah
- Compare **Genesis 20:12** — Abraham marries his half-sister, which Leviticus 18:9 also forbids
- Compare **Exodus 6:20** — Amram marries his father's sister, which Leviticus 18:12 also forbids

## Analysis

**The founding marriage of Israel is one the Torah names and prohibits, and neither book mentions the
other.**

Leviticus 18 lists eighteen forbidden unions, almost all of them in the same clipped formula — *you
shall not uncover the nakedness of X*. **Verse 18 is the exception, and it is the exception in three
ways at once.** It is the only prohibition in the chapter that:

- **gives a reason** — ***liṣrōr***, *to be a rival*, *to vex*;
- **is limited in time** — *"while her sister is yet alive"*, so the union is permitted after a death,
  which none of the others are; and
- **describes, clause for clause, a marriage the Torah's own narrative reports without comment.**

**Jacob takes a wife in addition to her sister. The two become rivals. The elder is alive throughout.**
Genesis 30 then spends twenty-four verses on precisely the *ṣārar* that Leviticus gives as its
grounds: two women bidding for one man with children, slaves, and mandrakes, and naming eight sons
after the state of the contest.

### What the connection is and is not

- **Genesis passes no verdict.** It does not call the marriage wrong, does not have anyone object, and
  does not attach a consequence to it as a consequence. **What it does is narrate the rivalry at
  length**, which is a different thing from condemning the arrangement, and the wiki keeps the
  distinction. The narrator's one evaluative sentence in the whole business is at 29:30 — *"he loved
  also Rachel more than Leah"* — and it is a fact about Jacob, not a ruling about the marriage.
- **Leviticus does not mention Jacob.** No prophet, no law, and no New Testament writer ever cites
  Genesis 29 as the case Leviticus 18:18 is legislating. **The correspondence is between a
  prohibition's terms and a narrative's facts**, and it is the reader who holds both.
- **The patriarchs are outside the code generally, and Genesis knows it.** [[Abraham]] is married to
  his half-sister on his own account (20:12), which Leviticus 18:9 forbids; Moses' own parents are
  nephew and aunt (Exodus 6:20), which Leviticus 18:12 forbids. **The Torah does not go back and
  correct any of them.** The standing reading — that the law is given at Sinai and does not run
  backwards — is the ordinary one and the wiki adopts it as a description of what the text does, not
  as a theological settlement.
- **The temporal limit is doing real work.** Because 18:18 forbids the union only *"while her sister is
  yet alive"*, it is the one prohibition in the chapter that treats the marriage as objectionable for
  what it causes rather than for what it is. **The reason given is the rivalry**, and the rivalry is
  what Genesis 30 documents.

### The reading the Qumran community made of it

The Damascus Document (CD 4:20–5:2) uses 18:18 in an argument against polygamy in general, reading
*"her sister"* as *another woman* — a reading the Hebrew will bear, since *ʾiššâ ʾel-ʾăḥōtāh* is
elsewhere an idiom for *one to another*. **On that reading the verse is not about siblings at all.**
The wiki records the reading because it is ancient and because it is the one place the ambiguity has
consequences; the plain sense, and the one every English version takes, is literal sisters.

## Significance

- **It is the clearest case in the Torah of the law describing the patriarchs' own household and not
  saying so.** The same pattern runs through [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — where Deuteronomy
  21:15–17 forbids exactly what Genesis keeps doing — and it is the reason both entries in this wiki
  point at each other. See [[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]].
- **It tells you what the Torah thought the danger of the arrangement was**, and it is not
  consanguinity. Every other verse in Leviticus 18 is about blood or affinity; **this one is about
  what two women in one household do to each other**, and it names it.
- **And it does not diminish what came out of the marriage.** Twelve tribes, the priesthood, and the
  royal line all descend from a union Leviticus would not permit — **which is the Bible's ordinary
  posture toward this family**, and the reason Ruth 4:11 can bless a bride to be *"like Rachel and
  like Leah, which two built the house of Israel"* without a word of qualification. See
  [[Like Rachel and Like Leah - The Builders of the House of Israel]].
