# A King Under Instruction - Proverbs 31.1-5 and Deuteronomy 17.17-20

## Passages involved

- [[The Words of King Lemuel]] — Proverbs 31:1–5, with the goal in 8–9.
- Deuteronomy 17:17–20 — the king’s restraint concerning wives and wealth, his copy of the law,
  and lifelong learning; verified WEB, not yet ingested.
- [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]] — Proverbs 1:8, maternal instruction beside paternal instruction.
- [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] — Proverbs 6:20, the mother’s teaching retained.

## Analysis

Both instructions place a king under a standard he did not invent. Lemuel hears his mother;
Deuteronomy’s king writes a copy of the law and reads it throughout his life. In both, possession
of royal power leaves the need to learn intact.

The warnings about women overlap without being identical. Deuteronomy 17:17 forbids multiplying
wives so that the king’s heart will not turn away, alongside a prohibition on greatly multiplying
silver and gold. Proverbs 31:3 warns against giving strength to women and ways that destroy
kings, without specifying marriage, a number, or a particular offense. No equation of every
relationship with wrongdoing is warranted by that comparison.

The relation to law is also distinct. Deuteronomy specifies the king’s reading, obedience,
humility among his brothers, and a promised prolonging of his rule. Proverbs 31:4–5 warns of
drink making rulers forget the law and pervert an afflicted person’s justice. The latter ends
with a command to serve the poor, not the dynastic promise of Deuteronomy. The shared ethical
pattern does not prove quotation or identify Lemuel as an Israelite king.

Proverbs 1:8 and 6:20 commend the mother’s teaching to an unnamed son. Proverbs 31 credits a
particular mother, though unnamed herself, as teacher of a named king. Her authority belongs
to the heading and the address; it does not depend on identifying her with another biblical
woman. See [[Lemuel's Mother]] and
[[Honor Your Father and Mother - Exodus 20 and Ephesians 6]].

## Significance

[[Kingship and Empire]] does not place the ruler above [[Law and Obedience]]. Royal restraint
preserves his capacity to judge for other people. The comparison also gives [[Wisdom]] a
maternal voice whose instruction reaches public life, rather than ending at household order.
