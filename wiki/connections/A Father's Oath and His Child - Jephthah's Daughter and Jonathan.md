# A Father's Oath and His Child - Jephthah's Daughter and Jonathan

## Passages involved

- [[Jephthah's Vow]] — **Judges 11:30–40** — *If you will indeed deliver the children of Ammon into my hand, then it
  shall be, that whatever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me … I will offer it up for a burnt offering*
  (11:30–31); *Alas, my daughter! You have brought me very low, and you are one of those who trouble me; for I have
  opened my mouth to Yahweh, and I can't go back* (11:35). Stored WEB.
- [[Saul's Rash Oath]] — **1 Samuel 14:24–46** — *Cursed is the man who eats any food until it is evening, and I am
  avenged of my enemies* (14:24); *My father has troubled the land* (14:29); *God do so and more also; for you shall
  surely die, Jonathan* (14:44); *So the people rescued Jonathan, that he didn't die* (14:45). Stored WEB.

## Analysis

**Two fathers bound against a child.** In the books stored so far these are the two narratives in which a leader
of Israel, at war, binds himself by a sworn word, and the word falls on his own child, whom he did not have in mind.
Jephthah *vowed a vow*, *wayyiddar … neḏer* (Judges 11:30, WLC), a promise to Yahweh conditional on victory. Saul
*adjured the people*, *wayyōʾel*, with a curse on anyone who eats, and the people *feared the oath*, *haššᵊḇûʿâ*
(1 Samuel 14:24, 26, WLC). The two are different acts, a vow and an imposed oath, and the wiki does not merge them.
What they share is the shape of the story.

| | Judges 11 | 1 Samuel 14 |
|---|---|---|
| the word | a vow to Yahweh, before the battle (11:30–31) | a curse on the people, on the day of battle (14:24) |
| its purpose | victory over Ammon | *until … I am avenged of my enemies* |
| who it falls on | his daughter, *his only child* (11:34) | Jonathan, the first of his sons (14:49) |
| did the child know? | no; she comes out to meet him (11:34) | no; *Jonathan didn't hear* (14:27) |
| who is *troubling* | *you are one of those who trouble me* (11:35) | *My father has troubled the land* (14:29) |
| the child's answer | *do to me according to that which has proceeded out of your mouth* (11:36) | *behold, I must die* (14:43) |
| the father | *I can't go back* (11:35) | swears again, *you shall surely die* (14:44) |
| the outcome | *he did with her according to his vow* (11:39) | *the people rescued Jonathan* (14:45) |

(Stored WEB. Hebrew WLC.)

**The same verb, pointed the other way.** Both stories use *ʿāḵar*, *trouble*, the verb of Achan (Joshua 7:25). In
the WLC files searched (Genesis–2 Samuel) it occurs only at Genesis 34:30, Joshua 6:18; 7:25, Judges 11:35, and 1
Samuel 14:29 (morphhb lemma 5916). Jephthah applies it to his daughter, *wᵊʾatt hāyîṯ bᵊʿōḵᵊrāy*, *you are one of
those who trouble me*, and gives the cause in the same breath: *I have opened my mouth*. Jonathan applies it to his
father, *ʿāḵar ʾāḇî ʾeṯ-hāʾāreṣ*. Jephthah's word puts the trouble on the child; Jonathan's puts it back on the man
who swore. See [[The Valley of Achor - Trouble Turned to a Door of Hope]].

**Vengeance on enemies.** Saul's oath gives its purpose as *wᵊniqqamtî mēʾōyᵊḇay*, *I am avenged of my enemies*
(14:24, WLC). Jephthah's daughter gives the reason she accepts the vow: *Yahweh has taken vengeance for you on your
enemies*, *nᵊqāmôṯ mēʾōyᵊḇeyḵā* (Judges 11:36, WLC). Both use *nāqam*/*nᵊqāmâ* with *ʾōyēḇ*. In Judges the
vengeance is Yahweh's and already done; in 1 Samuel it is Saul's and still wanted.

**Where the stories part.** Jephthah's story has no one who speaks for the daughter but herself, and she speaks for
the vow. In Saul's, Jonathan says little, but the people speak: *Shall Jonathan die, who has worked this great
salvation in Israel? Far from it!* (14:45). They answer Saul's oath with their own, *As Yahweh lives*, and they
*rescued*, *wayyip̄dû* (WLC), the verb of redeeming the firstborn (Exodus 13:13–15, stored WEB). The law already
set a valuation for a person consecrated in a vow (Leviticus 27:1–8, stored WEB), and readers have long asked why
Jephthah did not use it (a common question, reported, not traced to a source here); the text of Judges does not raise
it, and 1 Samuel 14 does not cite it. The wiki records only that one
child was redeemed and the other was not.

**What the narrators say.** Neither narrator condemns the father in his own voice. Judges reports the vow kept and
the custom that followed (11:39–40). 1 Samuel reports the people's verdict and leaves Saul's oath broken without
comment, except that the next verse ends the pursuit (14:46). The judgment in each case is spoken by a character:
Jephthah's *you have brought me very low*, Jonathan's *My father has troubled the land*.

## Significance

The pair shows a sworn word outrunning the knowledge of the man who swore it, and a child paying, or nearly paying,
for it. It belongs with the other rash words of the books stored so far: Jacob's and his sons' over a search (see
[[Whoever You Find Your Gods With - Two Rash Oaths Over a Search]]) and the law of rash speech in Leviticus 5:4 (see
[[Sinning Unwittingly - Integrity, Ignorance, and Guilt]]). It differs from them in that the oath is sworn in war,
for victory, by the man leading Israel. Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 counsels against a vow made and not paid (see
[[Oaths and Vows]]); it cites neither narrative.
