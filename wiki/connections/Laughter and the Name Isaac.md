# Laughter and the Name Isaac

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 17:17, 19** — *"Then Abraham fell on his face, and laughed, and said in his heart, 'Will
  a child be born to him who is one hundred years old? Will Sarah, who is ninety years old, give
  birth?'"*, and four verses later *"You shall call his name Isaac."* See
  [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].
- **Genesis 18:12–15** — *"Sarah laughed within herself"*; *"Why did Sarah laugh, saying, 'Will I
  really bear a child, yet I am old?'"*; *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"*; and *"I didn't
  laugh," for she was afraid. He said, "No, but you did laugh."* See
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Genesis 21:6** — after the birth: *"God has made me laugh. Everyone who hears will laugh with
  me."*
- **Genesis 21:9** — *"Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham,
  mocking"* — one Hebrew participle, *məṣaḥēq*, from the same root, and the reason Ishmael is
  expelled.
- **Genesis 26:8** — Isaac *"caressing"* Rebekah, seen through a window by Abimelech: the same verb
  again, in its third sense, and it exposes another wife-sister ruse.

## Analysis

***Yiṣḥāq***, **Isaac**, is an ordinary Semitic name of a very common type — an imperfect verb with
an unstated divine subject, *may God smile* or *he laughs* — built the same way as *Jacob* and
*Joseph*. Genesis ignores the ordinary sense and builds four scenes on the plain one, so that the son
of the promise walks through the rest of the book carrying a joke about his own conception.

The root is **ṣ-ḥ-q**, and Genesis uses every register it has: to laugh at something absurd (17:17;
18:12), to laugh with delight (21:6), to mock (21:9), and to fondle (26:8). It is the book's most
overworked pun, and each turn of it costs somebody something.

### Two laughs, one arithmetic, and two different receptions

| | Abraham, 17:17 | Sarah, 18:12 |
|---|---|---|
| Where | *in his heart*, face down | *within herself*, behind the tent door |
| At what | *a hundred years old … ninety years old* | *"After I have grown old will I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"* |
| Divine response | none; the next words are the son's name | *"Why did Sarah laugh, saying, 'Will I really bear a child, yet I am old?'"* and *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"* |
| Aftermath | he asks for Ishmael instead | she denies it, and is contradicted |

**They are laughing at the same promise, and not at the same thing about it.** Abraham does the
years — *"a hundred years old … ninety years old"*. Sarah does the body: *"After I have grown old
will I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"* — *ʿednâ*, a word that occurs nowhere else in the
Hebrew Bible and is not about fertility. The narrator has already given her the grounds, in the
verse before: *"Sarah had passed the age of childbearing"*, which is the Bible's one statement of
menopause.

The wiki notes that a long tradition distinguishes
Abraham's laugh as joyful faith and Sarah's as unbelief — Calvin and much of the rabbinic material
both go that way, and the Targums soften 17:17 — and that Genesis supplies no basis for it. Neither
laugh is described with an adjective; the only difference the text records is that one is questioned
and one is not, and that Sarah is the one caught out.

**And the question she is asked is a misquotation.** Yahweh reports her as saying *"Will I really
bear a child, yet I am old?"* (18:13) and leaves out the clause about her husband's age. The Talmud
takes the omission as the Torah's own licence to alter a report for the sake of peace in a marriage
(*b. Yebamot* 65b; *Bava Metzia* 87a) — which, if it is right, means the one laugh that gets
questioned is also the one the text handles gently.

What Genesis does instead is let the name absorb both. When the child arrives, Sarah says *"God has
made me laugh"* — the causative, *ṣāḥaq* with God as subject — and adds *"Everyone who hears will
laugh with me"*, which turns a private, embarrassed laugh into a public one and makes the boy's name
a statement about God rather than about her. It is one of the very few moments in Genesis where
someone reinterprets their own worst moment out loud.

### And then it turns

Genesis 21:9 has Sarah see Ishmael *məṣaḥēq* — *Isaac-ing*, in the intensive — and demand that he be
cast out. The participle has no object in the Hebrew, which is why the translations diverge so widely
(*mocking*, *playing*, *laughing*, and in the Septuagint and Galatians 4:29 *persecuting*), and the
wiki records that the reason for the expulsion is genuinely underdetermined by the text. What is not
underdetermined is the wordplay: the son named *laughter* has a brother caught *laughing*, and the
household breaks over a verb.

Then the root's last appearance in the cycle, at 26:8, catches Isaac himself: Abimelech looks out of
a window and sees him *məṣaḥēq* with Rebekah, which proves she is his wife and not his sister. The
name that began as a joke about whether he could be conceived ends as evidence that he is married.
See [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].

## Significance

- **The promise's fulfilment is named after the reaction to it.** God does not name the boy *he
  believed* or *God provided*; he takes the word for what both parents did when they heard, and makes
  it the name of the covenant line. Genesis is not embarrassed by the laugh.
- **It is the strongest evidence that Genesis does not moralize its own patriarchs.** The laugh
  that would have to be condemned for the standard reading to work is Abraham's, and it is answered
  with a son.
- **And the root keeps the family honest for ten chapters.** Every time somebody laughs in the
  Abraham cycle it costs something — a demand for an explanation, an expulsion, an exposed lie — and
  the name of the promised son is the word for it.
