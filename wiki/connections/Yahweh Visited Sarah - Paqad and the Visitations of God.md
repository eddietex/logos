# Yahweh Visited Sarah - Paqad and the Visitations of God

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 21:1** — *"Yahweh visited Sarah as he had said, and Yahweh did to Sarah as he had
  spoken."* The verb's first occurrence in the Bible. See
  [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].
- **Genesis 50:24–25** — *"God will surely visit you, and bring you up out of this land to the land
  which he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob"* — Joseph's last words, and the last words of
  Genesis but three.
- **Exodus 3:16; 4:31; 13:19** — the promise cashed: *"I have surely visited you, and seen that which
  is done to you in Egypt"*, and the people believing *"when they heard that Yahweh had visited the
  children of Israel"*.
- **Ruth 1:6** — *"Yahweh had visited his people in giving them bread."*
- **1 Samuel 2:21** — *"Yahweh visited Hannah, and she conceived"* — the other closed womb, opened in
  the same word.
- **Job 7:18** — *"that you should visit him every morning, and test him every moment?"* — the same
  verb, and Job does not want it.
- **Exodus 20:5; 32:34; Amos 3:2; Jeremiah 5:9** — the other half of the word: *visiting the iniquity
  of the fathers*, *I will visit their sin on them*, *therefore I will visit on you all your
  iniquities*.
- **Luke 1:68, 78; 7:16** — *"he has visited and redeemed his people"*; *"God has visited his
  people"*. The Greek is *episkeptomai*, which is what the Septuagint uses for *pāqad*.

## Analysis

***Pāqad*** is one of the hardest verbs in the Hebrew Bible to translate, and English has never found
a single word for it. It is not *to come* and not *to appear*; it is **to turn one's attention to
someone with consequences** — to attend to, to take account of, to muster, to appoint, to call in.
The same root gives *pəquddîm*, the mustered men of Numbers, and *pāqîd*, an overseer. The King James
tradition renders it *visit*, which is archaic English for exactly this: an official coming, after
which something is different.

**It enters the Bible in Genesis 21:1, and its object is a barren ninety-year-old woman.**

That placement is not incidental. The verb's whole career in the Old Testament is about the interval
between a promise and its execution — about a God who is absent from a situation for a long time and
then is not. Sarah has been barren since 11:30, ten chapters and twenty-five years back; the promise
has been dated twice (17:21; 18:14) and laughed at twice; the chapter immediately before this one
took her into a foreign king's house and had to state outright that nothing happened. **Then one
verb, and the interval closes.** The rest of Genesis 21:1 is about nothing but the reliability of the
words: *as he had said*, *as he had spoken*, and in the next verse *at the set time of which God had
spoken to him*.

### The chain from Sarah to the Exodus

Genesis closes on the same verb, in an emphatic doubled form (*pāqōd yipqōd*), and puts it in
Joseph's mouth as the last promise of the book:

> **24** Joseph said to his brothers, “I am dying, but God will surely visit you, and bring you up out
> of this land to the land which he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.”

**A word that opened one womb is made to carry a nation out of Egypt.** Exodus picks it up on cue:
God tells Moses to say *"I have surely visited you"* (3:16), and the sign that the message is genuine
is the people's reaction at 4:31 — they believe *when they hear that Yahweh has visited them*. It is
the password of the exodus, and Genesis 21:1 is where it was minted.

The line continues: a famine ends because *"Yahweh had visited his people in giving them bread"*
(Ruth 1:6); another barren woman conceives because *"Yahweh visited Hannah"* (1 Samuel 2:21). **In
every one of these the visitation is a reversal of a long, specific, named absence** — barrenness,
slavery, famine — and never a general benevolence.

### And the same word means punishment

*Pāqad* is not a warm verb. The Decalogue uses it for the thing God does to iniquity — *"visiting the
iniquity of the fathers on the children"* (Exodus 20:5) — and the prophets use it that way constantly:
*"therefore I will visit on you all your iniquities"* (Amos 3:2), said to the one people God chose,
as the consequence of having been chosen.

**This is the same act described from the other side.** To be attended to by God is to be counted,
and being counted cuts both ways: it produces a son for Sarah and a reckoning for Israel. Job knows
it and wants none of it — *"that you should visit him every morning, and test him every moment?"*
(7:18) is his complaint that God's attention is the problem, not its absence. See
[[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]].

**English loses this and the Bible does not have to.** *Visitation* in older translations carries both
senses, which is why the older versions read more coherently here than modern ones that split the verb
into *came to help* and *punished* according to context.

### Into the New Testament

The Septuagint renders *pāqad* with *episkeptomai*, and Luke — who writes the most Septuagintal Greek
in the New Testament — uses it at exactly the load-bearing points of his first chapters. Zechariah's
song opens *"Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, for he has visited and worked redemption for his
people"* (1:68) and closes on *"the dawn from on high will visit us"* (1:78); and when the widow's
son at Nain is raised, the crowd says *"God has visited his people"* (7:16).

**Luke 1 is also a chapter about two closed wombs**, one of them an old woman's, and it is written
throughout in the language of Genesis 18 and 21 and 1 Samuel 1–2. When Elizabeth conceives and
Zechariah says *visited*, the word is doing the work it was given at Genesis 21:1.

## Significance

- **It is the Bible's word for the end of an interval.** Not for God's presence in general, which the
  Old Testament assumes, but for the specific moment when a promise long outstanding is executed. The
  first such moment in Scripture is a birth nobody except God still expected.
- **It ties the promise to the exodus by vocabulary and not by argument.** Genesis 21:1, Genesis
  50:24, Exodus 3:16 and Exodus 4:31 are one verb repeated across four hundred years of narrative
  time, and the Torah never has to say they belong together.
- **It refuses the split between mercy and judgment.** The same word does both, because both are what
  it means for God to take account of someone. See [[Judgment]] and [[Remembrance]] — *pāqad* and
  *zākar* are the two verbs Scripture uses for God turning back toward what he had left alone, and
  Genesis puts one at 8:1 and the other at 21:1.
