# Do Not Despise the Chastening - Job 5.17, Proverbs 3.11-12, and Hebrews 12

## Passages involved

- **Job 5:17–18** — Eliphaz: *"Behold, happy is the man whom God corrects. Therefore do not despise
  the chastening of the Almighty. For he wounds, and binds up. He injures, and his hands make
  whole."* — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]
- **Proverbs 3:11–12** — *"My son, don't despise Yahweh's discipline, neither be weary of his
  correction; for whom Yahweh loves, he corrects, even as a father reproves the son in whom he
  delights."*
- **Psalm 94:12** — *"Blessed is the man whom you discipline, Yah, and teach out of your law"*
- **Deuteronomy 8:5** — *"as a man disciplines his son, so Yahweh your God disciplines you"*
- **Job 33:14–30; 36:8–15** — Elihu's development of the same idea: God *"opens their ears to
  instruction"* through pain, and *"delivers the afflicted by their affliction"* (36:15)
- **Hebrews 12:4–13** — Proverbs 3:11–12 quoted from the Septuagint (*"whom the Lord loves, he
  disciplines, and chastises every son whom he receives"*), and expounded: *"It is for discipline
  that you endure. God deals with you as with children."*
- **Revelation 3:19** — *"As many as I love, I reprove and chasten"*
- **Job 2:3** — God's own word for Job's affliction: *ḥinnām*, *without cause* — [[The Second Test]]

## Analysis

**Job 5:17 and Proverbs 3:11 share a phrase.** *Mûsar … ʾal-timʾās*, *do not reject the discipline
of*, appears in both, with *Šadday* in Job where Proverbs has *Yahweh*; and both pair it with the
verb *yākaḥ*, *correct* or *reprove*. Job 5:17 opens with *ʾašrê*, *happy* or *blessed*, the
beatitude word that also opens Psalm 94:12 on the same subject. Whether Job's poet knew Proverbs, or
both drew on a saying already current in wisdom circles, the three verses are recognisably one
teaching: suffering can be God's instruction, and the right response is not to refuse it.

**What the three biblical uses add, and what Eliphaz's leaves out, is the father.** Proverbs grounds
the discipline in love — *"whom Yahweh loves, he corrects, even as a father"*. Deuteronomy 8:5 makes
it a father with a son. Hebrews builds a whole argument on that clause: discipline is the proof of
sonship, *"if you are without discipline … then you are illegitimate, and not children"* (12:8).
**Eliphaz's version has no father and no love.** God is *ʾĕlôah* and *Šadday*; the sufferer is
*ʾĕnôš*, a mortal; and the reason given for accepting the discipline is not that God loves you but
that he heals what he wounds (5:18) and will restore everything if you submit (5:19–26). The
teaching is the same; the relationship that makes it bearable has been taken out.

**Elihu, later in the book, gives the discipline account its fullest statement.** Chapters 33 and
36 describe God warning men through dreams and through pain on the sickbed so as to turn them from
pride and keep them from the pit. Elihu is not rebuked at 42:7, and many readers take his speeches
as a corrective that moves closer to the truth than the friends. But Elihu's account rests on the
same premise as Eliphaz's: that the pain is aimed at something in the sufferer that needs
correcting.

**The book of Job rules out that premise for this case, and only for this case.** The prologue
gives the reason for Job's suffering, and it is not correction. God calls it *ḥinnām*, *without
cause* (2:3), and the reader has watched the test proposed in heaven for reasons that have nothing
to do with any fault in Job. So the book does not deny that God disciplines; it denies that every
suffering is discipline, and that a sufferer can be told which kind his is by someone who was not
in the council.

**Hebrews applies the teaching where it fits.** Its audience is a church under pressure — *"You have
not yet resisted to blood, striving against sin"* (12:4) — and it reads that pressure through
Proverbs, as a Father training his children. It does not apply the saying to Job, and it is worth
noticing that when the New Testament does mention Job, at James 5:11, the lesson drawn is not
discipline but endurance and *"the end of the Lord"*: *"the Lord is full of compassion and mercy."*

**A coincidence of the canon.** Six verses after quoting Proverbs 3:11–12, Hebrews 12:12 quotes
Isaiah 35:3: *"lift up the hands that hang down and the feeble knees."* That is the language Eliphaz
used to praise Job's past ministry at Job 4:3–4 — *"you have strengthened the weak hands … made firm
the feeble knees."* Hebrews is not alluding to Job, but its one paragraph on suffering reuses both
images that frame Eliphaz's speech: the strengthened knees and the discipline of God.

## Significance

- The saying is one of the few places where Job's friends voice something the rest of Scripture
  teaches without qualification. That is what makes it the clearest case of the book's central
  problem: **a true teaching applied to the wrong sufferer is a false diagnosis.**
- It gives the [[Suffering]] theme's *discipline* account its canonical line, from Proverbs through
  Job and Elihu to Hebrews and Revelation, and it marks where Job's case sits off that line.
- It is a caution for pastoral use. Hebrews 12 is a text for people who can say, as its audience
  could, that they are being trained. Job 5:17, said to a man who could not, is the same text used
  as an accusation.
