# The Prayer of the Upright - Proverbs 15.8, 29 and Job 42.8

## Analysis

**Proverbs 15:8 does not set prayer against sacrifice.** It sets the wicked against the upright, and
lets each be represented by an act of worship. The wicked man's offering is an *abomination*; the
upright man's prayer is a *delight*. Swap the acts and the verdicts would stand, because the next verse
says what is actually being judged: *"The way of the wicked is an abomination to Yahweh, but he loves him
who follows after righteousness"* (15:9). Verse 29 completes the pair from the other side: Yahweh *"is
far from the wicked"* and *"hears the prayer of the righteous"*. The worship is received or refused
according to the worshipper.

**Proverbs develops the point three more times, each sharper.** Righteousness and justice are *"more
acceptable to Yahweh than sacrifice"* (21:3) — a comparison, not an abolition. The wicked man's sacrifice
is an abomination, *"how much more"* when brought *"with a wicked mind"* (21:27) — so that even the
wicked man's offering is worse when it is calculated. And at 28:9 the line reaches prayer itself: the
man who will not hear the law finds *"even his prayer is an abomination"*. **Prayer, which 15:8 made the
upright man's act, is not safe in the wrong mouth either.** None of these sayings disparages the
offerings Exodus commands; they place them under a condition.

**The book of Job had already narrated the principle.** Job's offerings for his children (1:5) are the
first worship in the book, made *"continually"* against sins that only *"may"* have happened. At the end,
God tells the three friends to bring seven bulls and seven rams — and then makes the acceptance hinge not
on the offering but on Job: *"my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him"* (42:8). The
narrator's report is exact: *"Yahweh accepted Job"* (42:9). The friends' sacrifice is accepted by way of
the upright man's prayer. **Proverbs 15:8 is a general sentence; Job 42:8–9 is a case of it**, with the
added irony that the *upright* man whose prayer is God's delight is the one the friends spent thirty
chapters calling wicked.

**What the parallel does not say.** Proverbs never names Job or alludes to the epilogue, and the two
texts are compared here by subject. Nor does Job 42 make the friends *wicked* in the sense of Proverbs
15:8 — their offence is that they *"have not spoken of me the thing that is right"* (42:8), and their
sacrifice is commanded, not refused. The connection is the shape the two share: worship whose acceptance
depends on the standing of the one who offers or prays.

### Proverbs 21 in context

Proverbs 21 ranks righteousness and justice above sacrifice (3), repeats 15:8's verdict on the
wicked man's sacrifice with *"how much more"* for wicked intent (27), and adds that the one deaf to
the poor will cry unheard (13). Job's unanswered cries (Job 19:7; 30:20) limit verse 13 to a warning
rather than a diagnosis. See

## Significance

The pairing gives [[Sacrifice and Offering]] and [[Prayer]] a joint rule that runs from the wisdom books
into the prophets: worship is not a transaction separable from the worshipper's way. It also adds one
more line to the wiki's reading of Job's epilogue — the man whose case the friends judged from outside is
the one whose prayer God hears. See also [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]] and [[Righteousness]].
