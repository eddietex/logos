# From Adam to Jesus - The Genealogy of Genesis 5 in Chronicles and Luke

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 5:1–32** — the register, with ages, fatherings, and deaths — [[From Adam to Noah]]
- **Genesis 11:10–26** *(not yet ingested)* — the second ten-generation register, Shem to Abram,
  built on the same template
- **1 Chronicles 1:1–4** — the same ten names, stripped of every number
- **Matthew 1:1** *(not yet ingested)* — *"The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ"*
- **Luke 3:36–38** *(not yet ingested)* — the same ten names in reverse, from Jesus back to *"Adam,
  the son of God"*

## Analysis

Genesis 5 is the first link in the longest chain the Bible draws, and the three later texts that
pick it up each do something different with it.

**1 Chronicles 1:1–4 removes everything but the names.** The Chronicler opens his whole work —
nine chapters of genealogy before a word of narrative — with *"Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan,
Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth."* No ages, no
fatherings, no deaths, no Enoch being taken, no Lamech naming his son. Thirty-two verses become
four. What that demonstrates is that the ages were never the point of the list for the tradition
that transmitted it: the line is the point, and the numbers are freight the Chronicler was willing
to drop. It is also a useful control on any argument that treats the numbers as the chapter's
purpose.

**Luke 3 runs it backwards, and past Adam.** Luke's genealogy of Jesus ascends rather than
descends, and where Matthew stops at Abraham, Luke keeps going: *"…the son of Enos, the son of
Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God"* (3:38). Two consequences. It makes Jesus's descent
universal rather than merely Israelite, which fits a Gospel written for gentile readers and
matching its emphasis elsewhere. And it puts *"son of God"* at the end of a chain of human
fathers, four verses after the voice at the baptism said *"You are my beloved Son"* — a deliberate
juxtaposition, and Luke's way of setting the two sonships beside one another.

One textual wrinkle worth knowing: most manuscripts of Luke 3:36 include a **second Cainan**,
between Arphaxad and Shelah, who is absent from the Hebrew of Genesis 11 but present in the
Septuagint. Luke is following the Greek text. It is a small thing with a large implication — the
biblical genealogies as transmitted do not all agree on the number of generations, which is
independent evidence that they were not being kept as arithmetic.

**Matthew 1:1 borrows the heading, not the list.** *Biblos geneseōs Iēsou Christou* — *"the book of
the genealogy of Jesus Christ"* — is the Septuagint's exact phrase at Genesis 5:1 (*biblos
geneseōs anthrōpōn*, and at 2:4 of the heavens and the earth). Matthew opens the New Testament by
quoting the formula Genesis uses to start the human race, and applies it to one man. Then he does
something Genesis 5 would not: he arranges the descent into three sets of fourteen (1:17) and, to
get there, omits three kings the Old Testament names between Joram and Uzziah. Matthew states the
count openly, so this is not error — it is a biblical genealogy telling the reader in its own voice
that it is selective by design.

**Which bears directly on Genesis 5.** The question every chronology built from this chapter has to
answer is whether the list is a complete father-to-son chain. The evidence pulls both ways, and
both halves are worth holding:

- *For gaps being possible:* the Hebrew verb *yālad* in the causative — WEB's *"became the father
  of"* — is used elsewhere of more distant descent, and Matthew 1 proves that a canonical
  genealogy can skip generations deliberately. Ancient genealogies generally served to establish a
  line, not to measure time.
- *Against gaps here:* Genesis 5 does not merely name fathers and sons — it gives each man's age at
  the fathering, which is what makes the list additive at all. Interpolating an unnamed generation
  breaks the arithmetic rather than extending it. Whatever this list is doing, the numbers are
  built to be summed, and the summing produces the AM chronology on [[From Adam to Noah]].

The tidy resolution — *Genesis 5 has gaps, so the earth can be old* — has the merit of the general
observation about genealogies and the difficulty that this particular genealogy is the one that
resists it. Anyone using the gaps argument should notice that it works much better on Genesis 11's
list, and better still on Matthew's, than on this one.

**Ten and ten and fourteen.** Adam to Noah is ten generations; Shem to Abram (11:10–26) is ten;
Matthew's three sets are fourteen each. Round, schematic numbers are how these lists are shaped
everywhere, which is a fact about the genre rather than an accusation against it. The Sumerian King
List likewise runs its antediluvian rulers in a set count with a flood after it.

## Significance

Genesis 5 is why the Bible can claim, at its other end, that Jesus is descended from the first man.
Everything between Luke 3:38 and Genesis 5:3 is one line, and the line is only unbroken because
this chapter recorded it — nine names that do nothing, said nothing, and went nowhere, kept because
the tenth one mattered and because someone after him would.

It also sets the pattern the wiki will keep meeting: a genealogy is never neutral. Chronicles uses
this one to say the story starts at Adam and runs to Israel; Luke uses it to say it runs to Jesus
and back to God; Matthew borrows its opening words to say a new *genesis* has begun. The same ten
names carry three different arguments.
