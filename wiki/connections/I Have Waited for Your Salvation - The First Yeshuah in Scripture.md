# I Have Waited for Your Salvation - The First Yeshuah in Scripture

## Passages involved

- Genesis 49:18 — *"I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh"* — **the first occurrence of the noun
  *yəšûʿâ* in the Bible**
- Exodus 14:13 — *"stand still, and see the salvation of Yahweh"* — the noun's next major use, at the
  sea
- Exodus 15:2 — *"Yah… has become my salvation"*, in the Song of the Sea
- Psalm 3:8 — *"Salvation belongs to Yahweh"*; Psalm 62:1; Psalm 119:166, *"I have hoped for your
  salvation, Yahweh"* — **the Psalter's near-quotation of Genesis 49:18**
- Isaiah 12:2–3; 52:10 — *"the wells of salvation"*; *"all the ends of the earth have seen the
  salvation of our God"*
- Jonah 2:9 — *"Salvation belongs to Yahweh"*, from inside the fish
- Luke 2:30 — Simeon: *"my eyes have seen your salvation"*

## Analysis

**Genesis 49:18 does not belong where it is, and that is the first thing to say about it.**

The poem is twelve sayings addressed to twelve sons. **Verse 18 is five Hebrew words, in the first
person, addressed to God** — ***lîšûʿātəkā qiwwîtî YHWH*** — **and it sits between [[Dan (person)]]'s
serpent (49:17) and [[Gad]]'s raiders (49:19), with nothing to connect it to either.** It is **the
only prayer in the chapter** and **the only occurrence of the name *Yahweh* in the chapter**, which
otherwise names God three times in two verses and always by a title.

**Three readings, and Genesis supports none of them explicitly:**

- **It belongs to Dan's saying.** The tribe that fights from ambush and produces Samson is the one
  that most needs deliverance, and the rabbinic tradition reads the verse as Jacob seeing Samson's
  end and praying.
- **It divides the poem.** Six sayings precede it and six follow; it sits at the hinge, and a caesura
  at that point is metrically defensible.
- **It is not placed at all** — a dying man's interjection, breath taken in the middle of a long
  speech, which the text preserves because it was spoken.

**The wiki records all three and adopts none.** What it does state is **the verb**: ***qiwwîtî***, *I
have waited*, from *qāwâ* — **the waiting that is straining toward, not the waiting that is passing
time.** It is the verb of Psalm 25:5, Psalm 130:5 and Isaiah 40:31, *"those who wait for Yahweh will
renew their strength"*. **The tense is perfect: he is reporting a lifetime of it, not beginning
one.**

**And the noun is the point.** ***Yəšûʿâ*** occurs about seventy-eight times in the Old Testament
and **this is the first.** It is the root of ***Yəhôšuaʿ*** (Joshua), ***Yəšaʿyāhû*** (Isaiah),
***Hôšēaʿ*** (Hosea), and ***Yēšûaʿ***, which is the name Matthew 1:21 glosses — *"you shall call
his name Jesus, for it is he who shall save his people from their sins."* **The word enters Scripture
in a deathbed poem in Egypt, with no definition, no object and no explanation.**

**What Genesis does not do is say what he is waiting for.** **There is no *from what* and no *for
whom*.** The next book supplies one — *"see the salvation of Yahweh, which he will work for you
today"*, at the edge of a sea (Exodus 14:13) — **but Genesis leaves the noun bare**, and that is how
the Psalter mostly uses it afterwards.

## Significance

**Psalm 119:166 is close enough to Genesis 49:18 to be a deliberate echo**: *śibbartî lîšûʿātəkā
YHWH*, *"I have hoped for your salvation, Yahweh"* — **the same three elements in the same order**,
with a different verb of waiting. **The wiki notes the resemblance and does not claim a citation**,
because the vocabulary is the Psalter's ordinary stock.

**The verse's real weight is where it falls in a life.** [[Jacob]] has been given a promise at
[[Bethel]], has wrestled for a blessing, has buried a wife on a road and mourned a son he thought
dead for twenty years, and is now dying in a foreign country with his family settled in it. **The
one thing he says directly to God in his last chapter is that he is still waiting.** **Genesis does
not tell us whether the waiting was answered**, and one chapter later he is carried to a cave in a
land his descendants will not hold for four hundred years. See
[[Buried in a Good Old Age - The Promise at Genesis 15.15 and Its Fulfilment]].

**Simeon in Luke 2:29–30 is the closest New Testament parallel and the wiki files it as a
parallel** — an old man at the end of his life, saying he has seen what he was waiting for, in a
scene the Gospel builds out of Old Testament waiting. **Genesis 49:18 is the same posture without
the sight.** See [[Now Let Me Die, Since I Have Seen - Jacob and Simeon]].
