# Abram the Hebrew - The First Use of the Name

## Passages involved

- Genesis 14:13 — *"One who had escaped came and told Abram, the Hebrew"*
- Genesis 10:21, 24–25; 11:14–17 — Eber, and the line named after him
- Genesis 39:14, 17; 40:15; 41:12; 43:32 — the word on Egyptian lips, and on Joseph's to Egyptians
- Exodus 1:15–19; 2:6–7, 11, 13; 3:18; 5:3; 7:16; 9:1, 13; 10:3 — Pharaoh, the midwives, and
  *"Yahweh, the God of the Hebrews"*
- 1 Samuel 4:6, 9; 13:3, 19; 14:11, 21; 29:3 — the Philistines' word for Israel
- Jonah 1:9 — *"I am a Hebrew"*, said to sailors
- Joshua 24:2–3 — *"beyond the River"*
- 2 Corinthians 11:22; Philippians 3:5 — Paul, *"a Hebrew of Hebrews"*

## Analysis

**It appears exactly once in the Abraham cycle, and in the one chapter with an international cast.**
*ʿIbrî* enters the Bible at Genesis 14:13, in the clause that identifies Abram to a stranger who has
run from a battle. It does not occur again until Potiphar's wife uses it, and the pattern of its
occurrences is unusually consistent:

- **Foreigners use it of Israelites.** Potiphar's wife (39:14, 17), the cupbearer describing Joseph
  (41:12), Pharaoh's daughter (Exodus 2:6), the Philistines throughout 1 Samuel — *"Let the Hebrews
  hear"*, *"the Hebrews are coming out of the holes"*.
- **Israelites use it of themselves when speaking to foreigners.** Joseph explaining himself to the
  cupbearer: *"I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews"* (40:15). Moses and Aaron to
  Pharaoh: *"Yahweh, the God of the Hebrews"*. Jonah to the sailors: *"I am a Hebrew."*
- **They almost never use it among themselves.** Israel calls itself Israel, or the sons of Jacob,
  or the people of Yahweh. *ʿIbrî* is an outward-facing word.

Genesis 14 is the only chapter so far in which Abram has dealings with anyone outside his household:
four eastern kings, five kings of the plain, three Amorite brothers, a priest-king of Salem, and an
escaped man with news. It is precisely where an outward-facing label would be needed, and it is the
only place the book uses one.

**The derivation is disputed, and both candidates are live.**

- **From [[Eber]]**, Shem's great-grandson. Genesis 10:21 introduces the whole Semitic branch as
  *"the father of all the children of Eber"* — an odd way to introduce Shem unless the name is
  doing work — and 11:14–17 puts Eber squarely in Abram's line. This is the etymology the Table of
  Nations itself seems to expect.
- **From *ʿābar*, to cross over**, which would make *ʿibrî* *the one from the other side*. Joshua
  24:2–3 supplies the geography: *"Your fathers lived of old time beyond the River … I took your
  father Abraham from beyond the River."* The Septuagint's rendering at Genesis 14:13 is *ho
  peratēs*, *the one who crossed over*, so the reading is ancient. On this account the word is
  originally a description of where someone came from rather than who he descends from.

The two are not exclusive — Eber's own name is built on the same root — and Genesis, which loves to
supply etymologies, supplies none here.

**A third possibility sits behind both.** The *ʿApiru* or *Habiru* of second-millennium Akkadian,
Egyptian, and Amarna texts are not an ethnic group but a social class: stateless people, migrants,
mercenaries, outlaws, and displaced labourers on the edges of settled society. The phonetic match to
*ʿibrî* is close and much argued over; the equation has been proposed, attacked, and revived for a
century and is not settled. What makes it interesting is not whether it is right but that a term
meaning *rootless outsider* would fit the man Genesis 14 is describing exactly: a resident alien in
a land he does not own, camped under someone else's trees, with a private army and a treaty and no
city.

## Significance

The word arrives at the moment Abram becomes visible to the world outside his tent. Everything the
chapter shows — the treaty with Amorites, the raid past Damascus, the audience with two kings, the
blessing accepted from a foreign priest and the money refused from a foreign king — is a man being
dealt with by outsiders. They need a name for him, and the name they use is the one that will follow
his descendants through Egypt, through the Philistine wars, onto a ship in Joppa, and into Paul's
list of credentials. It means, on either etymology, *the ones from the other side*.
