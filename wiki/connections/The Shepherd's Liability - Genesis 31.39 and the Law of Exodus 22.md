# The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 31:38–40** — *"That which was torn of animals, I didn't bring to you. I bore its loss. Of
  my hand you required it, whether stolen by day or stolen by night"* — [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]]
- **Exodus 22:10–13** — the law of the keeper: an oath for what dies or is driven away, restitution
  for what is stolen, and **no restitution for what is torn**
- **Amos 3:12** — the shepherd rescuing *"two legs, or a piece of an ear"* from a lion's mouth: the
  evidence the law asks for
- **John 10:11–13** — the good shepherd who does not run, against the hireling who does

## Analysis

**Jacob's longest speech in Genesis contains one clause of technical labour law, and it is a claim
about a liability the Torah will later refuse to impose.**

**The rule, stated in Exodus 22:10–13.** A man gives an animal into a neighbour's keeping. If it
dies, is injured, or is driven off with no witness, **the keeper swears an oath and is believed**
(22:10–11). If it is **stolen from him**, he makes restitution (22:12) — the assumption being that a
theft is something a keeper could have prevented. **But if it is torn by a predator, he brings the
carcass as evidence and pays nothing**: *"let him bring it for evidence. He shall not make good that
which was torn"* (22:13). **The loss falls on the owner, because a wild animal is not something a
shepherd can be expected to stop.**

**This was not a distinctively Israelite rule.** The Mesopotamian law collections make the same
division — a herdsman is not liable for losses to disease or predation in the fold, provided he can
show the carcass and swear he was not at fault, and is liable for what he negligently loses or
converts. **Exodus 22 is the Torah's version of an arrangement the whole ancient Near East ran on**,
and Laban would have known it.

**Jacob's clause denies himself every protection in it.**

- ***"That which was torn of animals, I didn't bring to you."*** ***Ṭərēpâ lōʾ-hēbēʾtî ʾēlêkā.*** The
  noun is the law's own — ***ṭərēpâ***, the torn thing — **and *bringing* it is precisely the
  procedure the exemption runs on.** He says he never used it.
- ***"I bore its loss."*** ***ʾĀnōkî ʾăḥaṭṭennâ***, the piel of *ḥāṭāʾ*, here *make good, bear the
  loss.* **He absorbed what the law says the owner absorbs.**
- ***"Of my hand you required it, whether stolen by day or stolen by night."*** **The theft clause,
  applied without limit.** Exodus 22:12 makes a keeper liable for theft; the reason is that a theft
  in daylight from a watched flock implies negligence. **Laban charged him for both, and Jacob says
  so as a single fact among others.**
- **And 31:40 supplies the reason it was hard**: *"in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost
  by night; and my sleep fled from my eyes."* **The clause about frost and sleeplessness is the
  answer to the standard defence** — he cannot claim he was not there.

**Genesis reports no ruling on any of it.** Laban does not answer the charge, and no verdict is
recorded. **The narrator supplies no comment**, exactly as it supplied none for the peeled rods.

## Significance

**The Torah's law of the keeper reads, from this side of it, like an answer to a specific complaint.**
**The wiki does not claim that it is one** — Exodus 22 is a general provision with parallels across
the region, and neither text refers to the other. **What can be said is that Genesis 31:39 states
precisely the burden Exodus 22:13 lifts**, and that the man stating it is the ancestor of the people
the law is given to.

**Read that way, the sequence is the same one the book uses elsewhere: a patriarch's experience
before there is a law, and the law afterwards.** Sacrifice before a priesthood, a tithe before a
tithing statute, a Sabbath before a commandment — **and here, twenty years of a shepherd paying for
lions and thieves, and then a rule saying he does not have to.** See
[[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]], [[The Tithe Before the Law]], and
[[Because Abraham Obeyed My Voice - Law Before Sinai]].

**It also settles what kind of speech Genesis 31:36–42 is.** It is not a lament and not a boast; **it
is a shepherd's account, laid out in the terms a court would use**, by a man who has just asked for
witnesses to be empanelled (31:37) and who opens with ***rîb***, the word for bringing a suit.
**The most legally literate paragraph in Genesis belongs to the patriarch the book is least willing
to praise.**

**And it is the one place Scripture shows what tending someone else's flock cost.** The Bible's
favourite image for a king, a prophet, and God himself is a shepherd; **Genesis 31:38–40 is the only
passage that itemises the job** — no miscarriages in twenty years, no rams eaten, losses absorbed,
drought, frost, and no sleep. **John 10:12–13 turns the same facts into a definition**: the hireling
runs because the sheep are not his, and Jacob's whole complaint is that for twenty years they were
not his and he did not run.
