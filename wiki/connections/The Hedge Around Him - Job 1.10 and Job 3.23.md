# The Hedge Around Him - Job 1.10 and Job 3.23

## Passages involved

- Job 1:6–12 — the accuser's opening complaint in the heavenly council —
  [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]]
- Job 3:20–26 — Job's closing question, in the last movement of his first speech —
  [[Let the Day Perish]]
- Hosea 2:6 — the same verb, unambiguously punitive: God hedging up an unfaithful Israel's way with
  thorns so that she cannot find her paths
- Job 19:8 · Lamentations 3:7, 9 — the enclosure image without this verb

## Analysis

**Two men use the same word about the same man, twenty-three verses apart, and mean opposite things
by it. Neither knows the other has spoken.**

> **1:10** Haven't you made a **hedge** around him, and around his house, and around all that he
> has, on every side? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his substance is increased in the
> land.

> **3:23** Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has **hedged in**?

The verb is *śûk / sûk*, *to fence in*, written with śin at 1:10 and with samek at 3:23 — two
spellings of one root — and **in both verses it takes the preposition *baʿad*, *around* or *about*,
with the same pronominal suffix: *baʿadô*, around him.** *Śaktā baʿadô* at 1:10; *wayyāsek ʾĕlôah
baʿadô* at 3:23. **These are the only two occurrences of the verb in the book of Job.**

**What each speaker sees in the fence is exactly inverted.**

| | Job 1:10 — the accuser | Job 3:23 — Job |
|---|---|---|
| Who is fenced | Job, his house, all he has | Job alone |
| What the fence does | keeps harm **out** | keeps Job **in** |
| Its effect on Job | prosperity, *"his substance is increased"* | blindness, *"whose way is hidden"* |
| The speaker's complaint | the fence is **there** | the fence is **there** |
| What the speaker wants | it taken down | it taken down |

**The last two rows are the finding.** The accuser and the sufferer agree on the fact and on the
demand, and disagree about nothing except what the fact means. **Both of them want God to stop
hedging Job, and they are the only two characters in the book who ask for it.** God grants the
accuser's version in 1:12 and never answers Job's.

**The irony is entirely for the reader, and the book is built so that it can only be for the
reader.** Job cannot know he is quoting; he was not in the council, nobody tells him it happened,
and the accuser is never mentioned again by anyone on the ground. **Job 3:23 is therefore the first
place in the book where the reader's private information becomes painful rather than merely
ironic** — Job has put his finger on the exact mechanism of his own story and has it precisely
upside down. **He complains of a hedge that was in fact removed on his behalf.**

**And the sequence is worth stating in order, because it is a small tragedy in four steps.**

1. **1:10** — a fence exists around Job, and the accuser objects that it invalidates Job's piety.
2. **1:12, 2:6** — God lowers it, twice, by explicit and limited permission.
3. **3:23** — Job, with the fence down and everything through the gap, describes himself as fenced.
4. **The book never corrects him.** Not the friends, who do not notice; not the narrator, who
   addresses only the reader; not God, who speaks from the whirlwind about wild goats.

**There is a second reversal inside the second verse, and it is the one that turns the image from
irony into a complaint.** A hedge that keeps harm out keeps the man's world intact; a hedge that
keeps a man in makes his world inaccessible. **Job's clause is not *whom God has hedged in* alone —
it is paired with *whose way is hidden*** (*ʾăšer-darkô nistārâ*). The fence and the hiddenness are
one idea: **he cannot see out, so he cannot see where he is going, so he cannot tell what he is
being punished for.** This is the complaint the rest of the book elaborates — that the case against
him is being tried somewhere he cannot attend — and it arrives here as an image before it arrives
anywhere as an argument.

**Elsewhere in Scripture the verb is unambiguous, and it is unambiguously punitive.** At Hosea 2:6
God hedges up unfaithful Israel's way with thorns and walls her in so that she cannot find her
paths — the enclosure as a discipline, imposed to stop someone getting where they want to go.
**Job 3:23 is in that register and 1:10 is not**, which is why the two verses read as different
words in translation and are not. *(Hosea is not in `raw/bible/`, so it is described here rather
than quoted.)*

## Significance

**The pair is the cleanest single demonstration of the book's central device**: the reader holds one
fact, nobody in the story holds it, and the story is arranged so that the gap keeps producing
statements that are true in a sense the speaker does not intend.

It also does something to the accuser's question. *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* (1:9) assumes
the hedge is a payment — that piety is purchased with protection, and would collapse without it.
**Job 3:23 is spoken from the far side of the experiment**, with the protection gone and the piety
still in place, and what Job says is not *you stopped paying me*. It is *you have fenced me in*.
**He is still complaining that God is too close, not that God has withdrawn** — which is the
accuser's premise failing at the level of the metaphor, several chapters before the epilogue fails
it at the level of the verdict. See [[The Fear of God]] and
[[Blameless Before Anyone Is Watching]].

The image returns at 19:8 — *"He has walled up my way so that I can't pass, and has set darkness in
my paths"* — where Job says the same thing with a different verb, and adds the darkness of chapter 3
to it, in the speech that ends in *I know that my Redeemer lives*; see
[[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]. **What was a question at 3:23 is a flat
statement by 19:8**, and the wiki reads the hardening as the effect of the friends: a man asks *why
am I fenced?*, is told for fifteen chapters that he built the fence himself, and stops asking.
