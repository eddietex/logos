# The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament

## Passages involved

- **Job 7:9** — *"As the cloud is consumed and vanishes away, so he who goes down to Sheol shall
  come up no more."* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Job 14:7–14** — *"There is hope for a tree, If it is cut down … But man dies, and is laid low …
  If a man dies, shall he live again?"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Job 19:25–27** — *"I know that my Redeemer lives … After my skin is destroyed, then in my flesh
  shall I see God."* — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Psalm 6:5; 88:10–12; 115:17** — the dead do not praise God; Sheol as the end of the relationship
- **Psalm 16:9–11** — *"you will not leave my soul in Sheol, neither will you allow your holy one to
  see corruption."*
- **Psalm 49:15** — *"But God will redeem my soul from the power of Sheol, for he will receive me."*
- **Psalm 73:23–26** — *"You will guide me with your counsel, and afterward receive me to glory."*
- **Genesis 5:24; 2 Kings 2:11** — Enoch taken, Elijah taken: two men removed from death rather than
  through it. See [[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]].
- **Isaiah 25:8** — *"He has swallowed up death forever!"*
- **Isaiah 26:19** — *"Your dead shall live. My dead bodies shall arise. Awake and sing, you who
  dwell in the dust."*
- **Ezekiel 37:1–14** — the valley of dry bones, and God's own gloss on it: *"these bones are the
  whole house of Israel"*
- **Hosea 6:2; 13:14** — *"After two days he will revive us"*; *"I will ransom them from the power
  of Sheol"*
- **Daniel 12:2–3** — *"Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to
  everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt."*
- **Matthew 22:23–33 / Mark 12:18–27 / Luke 20:27–40** — Jesus arguing the resurrection *from the
  Torah*, against the Sadducees
- **1 Corinthians 15:12–57** — the doctrine at full extension
- **Hebrews 11:19, 35** — Abraham reasoning that God could raise the dead, and those who *"were
  tortured, not accepting their deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection"*

## Analysis

**The Hebrew Bible's default position is that death ends everything human.** [[Sheol]] is the
common destination of the righteous and the wicked, it is dark and dusty, it contains no judgment
and no reward, and — the point the psalms make most often — it is out of range of the relationship
with God that makes life worth having. *"In death there is no memory of you. In Sheol, who shall
give you thanks?"* (Psalm 6:5). This is not a doctrine of annihilation and not a doctrine of
punishment; it is a doctrine of **irrelevance**, and it is what most of the Old Testament assumes
most of the time.

That default is what makes Job's problem insoluble on its own terms. If vindication can happen
after death, an unjust life is a temporary accounting error. If it cannot, then a man wrongly
condemned must be cleared before he dies or never, and everything the book's protagonist does — the
demand for a hearing, the urgency, the wish to have his words cut into rock so they outlast him —
follows from that. Job states the default himself, twice and flatly: *"he who goes down to Sheol
shall come up no more"* (7:9), and *"if a man dies, shall he live again?"* (14:14), which is a
question with an expected answer of *no*.

**Job 19:25–27 is therefore either the book's one breach of its own premise or it is not about the
afterlife at all**, and the Hebrew is too damaged to settle it. The disputed clause is
*ûmibbəśārî ʾeḥĕzeh ʾĕlôah* — *from my flesh I shall see God* — where the preposition *min* can
mean *from within* (in the body, hence after a resurrection) or *away from* (stripped of it, or
simply *from the vantage of* a wasted body still alive). The preceding half-verse, *"after my skin
is destroyed"*, renders a line whose verb is plural with no subject and whose object *zōʾt*,
*this*, has no antecedent; every English version there is a repair. The full apparatus is set out
on [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]. The wiki's reading is that Job most probably means
vindication before death — which is what the epilogue delivers — and that the verse cannot bear the
weight the resurrection tradition has put on it, while noting that the ambiguity is real and old.

**Where the hope does appear, it appears late and it appears corporately.** The two unambiguous
texts are Isaiah 26:19 and Daniel 12:2, and both are usually dated to the latest strata of the
Hebrew Bible. Ezekiel 37, which sounds like the clearest of all, is explicitly glossed by the
prophet as a figure for national restoration from exile — *"these bones are the whole house of
Israel"* — which is how Hosea 6:2 works too. The pattern is that Israel first learns to say *the
nation will be raised from the death of exile*, and the individual hope emerges out of that
vocabulary rather than the other way round. By the second century BC the individual doctrine is
established enough to be worth dying for (2 Maccabees 7) and worth denying (the Sadducees).

**The psalms occupy the interesting middle.** Psalm 49:15 and Psalm 73:24 both say that God will
*receive* the speaker — the verb *lāqaḥ*, the same word used of Enoch's being *taken* at Genesis
5:24 — in contexts that are explicitly about the deaths of the wicked and the psalmist's own end.
Neither verse describes a resurrection. Both refuse to accept that Sheol is the last word about a
man God has held onto. That is the hope in its native form: not a doctrine of what happens after
death, but a refusal to believe that the relationship is the kind of thing death can end.

**And that is the argument Jesus makes.** Pressed by Sadducees who accept only the Torah, he does
not cite Daniel 12. He cites Exodus 3:6 — *"I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God
of Jacob"* — and reasons that God *"is not the God of the dead, but of the living."* The argument
is not lexical; it is exactly the psalms' move, that a covenant relationship implies its own
continuation.

## Significance

The thread matters for reading Job honestly, and it cuts against the grain of how Job 19 is usually
quoted. The resurrection reading of 19:25–27 is ancient, it is fixed in English by the King James
Version and by Handel, and it is not impossible. But when it is assumed rather than argued, it
dissolves the book's problem: a Job who is confident of resurrection is not in the position the
first thirty-seven chapters put him in, and the whirlwind's answer — which offers no afterlife, no
compensation, and no explanation — becomes inexplicable as a response. God does not tell Job that
things will be evened out later. He shows him ostriches and storehouses of snow.

What the connection does establish is that the Hebrew Bible contains a pressure toward the
resurrection long before it contains the doctrine, and that Job is one of the places the pressure
is greatest. The pressure comes from two sources at once: the conviction that God is just, which
demands an accounting the visible world does not supply (see [[Retribution]] and
[[The Prosperity of the Wicked]]), and the conviction that God's hold on a person is not a thing
death can break. Job supplies the first in its most acute form and gestures at the second in a
verse nobody can construe. The later texts say plainly what he could not.
