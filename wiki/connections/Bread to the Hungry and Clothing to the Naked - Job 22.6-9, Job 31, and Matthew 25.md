# Bread to the Hungry and Clothing to the Naked - Job 22.6-9, Job 31, and Matthew 25

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 22:22–27; Deuteronomy 24:6, 10–13, 17** *(not yet ingested)* — the widow and the
  fatherless not to be afflicted, and the pledged cloak returned before the sun goes down, *"for
  that is his only covering"*
- **Job 4:3–4** — Eliphaz's first description of Job: *"you have strengthened the weak hands. Your
  words have supported him who was falling"* — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]
- **Job 20:19** — Zophar, of the wicked man in general: *"he has oppressed and forsaken the poor"* —
  [[Zophar's Second Speech]]
- **Job 22:6–9** — Eliphaz, to Job: *"you have taken pledges from your brother for nothing, and
  stripped the naked of their clothing. You haven't given water to the weary to drink, and you have
  withheld bread from the hungry … You have sent widows away empty, and the arms of the fatherless
  have been broken."* — [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]
- **Job 24:3, 9–10** *(not yet ingested)* — Job, of real oppressors: *"they take the widow's ox for a
  pledge … There are those who pluck the fatherless from the breast, and take a pledge of the poor,
  so that they go around naked without clothing. Being hungry, they carry the sheaves."*
- **Job 29:12–17** *(not yet ingested)* — *"I delivered the poor who cried, and the fatherless also
  … I caused the widow's heart to sing for joy … I was eyes to the blind, and feet to the lame. I was
  a father to the needy."*
- **Job 31:16–22** *(not yet ingested)* — the oath: *"If I have withheld the poor from their desire,
  or have caused the eyes of the widow to fail, or have eaten my morsel alone, and the fatherless has
  not eaten of it … if I have seen any perish for want of clothing … then let my shoulder fall from
  the shoulder blade, and my arm be broken from the bone."*
- **Isaiah 58:6–7** *(not yet ingested)* — the fast God chooses: bread to the hungry, the poor
  brought into the house, the naked covered
- **Ezekiel 18:5–9, 16** *(not yet ingested)* — the righteous man defined: restores the pledge,
  gives bread to the hungry, covers the naked with a garment
- **Matthew 25:35–36, 42–43** *(not yet ingested)* — *"I was hungry, and you gave me food to eat. I
  was thirsty, and you gave me drink … I was naked, and you clothed me"*, and the same list negated
- **James 2:15–16** *(not yet ingested)* — the brother or sister *"naked and in lack of daily food"*

## Analysis

**There is a list, and Scripture keeps using it.** From the Book of the Covenant to Matthew 25 the
Bible describes the righteous person not with an abstraction but with a short, stable inventory of
acts toward the people who cannot defend themselves: *the hungry* get bread, *the thirsty* water,
*the naked* clothing; the *widow* and the *fatherless* are not afflicted; a *pledge* is not taken
from someone who cannot spare it. Ezekiel 18 uses the list as a legal definition of *ṣaddîq*; Isaiah
58 uses it as the true meaning of fasting; Jesus uses it as the criterion of the last judgment. Its
items barely change across a thousand years.

**Job 22:6–9 is that list, negated item by item.** Eliphaz needs a sin large enough to account for
Job's suffering, and what he produces is not a crime anyone saw but the canonical portrait of the
righteous man with every clause turned to its opposite — the pledge taken *"for nothing"*, the naked
stripped instead of clothed, water and bread withheld, widows sent away empty, the fatherless
broken. That is what makes the charges recognisably invented: they are not specific to Job at all.
They are the definition of righteousness, inverted, applied to a man of Job's wealth.

**They also contradict the only first-hand testimony Eliphaz gave.** At 4:3–4 he said Job had
*"strengthened the weak hands"* and *"made firm the feeble knees"*. At 22:9 the *arms* of the
fatherless are broken. Nothing has happened between the two speeches except that Job has refused to
confess.

**Job answers with the same list three times, each time differently.**

1. **In chapter 24 he gives the list a real defendant.** The pledged ox of the widow, the fatherless
   torn from the breast, the poor who *"go around naked without clothing"* and carry sheaves while
   hungry — every count of 22:6–9 is there, committed by people who move landmarks and are never
   caught, and *"God doesn't regard the folly"* (24:12). Eliphaz had used the list to explain why God
   was punishing Job; Job uses it to ask why God punishes no one.
2. **In chapter 29 he gives his own record**, positively: the poor delivered, the widow singing, a
   father to the needy, the jaws of the unrighteous broken. *"I put on righteousness, and it clothed
   me"* (29:14) — the man accused of stripping the naked describes himself as clothed in justice.
3. **In chapter 31 he swears to it**, under a self-imprecation that answers 22:9 in its own image:
   if he has lifted his hand against the fatherless, *"let my arm be broken from the bone"* (31:22).
   Eliphaz said the fatherless's arms were broken; Job offers his own.

**Matthew 25 is the list's last and largest form.** In the parable of the sheep and the goats the
list is the whole of the judgment — the nations are separated by nothing else — and the twist is
that the needy were the judge himself: *"inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these my
brothers, you did it to me."* Two features of it bear on Job. First, the righteous do not know they
were righteous (*"Lord, when did we see you hungry?"*); their deeds were not performed as credit. Job
29 is a man remembering his deeds because he has been accused, not because he is presenting a bill.
Second, the condemned are condemned for exactly the omissions of Job 22:7 — *"I was hungry, and you
didn't give me food to eat; I was thirsty, and you gave me no drink"* (25:42). Eliphaz brought the
charge of the last judgment against the one man in his book the narrator says it could not be
brought against.

## Significance

- It shows how the friends' doctrine generates evidence. A principle that infers sin from suffering
  will, when no sin is known, reach for the most standard sins available — and the most standard
  sins of a rich man are the omissions on this list.
- It makes Job 29–31 legible as a reply to a specific speech rather than a general apologia. The
  oath of chapter 31 is, in large part, Job 22:6–9 answered clause by clause.
- It sets Job inside the canon's most stable ethical tradition. Job's righteousness, as the book
  defines it, is not ritual purity or doctrinal correctness; it is this list. See [[Righteousness]],
  [[Compassion and Mercy]], [[Nakedness and Covering]], and [[Hospitality]].
