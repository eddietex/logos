# When Your Children Ask - Exodus, Deuteronomy, and Joshua

## Passages involved
- [[The Passover]] — Exodus 12:26–27.
- [[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] — Exodus 13:8–16.
- [[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]] — Deuteronomy 6:20–25.
- [[The Twelve Stones from the Jordan]] — Joshua 4:6–7, 21–24.
- [[The Altar of Witness by the Jordan]] — Joshua 22:24–28; the question the builders fear.

## Analysis

A future generation’s question calls for an account of deliverance. Exodus 12 explains the
Passover service through the sparing of Israel’s houses. Exodus 13 explains firstborn practices
through departure from bondage. Deuteronomy 6 answers a question about statutes by telling how
slaves were brought out to receive the land. Joshua 4 makes stones the prompt: Israel crossed
on dry ground, as at the Red Sea.

These are related patterns, not identical rites. The stones are not called an altar, and the
chapter commands neither sacrifice to them nor an annual stone ceremony. One representative
per tribe brings a stone; the parents’ explanation gives this national memorial its meaning.

### The question reversed — Joshua 22:24–28

Joshua 22 turns the pattern around. The eastern tribes imagine the western tribes' children
telling theirs, *What have you to do with Yahweh, the God of Israel?* and *You have no portion
in Yahweh* (24–25). The altar is built so that their own reply is ready: *Behold the pattern of
Yahweh's altar, which our fathers made … a witness between us and you* (28). Here the children's
question excludes rather than asks, and the sign is meant to answer it. Like the stones of Joshua
4, the altar stands by the Jordan. Unlike them, it imitates an altar while being denied any
sacrificial use (26, 29). See also
[[A Witness Between Us - Galeed, the Jordan Altar, and the Shechem Stone]].

Source: stored WEB Joshua 22:24–29.

## Significance

[[Remembrance]] is active transmission: a visible practice or object opens a conversation,
and God’s remembered act explains the present community. Joshua joins entry to exodus and
extends its purpose to all peoples’ knowledge and Israel’s enduring fear of Yahweh.
