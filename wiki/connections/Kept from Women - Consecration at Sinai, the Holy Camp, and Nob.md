## Analysis

**Three days before Sinai.** In Exodus 19:10–15, Israel prepares to meet Yahweh on the third day by washing garments and
abstaining from sexual relations: *"Be ready by the third day. Don't have sexual relations with a woman"* (19:15). The
law treats marital relations not as sin, but as producing temporary ritual uncleanness until evening after bathing
(Leviticus 15:18), incompatible with approaching the holy presence.

**The holy war camp.** Deuteronomy 23:9–14 extends sanctuary-like purity to the military camp: *for Yahweh your God
walks in the middle of your camp, to deliver you, and to give up your enemies before you. Therefore your camp shall be
holy* (23:14; see [[Yahweh Walks in the Camp - Genesis 3.8, Leviticus 26.12, and Deuteronomy 23.14]]). A man rendered
unclean by a nocturnal emission must go outside the camp until evening and bathe (23:10–11) — the same one-day impurity
Saul assumes for David's empty seat at the new-moon meal in 1 Samuel 20:26 ([[Jonathan's Covenant and the Arrows]]).

**Applied at Nob — and stretched.** When David asks for bread at [[Nob]], [[Ahimelech (son of Ahitub)]] has only the
replaced show bread, which Leviticus 24:9 reserves for priests. Ahimelech does not cite priestly descent; he asks
instead for the purity rule of Sinai and the war camp: *"if only the young men have kept themselves from women"*
(1 Samuel 21:4). David answers in the exact timeframe of Exodus 19:15 — *"as usual these three days"* — and adds a
lesser-to-greater argument: even on a *common journey* his men's *vessels* (gear, or euphemistically their bodies — the
text leaves *vessels* open) are kept holy, *how much more then today* (21:5). The phrase *as usual* indicates a standing
custom of sexual abstinence for men going out on a military expedition — the very custom Uriah later refuses to break
when David tries to send him home to Bathsheba in 2 Samuel 11:11 (recalled, not checked).

**A tension left intact.** Under Leviticus 22:10–16 and 24:9, ritual cleanness from sexual impurity is necessary for
priests eating holy food, not sufficient for laypeople to eat *most holy* show bread. Ahimelech treats war-camp and
Sinai purity as sufficient in David's emergency; the narrator passes no judgment, and 1 Samuel 21 becomes the precedent
Jesus cites in Matthew 12:3–4 (see [[The Bread of the Presence - David's Hunger and Matthew 12]]).

## Significance

- **Ritual purity links sanctuary, theophany, and warfare.** The same boundary — abstaining from sexual relations for
  three days or while campaigning — governs Israel before Sinai, the war camp in Deuteronomy, David's men in 1 Samuel 21,
  and Uriah's fidelity in 2 Samuel 11.
- **Holiness without moralizing sex.** Across Exodus 19, Leviticus 15, Deuteronomy 23, and 1 Samuel 21, abstaining from
  women is a boundary of ritual holiness (*qōdeš*) around Yahweh's presence and holy things, not a condemnation of
  marriage.
