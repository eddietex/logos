# The Tithe Before the Law

## Passages involved

- Genesis 14:20 — *"Abram gave him a tenth of all"*
- Genesis 28:20–22 — Jacob's vow at Bethel
- Leviticus 27:30–33 — a tenth of seed, fruit, and herd is holy to Yahweh
- Numbers 18:21–32 — the tithe as Levi's inheritance, and Levi's tithe of the tithe
- Deuteronomy 12:5–19; 14:22–29; 26:12–15 — the tithe eaten at the sanctuary, and the third-year tithe
- 2 Chronicles 31:4–12; Nehemiah 10:37–39; 13:10–12 — the reform tithes
- Amos 4:4; Malachi 3:8–10 — the prophets on both ends of it
- Matthew 23:23; Luke 11:42; 18:12 — mint, dill, and cumin
- Hebrews 7:1–10 — Levi tithed in Abraham's body

## Analysis

**Two tenths are given in Scripture before any tenth is required, and both are anomalies.**

| | Abram, Genesis 14:20 | Jacob, Genesis 28:22 | The law, Leviticus 27 / Numbers 18 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occasion | after a rescue, out of war spoil | fleeing his brother, with nothing | annual, out of produce and herd |
| Prompted by | nothing stated | a vow he proposes himself | commandment |
| Given to | a foreign priest | *"to you"* — God, at a stone | the Levites |
| Conditional | no | yes: *"If God will be with me"* | no |
| Repeated | never mentioned again | never mentioned again | perpetual |

Neither patriarch is recorded tithing twice. Genesis has two one-off tenths at two turning points
and no practice.

**The command, when it comes, is not one thing.** Leviticus 27:30–33 declares a tenth of seed,
fruit, and herd *holy to Yahweh*, redeemable at a fifth above value. Numbers 18:21–32 gives that
tenth to Levi *"in return for their service"*, because Levi has no land inheritance, and requires
the Levites to tithe a tenth of it to the priests. Deuteronomy 14:22–29 has the giver eat his tithe
at the central sanctuary — converting it to silver if the road is long, and spending it on
*"whatever your soul desires"*, wine and strong drink included — with the third year's tithe stored
in the towns for the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow. Harmonizing these
produced the rabbinic scheme of a first tithe, a second tithe, and a poor tithe; the historical-
critical reading takes them as successive legislations from different periods. Either way the
biblical tithe is not a single flat tax: it funds a landless tribe, a pilgrimage feast, and a local
welfare fund, in different proportions in different texts.

**Then the prophets pull it both ways.** Amos 4:4 mocks it — *"bring your sacrifices every morning,
your tithes every three days"* at a shrine whose worship is worthless. Malachi 3:8–10 makes
withholding it robbery of God and issues the Bible's one explicit invitation to test him. Both are
about the same act, and the difference is entirely the state of the people doing it.

**Jesus mentions it twice and never as the subject.** *"You tithe mint, dill, and cumin, and have
left undone the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faith. But you ought to have done
these, and not to have left the other undone"* (Matthew 23:23) affirms the practice inside a rebuke
about proportion; the Pharisee of Luke 18:12 tithes everything he gets and goes home unjustified.

**And Hebrews uses Genesis 14:20 for something else entirely.** *"Levi, who receives tithes, has
paid tithes through Abraham, so to speak; for he was yet in the body of his father when Melchizedek
met him"* (7:9–10). The tithe is not being commended or commanded there; it is evidence about rank.
The epistle's interest is in who paid whom.

**Which leaves the question the New Testament never answers.** No apostolic letter commands a tenth.
The giving language is proportional and voluntary — *"as he may prosper"* (1 Corinthians 16:2),
*"not grudgingly, or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver"* (2 Corinthians 9:7). One
side of the old argument reads the pre-Mosaic tithes of Genesis 14 and 28 as evidence that the tenth
predates and outlives the law; the other reads the same two texts as spontaneous, unrepeated acts
that were never a rule until Sinai made them one and never a rule again after. The texts genuinely
support both, and the wiki does not choose.

## Significance

The thread's oddity is where it starts. The first tenth in the Bible is paid by a man who has just
sworn not to take *"a thread nor a sandal strap"* from a king — so the same scene contains the
Bible's first gift to God and its first refusal of a gift from a man. Abram gives away a tenth of
what he is entitled to and returns the other nine. What the law will later organize into a system of
support for a landless tribe begins as a gesture by someone with no obligation, to a priest he had
never met, on a day he had already decided to keep nothing.

**And the second tenth is the mirror image of the first.** Abram gives a tenth of what he already
holds, to a priest standing in front of him, having just refused to keep anything for himself. Jacob
pledges a tenth of what he does not yet have, to God directly, with no priest and no sanctuary, as
the back half of a bargain that also asks for bread and clothing — and **Genesis never records him
paying it.** Two tenths, and neither one looks like the practice the law will legislate.
