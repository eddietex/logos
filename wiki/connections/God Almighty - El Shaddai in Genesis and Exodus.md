# God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 17:1** — *"I am God Almighty. Walk before me, and be blameless."* The name's first
  occurrence in Scripture. See [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].
- **Genesis 28:3** — Isaac sending Jacob to Paddan Aram: *"May God Almighty bless you, and make you
  fruitful, and multiply you."*
- **Genesis 35:11** — at Bethel, with Jacob's renaming: *"I am God Almighty. Be fruitful and multiply.
  A nation and a company of nations will be from you, and kings will come out of your body."*
- **Genesis 43:14** — Jacob sending his sons back to Egypt: *"May God Almighty give you mercy before
  the man."*
- **Genesis 48:3; 49:25** — Jacob's last two uses, quoting Bethel and blessing Joseph.
- **Exodus 6:2–3** — *"I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty; but by my name
  Yahweh I was not known to them."*
- **Numbers 24:4, 16** — Balaam, a foreign seer, *"who sees the vision of the Almighty"*.
- **Job** — thirty-one occurrences, more than the rest of the Bible combined; the standard name for
  God in the mouths of Job and his friends. See [[Job (person)]].
- **Ruth 1:20–21; Psalm 68:14; 91:1; Isaiah 13:6; Joel 1:15; Ezekiel 1:24; 10:5** — the scattered
  later uses.
- **2 Corinthians 6:18; Revelation 1:8 and eight more** — *pantokratōr*, the Septuagint's rendering,
  in the New Testament.

## Analysis

**Nobody knows what *Šadday* means.** It is one of the two or three most disputed words in the
Hebrew Bible, and the four standing proposals all have something going for them:

| Derivation | Sense | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Akkadian *šadû*, mountain | *God of the mountain* | the most widely held modern view; fits the *ʾĒl* titles Genesis attaches to places |
| Hebrew *šad*, breast | *the God who nourishes / suffices* | Genesis 49:25 pairs *Šadday* with *"blessings of the breasts, and of the womb"* |
| Hebrew *šādad*, to deal violently | *the Destroyer* | Isaiah 13:6 and Joel 1:15 pun on it: *kəšōd miš-Šadday*, *as destruction from the Almighty* |
| Rabbinic *ša-day*, *who is enough* | *the Sufficient One* | the route by which the Septuagint's *pantokratōr* and the Vulgate's *omnipotens* became English *Almighty* |

The last of these is a reading rather than an etymology, and it is the one every English Bible
inherits — so *Almighty*, the word most readers meet, is the least likely of the four to be what the
name originally meant.

### Where it is used, which is more informative than what it means

The distribution is startlingly non-random. **In Genesis the name appears six times, and every one of
them is at a transfer of the promise**: to Abraham when the covenant gets its sign (17:1), to Jacob
when Isaac sends him away to find a wife (28:3), to Jacob when he is renamed at Bethel (35:11), and
three times from Jacob's own mouth as he sends his sons to Egypt and blesses them at the end (43:14;
48:3; 49:25). It is the name under which offspring are promised, and the vocabulary that follows it
is always *fruitful*, *multiply*, *nations*, *kings*.

**In Job it appears thirty-one times** — more than everywhere else in the Bible put together — in a
book with no Israelites in it, set outside the covenant, in which nobody uses the name *Yahweh*
except the narrator and the voice from the whirlwind. That is a strong hint that *Šadday* was
remembered as an old, patriarchal, non-Israelite divine title, exactly as Genesis and Numbers 24 use
it: Balaam, a Mesopotamian diviner, has it too.

### Exodus 6:3, and the largest question in Pentateuchal criticism

*"I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty; but by my name Yahweh I was not
known to them."*

The verse says the patriarchs knew God as *ʾĒl Šadday* and not as *Yahweh*. Genesis, as it stands,
has the patriarchs using *Yahweh* constantly — from 4:26 onward, and Abram swears by *Yahweh* at
14:22 and is addressed by *"I am Yahweh"* at 15:7. Genesis 17:1 is the sharpest form of the problem
in a single verse: *"Yahweh appeared to Abram and said to him, 'I am God Almighty.'"*

Three readings, and this wiki adopts none:

1. **Source-critical.** P uses *ʾĕlōhîm* and *ʾĒl Šadday* before Exodus 6 and *Yahweh* after; E
   similarly reserves the name until Exodus 3; J uses it from the beginning. Exodus 6:2–3 is P's own
   statement of its practice, and Genesis 17 — a P chapter — obeys it exactly. On this reading the
   narrator's *Yahweh* in 17:1 is a later harmonizing touch or a J fragment.
2. **Rhetorical.** *Yādaʿ*, to know, regularly means to experience rather than to be informed of;
   the claim is that the patriarchs heard the name but did not know it in its covenant-keeping force,
   which the exodus is about to demonstrate. Exodus's refrain *"you shall know that I am Yahweh"*
   supports the sense.
3. **Grammatical.** The verse can be pointed as a rhetorical question — *"and by my name Yahweh was I
   not known to them?"* — a reading proposed since the eighteenth century and still defended, though
   it requires an interrogative particle the text does not have.

See [[The Names of God]] and [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].

## Significance

- **It marks the promise, not the rescue.** *ʾĒl Šadday* belongs to the part of the story where a
  family is being given children and a land it does not own; *Yahweh* belongs to the part where a
  nation is brought out of Egypt. Exodus 6:3 is a statement about which half of the story a reader is
  in.
- **It is a name Israel did not invent and did not keep to itself.** Balaam uses it, Job uses it,
  Naomi uses it in bitterness (*"the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me"*), and Genesis puts it
  in the mouth of God himself before Israel exists.
- **And the English word is a translation of a guess.** *Almighty* has shaped how the name is heard in
  every English-speaking pulpit for four hundred years, and it rests on the least defensible of the
  four etymologies. Where it matters, this wiki gives the Hebrew.
