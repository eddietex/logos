# A Garment and a Goat - Jacob Deceives and Is Deceived

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 27:9, 15–16** — *"Go now to the flock and get me two good young goats from there"* …
  *"Rebekah took the good clothes of Esau, her elder son, which were with her in the house, and put
  them on Jacob, her younger son. She put the skins of the young goats on his hands, and on the
  smooth of his neck."*
- **Genesis 37:31–33** — *"They took Joseph's coat, and killed a male goat, and dipped the coat in
  the blood. They took the coat of many colors, and they brought it to their father, and said, 'We
  have found this. Examine it, now, whether it is your son's coat or not.' He recognized it, and
  said, 'It is my son's coat. An evil animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn in
  pieces.'"*
- **Genesis 38:17, 20** — Judah promises *"a young goat from the flock"* to a veiled woman at the
  roadside and cannot find her to deliver it.
- **Genesis 3:21** — *"Yahweh God made coats of animal skins for Adam and for his wife, and
  clothed them."* The first garments in Scripture, and the first animal skins.
- **Genesis 39:12** — *"She caught him by his garment … and he ran outside"*, and the garment is
  produced as evidence of a thing that did not happen.
- **Genesis 44:1–13** — the silver cup in Benjamin's sack: a planted object, one generation on.

## Analysis

**Two goats and a garment take a blessing from a father who cannot see. One goat and a garment take
a son from a father who can.** Genesis runs the same two props twice, ten chapters apart, on the same
man from both sides, and never once says so.

| | **Genesis 27** | **Genesis 37** |
|---|---|---|
| **the deceived** | Isaac, blind | Jacob, sighted |
| **who acts** | Rebekah and Jacob | Jacob's sons |
| **the garment** | Esau's *"good clothes"*, put on Jacob | Joseph's coat, taken off Joseph |
| **the goat** | two kids: skins for the hands, meat for the pot | one male goat: blood for the coat |
| **the question** | *"Are you really my son Esau?"* (27:24) | *"whether it is your son's coat or not"* (37:32) |
| **the verdict** | *"So he blessed him"* (27:23) | *"He recognized it"* (37:33) |
| **what is lost** | the elder son's blessing | the favoured son, for twenty-two years |

**The symmetry that matters is in the question.** Both scenes turn on a father being asked to
identify a son by an object, and both fathers get it wrong — **the blind one by overruling the sense
that told him the truth, the sighted one by being handed evidence and reasoning from it correctly to
a false conclusion.** *"It is my son's coat"* is true. Everything Jacob infers from it is false.

**And the method is the family's, not one generation's.** Genesis's characteristic deception is not
the false statement but the arranged object: a garment on the wrong body, blood on the right one, a
veil at a roadside, a cup in a sack. **The chain runs Rebekah → Jacob → Jacob's sons → Joseph**, and
the only person in it who is never shown deceiving anybody with a prop is Isaac, who is the one it is
first used on. See [[Deception]].

### What the parallel is and is not claiming

- **Genesis never states the correspondence.** No narrator's aside, no character's remark, no
  vocabulary link beyond the two nouns — ***gədî ʿizzîm*** (*kid of the goats*) at 27:9 and
  ***śəʿîr ʿizzîm*** (*male of the goats*) at 37:31 are different words for the animal, and the
  garments are *bəgādîm* in one scene and *kətōnet* in the other. **The parallel is in the props and
  the situation, not in the wording**, and the wiki says so rather than manufacturing a lexical link.
- **It is not a stated punishment.** Genesis 37 gives no indication that the brothers know what their
  father did at Beersheba, and the book does not present the loss of Joseph as retribution for the
  loss of the blessing. **What can be said is that Genesis chose these two props twice and put them
  on the same man from opposite ends.** Whether that is a moral or a motif is left open; the wiki
  records the fact and neither draws nor forbids the inference. See [[Retribution]].
- **The goat is also the family's ordinary currency**, which is part of why the motif works. It is
  the animal a household kills for a guest, the animal Judah owes a prostitute, and the animal the
  law will later fix as the sin offering (Leviticus 4:23–28) and as the scapegoat (Leviticus 16). **A
  kid is not a symbol in Genesis; it is what is to hand**, and the deceptions are built out of
  domestic materials.

### Where the clothes come from

**The first garments in the Bible are God's** — *"Yahweh God made coats of animal skins for Adam
and for his wife"* (3:21) — made of skins, to cover a shame the couple had already tried to cover
themselves. **After that, almost every significant garment in Genesis produces a false belief in
someone**: Esau's clothes on Jacob (27:15), Joseph's coat in his brothers' hands (37:31), Tamar's
veil in place of her widow's clothes (38:14), Joseph's cloak in Potiphar's wife's grip (39:12). And
the sequence ends with clothing used to reveal rather than conceal: Joseph gives his brothers
changes of clothing (45:22) in the chapter where he stops hiding. See [[Nakedness and Covering]].

## Significance

- **It is Genesis's clearest demonstration that its deceptions are inherited technique.** Nobody in
  the book is taught to lie; they are shown handling the same objects. The chapter that gives Jacob
  the blessing and the chapter that takes his son away are furnished from the same flock and the same
  clothes chest.
- **It puts Jacob on both sides of one act.** He is the beneficiary at 27:23 and the victim at 37:33,
  and Genesis gives him no line in either scene that connects them. **The reader is the only party
  who has read both**, which is the register the whole Jacob cycle is written in. See
  [[The Stolen Blessing]] and [[Jacob]].
- **It is the second of three times the family's method comes back on its user.** The other two are
  Laban substituting the elder daughter for the younger (29:23–26), and Judah being told *"She is
  more righteous than I"* by a woman who used a veil and a pledge (38:26). See
  [[The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban]].
