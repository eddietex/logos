# One Flesh — Genesis 2 and the New Testament on Marriage

## Passages involved

- [[The Garden of Eden]] — Genesis 2:24
- Matthew 19:4–6 · Mark 10:6–8 *(not yet ingested)*
- 1 Corinthians 6:16 *(not yet ingested)*
- Ephesians 5:31–32 *(not yet ingested)*

## Analysis

One verse of Genesis is quoted directly four times in the New Testament, by both Jesus and Paul,
in three completely different arguments:

> **Genesis 2:24** Therefore a man will leave his father and his mother, and will join with his
> wife, and they will be one flesh.

**Jesus, on divorce (Matthew 19:4–6, Mark 10:6–8).** Asked whether divorce is lawful "for any
cause", Jesus does not answer from Deuteronomy 24, the text the question was built on. He goes
behind the law to the creation accounts and quotes two verses together — Genesis 1:27 (*"made
them male and female"*) and 2:24 — then draws a conclusion neither verse states: *"So then they
are no more two, but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear
apart."* Two moves are worth marking. First, he treats Genesis 2:24 as prescriptive, not merely
descriptive of a custom. Second, he ranks it above the Mosaic permission, which he explains as a
concession *"because of the hardness of your hearts"* — beginning is more authoritative than
legislation.

**Paul, on prostitution (1 Corinthians 6:16).** *"Don't you know that he who is joined to a
prostitute is one body? For, 'The two', he says, 'will become one flesh.'"* Paul applies the
verse where no marriage exists at all, which requires reading "one flesh" as something that
*happens* to bodies rather than something a covenant declares. It is the strongest New Testament
statement that the union of 2:24 is ontological, not ceremonial.

**Paul, on Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32).** After instructing husbands and wives, he
quotes 2:24 in full and adds: *"This mystery is great, but I speak concerning Christ and of the
assembly."* Genesis's marriage becomes a figure of something it never mentions — the union of
Christ with his people — with the argument running in both directions at once: marriage is
explained by Christ, and Christ is explained by marriage.

**What the Genesis verse itself does.** In its own context, 2:24 is the narrator's aside to the
reader, stepping outside a scene in which nobody has a father or mother to leave. It explains an
observed reality (why men leave their households for wives) by grounding it in an origin (she was
taken from his body, so joining is a return). The New Testament takes an etiology and reads it as
a norm — a legitimate move on the text's own terms, since the narrator has already generalized
from the scene to "a man" and "his wife" in general.

## Significance

Genesis 2:24 is the single most load-bearing verse in the Bible's theology of marriage, and its
weight comes almost entirely from how the New Testament uses it. Every major Christian argument
about marriage — its permanence, its exclusivity, its bodily reality, its symbolic freight — is
built on this verse rather than on legislation.

Note also the pattern of Jesus's reasoning: when law and creation conflict, he argues from the
beginning. The same move appears elsewhere in the Gospels — the sabbath *"made for man"* (Mark
2:27) reaches back past the fourth commandment to the seventh day of
[[The Creation of the World]], Genesis 2:2–3 — and it is worth watching for as the Gospels are
ingested. Compare
[[Creation as the Ground of the Sabbath Command]], where the Torah itself argues the same way.
