# Am I a Sea or a Sea Monster - The Guard on the Waters

## Passages involved

- **Job 7:12** — *"Am I a sea, or a sea monster, that you put a guard over me?"* — *hăyām-ʾānî
  ʾim-tannîn kî-tāśîm ʿālay mišmār* — [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]
- **Job 3:8** — *"those who are ready to rouse up leviathan"* — [[Let the Day Perish]]
- **Genesis 1:2, 9–10, 21** — the deep, the gathered waters, and *"God created the large sea
  creatures (hattannînim)"* — [[The Creation of the World]]
- **Job 9:8, 13; 26:12–13** — God who *"treads on the waves of the sea"*, the *"helpers of Rahab"*,
  and the sea stirred up and Rahab struck — [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] and
  [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]]
- **Job 38:8–11** — *"Who shut up the sea with doors, when it broke out of the womb … and said,
  'Here you may come, but no further. Here your proud waves shall be stayed'?"*
  — [[Out of the Whirlwind]]
- **Job 40:15–41:34** — Behemoth and Leviathan
- **Psalm 74:13–14; 89:9–10** — *"You broke the heads of the sea monsters (tannînim) in the
  waters. You broke the heads of Leviathan in pieces"*; *"You rule the pride of the sea… You have
  broken Rahab in pieces"*
- **Psalm 104:6–9, 26** — the waters given a boundary they may not pass; and *"Leviathan, whom you
  formed to play there"*
- **Jeremiah 5:22** — *"I have placed the sand for the bound of the sea, by a perpetual decree, that
  it can't pass it"*
- **Isaiah 27:1; 51:9–10** — Leviathan the fleeing serpent punished; *"Isn't it you who cut Rahab
  in pieces, who pierced the monster (tannîn)?"*
- **Revelation 21:1** — *"and the sea is no more"*

## Analysis

**Job 7:12 assumes a picture of the world that Genesis 1 deliberately mutes and the rest of the
Hebrew Bible speaks openly.** In that picture the sea — *yām*, which is also the name of the
Canaanite sea-god Yamm at Ugarit — and the sea monster — *tannîn*, the dragon — are powers of chaos
that God subdued at creation and keeps under guard. The poetry of the Psalms and Isaiah tells it as
a combat: heads of Leviathan broken, Rahab cut in pieces, the dragon pierced (Psalm 74:13–14;
89:10; Isaiah 51:9). Genesis 1 tells the same ordering without a fight — the *tannînim* are simply
creatures God made on the fifth day (1:21) — and the difference is usually read as a quiet polemic:
the monsters of other peoples' cosmologies are, in Israel's, pieces of God's handiwork. See
[[Let That Day Be Darkness - Job 3 as Counter-Creation]].

Job reaches for the combat version. **His question is whether God regards him as one of the
defeated powers** — a chaos-monster needing a *mišmār*, a guard post, a watch set over it. The word
is military and penal: a guard, a custody (Genesis 40:3–4, where Joseph and Pharaoh's officials are
*bəmišmār*, *in custody*). Job feels kept under the kind of watch that is set on a threat.

**The book answers this question, at length, and the answer is double.** First, yes: God does guard
the sea. The whirlwind speech's first image after the earth's foundations is precisely the sea's
boundary — *"Who shut up the sea with doors… and said, 'Here you may come, but no further'"* (38:8–
11). Job was right that the sea is under guard. **But the image is not of a jailer and a prisoner.
It is of a birth.** The sea *"broke out of the womb"*, and God wrapped it in cloud *"as its
garment"* and darkness as its swaddling band (38:8–9). The guard on the sea is a parent's boundary
around a newborn. Job had taken the guard for hostility; God describes it as care that also limits.

Second, God takes up the sea monster himself — and spends a whole chapter on it (41). Leviathan,
whom Job's chapter 3 wanted roused (3:8) and whom Job feared he was being treated as (7:12), is
described at the end of the book not as an enemy God is still fighting but as a creature God made
and delights in: *"I will not keep silence concerning his limbs, nor his mighty strength, nor his
goodly frame"* (41:12). Psalm 104:26 had already said it: *"Leviathan, whom you formed to play
there."* **Job asked whether he was a monster under guard. God's answer is to show him a monster
that is not under guard at all, and is not a threat to God, and is admired.**
**Job's last hymn (26:12–13) uses the imagery without complaint.** *"He stirs up the sea with his
power, and by his understanding he strikes through Rahab … His hand has pierced the swift serpent."*
The *swift* or *fleeing serpent* is *nāḥāš bārîaḥ*, the exact phrase of Isaiah 27:1's *"leviathan,
the fleeing serpent"*, so the creature of 3:8 is in view by another name. Where 7:12 put Job in the
monster's place, 26:12–13 puts the monster where the combat tradition keeps it, under God's hand —
and then calls all of it *"the outskirts of his ways"* (26:14). See [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]].

## Significance

- **It is one of Job's threads that the whirlwind speeches pick up explicitly**, together with
  light and darkness (chapter 3 against 38:12–15) and the hunting of the wicked (Job's *"you hunt me
  like a lion"*, 10:16, against God's *"Can you hunt the prey for the lioness?"*, 38:39). The
  speeches are often called non-answers; they are answers to the images rather than the arguments.
  See [[The Whirlwind Answers Chapter 3]].
- **It shows how the Hebrew Bible uses the chaos imagery it inherited.** Job 7:12 takes it at face
  value and applies it to himself; Job 26:12–13 uses it of God's power; Job 38–41 revises it, so that
  the sea is a baby and Leviathan a pet. The development inside one book is a compressed version of
  the Bible's whole handling of the motif.
- **The thread ends where the sea does.** *"The sea is no more"* (Revelation 21:1) is the final
  removal of the power the guard was set on, in a book that has also used *"the sea"* for the
  origin of the beast (13:1). No guard is needed in the new creation because nothing is left that
  needs one.
