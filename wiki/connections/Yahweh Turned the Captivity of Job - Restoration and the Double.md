# Yahweh Turned the Captivity of Job - Restoration and the Double

## Passages involved

- **Job 42:10** — *"Yahweh turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends. Yahweh gave Job twice
  as much as he had before."* — *šāb ʾet-šəbût ʾiyyôb … ləmišneh* — [[The Restoration of Job]]
- **Job 42:12** — the inventory of 1:3 doubled item by item — [[Job of Uz]]
- **Deuteronomy 30:3** *(not yet ingested)* — *"that then Yahweh your God will release you from captivity, have
  compassion on you, and will return and gather you from all the peoples where Yahweh your God has scattered
  you."*
- **Psalm 14:7** *(not yet ingested)* — *"When Yahweh restores the fortunes of his people, then Jacob shall
  rejoice, and Israel shall be glad."*
- **Psalm 126:1, 4** *(not yet ingested)* — *"When Yahweh brought back those who returned to Zion, we were like
  those who dream … Restore our fortunes again, Yahweh, like the streams in the Negev."*
- **Jeremiah 29:14** *(not yet ingested)* — *"I will be found by you," says Yahweh, "and I will turn again your
  captivity"*
- **Amos 9:14** *(not yet ingested)* — *"I will bring my people Israel back from captivity, and they will
  rebuild the ruined cities"*
- **Exodus 22:4, 7, 9** *(not yet ingested)* — the thief's restitution: *"he shall pay double"*; and *"the
  cause of both parties shall come before God. He whom God condemns shall pay double to his neighbor."*
- **Isaiah 40:1–2** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Comfort, comfort my people," says your God. "Speak
  comfortably to Jerusalem … that she has received of Yahweh's hand double for all her sins."*
- **Isaiah 61:7** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Instead of your shame you will have double."*
- **Zechariah 9:12** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Turn to the stronghold, you prisoners of hope! Even today I
  declare that I will restore double to you."*

## Analysis

### A national phrase for one man

*Šûb šəbût* — the WEB's *turn the captivity*, more often rendered *restore the fortunes* — is a fixed
expression, and its home is the prophets' and psalmists' language about Israel: the people scattered and
brought back, Zion restored, the exile ended. Deuteronomy 30:3, Jeremiah 29:14, and Amos 9:14 use it of a
return from captivity; Psalm 14:7 and Psalm 126:4 pray for it; the WEB's own renderings move between
*captivity* and *fortunes* from verse to verse. Whether the noun *šəbût* comes from *šābâ*, *to take
captive*, or from *šûb*, *to turn* — so that the phrase means *to turn the turning*, *to reverse a fate* — is
disputed, and the double sense is part of why translators split.

**Job 42:10 is one of the few places it is said of an individual**, and that makes the choice of phrase
the point. Job was never taken captive. But by restoring him in the words used for Israel's return, the
narrator makes his ash-heap an exile and his restoration a homecoming — **a non-Israelite from Uz, restored in
the vocabulary of Zion.** Readers who see Job as a figure for Israel in exile have leaned heavily on this
verse; the wiki records the reading and notes that the book itself gives Job no Israelite identity at all
(see [[Job (person)]]). The phrase can carry the resonance without making Job an allegory.

### When he prayed for his friends

The phrase has a time clause attached, and in the Psalms and prophets it never has this one. **The restoration
comes *when he prayed for his friends* — not when he confessed (42:1–6), and not when the friends sacrificed
(42:9), but at Job's intercession for the men who had wronged him.** In the national texts restoration
follows Israel's return to God (Deuteronomy 30:2–3). In Job it follows Job's turn toward others. See
[[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]] and [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

### Twice as much

*Ləmišneh*, the double, has two backgrounds, and they point in different directions.

**The law of restitution.** In Exodus 22 *double* is what a thief repays (22:4, 7). And 22:9 sets the scene
in a court that sounds like the one Job demanded: *"the cause of both parties shall come before God. He whom
God condemns shall pay double to his neighbor."* On this reading the epilogue settles Job's lawsuit by the
book: Job, the wronged party, is paid double. **The wiki records the reading and does not adopt it.** The
difficulty is the payer. Exodus pays the double out of the condemned party's goods, and the only party God
condemns in the epilogue is the friends — who pay a sacrifice, not Job's sheep. Nothing in 42:10 says God is
making restitution, and the book has spent four chapters establishing that God is not in Job's debt
(*"Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?"*, 41:11; see
[[Who Has First Given to Me - Job 41.11 in Romans 11.35]]).

**The double of comfort.** Isaiah 40:2 announces that Jerusalem *"has received of Yahweh's hand double for all
her sins"*; Isaiah 61:7 promises *"Instead of your shame you will have double"*; Zechariah 9:12 tells the
*"prisoners of hope"* that God *"will restore double"*. In these the double is not a debt repaid but a
superabundance after exile — the same setting as *turned the captivity*. **This is the nearer parallel**: the
two halves of Job 42:10, the national phrase and the double, sit together in the prophets' picture of
restoration after exile, and the chapter Isaiah 40:2 belongs to opens *"Comfort, comfort my people"*
(40:1) — *naḥămû*, the verb of Job 42:6 and 42:11.

### What is not doubled

The livestock is doubled exactly (42:12). The children are not (42:13), and the *"very great household"* of
1:3 is not mentioned. Whatever the double is, it is not an accounting in which every loss is made good; see
[[Job's Children]].

## Significance

- It places the end of Job inside the Bible's larger story of exile and return without making Job an
  Israelite: one man's restoration told in the words the nation will need.
- It ties the restoration to intercession rather than to confession, which is the epilogue's sharpest
  departure from the friends' scheme. See [[Retribution]].
- The two readings of the *double* — restitution and comfort — are a choice between reading the epilogue as
  the settlement of a lawsuit and reading it as grace after exile. The book's own vocabulary (*nḥm* at 42:6 and
  42:11; *šûb šəbût*) leans toward the second.
