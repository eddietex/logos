# Seventy-Sevenfold - Lamech's Boast and Jesus on Forgiveness

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 4:15** — *"Yahweh said to him, 'Therefore whoever slays Cain, vengeance will be taken on
  him sevenfold.'"* — [[Cain and Abel]]
- **Genesis 4:23–24** — Lamech's Song of the Sword: *"If Cain will be avenged seven times, truly
  Lamech seventy-seven times."* — [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]]
- **Matthew 18:21–22** — *"Then Peter came and said to him, 'Lord, how often shall my brother sin
  against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?' Jesus said to him, 'I don't tell you until
  seven times, but, until seventy times seven.'"*

## Analysis

The link is not a thematic resemblance. It is a phrase.

The Hebrew of Genesis 4:24 is *šibʿîm wešibʿâ*, "seventy and seven". The Septuagint renders it
*hebdomēkontakis hepta*. Matthew 18:22 has Jesus answer Peter with *heōs hebdomēkontakis hepta* —
the same three words. In the whole Greek Bible that expression occurs in exactly these two places.
Whatever else is going on, Jesus is quoting the worst man in Genesis.

That makes the trajectory legible in a way it otherwise would not be:

| | Genesis 4:15 | Genesis 4:24 | Matthew 18:22 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who sets the number | God | Lamech | Jesus |
| What it measures | vengeance | vengeance | forgiveness |
| Who it protects | the guilty man | the man declaring it | the offender |
| Occasion | a murder already committed | a wound | a sin against you, repeated |
| Function | to prevent a killing | to threaten one | to end the accounting |

Peter's *"until seven times?"* is already generous by the standards of the rabbinic discussion he
would have known, which commonly settled on three. He offers seven and expects to be praised for
it. The answer he gets replaces his number with the one number in Scripture that is larger, and it
is the number of a man who killed someone for hitting him.

Two things follow.

**The escalation is reversed on its own terms.** Genesis 4 runs a curve: God gives Cain sevenfold
protection; Lamech converts it into seventy-sevenfold threat. The direction is set by the second
speaker, who takes what was granted and makes it a weapon. Matthew 18 runs the curve backwards
along the same numbers — from the seven a disciple proposes to the seventy-seven a murderer
boasted — and the direction is set by the second speaker again, who takes what was a weapon and
makes it a mercy. The two passages are the same arithmetic performed in opposite directions.

**"Seventy-seven" or "seventy times seven" is the smaller question.** The Greek is genuinely
ambiguous, and translations split: KJV, WEB, and NKJV read "seventy times seven" (490); NRSV,
NIV, ESV, and most modern versions read "seventy-seven". The Hebrew of Genesis 4:24 is
unambiguously seventy-seven, which is the main argument for reading Matthew that way, since Matthew
is quoting it. But the point does not turn on the total. Both readings mean *stop counting*, and the
parable Jesus tells immediately afterwards (the unforgiving servant, Matthew 18:23–35) makes the
same point with a debt so large that it is not a quantity either — ten thousand talents against a
hundred denarii, a ratio of about six hundred thousand to one. The lesson of the passage is that
the ledger is the wrong instrument.

**A caution on how far to press it.** Matthew does not cite Genesis, name Lamech, or signal a
quotation. The verbal identity is striking and the reversal it produces is coherent, but a reader
should be clear that the connection rests on the Septuagint phrase and on Jesus and his audience
knowing Genesis 4 well — which is a reasonable assumption and not a stated one. Commentators who
resist the allusion generally argue that "seventy-seven" is simply an idiom for an uncountable
number, which would make the overlap coincidental. The strongest reply is that the idiom's one
attestation *is* Genesis 4:24.

## Significance

Genesis 4 is where the Bible shows human retribution getting away from anyone's control, and it
does it with a number. The Torah's later legislation is largely an effort to cap that number — *eye
for eye* is a ceiling, not a licence, and the cities of refuge exist to put a court between the
avenger and the killer. Matthew 18 does not cap it. It takes the figure Lamech used to make himself
untouchable and assigns it to the one thing that cannot be over-supplied.
