# The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 27:19** — *"Jacob said to his father, 'I am Esau your firstborn.'"*
- **Genesis 27:35** — *"He said, 'Your brother came with deceit, and has taken away your
  blessing.'"*
- **Genesis 29:23–26** — [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]] — *"In the evening, he took Leah his daughter, and brought her to him. He went
  in to her. … In the morning, behold, it was Leah! He said to Laban, 'What is this you have done to
  me? Didn't I serve with you for Rachel? Why then have you deceived me?' Laban said, 'It is not done
  so in our place, to give the younger before the firstborn.'"*
- **Genesis 25:20; 27:43** — Laban planted in a pedigree that did not need him, and named as the
  destination of the flight.
- **Genesis 31:7, 41** — *"Your father has deceived me, and changed my wages ten times."*
- **Genesis 12:18; 20:9; 26:10** — *"What is this you have done to me?"*, the question three foreign
  kings put to this family before Jacob puts it to his uncle.

## Analysis

**Jacob passes himself off as the firstborn and is repaid with a firstborn passed off as the
younger.** The two scenes are two chapters apart in the reading and twenty years apart in the story,
and Genesis marks the connection with a single sentence of dialogue that no one in the book comments
on.

***"It is not done so in our place, to give the younger before the firstborn."*** ***Lōʾ-yēʿāśeh kēn
bimqômēnû lātēt haṣṣəʿîrâ lipnê habbəkîrâ*** (29:26) — and the two nouns are the exact feminine
counterparts of the pair the whole Jacob cycle runs on. ***Bəkîrâ*** is from ***bəkōr***, the
firstborn, the root of the ***bəkōrâ*** Jacob bought at 25:33. ***Ṣəʿîrâ*** is the feminine of
***ṣāʿîr***, the younger — **the very word of the oracle: *rab yaʿăbōd ṣāʿîr*, "the elder will serve
the younger"** (25:23).

**Laban is stating, as a local custom and as a rebuke, the exact rule Jacob spent chapter 27
breaking.** He does not know he is doing it. He is explaining a wedding-night substitution to a
son-in-law and he happens to use the two words that name that son-in-law's entire life.

**And the vocabulary is his, not the chapter's** — which is the sharpest form of the point and is
easy to miss in translation. **The narrator's own words for the sisters at 29:16 are *haggədōlâ* and
*haqqəṭannâ*, the big one and the little one**, and Jacob uses the narrator's word when he names his
price: *"Rachel, your **younger** daughter"*, ***bitəkā haqqəṭannâ*** (29:18). **Nobody in the chapter
says *firstborn* until Laban says it in the refusal.** Jacob asks in the neutral register and is
answered in the register of the oracle, by the one man in the scene with no reason to know it exists.

### The correspondence, line by line

| | **Genesis 27** | **Genesis 29** |
|---|---|---|
| **the deceived** | Isaac, who cannot see | Jacob, in the dark |
| **the substitution** | younger passed off as elder | elder passed off as younger |
| **the disguise** | a brother's clothes and goat skins | a veil, and a wedding night |
| **the discovery** | *"Isaac trembled violently"* (27:33) | *"In the morning, behold, it was Leah!"* (29:25) |
| **the charge** | *"Your brother came with deceit"* (27:35) | *"Why then have you deceived me?"* (29:25) |
| **the ruling** | *"Yes, he will be blessed"* — it stands | seven more years — it stands |

**Both scenes end with the deceived party accepting the outcome**, and in both the acceptance is the
scene's real content. Isaac cannot recall a blessing; Jacob cannot unmarry a wife. **Genesis is
consistent about this: what has been done in these transactions is not undone**, whatever the fraud
that produced it. See [[Yes, He Will Be Blessed - The Irrevocable Word]].

### *"Why then have you deceived me?"*

***Wəlāmmâ rimmîtānî*** — from ***rāmâ***, and it is **the first time in Genesis that a member of
this family asks the question rather than answering it.**

The question itself is old. Pharaoh asked Abram *"What is this that you have done to me?"* (12:18);
Abimelech asked Abraham *"What have you done to us?"* (20:9) and asked Isaac *"What is this you have
done to us?"* (26:10). **Three foreign rulers, three patriarchs, and the moral vocabulary belongs to
the outsider every time.** At 29:25 Jacob uses the identical formula — *"What is this you have done
to me?"* — **and he is the injured party.** The man who told the only flat lie in the family's
repertoire two chapters earlier is now the one demanding an explanation, in the exact words an
Egyptian king used on his grandfather. See [[Deception]].

**And Laban's answer is not an apology.** It is a statement of custom, delivered without embarrassment
and with no acknowledgment that anything improper occurred — which is precisely the posture Jacob
took at 27:19–24. Neither man in the exchange has any standing.

### What the parallel does not settle

- **Genesis never calls it a punishment.** There is no narrator's aside, no divine speech, and no
  remark from Jacob linking his wedding night to his father's bedside. **The only link the text
  supplies is Laban's vocabulary**, and Laban is not being ironic — he is explaining a rule. The
  reading that this is measure-for-measure justice is an inference the reader makes and the book
  permits; **the wiki records it as an inference.** See [[Retribution]].
- **The instrument is Rebekah's brother.** *"Flee to Laban, my brother"* (27:43) is the last practical
  instruction she gives, and it sends her son to the man who will run her own technique back on him.
  Genesis planted Laban at 25:20 in a pedigree that had no need of him, three chapters before he was
  wanted. **The book has been holding him in reserve since before the twins were born.** See
  [[Laban]].
- **It does not make [[Leah]] an accomplice or a victim, because Genesis does not say.** She is given
  no word in the chapter, no consent scene, and no reaction. The book that recorded Rebekah's *"I will
  go"* records nothing at all here, and the wiki notes the silence rather than filling it.
- **The mechanism is not explained either.** There is no veil in the text — readers import it from
  24:65 — and no drug, no enjoined silence, and no described darkness. **What Genesis gives is a
  *mišteh*, a drinking feast, then evening, then morning**, and it leaves the rest to the reader,
  which is the same restraint it showed at 19:33–35. See [[Deception]].

## Significance

- **It is the cycle's own commentary on chapter 27, delivered as a stranger's house rule.** Genesis
  declines to editorialise about Jacob anywhere in the Jacob material; what it does instead is
  arrange for the vocabulary of his offence to be spoken back to him by a man with no idea what he is
  saying. **The verdict is in the plot, not in the narration.** See [[The Stolen Blessing]].
- **It begins the twenty years the blessing costs.** Jacob leaves Beersheba with a stolen blessing
  and nothing else, and the first thing that happens to him at the far end of the road is that the
  same trick is played on him for seven additional years of labour. **The blessing holds; the price
  is itemized.**
- **It is the pivot of the whole *elder and younger* theme.** Genesis inverts the birth order six
  times and never gives a reason grounded in merit; here it briefly lets a Mesopotamian household
  state the ordinary rule out loud — *the younger is not given before the firstborn* — and the man
  who hears it is the standing exception to it. See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] and
  [[The Elder Will Serve the Younger - Genesis 25.23 in Malachi and Romans]].
