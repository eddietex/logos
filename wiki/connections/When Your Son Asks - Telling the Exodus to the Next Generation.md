## Analysis

**Five times the Torah and Joshua build a child's question into a rite, and four of them use one Hebrew
clause.** *"When your son asks you in time to come, saying"* — ***kî-yišʾālkā binkā māḥār lēʾmōr*** — is
identical at Exodus 13:14 and Deuteronomy 6:20, and the plural form *kî-yišʾālûn bənêkem māḥār lēʾmōr* opens
Joshua 4:6 and (with *ʾet-ʾăbôtām*, *their fathers*) 4:21 (WLC). Exodus 12:26 has the same shape with a
different verb (*kî-yōʾmərû ʾălêkem bənêkem*, *when your children say to you*). ***Māḥār***, *tomorrow*, is
what WEB renders *"in time to come"* and *"in the future"*. **The question always belongs to tomorrow:
it is written for a generation that was not there.**

| | the son sees | the son asks | the father answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exodus 12:26–27 | the Passover sacrifice | *"What do you mean by this service?"* | *"Yahweh … spared our houses"* |
| Exodus 13:8 | the seven days unleavened | — (told unasked) | *"what Yahweh did for **me** when **I** came out"* |
| Exodus 13:14–15 | a firstling given, a son redeemed | *"What is this?"* | *"Yahweh killed all the firstborn … therefore I redeem"* |
| Deuteronomy 6:20–21 | the whole law | *"What do the testimonies … mean?"* | *"**We** were Pharaoh's slaves"* |
| Joshua 4:6–7, 21–22 | twelve stones from the Jordan | *"What do you mean by these stones?"* | *"the waters of the Jordan were cut off"* |

**Three things hold constant.**

1. **The answer is always a story, never a rule.** The son of Deuteronomy 6:20 asks about statutes and
   ordinances and is told *"We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt"*. The law is explained by what God did.
2. **The answering voice is first person.** *"For me when I came out"* (Exodus 13:8, *lî … bəṣēʾtî*),
   *"brought us out"* (13:14, 16), *"We were Pharaoh's slaves"* (Deuteronomy 6:21). **The speaker is any
   father in any later year, and he says *I* and *we*.** The exodus is told as the teller's own.
3. **The rite exists to provoke the question.** Exodus 10:2 gave the plagues a second audience — *"your
   son, and … your son's son"*; from 12:26 on, the practices are designed so that a child will ask.

**The companion formula — the sign on the hand and between the eyes — goes with the telling.** At Exodus
13:9 the sign is set *"that Yahweh's law may be in your mouth"*; at Deuteronomy 6:7–8 it follows *"you
shall teach them diligently to your children"* (WEB, 6:7; *not yet ingested*). Whether the sign is an object
or a figure, its stated purpose both times is speech. The literal reading became the later practice of
*tefillin* (reported, not checked in this pass); the passage page on
[[The Consecration of the Firstborn]] records both readings and adopts neither.

## Significance

- **It is the Torah's theory of how a story outlives its witnesses.** The generation that left Egypt dies
  in the wilderness; the commands for telling it are given within days of leaving, before Sinai.
- **It explains why Exodus keeps stopping its narrative for law** (12:1–28, 43–49; 13:1–16): the night is
  never told without instructions for telling it again. See [[Remembrance]].
- **It anticipates Joshua**, where the formula moves from Egypt to the Jordan and from a rite to a
  monument. When Joshua is ingested, this page is where that is traced.
