# A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue

## Passages involved

- **Job 5:19–26** — Eliphaz's promise to Job, on condition of accepting God's correction:
  deliverance *"in six troubles; yes, in seven"*, peace in his tent, a fold with nothing missing,
  offspring *"as the grass of the earth"*, and *"You shall come to your grave in a full age, like a
  shock of grain comes in its season."* — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]
- **Job 5:1** — *"Call now; is there any who will answer you? To which of the holy ones will you
  turn?"*
- **Job 5:8** — *"But as for me, I would seek God. I would commit my cause to God"*
- **Job 42:7–9** — God to Eliphaz: *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my
  servant Job has … my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him"*
- **Job 42:10–17** — Job's restoration: *"Yahweh gave Job twice as much as he had before"*, fourteen
  thousand sheep, seven sons and three daughters, a hundred and forty years, four generations, and
  *"So Job died, being old and full of days."*
- **Job 8:5–7; 11:13–19** — Bildad's and Zophar's shorter versions of the same conditional promise —
  [[Bildad's First Speech]], [[Zophar's First Speech]]
- **Job 13:7–10** — Job warning the friends: *"Will you show partiality (tiśśāʾûn pānāyw) to him?…
  He will surely reprove you"* — the idiom God uses of Job at 42:8, *pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, *I will lift his
  face*. The friends lifted God's face, and God lifts Job's — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Job 21:32** — the wicked man's tomb: *"Men shall keep watch over the tomb (gādîš)"* — Eliphaz's
  *shock of grain* of 5:26, the same noun, now the grave-mound of a man who told God to depart —
  [[Why Do the Wicked Live]]
- **Job 22:21–30** — Eliphaz's last version of it: *"You shall make your prayer to him, and he will
  hear you … He will even deliver him who is not innocent. Yes, he shall be delivered through the
  cleanness of your hands"* (22:27, 30) — [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]
- **Job 24:24** — Job of the wicked: *"they are taken out of the way as all others, and are cut off as
  the tops of the ears of grain"* — [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]]

## Analysis

**Eliphaz's promise comes true, item by item.** What he offered Job in 5:19–26, the epilogue
delivers in 42:10–17.

| Job 5 | Job 42 |
|---|---|
| *"You shall visit your fold, and shall miss nothing"* (5:24) | *"fourteen thousand sheep, six thousand camels, one thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand female donkeys"* (42:12) — twice the inventory of 1:3 |
| *"your offspring shall be great, your offspring as the grass of the earth"* (5:25) | *"seven sons and three daughters"* (42:13); *"saw his sons, and his sons' sons, to four generations"* (42:16) |
| *"You shall know that your tent is in peace"* (5:24) | his brothers, sisters, and acquaintances *"ate bread with him in his house"* (42:11) |
| *"You shall come to your grave in a full age, like a shock of grain comes in its season"* (5:26) | *"So Job died, being old and full of days"* (42:17) |

**The outcome is right; the route is wrong.** Eliphaz made the promise conditional on Job accepting
that he was being corrected (5:17) and committing his cause to God in the posture of a penitent.
Job did commit his cause to God (5:8) — more literally and more aggressively than Eliphaz meant, as a
lawsuit, demanding a hearing and an umpire. He never accepted the correction, because there was
nothing to correct. And the restoration came anyway, after God had said Job *"spoke rightly"* and
Eliphaz did not. **The friends' theology predicted the ending and misidentified its cause.**

**Bildad's short version is the most exactly fulfilled of all.** *"Though your beginning
(rēʾšîtkā) was small, yet your latter end (ʾaḥărîtkā) would greatly increase"* (8:7). The epilogue:
*"So Yahweh blessed the latter end (ʾaḥărît) of Job more than his beginning (rēʾšît)"* (42:12). The
same two nouns, the same contrast, the same direction — Eliphaz's promise is fulfilled item by item,
but Bildad's is fulfilled in its own words. And its condition is the same as Eliphaz's and fails the
same way: *"If you want to seek God diligently… If you were pure and upright"* (8:5–6). Job was
already *yāšār*, upright (1:1), and he did seek God, as a litigant rather than a penitent. Bildad's
closing line is fulfilled too, with an irony he could not see: *"Those who hate you shall be clothed
with shame"* (8:22) — and at 42:7–9 the men who must be covered by Job's prayer are the three
friends.

**Zophar's version is fulfilled at Zophar's expense.** His promise is the most generous of the three
and follows the harshest accusation — *"God exacts of you less than your iniquity deserves"* (11:6)
— and two of its clauses come true in the epilogue with Zophar standing in them. *"Surely then you
shall lift up your face (tiśśāʾ pānêkā) without spot"* (11:15): at 42:8 God says of Job *"I will
accept him"*, *pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, **I will lift his face**, and 42:9 reports that it happened — *"So
Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went… and Yahweh accepted
Job."* And *"many shall court your favor"* (*ḥillû pānêkā*, 11:19), the idiom of petitioners
approaching the great with a gift: the first to come to Job with offerings in hand are the three
friends, with seven bulls and seven rams, because his prayer is the only thing that will turn God's
wrath from them; then at 42:11 *"all his brothers, and all his sisters"*, each with a piece of money
and a ring of gold. **Zophar promised Job a lifted face and a line of suppliants, and was in the
line when the face was lifted.** The 11:6 half of his speech, meanwhile, is reversed: not less than
his iniquity deserved, but *"twice as much as he had before"* (42:10).

**5:1 is reversed exactly.** *"To which of the holy ones will you turn?"* — Eliphaz's dismissal of
the idea that anyone could intercede for Job. In 42:8 God sends Eliphaz to find an intercessor for
himself, and the intercessor is Job: *"my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him."*
And 42:10 makes Job's restoration follow on that prayer: *"Yahweh turned the captivity of Job, when
he prayed for his friends."* The man Eliphaz said had no one to turn to is the one Eliphaz must turn
to, and the blessing Eliphaz promised Job arrives when Job prays for Eliphaz.

**The word for "full age" occurs once more.** *Kelaḥ* (5:26), a rare noun meaning something like
*ripeness* or *full vigour*, appears elsewhere in the Bible only at Job 30:2, where Job uses it with
contempt for the fathers of the men who now mock him: *"men in whom ripe age has perished"*
([[Now They Mock Me]]). The
epilogue does not reuse the word. It uses the patriarchs' formula instead — *zāqēn ûśəbaʿ yāmîm*,
*old and full of days*, said otherwise only of Isaac (Genesis 35:29), David (1 Chronicles 23:1;
29:28), and Jehoiada (2 Chronicles 24:15). Job's death is told in the vocabulary of the fathers of
Israel, not of Eliphaz's harvest. See
[[Buried in a Good Old Age - The Promise at Genesis 15.15 and Its Fulfilment]].

**Job had already taken the image away from Eliphaz.** The *shock of grain* of 5:26 is a *gādîš*,
and the word occurs in Job only once more: at 21:32, of the wicked man's burial — *"Yet he will be
borne to the grave. Men shall keep watch over the tomb (wəʿal-gādîš yišqôd)."* Eliphaz offered the
heaped sheaf as the emblem of the righteous man's harvest-time death; Job points out that the wicked
get the heap too, with a vigil kept over it and *"all men"* following the bier (21:33). The promise
of 5:26 was true, and it was not evidence of anything — which is also how the epilogue keeps it.

**The doubling is not complete, and Eliphaz's promise does not pretend it would be.** The livestock
of 42:12 is exactly twice that of 1:3. The children are not: ten in chapter 1, ten in chapter 42.
[[Job's Children]] keeps the three readings of that asymmetry side by side — persons are not stock,
the first ten are counted as living, the naming of the daughters is the compensation — and adopts
none of them. Eliphaz's 5:25 promised *offspring* as the grass, not the children back. Nothing in
his promise or in the epilogue makes the second family a replacement for the first, and Eliphaz,
who had used the first family's deaths as an exemplum at 5:4, is in no position to offer one.

**Eliphaz's last version of the promise describes the epilogue more exactly than his first, and
casts him in it.** 22:21–30 is the fullest form the friends' promise ever takes, and its last three
verses are the ones chapter 42 keeps most literally. *"You shall make your prayer to him, and he will
hear you"* (22:27): at 42:8–9 Job prays and *"Yahweh accepted Job"*. *"You shall lift up your face to
God"* (22:26) — Zophar's idiom from 11:15 — and God says of Job *pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, *I will lift his
face* (42:8). And *"He will even deliver him who is not innocent (ʾî-nāqî). Yes, he shall be
delivered through the cleanness of your hands"* (22:30): at 42:8 the man who is not innocent is
Eliphaz, God's wrath is turned from him *"that I not deal with you according to your folly"*, and the
hands whose cleanness delivers him are Job's. **5:1 had denied Job an intercessor; 22:30 promises
that Job will be one; 42:8 makes him Eliphaz's.** The promise was true at every stage. Its condition
— *"If you return to the Almighty … if you put away unrighteousness far from your tents"* (22:23),
meaning the crimes of 22:6–9 — is the only part of it that never happens, because there was nothing
to put away.

**Job takes the harvest image back, for the wicked.** In 24:24 the men who moved the landmarks and
starved the harvesters are *"cut off as the tops of the ears of grain"* — after a lifetime of the
security God gave them (24:23), and *"as all others"*. Eliphaz's shock of grain came in *"in its
season"* as the reward of the righteous; Job's ears of grain are cut at the same harvest as
everyone's. Death is not the verdict Eliphaz made it; it is the reaping that takes the righteous and
the wicked alike — the point Job made in 21:23–26 — and the epilogue's *"old and full of days"*
(42:17) is the only place the book gives Eliphaz's image to a man as a blessing.

## Significance

- It is the clearest case in Job of the friends being right about *what* and wrong about *why*. The
  book does not mock the hope of restoration; it gives it. What it denies is the friends'
  arithmetic, in which restoration is the payment for confession.
- It makes the epilogue harder to dismiss as a conventional happy ending tacked onto a subversive
  poem. The ending is the friends' ending, deliberately — and it arrives on Job's terms.
- 5:1 and 42:8 between them frame the whole dialogue as a story about mediation: it opens with the
  denial that anyone can stand between, and closes with the sufferer standing between God and the
  men who denied it. See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].
