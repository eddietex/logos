# A Bill of Divorce - Deuteronomy 24.1-4, Isaiah 50.1, and Jeremiah 3

## Passages involved

- [[Divorce, Remarriage, and the Newlywed]] — Deuteronomy 24:1–4; the bill of divorce, the second
  marriage, and the first husband who *"may not take her again to be his wife, after that she is
  defiled … You shall not cause the land to sin"*.
- Jeremiah 3:1 *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — *"They say, 'If a man puts away his

- Jeremiah 3:8 *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — *"I had put her away and given her a

- Malachi 2:16 *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — WEB *"For I hate divorce", says

## Analysis

**The phrase.** *Sēper kǝrîtut*, *a document of cutting off*, occurs in Deuteronomy 24:1, 3; Isaiah
50:1; and Jeremiah 3:8. Those are its only occurrences in the books checked (WLC lemma search,
Genesis–Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea, Malachi). That they are its only occurrences in the
Hebrew Bible is reported from general knowledge. Both prophets apply the law to Yahweh and his people.
Each uses the piel *šillaḥ*, *send away*, which is Deuteronomy's verb (WLC).

**Jeremiah takes the case of Deuteronomy 24:4 and answers it the other way.** The question of 3:1 is
the law's case, step by step: a man sends his wife away, she goes, she *"becomes another man's"*. Then
the law's question: *"should he return to her again?"* Deuteronomy says he may not, and says the land
would be made to sin. Jeremiah asks *"Wouldn't that land be greatly polluted?"* The verb is *ḥānap*,
not Deuteronomy's *ḥāṭāʾ*. In the Torah *ḥānap* occurs only at Numbers 35:33, where *"blood pollutes
the land"* (WLC lemma search). Jeremiah's case is worse than Deuteronomy's: Israel has not married one
other husband but *"played the prostitute with many lovers"*. Then Yahweh says, *"yet return again to
me"*. The return the law forbids a husband, Yahweh asks for.

Whether *"yet return again to me"* is an invitation or an ironic question is debated. The WEB reads it
as an invitation. Some translations read *and would you return to me?* That is reported from general
knowledge; the Hebrew *wǝšôb ʾēlay* (WLC) is an infinitive absolute and can be taken either way. Later
in the chapter the invitation is plain: *"Return, you backsliding children"* (3:14, 22, by reference;
not checked in this pass).

**Jeremiah 3:8 says the bill was given.** Yahweh *"put her away and given her a bill of divorce"*. The
wife is northern Israel, sent away for adultery; Judah, *"her sister"*, saw it and did the same. The
divorce here is for adultery, not for Deuteronomy's *"unseemly thing"*. Deuteronomy 22:22 punishes
adultery with death, not divorce.

**Isaiah 50:1 says there is no bill.** *"Where is the bill of your mother's divorce, with which I have
put her away?"* The question expects that none can be produced. The mother was *"put away"* (*šullǝḥâ*,
pual, WLC) for the children's transgressions, but no bill was written; and if no bill, the marriage
stands. The parallel question about creditors is the same: Yahweh has not sold them to pay a debt. They
were *"sold for your iniquities"*.

**Recorded, not resolved: Jeremiah 3:8 and Isaiah 50:1.** Jeremiah says Yahweh gave Israel a bill of
divorce. Isaiah asks where the bill is, implying there is none. Readings: the two address different
peoples, northern Israel in Jeremiah and Zion's children in Isaiah; Isaiah's question is rhetorical
and means the separation is not final, whatever was written; or the two prophets simply use the
figure differently. The wiki adopts none.

**Malachi 2:16.** The WEB reads *"For I hate divorce", says Yahweh*. The Hebrew is *kî-śānēʾ šallaḥ*,
*for hating, sending away*: the verbs are the two that describe the second husband in Deuteronomy 24:3,
*śānēʾ* and *šillaḥ* (WLC). *Śānēʾ* is a qal perfect third person, not *I hate*, and *šallaḥ* is a
piel infinitive. Some translations take the man as the subject, *if he hates and divorces*, rather than
Yahweh; that is reported from general knowledge. The WEB's rendering is one reading of a difficult
verse. See [[The Wife of Your Youth - Proverbs 2.17, Malachi 2.14, and Jeremiah 3.4]].

## Significance

Deuteronomy 24:1–4 is a narrow case law, one sentence long, about a woman married twice. Two prophets
used it to describe Yahweh and his people, and both had to bend it. Jeremiah has a wife worse than the
law imagined and a husband who asks her to return anyway. Isaiah has a separation without a bill. In
both, Yahweh does what the law does not allow a husband to do. See [[Marriage]] and
[[Divorce, Remarriage, and the Newlywed]]. The Gospels' use of the same law is on
[[Because of the Hardness of Your Hearts - Deuteronomy 24.1-4 and the Gospels]].
