# Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4

## Passages involved

- **Job 2:13** — *"So they sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one
  spoke a word to him"*: the friends' silence before the gesture is named —
  [[Seven Days of Silence]]
- **Job 13:5** — *"Oh that you would be completely silent! Then you would be wise."* —
  [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Job 21:5** — *"Look at me, and be astonished. Lay your hand on your mouth."* Job to the friends —
  [[Why Do the Wicked Live]]
- **Job 29:9** — *"The princes refrained from talking, and laid their hand on their mouth."* Job
  remembering how the powerful fell silent before him — [[The Months of Old]]
- **Job 40:4** — *"Behold, I am of small account. What shall I answer you? I lay my hand on my
  mouth."* Job before Yahweh *(not yet ingested)*
- **Job 31:14, 35** — *"When he visits, what shall I answer him?"*; *"oh that I had one to hear me!
  … Let the Almighty answer me!"* The question Job asks of himself in the oath is the one he asks God
  at 40:4, with the same verb (*ʾăšîbennû*, *ʾăšîbekā*) — [[Job's Oath of Innocence]]
- **Judges 18:19** — the Danites to the Levite of Micah's house: *"Hold your peace, put your hand on
  your mouth, and go with us"*
- **Proverbs 30:32** — *"If you have done foolishly in lifting up yourself, or if you have thought
  evil, put your hand over your mouth."*
- **Micah 7:16** — *"The nations will see and be ashamed of all their might. They will lay their hand
  on their mouth."*
- **Isaiah 52:15** — *"kings will shut their mouths at him"*, at the servant whose appearance was
  marred

## Analysis

**The gesture means one thing in every occurrence: speech yielding to something larger than
itself.** The hand goes over the mouth when a speaker realises that what is in front of him exceeds
anything he could say about it — awe, shame, or a superior's command. Proverbs 30:32 makes it the
remedy for self-exaltation; Micah 7:16 gives it to nations who have seen God act; Judges 18:19 is
the coarse version, a priest told to be quiet by men with swords.

**In Job it occurs three times, and it travels.** At 21:5 Job tells the friends to make it: *"Look at
me, and be astonished. Lay your hand on your mouth."* What they should be astonished at is Job
himself — a blameless man destroyed — and the right response to that sight is the one they managed
for seven days in 2:13 and abandoned in chapter 4. At 29:9 Job remembers receiving it: in the days
of his greatness, *"the princes refrained from talking, and laid their hand on their mouth"* when he
came to the city gate. The chapter adds the rest of the scene: the nobles' tongues stuck to the roofs
of their mouths (29:10), and men who *"kept silence for my counsel. After my words they didn't speak
again"* (29:21–22) — **the order of speech the dialogue has overturned**, with three men who will not
stop talking. And at 40:4, when Yahweh has spoken out of the whirlwind, Job makes it
himself: *"I lay my hand on my mouth."*

**So the gesture passes from what Job demands, to what Job was once given, to what Job gives.** He
asked the friends to be silenced by the sight of his suffering; he had once silenced princes by his
standing; and he is silenced at last not by an argument but by a presence. Each time it is the right
response to what is in front of the speaker, and each time the thing in front of him is greater: a
man's ruin, a man's honour, and God.

**The friends never make it.** That is the quiet indictment in the thread. Job's request at 21:5 is
ignored — Eliphaz answers in chapter 22 with a list of crimes Job did not commit — and in 42:7 it is
the friends who are told they have not spoken rightly. Their seven days on the ground (2:13) were the
best thing they did in the book, and they are the only time they did what 21:5 asked.

## Significance

The thread gives the book's ending a shape the reader can feel before the epilogue explains it. Job
spent thirty-five chapters asking to speak to God and be answered (13:22; 23:3–7; 31:35), and when
the answer comes, the first thing he does is the thing he told his friends to do at 21:5. The
gesture he demanded of others in the face of suffering is the one he makes himself in the face of
God. It is not a defeat in an argument — Job is never told he was wrong about the wicked — but the
same recognition, on a larger scale, that the friends were asked for and refused.

Isaiah 52:15 carries the same silence to the servant: kings shutting their mouths at a sufferer
whose disfigurement was beyond human likeness. The reader of Job hears 21:5 there — *Look at me, and
be astonished.*
