# Eye for Eye - Exodus 21, Leviticus 24, and Matthew 5

## Passages involved

- [[Laws of Violence and Injury]] — Exodus 21:22–27: injury in a fight, proportionate penalties, and release of an
  injured servant.
- [[The Blasphemer and One Law for All]] — Leviticus 24:17–22: matching injury and one law
  for native and foreigner; now ingested from stored WEB.
- Matthew 5:38–42 — Jesus quotes the eye-and-tooth rule and commands nonretaliation; verified WEB,
  not yet ingested.

## Analysis

The full Leviticus unit places the injury rules inside the blasphemy case:
custody and divine sentence precede them, execution follows (24:10–23).
This narrative frame matters: corresponding injury is given as public law,
not as the offender’s justification for the fight. The killing of an animal
requires replacement (18, 21), not the animal-killer’s death. Source for this
addition: stored WEB Leviticus 24:10–23.

Exodus introduces the matching scale within a case involving a pregnant woman injured in a fight.
Its immediate surroundings include fines, payment for lost time and healing, and freedom for a
servant whose eye or tooth a master destroys. **Proportionality is explicit; the passage does not
supply a history of how its courts applied it.** Reading the scale as a restraint on escalating
revenge is a synthesis of its matching terms, not proof that every penalty meant money alone.

Leviticus 24:19–20 states the correspondence directly: the injury inflicted is to be inflicted on
the offender. It also distinguishes replacing an animal from the death penalty for killing a
human (24:17–21), and ends by requiring one law for foreigner and native-born (24:22). This wider
formula does not erase the different remedies for enslaved victims recorded in Exodus 21:20–32.

Matthew 5:38 quotes *"An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth."* Jesus then tells the hearer to
turn the other cheek, yield the cloak as well, go the second mile, and give or lend (5:39–42).
**There is a real difference of instruction:** the Torah cases demand corresponding redress;
Jesus directs the person addressed beyond insisting on it. A distinction between public judgment
and personal response helps explain the settings, but these five verses do not themselves settle
every question about courts or political force. Matthew's quotation matches more than one Torah
formulation; Exodus is not claimed as its uniquely identifiable source.

## Significance

The legal scale and the instruction to relinquish retaliation belong in the same account without
making either say the opposite of what it says. See [[Vengeance]], [[Retribution]], and
[[Strife and Peacemaking]].
