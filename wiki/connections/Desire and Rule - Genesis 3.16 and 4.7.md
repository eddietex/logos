# Desire and Rule — Genesis 3:16 and 4:7

## Passages involved

- [[The Fall]] — Genesis 3:16
- [[Cain and Abel]] — Genesis 4:7

## Analysis

Two sentences, one chapter apart, built out of the same two words.

> **3:16** To the woman he said, “I will greatly multiply your pain in childbirth. In pain you
> will bear children. Your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you.”

> **4:7** If you do well, won’t it be lifted up? If you don’t do well, sin crouches at the door.
> Its desire is for you, but you are to rule over it.

The shared pair is *tešûqâ* ("desire") and *māšal* ("rule"). *Tešûqâ* occurs **three times in the
entire Hebrew Bible** — 3:16, 4:7, and Song of Songs 7:10 — and in two of those three it stands
beside *māšal*, in the same syntactic frame: *X's tešûqâ is toward Y, and Y will māšal over X*.
For a word that rare, one chapter apart, in a text as compositionally deliberate as Genesis 1–11,
the repetition is not plausibly accidental. Whichever verse a reader starts from, the other one is
the closest thing to a definition available.

**Reading 3:16 by 4:7.** In 4:7 the desire is unmistakably an appetite to **master**: sin crouches
at the door wanting Cain, and Cain is told to rule it instead. Carry that sense back to 3:16 and
the woman's sentence is not about longing or dependence but about a contest — she will want to
master her husband, and he will master her. This is the basis for renderings like *"your desire
will be **contrary to** your husband"* (ESV 2016), and it turns the verse from a description of
female attachment into a description of mutual struggle. It is the strongest philological argument
in the debate over 3:16, and it is an argument from exactly one parallel verse.

**Reading 4:7 by 3:16 — the reverse direction.** Run the analogy the other way and it does
something less often noticed. If sin's desire for Cain works like the woman's desire in 3:16, and
Cain's rule over sin works like the husband's rule in 3:16, then 4:7 describes Cain's situation in
the vocabulary of a **broken relation under sentence** — which fits a chapter about a man who is
about to be mastered by the thing he was told to master.

**What each verse does that the other does not.** The asymmetry is the interesting part, and it
cuts against reading them as simply the same sentence twice:

- **3:16 is a sentence pronounced; 4:7 is a warning offered.** One states what will be; the other
  states what could be avoided. *"You are to rule over it"* is second-person, addressed, and — the
  passage is emphatic on this — winnable.
- **3:16's *māšal* has a human subject and a human object.** 4:7's has a human subject and an
  abstraction, personified, for its object. Ruling sin and ruling a spouse are not the same act,
  and an argument that moves between them is borrowing force from a grammatical resemblance.
- **In 4:7 the ruling is a duty and its failure is the tragedy.** If 3:16 is read strictly by 4:7,
  the husband's rule becomes the good outcome by analogy — which is precisely the inference the
  descriptive reading of 3:16 rejects, on the grounds that the verse sits in a list beside thorns
  and pain, and no one treats thorns as a mandate.

That last point is where the parallel is most often over-drawn. It licenses reading *tešûqâ* as an
appetite to control. It does not, on its own, settle whether *"he will rule over you"* describes
a distortion or prescribes an order — the two questions are separable, and the parallel only
touches the first.

## Significance

This is the wiki's first case of a **word-level internal parallel** doing real interpretive work:
two verses that are only connected by vocabulary, and whose connection changes how one of the
Bible's most disputed sentences is read. It is worth keeping visible for two reasons.

The first is the state of the evidence. Everything above rests on a three-occurrence word, and one
of the three (Song of Songs 7:10, *"I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me"*) points the
other way — there *tešûqâ* is plainly desire in the ordinary sense, with no contest and no *māšal*
anywhere near it. A conclusion drawn from two of three occurrences, with the third dissenting, is
worth holding at the strength the sample supports.

The second is structural. Genesis 4 is written as a rerun of Genesis 3 with the stakes raised — a
questioning that echoes *"Where are you?"*, a curse formula that moves from the ground to a
person, a sentence answered by a provision — and the *tešûqâ*/*māšal* pair is the verbal thread
running through the whole correspondence. Whoever composed these chapters meant them to be read
against each other. Both passage pages record the individual arguments; this page records the
link.
