# If I Am Bereaved, I Am Bereaved — Jacob and Esther

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 43:14** — [[The Second Journey]]; [[Jacob]]: *"If I am bereaved of my children, I am
  bereaved."*
- **Esther 4:16** — *"if I perish, I perish"*
- **Genesis 42:36** — [[The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt]]; *"You have bereaved me of my
  children!"* — the same verb, with somebody to blame
- **2 Kings 7:3–4** — the four lepers at the gate of Samaria
- **2 Samuel 15:25–26** — David sending the ark back
- **Genesis 32:9–12** — [[Wrestling at the Jabbok]]; Jacob's other prayer, which argues

## Analysis

### The construction

**Hebrew doubles a verb to mean *and that is that*.**

> **14** May God Almighty give you mercy before the man, that he may release to you your other
> brother and Benjamin. If I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved. (43:14)

***Waʾănî kaʾăšer šākōltî šākāltî*** — *and as for me, as I have been bereaved, I am bereaved.* **Two
perfects of the same root, ***šākōl***, joined by nothing.** The first states a condition already
entered; the second accepts it as settled. **There is no verb of hoping in the sentence and no verb
of fearing.**

**Esther says it in the same shape.**

> **16** … I will go in to the king, which is against the law; and if I perish, I perish. (Esther
> 4:16)

***Wəkaʾăšer ʾābadtî ʾābādtî*** — **the identical grammar with a different root**, ***ʾābad***, to
perish. **These are the two clearest instances of the construction in the Hebrew Bible**, and they
are doing the same work: **a person who has calculated the odds, found them bad, and announced that
they are going ahead anyway.**

### Why the pairing holds beyond the grammar

| | **Jacob**, Genesis 43:14 | **Esther**, Esther 4:16 |
|---|---|---|
| **the risk** | sending [[Benjamin]] into a foreign court | going into a foreign court herself |
| **the power** | *"the man"* — an Egyptian governor with absolute say | Ahasuerus, who kills by not extending a sceptre |
| **the argument that moved them** | [[Judah]]: *"so that we may live, and not die, both we, and you, and also our little ones"* (43:8) | Mordecai: *"who knows if you haven't come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"* |
| **God named** | ***ʾĒl Šadday*** (43:14) | **not at all** — the book of Esther never names him |
| **what precedes the line** | a prayer | a fast |
| **the line's function** | the last word before the doors open | the last word before the doors open |

**Both speakers are being asked to spend a life they consider irreplaceable.** Jacob has said
explicitly that Benjamin is all he has left (42:38; 44:20 quotes him saying *"he alone is left of his
mother"*). Esther is spending her own.

**And in both cases the surrender turns out to be unnecessary.** Nobody is bereaved; nobody perishes.
**The reader of Genesis 43 already knows this when Jacob says it** — the man he is afraid of is his
son, and the compassion he prays for is already there. **The reader of Esther does not know it yet.**
See [[Providence]].

### The same verb, eight verses earlier

**Jacob has used *šākōl* once before, and the difference is the whole movement of the chapter.**

> **36** Jacob, their father, said to them, "You have bereaved me of my children! Joseph is no more,
> Simeon is no more, and you want to take Benjamin away. All these things are against me." (42:36)

***Šikkaltem*** — **second person plural: *you did this.*** Fourteen verses later the same root is
first person and intransitive: **there is nobody left to accuse.** *"You have bereaved me"* becomes
*"I am bereaved."* **He has stopped arguing with his sons and started arguing with the outcome**, and
the second position is the one from which he can finally let Benjamin go.

### The other resignation formulas

**The construction is rare; the posture is not.**

- **2 Kings 7:3–4** — four lepers outside besieged Samaria reason it out in full and arrive at the
  same place: *"if they kill us, we will only die."* **It is Jacob's logic with the arithmetic shown**
- **2 Samuel 15:25–26** — David sends the ark back into Jerusalem as he flees Absalom: *"if he says,
  'I have no delight in you,' behold, here I am. Let him do to me as seems good to him."* **A man
  refusing to take a guarantee with him**
- **Daniel 3:17–18** — *"but if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods"*.
  **The confidence is greater and the structure is the same**: the outcome is conceded in advance so
  that the decision does not depend on it

**What separates Jacob from all of them is that he is not the one going.** David risks himself,
Esther risks herself, the lepers risk themselves. **Jacob risks Benjamin**, and the sentence he
resigns himself with is about his own loss. **Genesis does not comment on that either.**

## Significance

**Genesis 43:14 is the end of Jacob's resistance and the beginning of the Joseph cycle's resolution**
— nothing in chapters 44–45 can happen until Benjamin is on the road. **The verse is the hinge, and
what swings it is not faith exactly but exhaustion formalised into consent.**

**Set beside his other recorded prayer, the contrast is stark.** At the Jabbok he prayed at length
and argued from the covenant — *"You said, 'I will surely do you good, and make your offspring as the
sand of the sea'"* (32:12). **Here he invokes *ʾĒl Šadday*, asks for *raḥămîm*, and then says the
thing you say when you have no leverage at all.** See [[Prayer]] and [[Compassion and Mercy]].

**And the Esther parallel is not decoration.** Both books are about Jews in a foreign court where the
God of Israel is never visibly at work — **Esther does not name him once, and Genesis's narrator says
nothing about him from 39:23 to 46:2.** In both, **deliverance arrives through a court official who
is secretly one of the family**, through a sequence of coincidences the characters read as disaster,
**and the resignation formula is spoken at exactly the moment the deliverance is already in place and
invisible.** See [[The Signet Ring and the Second Chariot - Joseph, Mordecai, and Daniel]].
