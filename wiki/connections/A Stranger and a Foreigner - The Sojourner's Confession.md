# A Stranger and a Foreigner - The Sojourner's Confession

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 23:4** — *"I am a stranger and a foreigner living with you. Give me a possession of a
  burying-place with you, that I may bury my dead out of my sight."*
- **Leviticus 25:23** — *"The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are
  strangers and live as foreigners with me."*
- **1 Chronicles 29:15** — *"For we are strangers before you, and foreigners, as all our fathers
  were. Our days on the earth are as a shadow, and there is no remaining."*
- **Psalm 39:12** — *"For I am a stranger with you, a foreigner, as all my fathers were."*
- **Hebrews 11:13–16** — *"having confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth …
  they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one."*
- **1 Peter 2:11** — *"Beloved, I beg you as foreigners and pilgrims, to abstain from fleshly
  lusts."*
- Acts 7:5 — *"He gave him no inheritance in it, no, not so much as to set his foot on."*

## Analysis

**The phrase is a legal self-description, and it starts as one.**

*Gēr wətôšāb ʾānōkî ʿimmākem* — two nouns for the same predicament, doubled for precision the way a
deed doubles its terms. A ***gēr*** is a resident alien: present lawfully, protected by custom, and
holding no land. A ***tôšāb*** is a settler, a sojourner — someone dwelling among a people who are
not his. **Neither word can own ground**, and Abraham says both of them in the sentence in which he
asks to buy some. See [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] and [[Land and Sojourning]].

That is the whole tension, and Genesis states it without commentary. The man has been promised *"all
the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession"* (17:8). He introduces himself to its inhabitants
as somebody with no claim on any of it. Both statements are true, and the gap between them is four
hundred years wide.

### The phrase turns around

**Leviticus 25:23 takes Abraham's confession and makes God say it about Israel.**

> The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are **strangers and live as
> foreigners with me**.

The same pair — *gērîm wətôšābîm* — now spoken by the landlord to the tenants, **after** the conquest,
about land Israel actually holds. It is the ground of the jubilee: land cannot be alienated forever
because nobody in Israel owns it outright in the first place. **The status Abraham was in because he
had no title becomes the status Israel is in because of who does.** What was a disadvantage in
Genesis 23 is a theology in Leviticus 25.

### Israel prays it back

David, dedicating the materials for a temple he is not allowed to build:

> **15** For we are strangers before you, and foreigners, as all our fathers were. Our days on the
> earth are as a shadow, and there is no remaining. *(1 Chronicles 29:15)*

And the psalmist, in almost identical words, at the end of a psalm about the brevity of a life
(Psalm 39:12). **Both add a second meaning the Genesis verse did not have**: not merely landless in
Canaan, but transient on the earth. *"as all my fathers were"* points the phrase back at Abraham
explicitly, and *"our days on the earth are as a shadow"* enlarges it from a property question into a
mortality one. **The confession stops being about a deed and becomes about a lifespan** — which is
already latent in Genesis 23, where the only reason Abraham needs land at all is that his wife has
died.

### Hebrews makes the landlessness deliberate

> **13** These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them and embraced
> them from afar, and having confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth.

**Hebrews 11 reads the patriarchs' whole situation as chosen rather than endured.** They lived in
tents *"in the land of promise, as in a land not his own"* (11:9) because they were *"looking for the
city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God"* (11:10) — a city set explicitly against
the one built at Babel to keep its builders from being scattered (Genesis 11:4). And the argument
turns on a striking claim about what they did *not* do: *"if indeed they had been thinking of that
country from which they went out, they would have had enough time to return"* (11:15).

**On this reading, Genesis 23 is not a disappointment but an exhibit.** A man who could own only a
grave, and bought one anyway, at full price, in public, is a man betting on a timescale longer than
the deed. The wiki notes that this is Hebrews' construction and not Genesis's: **Genesis reports the
purchase and offers no interpretation of Abraham's motives at all.**

### And 1 Peter hands it to the church

*"as foreigners and pilgrims"* — *paroikous kai parepidēmous*, the Septuagint's exact pair for *gēr
wətôšāb*, and the same two words Hebrews 11:13 uses. 1 Peter has already opened by addressing its
readers as *"the chosen ones who are living as foreigners in the Dispersion"* (1:1). **The
self-description Abraham used to a Hittite assembly at a city gate becomes, by the end of the canon,
the standing description of a religious community's relation to the world it lives in.**

## Significance

**Four hundred shekels bought one field, and the sentence Abraham said while buying it outlasted
every other clause in the transaction.**

The trajectory is unusually clean for a biblical phrase:

| stage | who says it | of whom | about what |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 23:4 | Abraham | himself | a legal fact: he cannot own land here |
| Leviticus 25:23 | God | Israel in the land | why the land cannot be sold in perpetuity |
| 1 Chronicles 29:15; Psalm 39:12 | Israel | itself | landlessness **and** the shortness of life |
| Hebrews 11:13 | the writer | the patriarchs | a confession of something better, made deliberately |
| 1 Peter 2:11 | Peter | the church | how to live in a world you do not belong to |

**Acts 7:5 supplies the flat version** in Stephen's speech: *"He gave him no inheritance in it, no,
not so much as to set his foot on. He promised that he would give it to him for a possession, and to
his offspring after him, when he still had no child."* Stephen states the gap between promise and
possession without softening it, in a speech whose whole argument is that God has never been
confined to a plot of ground — which is Genesis 23's own situation used as a premise.

**What holds the whole chain together is that nobody in it tries to close the gap.** Abraham does not
claim the land by promise; he pays for a corner of it. Leviticus does not tell Israel it owns
Canaan; it tells them they are tenants. The psalmist does not ask for permanence; he asks for a
hearing before he goes. And Hebrews says the patriarchs *could* have gone home and did not.
