# Who Will Contend with Me - Job 13, Isaiah 50, and Romans 8

## Passages involved

- **Job 13:18–19** — *"See now, I have set my cause in order. I know that I am righteous. Who is he
  who will contend with me? For then would I hold my peace and give up the spirit."* —
  [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Job 13:28** — *"though I am decaying like a rotten thing, like a garment that is moth-eaten."*
- **Isaiah 50:7–9** — *"Therefore I have set my face like a flint, and I know that I won't be
  disappointed. He who justifies me is near. Who will bring charges against me? Let us stand up
  together. Who is my adversary? Let him come near to me. Behold, the Lord Yahweh will help me! Who
  is he who will condemn me? Behold, they will all grow old like a garment. The moths will eat them
  up."*
- **Isaiah 51:8** — *"For the moth will eat them up like a garment, and the worm will eat them like
  wool; but my righteousness will be forever."*
- **Romans 8:31–34** — *"If God is for us, who can be against us? … Who could bring a charge against
  God's chosen ones? It is God who justifies. Who is he who condemns? It is Christ who died, yes
  rather, who was raised from the dead, who is at the right hand of God, who also makes intercession
  for us."*
- **Job 9:2–3, 19–20, 33** — *"How can man be just with God? If he is pleased to contend with him,
  he can't answer him one time in a thousand… If it is a matter of justice, 'Who,' says he, 'will
  summon me?' … There is no umpire between us."* — [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]]
- **Job 23:3–7** — *"Oh that I knew where I might find him!… I would set my cause in order before
  him… There the upright might reason with him, so I should be delivered forever from my judge."* —
  [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]]
- **Job 40:2** — *"Shall he who argues contend with the Almighty?"*

## Analysis

**The same courtroom, three times.** In each passage a defendant asks *who is it that will
contend with me?* — *mî-hûʾ yārîb ʿimmādî* (Job 13:19), *mî-yārîb ʾittî* (Isaiah 50:8) — and the
question is not a request for an opponent but a claim that none can win. Both use the vocabulary of
the lawsuit (*rîb*, *to contend*; *ṣādaq*, *to be in the right*; *mišpāṭ*, *the case*), and both end
in the image of a garment eaten by moths. **In Job the moth-eaten garment is the defendant**
(13:28); **in Isaiah it is the accusers** (50:9; 51:8).

**What the Servant has that Job lacks.** Job 13:18 says *"I know that I am righteous (ʾeṣdāq)"* —
the verb in the first person, the defendant declaring his own innocence. Isaiah 50:8 says *"He who
justifies me (maṣdîqî) is near"* — the same root in the causative, God declaring the Servant
innocent. That is precisely the difference between the two situations. Job's whole problem in
chapters 9–14 is that the party who should vindicate him is the party he is in dispute with, and so
the only person who can say *ṣaddîq* of Job is Job. *"There is no umpire between us"* (9:33). **The
Servant of Isaiah 50 has the umpire** — the Lord Yahweh is near and *helps* him (50:7, 9) — and so he
can issue Job's challenge with confidence Job does not have. Isaiah's language is so close to Job's
that a literary relationship is likely in one direction or the other; the wiki does not decide the
direction.

**Paul takes Isaiah's version and adds the intercessor.** Romans 8:33–34 is Isaiah 50:8–9 almost
clause by clause — *"Who could bring a charge?… It is God who justifies. Who is he who condemns?"* —
and then supplies what neither Job nor the Servant names: *"It is Christ who died… who also makes
intercession for us."* That is the third party Job asked for in 9:33 and 16:19–21, the one who would
*"maintain the right of a man with God"*. Romans 8 thus completes an argument that runs through all
three texts: Job's *I know I am righteous* (self-vindication, against God), the Servant's *he who
justifies me is near* (vindication by God), and Paul's *it is God who justifies… Christ who
intercedes* (vindication by God, with an advocate).

**By chapter 23 Job no longer asks for an umpire.** *"Would he contend (yārîb) with me in the
greatness of his power? No, but he would listen to me. There the upright might reason with him"*
(23:6–7). The same verb, *rîb*, and the opposite fear: in chapter 9 a trial was hopeless because God
would overpower him, and in chapter 23 it would be won if only God could be found. The obstacle has
moved from God's power to God's absence, and the mediator of 9:33 drops out of the picture — the
nearest Job comes to the Servant's *"He who justifies me is near"*, said of a God who is nowhere.

**Job's challenge is answered, and not the way he expected.** At 40:2 God turns the verb on Job:
*"Shall he who argues (rōb) contend with the Almighty?"* Job had asked who would contend with him;
God replies by asking whether Job will contend with *him*. And then the verdict of 42:7–8 vindicates
Job anyway — *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has"* — using the
title *my servant* that Isaiah gives his figure.

## Significance

- The three passages together trace the book of Job's central legal problem — the absence of an
  umpire — to its two biblical answers: God as the one who justifies, and an intercessor at God's
  right hand. See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].
- The moth-eaten garment changes owner between Job 13 and Isaiah 50: the wasting of the innocent
  becomes the wasting of his accusers. Isaiah 51:8 sets it against *"my righteousness will be
  forever"*.
- *"My servant Job"* (1:8; 2:3; 42:7–8) and *"my servant"* of Isaiah 42–53 are the two great
  innocent sufferers of the Hebrew Bible, and they share this challenge. See also
  [[They Didn't Recognize Him - Marred Beyond Knowing]].
