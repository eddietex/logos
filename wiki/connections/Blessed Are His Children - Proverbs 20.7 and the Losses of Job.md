# Blessed Are His Children - Proverbs 20.7 and the Losses of Job

## Passages involved

- [[Wine Is a Mocker]] — Proverbs 20:7, 9.
- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:21.
- [[Job of Uz]] — Job 1:1–5.
- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — Job 1:6–22.
- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:1–10.
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:7–9.

## Analysis

Proverbs 20:7 pronounces blessing on the children of the righteous man who walks in integrity.
Proverbs 11:21 similarly promises deliverance for righteous offspring. Neither sentence names a
particular family or says exactly what form the blessing takes. Both commend a way of living
whose good reaches beyond the individual.

**Explicit tension:** Job is introduced as blameless and upright (Job 1:1, 8), yet his children
die together in a collapsed house (1:18–19). God subsequently affirms his continued integrity
and describes his ruin as without cause (2:3). If the proverb is turned into an exceptionless
promise of children's survival or immediate security, Job's narrative contradicts that reading.
The general blessing must not be made a basis for accusing bereaved parents.

Job 21:7–9 supplies the reverse observation: wicked people grow powerful while their children
are established before them and their houses remain safe. Present household prosperity is
therefore no reliable proof of parental righteousness either. These passages are retained side
by side; no unmentioned sin, delayed explanation, or guaranteed timetable is invented to remove
the tension.

Proverbs 20 itself distinguishes integrity from a claim to sinlessness: verse 7 commends
righteous conduct, while verse 9 questions who can claim a self-purified heart. The second
saying does not cancel the first or authorize the friends' diagnosis of Job.

## Significance

The comparison links [[Blessing]], [[Integrity]], [[Righteousness]], [[Retribution]], and [[Suffering]]. It keeps
the proverb's intergenerational hope while refusing to use a family's losses as a moral
verdict.
