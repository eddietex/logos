# Whoever Sheds Man's Blood

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:5–6 — *"Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in
  his own image"* — [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- Genesis 4:10–15, 23–24 — the blood that cries from the ground, the sevenfold protection of the
  murderer, and Lamech's seventy-sevenfold — [[Cain and Abel]] and
  [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]]
- Genesis 1:26–27 — the image, which 9:6 gives as its reason —
  [[The Creation of the World]]
- Exodus 20:13; 21:12–14, 28 — *"You shall not murder"*; intent distinguished from accident; the ox
  that gores
- Leviticus 24:17–21 — *life for life*, and the *lex talionis* as a ceiling
- Numbers 35:9–34 — the cities of refuge, the avenger of blood, and blood that pollutes the land
- Deuteronomy 19:1–13 — the same institution, with the elders of the city between the avenger and
  the killer
- Matthew 26:52 — *"all those who take the sword will die by the sword"*
- Romans 13:1–4 — the authority that *"doesn't bear the sword in vain"*
- James 3:9 — cursing people *"who are made in the image of God"*
- Revelation 13:10 — the same form, turned into a call for endurance

## Analysis

**Genesis has been building to this statute for five chapters, and it is worth tracing the arc
before reading the verse.**

- **Genesis 4:10.** The first murder is discovered because *"the voice of your brother's blood cries
  to me from the ground"*. Blood has a voice; God answers it. Nobody is authorized to do anything
  about it.
- **Genesis 4:15.** The murderer is not executed. He is sentenced, and then explicitly **protected**:
  *"whoever slays Cain, vengeance will be taken on him sevenfold"*, plus a sign to make sure nobody
  tries. The Bible's first statement about killing a killer is a prohibition of it.
- **Genesis 4:23–24.** Five generations on, Lamech takes the number into his own mouth and inflates
  it: *"If Cain will be avenged seven times, truly Lamech seventy-seven times"* — for a wound, and
  he has already killed over it. Private vengeance, uncapped.
- **Genesis 6:11, 13.** The earth is *"filled with violence"* — *ḥāmās* — and that is the stated
  ground of the flood.
- **Genesis 9:5–6.** The reckoning is required by God, capped at one life for one, and placed in
  human hands.

Read in that order, 9:6 is not the introduction of retribution into a peaceable world. It is the
**regulation** of something the world has already demonstrated it will do without any authorization
at all, in a chapter given to the survivors of a judgment brought on by exactly that.

**The verse is poetry, and its shape is its argument.** In Hebrew it is six words in a mirror:

> *šōpēk* / *dam* / *hāʾādām* — **bāʾādām** — *dāmô* / *yiššāpēk*
> (shedder / blood-of / the-man — **by-man** — his-blood / shall-be-shed)

The verb *šāpak* opens and closes; *dām*, blood, appears on both sides; and the word at the pivot is
*ʾādām*, **man**. What is done returns on the doer, and the axis it turns on is a human being. It is
the tightest piece of construction in Genesis 9 and the only verse in the pericope with a poetic
form.

**The reason clause is the part that matters most and gets quoted least.** *"For God made man in his
own image."* Three things follow from where that sentence sits.

*The image survives.* This is the first mention of the *tselem* since Genesis 5:3, and it comes after
the fall, after Cain, and after a flood sent because *"every imagination of the thoughts of man's
heart was continually only evil"*. If the image had been forfeited at any of those points the statute
would have no premise. Genesis states it in the present tense of a standing fact, and James 3:9 still
argues from it in the first century. See [[Image of God]].

*The crime is defined by the victim, not by the perpetrator.* Murder is capital here not because
killing is generally wrong, and not because society requires deterrence, but because the person
killed is a likeness of God. The offence is closer to iconoclasm than to theft: it destroys something
that represents someone. This is also the reason the rule is universal — it is grounded in what every
human being *is*, not in a national law, which is why the Torah's homicide legislation applies to the
resident alien as well (Numbers 35:15).

*And it cuts both ways.* The murderer is also made in the image, which is the standing objection to
using this verse as a straightforward warrant for execution, and the objection has to be met rather
than dismissed.

**The Torah then does two things with it, and both are limits.** *Life for life* (Leviticus 24:17–21;
Exodus 21:23–25) is universally misremembered as a licence for revenge; it is a **ceiling** — one
life, not a family, not seventy-seven, which is Lamech's arithmetic capped. And the cities of refuge
(Numbers 35; Deuteronomy 19) take the *gōʾēl haddām*, the avenger of blood — an institution the
culture already had and the law does not abolish — and put a trial, a distance, and a body of elders
between him and the killer, with an explicit distinction between the man who *"lay in wait"* and the
man whose axe head flew off the handle. Numbers 35:33 then restates 9:6's premise as a doctrine of
the land: *"blood, it pollutes the land, and no expiation can be made for the land, for the blood
that is shed therein, but by the blood of him who shed it."* The ground that received Abel's blood is
still the party to the transaction.

**The New Testament uses the sentence's form to say something else with it.** *"All those who take
the sword will die by the sword"* (Matthew 26:52) is Genesis 9:6's structure — the deed returning on
the doer — and Jesus says it to *stop* a disciple from using a weapon, in a scene where the person
about to be killed is the one speaking. Revelation 13:10 uses the same shape again, and draws from it
*"the endurance and the faith of the saints"*: the assurance that the account will be settled becomes
a reason not to settle it yourself. The line runs the way Deuteronomy 32:35 and Romans 12:19 run —
*"Vengeance is mine, and recompense"* — and Romans 13:4 then locates the sword in the hands of the
governing authority rather than the individual, which is the passage most often joined to Genesis 9:6
in Christian argument. See [[Vengeance]].

## Significance

**It is the first delegation of judicial authority in the Bible.** Everything before it kept the
reckoning in God's hands: Cain was protected, Lamech was not answered at all, and the flood was
executed by God alone. Genesis 9:6 hands something over, and the something is lethal. Whatever one
concludes about capital punishment, this is the verse where human beings are first said to act on
God's behalf in a matter of life and death — which makes the whole later apparatus of courts,
witnesses, elders, and cities of refuge an attempt to make that delegation survivable.

**It is a limit as much as a licence, and reading it as only one or the other misses the passage.**
The immediate context is a world remade after being destroyed for *violence*, and the statute's
effect is to bound retaliation at parity and to give the reckoning a ground outside the offended
party's judgment. The same verse that authorizes a death forbids seventy-seven.

**And it is where the doctrine of the image acquires a practical edge.** Genesis 1:27 states the
image; Genesis 5:3 says it is inherited; Genesis 9:6 makes it *actionable* — the first place in
Scripture where a rule of conduct is derived from what human beings are rather than from what God has
commanded about a particular case. Every later appeal of that kind, from James 3:9's argument about
speech to the whole modern vocabulary of human dignity, has this verse somewhere behind it.

**A note on its afterlife.** No verse in Genesis has carried more public weight. It is the standard
scriptural warrant for capital punishment, cited from the rabbis through Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin,
and the colonial capital codes, with the usual argument being that since 9:6 predates the Mosaic law
and is addressed to all humanity, the abrogation of that law does not touch it. It has been read the
opposite way with equal seriousness — as descriptive rather than prescriptive (this is what happens
to those who shed blood, in the manner of Matthew 26:52), or as a principle whose grounding clause
undercuts its own application, since the murderer bears the image too. The exegetical points that are
not in dispute: the verse gives a reason, the reason is about the victim, and its immediate literary
function is to cap an escalation the book has just spent five chapters documenting.
