# Whoever Sheds Man's Blood

## Passages involved

- Genesis 4:8–15 — Cain's murder, the blood that cries from the ground, and the mark that protects
  the murderer from being killed ([[Cain and Abel]])
- Genesis 4:23–24 — Lamech's sword-song: *"If Cain will be avenged seven times, truly Lamech
  seventy-seven times"* ([[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]])
- Genesis 6:11, 13 — the earth *"filled with violence"* (*ḥāmās*), which is why the flood comes
- Genesis 9:5–6 — *"Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in
  his own image"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Exodus 20:13; 21:12–14 — *"You shall not murder"*, and the distinction between lying in wait and
  an unintended death
- Numbers 35:9–34 — cities of refuge, the avenger of blood, the two-witness rule, and *"blood
  pollutes the land"*
- Deuteronomy 19:1–13 — the same law restated, with the elders empowered to hand over a deliberate
  killer
- Matthew 5:21–22 — *"whoever murders will be in danger of the judgment"*, extended to anger
- Matthew 26:52 — *"all those who take the sword will die by the sword"*
- John 8:1–11 — a capital case declined
- Romans 12:19 — *"Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay"*
- Romans 13:1–4 — *"he doesn't bear the sword in vain"*
- James 3:9 — *"we curse men, who are made in the image of God"*
- Revelation 13:10 — *"If anyone is to go into captivity, he will go into captivity. If anyone is to
  be killed with the sword, he must be killed"*

## Analysis

**Genesis contradicts itself about murderers, and it does so deliberately.** In Genesis 4 God
prevents the execution of the first killer: *"whoever slays Cain, vengeance will be taken on him
sevenfold,"* and a mark is given *"so that anyone finding him would not strike him."* In Genesis 9
God requires it: *"Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will be shed by man."* The two verses give
opposite instructions about the same act, they are five chapters apart, and the second is spoken to
the survivors of a flood that came because the earth had filled with bloodshed. Reading them as a
sequence rather than as a doctrine is what makes sense of both.

**The sequence is an escalation, and then a stop.**

| | Genesis 4:15 | Genesis 4:24 | Genesis 9:6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speaker | God | Lamech | God |
| Ratio | sevenfold | seventy-sevenfold | one for one |
| Who acts | nobody — the mark prevents it | Lamech himself | *"by man"*, unspecified |
| Occasion | protecting a murderer | boasting of a killing | legislating for a new world |
| Ground | none given | wounded pride: *"for wounding me"* | *"God made man in his own image"* |

Between the first column and the second, private blood-vengeance inflates by a factor of eleven and
loses any connection to proportion — Lamech kills a man for a bruise and sets his own tariff. That
is the world Genesis 6:11 describes as *"filled with violence"*, and it is the world the flood ends.
So when 9:6 arrives, what it does to the sequence is cap it. Not seven, not seventy-seven: one life
for one life, and no more.

Read this way, Genesis 9:6 is a **restraint on bloodshed rather than a licence for it**, which is
also how the *lex talionis* works in its own setting — *"eye for eye, tooth for tooth"* (Exodus
21:24) reads as savage only to readers who have not met the alternative, which is Lamech. The formula
is a ceiling.

The other reading, not exclusive to it: the **subject changes**. Genesis 4:15 suppresses the
*family avenger*, the man who "finds" Cain and acts on his own account. Genesis 9:6 institutes
requital that is universal, stated as law, and grounded in a reason external to the avenger's grief.
On this account the two verses are not in conflict at all — one forbids private revenge, the other
establishes public justice — and the Torah's later homicide law backs it up, since Numbers 35 hedges
the avenger of blood about with cities of refuge, a two-witness requirement, and a distinction of
intent that no private avenger would observe.

**The shape of the verse is part of its argument.** In Hebrew the first half of 9:6 is six words in
perfect mirror — *šōpēk dam hā-ʾādām / bā-ʾādām dāmô yiššāpēk* — with the verb *šāpak* at both ends,
active then passive, *dam* second from each end, and *ʾādām* twice at the centre facing itself. The
line enacts the equivalence it states. It is the first poetry in Genesis since Lamech's sword-song,
which is unlikely to be an accident: the answer to the song comes in the song's own form.

**The preposition the whole argument turns on.** *Bā-ʾādām* is read two ways, both ancient:

- **Instrumental — "by man."** Human beings are the agents; the verse delegates lethal judicial
  authority. This is what WEB gives and what nearly every English version gives, and it is the
  reading on which 9:6 is the Bible's foundational warrant for capital punishment. Targum Onqelos
  goes further and supplies *by witnesses, on the word of judges*, which is the reading that
  produces the rabbinic requirement of due process.
- **Price — "in exchange for the man."** The *bet pretii*, a life for a life, with the agent left
  unnamed. On this reading the verse states an equivalence — blood is answered by blood — without
  specifying who does the answering, and does not by itself authorize anyone.

The instrumental reading has the weight of tradition and translation behind it. The price reading
has the merit of fitting a sentence that is otherwise entirely about *God's* claim: 9:5 says three
times over *I will require*, and it is odd for the very next line to hand the requiring off without
comment.

**What the verse does not supply.** It is a maxim inside a narrative, not a statute. It names no
procedure, no evidentiary standard, no distinction between deliberate and accidental killing, and no
court. All of those arrive later and are considerably more careful than the maxim: Exodus 21:13
separates ambush from accident, Numbers 35:30 requires more than one witness, and Numbers 35:9–28
builds six cities to keep the avenger of blood away from a man who killed without intent. Anyone
citing 9:6 as though it settled how a society should punish homicide is citing the shortest and
least procedural verse on the subject.

**The reason clause, which is the part everything else rests on.** *"For God made man in his own
image."* The prohibition of murder is not grounded in deterrence, social stability, or the victim's
value to anyone. It is grounded in whose likeness the victim bears. Three consequences follow, and
Scripture draws all three:

1. **The image survives.** Whatever Genesis 3 did to humanity and whatever Genesis 6:5 says about the
   human heart, 9:6 is spoken *after* both and treats the image as intact and load-bearing. Genesis
   5:3 had already shown it passing from father to son. See [[Image of God]].
2. **It applies to everyone.** There is no qualification by nation, righteousness, or standing —
   this is said to all humanity through Noah, before there is a covenant people. Every human being
   is protected by it, including the murderer, which is why the law that follows works so hard to
   distinguish the guilty from the merely accused.
3. **It reaches past killing.** James 3:9 makes the argument about *speech*: *"With it we bless our
   God and Father, and with it we curse men, who are made in the image of God."* The premise of 9:6,
   applied to the tongue. Jesus does the same thing to anger in Matthew 5:21–22.

**The New Testament holds both sides, four verses apart.** Romans 12:19 forbids the believer to
avenge — *"Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay, says the Lord"* — and Romans 13:4 says the
governing authority *"doesn't bear the sword in vain"* and is *"an avenger for wrath to him who does
evil."* Paul puts them in the same argument without apparent discomfort, and the distribution is
exactly the one that resolves Genesis 4:15 against Genesis 9:6: private vengeance is God's to
forbid, public requital is God's to delegate. Against that, Matthew 26:52 has Jesus stop an armed
disciple with a sentence in the form of 9:6 — *"all those who take the sword will die by the
sword"* — and John 8 has him decline to carry out a capital sentence the law permitted. The New
Testament does not settle the question, and neither does this page.

## Significance

Genesis 9:6 does three things that the rest of the canon builds on.

It **prices a human life at exactly one human life**, in a narrative that has just shown what
happens without a ceiling — Cain protected, Lamech boasting, and an earth so full of violence that
God ends it.

It **locates the wrong of murder in the victim rather than in the act's consequences**, which is why
the argument still works when transposed to cursing (James 3:9) or to anger (Matthew 5:22), and why
it does not depend on the victim being innocent, useful, or known.

And it **states that the image of God survived the flood**. The verse is usually read for what it
authorizes. Its more durable claim is the one in the subordinate clause: after Eden, after Cain,
after the verdict of 6:5 and the water that followed it, God still describes human beings as made in
his image and legislates on that basis.
