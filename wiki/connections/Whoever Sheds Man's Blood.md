# Whoever Sheds Man's Blood

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:5–6 — *"I will surely require your life's blood… Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood
  will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Genesis 4:8–15 — the first killing, and God protecting the killer with a sevenfold threat
  ([[Cain and Abel]])
- Genesis 4:23–24 — Lamech's seventy-sevenfold, for a wound
  ([[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]])
- Genesis 6:11, 13 — *"the earth is filled with violence"*, the reason for the flood
  ([[Noah and the Flood]])
- Genesis 1:26–27; 5:1–3 — the image granted, and transmitted
  ([[The Creation of the World]], [[From Adam to Noah]])
- Exodus 21:12–14, 22–25, 28–29 — the homicide law: intentional and unintentional distinguished,
  *"life for life, eye for eye"*, and the goring ox stoned
- Leviticus 24:17–22 — *"He who strikes any man mortally shall surely be put to death"*, with one
  law for the foreigner and the native
- Numbers 35:9–34 — cities of refuge, the avenger of blood, the requirement of more than one
  witness, the ban on ransom, and *"blood pollutes the land"*
- Deuteronomy 19:1–21; 21:1–9 — the refuge system restated, *"life for life"*, and the rite for an
  unsolved killing
- 2 Samuel 12:9–10; 1 Kings 21:19 — the prophetic application to kings: David and Uriah, Ahab and
  Naboth
- Isaiah 1:15; Jeremiah 22:17; Ezekiel 22:1–13 — *"your hands are full of blood"* as the prophets'
  standing indictment
- Matthew 5:21–26 — *"You have heard that it was said to the ancient ones, 'You shall not murder'…
  but I tell you that everyone who is angry with his brother"*
- Matthew 26:52 — *"all those who take the sword will die by the sword"*
- Romans 12:19; 13:1–4 — *"Don't seek revenge yourselves… for it is written, 'Vengeance belongs to
  me'"*, and the magistrate who *"doesn't bear the sword in vain"*
- James 3:9 — cursing people *"who are made in the likeness of God"*
- Revelation 13:10 — *"If anyone is to go into captivity, he will go into captivity. If anyone is to
  be killed with the sword, he must be killed"*

## Analysis

**Genesis 9:6 is the Bible's first law about killing, and it arrives late.** By the time it is
given, Genesis has already reported a murder, a boast about a murder, and a global judgment
explicitly caused by *ḥāmās*, violence. The law is not the beginning of the problem; it is the
world's response to having been destroyed over it. That sequence is the key to reading the verse:
God has just promised never again to answer human violence with a flood (9:11), and 9:6 is the
substitute. Judgment does not stop, it changes venue — from the sky to a human court.

**Six words that mirror.** In Hebrew, 9:6a is:

> *šōpēk* — *dam* — *hāʾādām* ‖ *bāʾādām* — *dāmô* — *yiššāpēk*
> *shedder — blood of — the man* ‖ *by the man — his blood — shall be shed*

The second half is the first half reversed, term for term, with the participle at one end answering
the passive verb at the other. The sentence is the *lex talionis* executed as syntax: the penalty is
literally the crime read backwards. Hebrew narrative rarely does this, and the fact that it is done
here suggests the writer regarded the proportion — one life, exactly, for one life — as the
substance of the ruling and not merely its measure.

**Which is the point against Genesis 4.** Set the three numbers side by side. Cain kills, and God
guarantees *sevenfold* vengeance on anyone who kills him (4:15) — a protection, but an escalating
one. Lamech takes the number, claims it for himself, and inflates it to *seventy-sevenfold* for a
young man who wounded him (4:24). Genesis 9:6 sets the ratio at **one**. The trajectory that runs
through Cain's line — each generation demanding more than the last — is not condemned in a speech.
It is stopped by being converted into arithmetic. See [[Vengeance]].

**The ground of it is not social order.** *"For God made man in his own image"* is the reason given,
and it is the only reason given. Three consequences follow, and the later law honours all three.

- **Murder is uniquely non-compensable.** Israelite law permits monetary settlement for a very wide
  range of injuries, including a fatal goring by a negligent owner's ox (Exodus 21:30). It forbids
  it for murder: *"you shall take no ransom for the life of a murderer who is guilty of death"*
  (Numbers 35:31). If what is destroyed is a likeness of God, nothing else is the right size to pay
  for it.
- **The protection is universal.** The image belongs to *hāʾādām*, humanity, with no qualification.
  Leviticus 24:22 draws the line where Genesis 9 does: *"You shall have one kind of law for the
  foreigner as well as the native-born."*
- **The image survives everything.** 9:6 is spoken after the fall, after the first murder, after a
  flood sent for total corruption, and in the same speech that has just diagnosed the human heart as
  evil from its youth (8:21). Whatever Genesis 3 damaged, this is the text that shows it did not
  revoke the status of 1:27. James 3:9 is still arguing from it in the New Testament — and arguing
  about speech, which is the furthest extension of the same principle. See [[Image of God]].

**Who wields it: the preposition problem.** Everything about the verse's application turns on
*bāʾādām*. Read instrumentally, *by man*, it deputizes human beings to carry out the sentence, and
9:6 becomes the charter of human judicial authority — the seventh of the rabbinic Noahide
commandments is precisely the obligation to establish courts, and it is derived from here. Read as a
*beth* of price, *in exchange for the man*, it fixes the equivalence without naming an executioner.
Read locally, *among men* or in the presence of witnesses, it points at due process. The
instrumental reading is the majority one and best fits the contrast the passage draws — 9:5 says God
will require it and 9:6 says how — but the Hebrew does not settle it, and a great deal of argument
about capital punishment has been conducted on top of a preposition that will bear more than one
load.

**The later law adds what 9:6 leaves out.** Read alone, the verse is a bare rule with no procedure.
The Torah supplies the procedure, and almost all of it is restrictive: intentional killing
distinguished from accidental (Exodus 21:13–14), six cities of refuge for the killer who did not
lie in wait (Numbers 35:9–15), a requirement of more than one witness before anyone is executed
(Numbers 35:30; Deuteronomy 17:6), a hearing before the congregation, and a rite for the case where
no killer can be found (Deuteronomy 21:1–9). Anyone quoting 9:6 as a self-sufficient rule is
quoting it without the system the same canon attaches to it.

**And the New Testament does something particular with it.** Jesus quotes the shape of 9:6 in
Gethsemane — *"all those who take the sword will die by the sword"* (Matthew 26:52) — but he says it
to **stop** a killing, not to require one, and the sentence functions as a warning about
consequences rather than as an instruction to a court. Paul splits the verse in two: private
retaliation is refused outright (*"Don't seek revenge yourselves"*, Romans 12:19), and the
magistrate's sword is affirmed in the next breath (13:4). That division is the most straightforward
New Testament reading of Genesis 9 — the blood is required, and not by you. Revelation 13:10 turns
the same formula toward the persecuted as a call for endurance rather than resistance.

## Significance

Genesis 9:6 does three things at once, and they are usually discussed separately.

It **limits** violence, by fixing a ratio that Genesis 4 had shown to be otherwise unbounded. It
**grounds** the value of human life in something that cannot be forfeited by conduct, nationality,
or ability, because the image is granted rather than earned. And it **delegates**: for the first
time, something God has reserved to himself is placed in human hands — which is a very large grant
to make to a species whose heart the previous verse called evil from its youth, and which the rest
of the Bible spends considerable effort hedging with witnesses, refuges, and prophets who tell kings
that Naboth's blood is on them.

Read together with 9:11, the arrangement is coherent. God undertakes not to drown the world again;
in exchange, the world is given a rule against bloodshed and the authority to enforce it. The flood
is off the table, and justice is now everybody's problem.
