# The Protoevangelium — Genesis 3:15 and the Seed of the Woman

## Passages involved

- [[The Fall]] — Genesis 3:15
- [[Cain and Abel]] — Genesis 4:1, 4:8
- Romans 16:20 · Galatians 4:4 · Hebrews 2:14 · 1 John 3:8 · Revelation 12:1–17 *(not yet
  ingested)*

## Analysis

> **Genesis 3:15** I will put hostility between you and the woman, and between your offspring and
> her offspring. He will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel.

The verse sits inside the sentence on the serpent, not inside a word of comfort to the humans —
the first hint of hope in the Bible is delivered as part of a curse on someone else. *Protoevangelium*,
"first gospel", is the name Christian tradition gave it; the term is Irenaean in substance and
became standard in Reformation-era exegesis.

**What the Hebrew says.** The verb in both halves is *šûp*, one word used twice, which WEB renders
*bruise* both times. So the asymmetry is not in the verbs but in the anatomy: a blow to the head
kills, a blow to the heel wounds. *Zeraʿ* ("offspring", "seed") is a collective noun that can mean
one descendant or a whole line, and Genesis will keep exploiting that ambiguity — the promised
*seed* of Abraham runs through the rest of the book.

**The plain reading.** Taken at ground level the verse is an etiology: it explains why humans and
snakes are enemies, why snakes go on their bellies, and why the encounter tends to end with a
heel and a crushed head. Jewish interpretation has generally read it this way or as a moral
allegory of the struggle with the evil inclination, and has not treated it as messianic.

**The messianic reading and where it comes from.** Three things push toward it.

1. **The Septuagint.** *Sperma* ("offspring") is neuter, so Greek grammar calls for the neuter
   pronoun *auto*. The LXX writes the masculine **αὐτός** (*autos*), "**he** will bruise your
   head" — a deliberate departure, centuries before the New Testament, that individualizes the
   offspring.
2. **"Her offspring."** Genealogy in Genesis runs through men; *the woman's* seed is an unusual
   phrase, and readers from Irenaeus on have connected it with Galatians 4:4 — *"God sent out his
   Son, born of a woman"* — as pointing to a descendant reckoned through the mother.
3. **The New Testament's use of the imagery.** Romans 16:20 promises that *"the God of peace will
   quickly crush Satan under your feet"* — the verb *syntribō*, the picture of 3:15, applied to
   the church rather than to one man. Hebrews 2:14 has Christ sharing flesh and blood *"that
   through death he might bring to nothing him who had the power of death"*. 1 John 3:8: *"that he
   might destroy the works of the devil."* Revelation 12 stages the whole verse as a drama — a
   woman, her child, and *"the old serpent"* who fails to devour him and then makes war on *"the
   rest of her offspring"* (12:17).

**The heel and the head, read at the cross.** The classic Christian synthesis takes the two blows
as one event: the crucifixion is the wound to the heel and the defeat of the serpent in the same
act. It is the reading that gives the verse its weight in Christian theology, and it is a reading
*back* from the New Testament, not something a first-time reader of Genesis 3 would derive.

**One translation to set aside.** The Vulgate has *ipsa conteret caput tuum* — "*she* shall crush
your head" — a feminine pronoun with no support in the Hebrew or the Greek, most likely a scribal
slip that hardened into tradition. It is the source of the Marian iconography of a woman standing
on a serpent, and modern Catholic translations no longer follow it.

**The next verse the seed shows up in.** Genesis 4:1 has Eve say, over her firstborn, *"I have
gotten a man with Yahweh's help"* — the first birth after the promise of an offspring, announced
with God credited for it. The Hebrew (*qānîtî ʾîš ʾet-YHWH*) is difficult enough that the Targums
and Luther could read it as a claim about *who* the child is, and a strand of interpretation from
antiquity onward hears Eve expecting this son to be the one 3:15 spoke of. Genesis then does two
things with that expectation inside sixteen verses: the candidate becomes the first murderer, and
the only other candidate is his victim. Whether or not 4:1 is meant to sound like 3:15, the
narrative sequence — promise, birth, fratricide — is the pattern the rest of Genesis runs on,
where the line is repeatedly threatened by the family carrying it. It resumes at 4:25, where Seth
is given *"instead of Abel"*, and the *toledot* of chapter 5 follow **his** line rather than
Cain's. See [[Cain and Abel]].

## Significance

This is where the Bible's largest plot line is first traceable: from the promise of enmity here,
through the narrowing of *the seed* to Abraham, Judah, and David, to the New Testament's claim
that it terminates in one person, and out again in Revelation 12 to everyone who belongs to him.
Whether Genesis 3:15 *means* all of that or *has been read to mean* it is exactly the kind of
question this wiki should keep visible rather than settle — both claims are recorded above with
what supports them.
