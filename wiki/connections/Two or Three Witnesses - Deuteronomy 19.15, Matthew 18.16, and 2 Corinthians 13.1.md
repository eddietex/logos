## Analysis

**The rule widens in three steps.** Numbers 35:30 states it for murder. Deuteronomy 17:6 states it for a
capital charge of worshipping other gods. Deuteronomy 19:15 makes it general: *"any iniquity, or … any sin"*.
By 19:15 it is no longer a rule for one crime. It is how any charge is proved. Deuteronomy 19:16–19 then adds
what the earlier texts lacked, a penalty for the witness who lies: he receives what he *"had thought to do to
his brother"*.

**The New Testament quotes the general form.** Matthew 18:16 and 2 Corinthians 13:1 both have *"every word
may be established"* / *"shall every word be established"*, the closing clause of 19:15, *"shall a matter be
established"*. In WEB the English differs only in *word* for *matter*. The Hebrew *dābār* (19:15, WLC) means
both, so the change may only be translation. Paul marks his words as a quotation. 1 Timothy 5:19 and Hebrews
10:28 have *"two or three witnesses"* without the clause; Hebrews' *"dies"* matches 17:6 better than 19:15.
The wiki has not checked the Greek of any of these verses or the Septuagint of Deuteronomy, so it makes no
claim about which form of the text they quote.

**What the New Testament uses it for.** None of the uses is a criminal trial. In Matthew 18 the witnesses are
brought into a dispute between brothers after a private rebuke has failed, before the matter goes to *"the
assembly"*. In 2 Corinthians 13 Paul applies it to his own third visit; whether he means his visits as the
witnesses, or that charges will be tested by witnesses when he comes, is debated, and the wiki adopts neither.
1 Timothy 5:19 applies it to accusations against elders. John 8:17 cites the law's two-witness principle for
Jesus' testimony about himself, with the Father as the second witness (8:18, not quoted here). The rule moves
from the court to the church, and in each case it still guards against a single accuser.

**Two witnesses are not enough if they are lying.** The rule's weakness is the case 19:16–19 was written
for, and Scripture records it. Jezebel *"set two men, wicked fellows"* against Naboth (1 Kings 21:10) because
the law required two. Naboth had refused to give up *"the inheritance of my fathers"* (21:3), so the same
narrative breaks both 19:14 and 19:15–19. He is executed, and the land taken. At Jesus' trial the council,
in Matthew's account, finds no testimony *"even though many false witnesses came forward"*, until *"at last
two false witnesses came forward"* (26:60). Mark says instead that *"their testimony didn't agree"* (14:56,
59). The two Gospels are reporting the same hearing, and the wiki records that they describe the witnesses'
outcome differently; they are compared when the Gospels are ingested.

## Significance

The two-witness rule protects the accused from a single accuser. Deuteronomy 19:16–19 is needed because the
rule can be satisfied by two liars. The rule appears through the whole of Scripture and in the church's
discipline as well as Israel's courts. Both texts in which it is abused — Naboth's trial and Jesus' — end in
the death of an innocent man. See [[Judgment]], [[Deception]], and
[[A False Witness Shall Not Be Unpunished - Proverbs 19 and Exodus 23]].
