# Open Your Mouth for the Mute - Proverbs 31.8-9, Job 29, and Exodus 23

## Passages involved

- [[The Worthy Woman]] — Proverbs 31:20, 26.

- [[The Words of King Lemuel]] — Proverbs 31:5, 8–9.
- [[The Months of Old]] — Job 29:12–17.
- [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] — Job 31:13–23.
- [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]] — Exodus 23:1–9, especially 3 and 6.
- [[Where There Is No Vision]] — Proverbs 29:7, 14.

## Analysis

Lemuel’s mother makes advocacy a royal responsibility: the king must open his mouth for the
mute and serve justice to the poor and needy. The afflicted already have justice due to them
(31:5); the ruler can pervert it by forgetting the law. The command therefore goes beyond
spontaneous generosity to the reliable exercise of public authority.

Job remembers doing what the instruction requires. He delivered the poor and fatherless,
helped the blind and lame, and investigated a cause belonging to someone he did not know
(Job 29:12–17). His oath also denies dismissing a servant’s case or exploiting his influence
at the gate against the fatherless (31:13–23). These are thematic parallels, not evidence that
Job was Lemuel, that Job was a king, or that one passage quotes the other.

**The apparent tension with impartiality must be stated.** Exodus 23:3 forbids favoring a poor
person in a cause; 23:6 forbids denying justice to the poor. Proverbs 31:9 requires righteous
judgment, not a verdict manufactured out of pity. The duty is to ensure representation and
hear the case fairly; a poor litigant’s lack of influence is not permission to ignore the case,
and poverty alone is not proof of its merits.

Proverbs 29:7, 14 already connects righteous rule with attention to poor people’s causes.
Lemuel’s mother makes that general ideal a direct charge. Job’s later humiliation in 30:9–10
remains a counterexample to assuming the defender of the vulnerable will always be defended
in turn; it does not contradict an imperative that promises no guaranteed return.

### The worthy woman — Proverbs 31:20, 26

The poem following Lemuel’s instruction extends concern for the poor into a household.
The woman opens her arms to the needy (31:20) and opens her mouth with wisdom (31:26).
Giving and teaching answer the earlier unit’s speaking and judging without being identical
tasks: no court case or office is assigned to her. Job’s feeding and clothing of the needy
(Job 31:16–20) offers a further practical comparison. Generosity does not remove the ruler’s
obligation to secure justice.

## Significance

[[Mediation and Advocacy]] includes an earthly task: using access to secure a hearing for
someone without it. [[Judgment]] and [[Compassion and Mercy]] meet in that task without
abolishing impartiality. The passage gives [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] a claim on the
ruler’s attention, rather than making their hope depend on his mood.
