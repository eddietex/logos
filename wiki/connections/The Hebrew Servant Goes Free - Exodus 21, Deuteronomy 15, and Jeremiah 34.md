# The Hebrew Servant Goes Free - Exodus 21, Deuteronomy 15, and Jeremiah 34

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 21:2–11** — six years and release in the seventh; the wife and children; the ear bored at
  the door; the daughter who *"shall not go out as the male servants do"* —
  [[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]]
- **Deuteronomy 15:12–18** — the same six years, extended explicitly to *"a Hebrew woman"*, with
  provisions for the journey and the memory of Egypt as the motive *(not yet ingested)*
- **Leviticus 25:39–46** — the impoverished brother who is not to *"serve as a slave"*, released at
  the Year of Jubilee; foreign slaves held *"forever"* — [[The Poor Brother and Redemption from Service]]
- **Jeremiah 34:8–17** — Zedekiah's covenant of release, the freed servants taken back, and God's
  answering *"liberty … to the sword"* *(not yet ingested)*

## Analysis

**Four texts treat the release of Israelite servants, and they do not say the same thing.** The WEB
quotations below were fetched from bible-api.com (`translation=web`) on 2026-10-23. The Hebrew notes
are from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures `morphhb`).

| | Exodus 21 | Deuteronomy 15 | Leviticus 25 | Jeremiah 34 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **who** | *"a Hebrew servant"* (21:2) | *"your brother, a Hebrew man, or a Hebrew woman"* (15:12) | *"your brother"* who *"sells himself to you"* (25:39) | *"a Hebrew or a Hebrewess"* (34:9) |
| **term** | six years; out *"in the seventh"* | six years; free *"in the seventh year"* | *"until the Year of Jubilee"* (25:40) | *"At the end of seven years"* (34:14) |

**What the texts share is vocabulary.** *Ḥopšî*, *free*, is the release word of Exodus 21:2, 5 (and
21:26–27); Deuteronomy 15:12, 13, 18; and Jeremiah 34:9, 10, 11, 14, 16 (WLC lemma search). The awl,
*marṣēaʿ*, occurs in the Torah only at Exodus 21:6 and Deuteronomy 15:17. In the WEB, Jeremiah's
*"house of bondage"* is the phrase of Exodus 13:3 and 20:2; Jeremiah's Hebrew was not checked for
it. **And Jeremiah 34:14 cites the law** — *"At the end of seven years, every man of you shall
release his brother who is a Hebrew, who has been sold to you, and has served you six years"* — in
wording closer to Deuteronomy 15:12 than to Exodus 21:2, since it has *"sold to you"* and
*"brother"*. This is an observation about the English and the WLC words checked here, not a claim
about which text Jeremiah used.

**The tensions should be stated, not smoothed.**

1. **The woman.** Exodus 21:7 says the sold daughter *"shall not go out as the male servants do"*;
Deuteronomy 15:12, 17 gives the Hebrew woman the same release and the same ear-boring as the man,
and Jeremiah 34:9 frees *"a Hebrew or a Hebrewess"*. One reading holds that Exodus 21:7 concerns
only a daughter sold for marriage, whose protections in 21:8–11 replace the six-year rule. On that
reading the two laws address different women. Another holds that Deuteronomy extends to women what
Exodus withheld. The texts do not decide between these readings. 2. **For ever, or until the
Jubilee.** Exodus 21:6 and Deuteronomy 15:17 both say the servant who stays serves *"for ever"* (WLC
*ləʿōlām*, *ʿebed ʿôlām*). Leviticus 25:40–41 releases the Israelite in the Jubilee. It also forbids
treating him as a slave at all (25:39, 42) and reserves *"forever"* for foreign slaves (25:46).
Rabbinic tradition is often reported as reading *"for ever"* as *"until the Jubilee"*. That is a
later harmonization, reported here and not verified in this pass. Exodus does not make it. 3. **The
family.** Exodus 21:4 keeps a master-given wife and her children with the master; Leviticus 25:41
sends the man out *"he and his children with him"*. The cases may differ, since Leviticus does not
mention a master-given wife, but the outcomes for children do not match. 4. **The motive.** Exodus
21 gives no reason for the release. Deuteronomy and Leviticus both give the exodus as the reason,
though in different forms. In Deuteronomy, Israel remembers it was a slave. In Leviticus, God says
Israelites are already his servants and cannot be sold as slaves.

**Leviticus 25 now read in full.** Its table entries stand against stored WEB. The law also
adds a case the others lack: sale to a resident alien, with redemption by kin or by the man himself
(47–52) and release at Jubilee (54). It neither cites the six-year rule nor sets it aside. A Jubilee
term can be far longer than six years or far shorter, and the text does not relate the two. See
[[The Poor Brother and Redemption from Service]].

## Significance

**Jeremiah 34 is the law tested in history, and failed.** Zedekiah's covenant frees the Hebrew
servants; *"afterwards they turned"* and *"brought them into subjection"* again (34:11). God's
answer turns the release word against them: *"Behold, I proclaim to you a liberty … to the sword, to
the pestilence, and to the famine"* (34:17). The verb of the reversal is *ḥālal*: *"you turned and
profaned my name"* (34:16, WLC). It is the verb Exodus 20:25 uses for the altar a tool has
*"polluted"*. **The altar law and the release law sit side by side in Exodus 20:22–21:11, and
Jeremiah charges Judah with profanation in the matter of the second.** The pairing is an observation
from shared vocabulary; Jeremiah does not cite Exodus 20:25.

**The development is real but not a straight line.** Deuteronomy adds the woman, the provisions for
the journey, and the memory of Egypt. Leviticus goes further in one direction, since an Israelite
is not to be a slave at all, and stays behind in another, since foreigners may be held for ever.
None of the texts abolishes the institution. See [[Slavery and Servitude]], where the same tension
runs through the whole theme.
