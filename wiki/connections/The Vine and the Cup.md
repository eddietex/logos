# The Vine and the Cup

## Passages involved

- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — Genesis 9:20–21, the first vineyard and the first
  drunkenness
- Genesis 19:30–38 — Lot made drunk and uncovered
- Genesis 49:11–12 — Judah's blessing: *"He has washed his garments in wine… his eyes will be red with
  wine"*
- Numbers 6:1–4 — the Nazirite, separated from wine, vinegar, grapes, skins, and seeds
- Numbers 13:23 — the cluster from Eshcol carried on a pole between two men
- Deuteronomy 8:8; Psalm 104:15 — the vine as a mark of the good land, and wine that gladdens
- Proverbs 20:1; 23:29–35; 31:4–7 — wine the mocker, the drunkard, and the cup for the perishing
- Isaiah 5:1–7; Psalm 80:8–16 — Israel as the vineyard that yields wild grapes
- Isaiah 25:6 — the feast of *"well-aged wines"* on the mountain
- Jeremiah 25:15–29; Psalm 75:8; Revelation 14:10 — the cup of wrath, drunk to the dregs
- Isaiah 63:1–6; Revelation 14:19–20; 19:15 — the winepress trodden in judgment
- John 2:1–11 — water into wine at Cana, the first sign
- John 15:1–8 — *"I am the true vine"*
- Matthew 26:27–29 and parallels — *"this is my blood of the new covenant"*, and the vow not to drink
  again until the kingdom
- Ephesians 5:18 — *"Don't be drunken with wine… but be filled with the Spirit"*

## Analysis

Scripture never settles on a single verdict about wine, and the reason is that it uses the vine for
four different things. All four are already implicit in the first vineyard.

**Wine as gladness and as the sign of a good land.** Psalm 104:15 has wine *"that makes glad the heart
of man"* listed beside bread and oil as what the earth is for. Deuteronomy 8:8 names the vine among
the seven species that make Canaan worth having, and Numbers 13:23 has the spies carry a single
cluster from the valley of Eshcol slung on a pole between two men — the land advertising itself.
Genesis 9:20 is where this begins, and it begins in the right place: **Noah is named at 5:29 for
relief from the ground Yahweh cursed**, and the first thing the relieved ground gives him is wine. On
the good reading, the comfort his father hoped for arrives as a crop. See
[[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]].

**Wine as stupor and exposure.** Then the same verse's crop lays him out uncovered in his tent, and
the Bible's first vineyard produces its first drunkenness eleven Hebrew words later. The pairing of
drink with shameful exposure recurs deliberately: Lot in the cave (Genesis 19:33, 35), Habakkuk 2:15's
woe on *"him who gives his neighbor drink… that you may gaze at their naked bodies"*, Lamentations
4:21's Edom *"drunken, and… naked"*, and Nahum 3:5. Proverbs 23:29–35 is the extended portrait, and it
ends with the drunkard planning the next one. Whatever Genesis 9 declines to say about Noah, the rest
of the canon is not neutral about the state he was in.

**Wine as wrath.** From the stupor the prophets build the Bible's most violent image. If drunkenness
is a loss of control the drinker cannot escape, then a cup handed to a nation is a judgment it cannot
put down: *"Take this cup of the wine of wrath at my hand, and cause all the nations… to drink it"*
(Jeremiah 25:15). Psalm 75:8, Isaiah 51:17, and Revelation 14:10 keep the figure exactly. Its
counterpart is the **winepress**, where the wine is the blood of those trodden (Isaiah 63:3;
Revelation 14:19–20; 19:15). It is a short distance from a man face down in a tent to the treading of
the winepress of the wrath of God, and the route runs through the loss of self-command.

**Wine as covenant and consummation.** And then the figure turns over. Israel is itself the vineyard —
planted, walled, and yielding wild grapes (Isaiah 5:1–7; Psalm 80:8–16) — and the failure of the crop
is the indictment. Jesus takes the image and closes it: *"I am the true vine"* (John 15:1), the
vineyard that finally bears. His first sign is a hundred and twenty gallons of good wine at a wedding
that had run out (John 2:1–11), which is Isaiah 25:6's mountain feast arriving early and quietly; and
at the last supper the cup is his blood *"of the new covenant"* (Matthew 26:28), with a vow attached
not to drink of the fruit of the vine again *"until that day when I drink it anew with you in my
Father's Kingdom"*. The cup of wrath and the cup of the covenant are the same cup, and Gethsemane's
prayer — *"let this cup pass away from me"* (Matthew 26:39) — is what joins them.

**The counter-current.** Running against all of it is a strand of deliberate abstention: the Nazirite
vow, which bars not just wine but vinegar, grapes, skins, and seeds (Numbers 6:3–4); the Rechabites,
commended for keeping their ancestor's rule (Jeremiah 35); the priests forbidden wine on duty
(Leviticus 10:9, immediately after Nadab and Abihu); and Proverbs 31:4–7, which reserves strong drink
for the perishing and forbids it to kings *"lest they drink, and forget the law"*. Ephesians 5:18 does
not prohibit the cup but names the alternative to being filled by it.

## Significance

The thread's use is that it keeps two readings of Genesis 9:20–21 from cancelling each other. The
vineyard is not a mistake — Scripture will not let it be one, since the vine is a blessing of the land
and the figure of the Messiah's own life. And the drunkenness is not neutral — Scripture will not let
that go either, since it makes the loss of self-command the master image of divine judgment.

Genesis reports both in one sentence and comments on neither. That is the pattern the wiki keeps
finding in the primeval history: the origin of a good thing and the first abuse of it recorded
together, without adjudication, in the same verse that names the craft. The forging of bronze and iron
sits four verses from the Song of the Sword (4:22–24); the first city is built by a man sentenced to
wander (4:17); and the first vineyard is planted by the only man in the book called blameless. See
[[City and Civilization]].
