# Now Let Me Die, Since I Have Seen - Jacob and Simeon

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 46:30** — [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]]. *"Israel said to Joseph, 'Now let me die,
  since I have seen your face, that you are still alive.'"*
- **Luke 2:25–32** — Simeon in the temple, holding the child: now you are releasing your servant in
  peace, for my eyes have seen your salvation.
- **Genesis 45:28** — [[Joseph Reveals Himself]]. *"It is enough. Joseph my son is still alive. I will
  go and see him before I die."*

## Analysis

**Two old men say the same thing in the same shape: I have seen it, and now I can go.**

| | [[Jacob]], Genesis 46:30 | Simeon, Luke 2:29–30 |
|---|---|---|
| **the release** | *"Now let me die"* | *now you are releasing your servant… in peace* |
| **the ground** | *"since I have seen your face"* | *for my eyes have seen your salvation* |
| **the object** | a son believed dead for twenty-two years | a child six weeks old |
| **what follows** | he lives another seventeen years (47:28) | Luke does not report his death |

**Neither man dies in his scene, and that is not an oversight in either book.** **The sentence is not
a request to expire; it is a statement that the thing worth staying alive for has now happened.**
**Hebrew and Greek both use the language of discharge** — a servant released, a watch ended — and in
both cases the speaker has been waiting a long time on the strength of something he was told.

### Jacob's version has a history in his own mouth

**He said the first half of it two verses and one journey earlier**: *"It is enough. Joseph my son is
still alive. I will go and see him before I die"* (45:28). **At 46:30 the clause comes back
inverted** — **not *before I die* but *now let me die*** — **and the difference between them is the
three hundred miles and the face.** **Genesis lets him say it twice and marks the completion by the
word order.**

**And there is a third version behind both**, the one he had been saying since chapter 37: *"I will go
down to Sheol to my son mourning"* (37:35), repeated at 42:38 and 44:29. **He has announced his own
death four times in the Joseph cycle.** **The first three were grief and the fourth is release**, and
**the only thing that changed was that he saw something.** See [[Sheol]], [[Hope]] and
[[Seeing and Being Seen]].

### What each man is looking at

**The asymmetry is the interesting part.** **Simeon is holding an infant who has done nothing, and he
calls him *salvation* on the strength of a promise.** **Jacob is looking at a grown man in Egyptian
office who has fed the world through a famine, and he calls him *alive*.** **Simeon sees a future;
Jacob sees a fact.**

**Luke is not quoting Genesis and the wiki does not claim that he is.** **What the two passages share
is a form** — the aged waiter, the single act of sight, the dismissal — **which is common enough in
the ancient world to be a topos and pointed enough in these two cases to be worth setting side by
side.** **Luke's Simeon is *waiting for the consolation of Israel*, and the consolation of Israel in
Genesis 46 is a cart, a road and a son.**

### The verb that carries it

***Rāʾâ***, *to see*, runs through the whole reunion. ***"He presented himself to him"*** (46:29) is
***wayyērāʾ ʾēlāyw***, the Niphal of the same root — **the form that everywhere else in Genesis has
God as its subject.** **Then *"since I have seen your face"*** (46:30). **Two verses, two uses of
*see*, and the second is the one that ends a man's waiting.** Luke's Simeon makes the same word do the
same work: *my eyes have seen*.

## Significance

**The pattern the wiki draws out is that Scripture repeatedly makes sight, not report, the thing that
settles a matter.** **[[Jacob]] was told the truth at 45:26 and *"his heart fainted, for he didn't
believe them"*; he believed the wagons** (45:27); **and he was released by the face** (46:30). **Three
stages, and testimony is the one that failed.** See
[[A Garment Used as Evidence - The Coat and the Cloak]] and [[Faith]].

**It also marks the end of a plot.** **From 37:35 onward Jacob's death has been the Joseph cycle's
recurring threat** — the thing Judah's speech at 44:29–31 is built to prevent, the thing Joseph's
message at 45:9 is meant to outrun. **At 46:30 the threat is dismissed by the man it hangs over**, and
Genesis then gives him seventeen more years, two blessings and a funeral procession that leaves Egypt
with chariots and horsemen. **The release is granted and not taken up**, which is also what happens to
Simeon. See [[Life and Death]].
