# The Way of the Wind - Ecclesiastes 11.5 and John 3.8

## Passages involved

- [[Cast Your Bread on the Waters]] — **Ecclesiastes 11:5** — *"As you don’t know what is the way
  of the wind, nor how the bones grow in the womb of her who is with child; even so you don’t know
  the work of God who does all."*
- [[Vanity of Vanities]] — **Ecclesiastes 1:6** — *"The wind goes toward the south, and turns
  around to the north. It turns around continually as it goes, and the wind returns again to its
  courses."*
- **John 3:8** — *"The wind blows where it wants to, and you hear its sound, but don’t know where
  it comes from and where it is going. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit.”* Verified WEB,
  not yet ingested.

## Analysis

**The same comparison.** Both Ecclesiastes 11:5 and John 3:8 use the wind as an example of
something whose movement human beings cannot trace, and both apply it to something hidden. In
Ecclesiastes the hidden thing is *"the work of God who does all"*. In John it is the person
*"born of the Spirit"*. Both also join wind to birth: Ecclesiastes sets the wind beside the
bones in the womb, and John sets it in a conversation about being born.

**Wind and spirit.** In Ecclesiastes 11:5 the Hebrew word is *rûaḥ* (Sefaria, *Miqra according
to the Masorah*, read 2026-10-27), which can mean *wind*, *breath*, or *spirit*. The WEB
translates it as *wind*. The WEB of John 3:8 has both *wind* and *Spirit* in one verse. It is
commonly said that the Greek uses the same word, *pneuma*, for both, matching the range of
*rûaḥ*; that claim was **not checked against a Greek edition** in this pass and is recorded
here only as a common observation.

**Different conclusions.** Ecclesiastes 1:6 describes the wind's course as a circuit that
returns again and again. 11:5 says that the way of the wind is unknown. Ecclesiastes draws from
that ignorance a practical command: sow morning and evening, because you do not know which will
prosper (11:6). John 3:8 draws something else: the new birth is real and its effects can be
heard, even though its origin and direction cannot be seen. No literary dependence of John on
Ecclesiastes is claimed. The wind was an ordinary example of the invisible, available to both.

## Significance

The connection shows one image serving two purposes. In Ecclesiastes the unknown way of the
wind limits human knowledge and so makes humble, persistent work the sensible course. In John
the same unknown way describes the freedom of the Spirit. See [[Providence]], [[Creation]], and
[[Wisdom]].
