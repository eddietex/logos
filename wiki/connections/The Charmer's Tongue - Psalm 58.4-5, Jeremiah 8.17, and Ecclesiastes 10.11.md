# The Charmer's Tongue - Psalm 58.4-5, Jeremiah 8.17, and Ecclesiastes 10.11

## Passages involved

- [[Folly in High Places and Everyday Work]] — **Ecclesiastes 10:11** — *"If the snake bites before
  it is charmed, then is there no profit for the charmer’s tongue."*
- **Psalm 58:4–5** — *"Their poison is like the poison of a snake; like a deaf cobra that stops its
  ear, which doesn’t listen to the voice of charmers, no matter how skillful the charmer may be."*
  Verified WEB, not yet ingested.
- **Jeremiah 8:17** — *"“For, behold, I will send serpents, adders among you, which will not be
  charmed; and they will bite you,” says Yahweh."* Verified WEB, not yet ingested.

## Analysis

**A known craft.** All three texts take snake charming for granted as a recognized skill. None
describes the technique, and none comments on whether the practice is legitimate. Each uses the
craft for the moment when it fails.

**Three failures.** In Psalm 58 the snake is deaf: it *"stops its ear"* and will not listen, however
skillful the charmer. The image describes the wicked, who *"go astray from the womb"* (58:3) and
refuse to hear. In Jeremiah 8:17 the serpents *"will not be charmed"* because Yahweh sends them
as judgment; no skill can turn them aside. In Ecclesiastes 10:11 nothing is wrong with the snake or the charmer — he is simply too
late. The snake bites *before* it is charmed, and his skill earns him nothing.

**Ecclesiastes' version is about timing.** The verse follows the blunt ax of 10:10, where skill
brings success. Together they make one point: wisdom yields an advantage only when it is used in
time. In the Hebrew read in MAM, *yiṯrôn*, *advantage* or *profit*, appears in both 10:10 and
10:11 — the *gain* the book asks about in 1:3. The charmer is literally *baʿal hallāšôn*, *the
master of the tongue*, and the verse sets *nāḥāš* (*snake*) beside *lāḥaš* (*charm*). The Psalm and
Jeremiah, by contrast, are about a will that cannot be moved — the wicked's, or God's.

## Significance

The motif links [[Wisdom]] and [[Work]]: craft and skill are real goods, and still subject to
circumstance. It also touches [[Language and Speech]], since the charmer's tool is his voice — a
point Ecclesiastes 10:12–14 takes up at once with the words of the wise and the fool.
