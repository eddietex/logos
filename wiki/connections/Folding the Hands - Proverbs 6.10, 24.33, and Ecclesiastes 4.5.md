# Folding the Hands - Proverbs 6.10, 24.33, and Ecclesiastes 4.5

## Passages involved

- **Proverbs 6:10–11** — *"A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep:
  so your poverty will come as a robber, and your scarcity as an armed man."* —
  [[The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations]]
- **Proverbs 24:33–34** — *"a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep;
  so your poverty will come as a robber, and your want as an armed man."* —
  [[The Field of the Sluggard]]
- **Ecclesiastes 4:5** — *"The fool folds his hands together and ruins himself."* —
  [[Two Are Better Than One]]

## Analysis

**One gesture in three places.** Proverbs gives the same couplet twice, once as a direct address to
the sluggard sent to the ant (6:6–11), once as the lesson drawn from a ruined vineyard (24:30–34).
Ecclesiastes 4:5 uses the gesture in a single line. In Hebrew the noun of Proverbs, *ḥibbuq yāḏayim*,
and the participle of Ecclesiastes, *ḥōḇēq ʾeṯ-yāḏāyw*, share the root *ḥbq*, *to fold* or *embrace*
(Hebrew read in Mechon-Mamre's Masoretic text of Proverbs 6, Proverbs 24, and Ecclesiastes 4,
2026-10-27). The shared root establishes a common image; it does not by itself prove that one text
quotes the other.

**Different consequences.** In Proverbs the consequence comes from outside: poverty arrives *"as a
robber"* and want *"as an armed man"*. In Ecclesiastes the damage is internal — the WEB's *"ruins
himself"* renders a phrase that reads literally *"eats his own flesh"* (*ʾōḵēl ʾeṯ-bəśārô*, same
Mechon-Mamre text). Proverbs pictures loss by intrusion; Ecclesiastes pictures self-consumption.

**Different settings.** In Proverbs the folded hands stand alone as a warning. In Ecclesiastes the
line is flanked by a verdict on envious labor (4:4) and a saying that a handful with quietness is
better than two handfuls with labor (4:6). The Preacher thus rejects idleness without endorsing the
opposite extreme; Proverbs, in these two passages, addresses only the first danger. Both books keep
the distinction between the idle person and the oppressed poor: Ecclesiastes 4:1 has just described
victims of power, and Proverbs elsewhere warns against exploiting the poor (22:22). Neither passage on
folded hands is a general explanation of poverty.

## Significance

The image ties the sluggard of Proverbs to the Preacher's inquiry into labor, and shows Ecclesiastes
using a wisdom commonplace while placing it inside a larger question about how much work is enough.
It connects [[Work]], [[Folly]], and [[Wealth and Possessions]].
