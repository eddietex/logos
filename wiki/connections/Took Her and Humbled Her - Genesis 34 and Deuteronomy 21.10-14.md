# Took Her and Humbled Her - Genesis 34 and Deuteronomy 21.10-14

## Passages involved

- [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] — **Genesis 34:2** — *"Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite,
  the prince of the land, saw her. He took her, lay with her, and humbled her."*
- **Genesis 34:4, 8** — *"Get me this young lady as a wife"*; *"The soul of my son, Shechem, longs for
  your daughter. Please give her to him as a wife."*
- [[Captives, Firstborn Sons, and Public Shame]] — **Deuteronomy 21:11** — *"and see among the captives
  a beautiful woman, and you have a desire to her, and desire to take her as your wife"*
- **Deuteronomy 21:14** — *"You shall not deal with her as a slave, because you have humbled her."*
- Compare **Deuteronomy 22:29** — [[Marriage Accusations and Sexual Offenses]]: *"She shall be his wife, because he has
  humbled her. He may not put her away all his days."*
- Compare **Deuteronomy 7:7; 10:15** — the same verb of desire used of Yahweh's choice of Israel and of
  the fathers

All quotations from stored WEB.

## Analysis

**The same four words, in the same order of events.** A man *sees* a woman (*rāʾâ*: Genesis 34:2;
Deuteronomy 21:11), *desires* her (*ḥāšaq*: Genesis 34:8; Deuteronomy 21:11), *takes* her *as a wife*
(*lǝʾiššâ*, *as a wife*: Hamor's request at Genesis 34:8, the soldier's intention at Deuteronomy 21:11;
WEB has *"as a wife"* at 34:4 too), and
has *humbled* her (*ʿinnâ*: Genesis 34:2; Deuteronomy 21:14) (WLC, checked for Genesis 34:2, 8 and
Deuteronomy 21:11, 14). *Ḥāšaq* is rare: in the Torah it is used of a person only in Genesis 34:8,
Deuteronomy 7:7, 10:15, and 21:11 (WLC lemma 2836). Genesis 34 and Deuteronomy 21:10–14 are the only two
places in the Torah where it names a man's desire for a woman.

**What differs is the order and the setting.** In Genesis the humbling comes first — *"took her, lay with
her, and humbled her"* — and the desire and the request for marriage follow. In Deuteronomy the
humbling is named last, as something already done when the marriage ends. Shechem is a prince in his own
land acting on a girl of a visiting family; Deuteronomy's soldier has taken the woman in war, and the law
places a month of mourning in his house before the marriage. Genesis has no law to apply and ends in a
massacre; Deuteronomy has a law, and its whole content is restraint on the man who holds the power.

**What the law gives that Genesis did not.** Dinah is never asked, never quoted, and never heard from
again after Genesis 34. The captive of Deuteronomy is not asked either, but she is given a month, grief
for her father and mother, and, if she is dismissed, the right to go *"where she desires"* rather than be
sold. The word *ʿinnâ* in 21:14 is the ground of that protection: because the man has humbled her, he
has lost the right to treat her as property. Deuteronomy 22:29 applies the same reasoning in the other
direction: the man who has humbled an unbetrothed girl must marry her and may never send her away.

**What the connection does not claim.** Neither text mentions the other. The wiki does not claim that
Deuteronomy was written with Genesis 34 in view, and it does not read Genesis 34 as a case under the law.
The correspondence is in the vocabulary and the sequence. What it shows is that the Torah has one set of
words for a man who sees, desires, takes, and humbles a woman, and that it uses them both in narrative
that condemns nothing aloud and in law that limits what the man may do afterward. See [[Marriage]] and
[[Sexual Sin]].

**The verb of desire is also the verb of election.** *Ḥāšaq* is the word Moses uses when he says
*"Yahweh didn't set his love on you … because you were more in number"* (7:7) and *"Yahweh had a delight
in your fathers to love them"* (10:15). The text sets no boundary between the senses, and neither does
this page; it records that the same verb is used of Shechem, of the soldier, and of God.

### Deuteronomy 22:24, 29 now ingested

*ʿInnâ* returns twice in [[Marriage Accusations and Sexual Offenses]]: the man who lies with a pledged woman in the city *"has humbled his
neighbor's wife"* (22:24), and the man who seizes an unpledged virgin must marry her *"because he has
humbled her"* (22:29). A lemma search of Deuteronomy (6031) finds the piel at 21:14; 22:24, 29, and also
of Yahweh, who *"humbled you"* in the wilderness (8:2, 3, 16), and of the Egyptians who *"afflicted us"*
(26:6) (WLC; stored WEB). In the law of 22:29 the verb grounds an obligation, as at 21:14: there the man
may not sell the woman he humbled, here he may not send her away.

## Significance

The pair shows how the Torah handles a situation it does not forbid outright: Genesis tells a story in
which the same acts end in vengeance, and Deuteronomy writes a law in which the same acts end in a woman
set free rather than sold. Neither text says the acts are good. See [[Dinah]] and
[[Shechem (son of Hamor)]].
