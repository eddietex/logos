# A Wild Donkey of a Man - Genesis 16.12 and Job 11.12

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 16:12** — the angel to Hagar: *"He will be like a wild donkey among men"* — *pereʾ ʾādām*
  — [[Hagar and Ishmael]]
- **Job 11:12** — Zophar: *"An empty-headed man becomes wise when a man is born as a wild donkey's
  colt"* — *ʿayir pereʾ ʾādām* — [[Zophar's First Speech]]
- **Job 6:5** — Job: *"Does the wild donkey bray when he has grass?"* — [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]
- **Job 24:5** — the dispossessed poor, *"as wild donkeys in the desert, they go out to their work"*
  — [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]]
- **Job 30:3–7** — the outcasts who mock Job, eating salt herbs in the waste and *"Among the bushes they
  bray"* — *nāhaq*, the verb of 6:5, and its only other occurrence — [[Now They Mock Me]]
- **Job 39:5–8** — God: *"Who has set the wild donkey free? … He scorns the tumult of the city,
  neither does he hear the shouting of the driver"* — [[Out of the Whirlwind]]
- **Jeremiah 2:24; Hosea 8:9** — Israel as a wild donkey in heat, and *"a wild donkey wandering
  alone"*

## Analysis

***Pereʾ ʾādām* occurs twice in the Bible**, and the two uses could hardly be further apart. In
Genesis it is an oracle given to a slave about her unborn son, and the wiki has argued on
[[Ishmael]] that its weight falls on freedom: the *pereʾ* is the animal that cannot be enslaved,
promised to the child of a woman who has just been told to go back to her mistress. In Job it is the
punchline of an insult. Zophar's proverb sets two impossibilities side by side — a hollow man getting
sense, a wild donkey giving birth to a man — and the second guarantees the first will never happen.

**The animal is the same in both.** What makes the *pereʾ* proverbial is that it cannot be made to
do anything. Zophar uses that as stupidity — the untrainable beast — where Genesis uses it as
liberty, and God, in Job 39, uses it as glory: *"Who has set the wild donkey free?"* — **God claims
the creature's untameability as his own gift to it**, gives it the wilderness for a home, and admires
it for scorning the city and the driver. The animal Zophar names as the type of the incorrigible is
the one God names as the type of the free.

**Job's donkey is a third use.** At 6:5 it is a complaint: an animal brays only when it lacks
pasture, so a cry is evidence of need. Job is defending his own loudness by appeal to the beast, and
Zophar's opening at 11:2–3 — *"the multitude of words"*, *"your boastings"* — answers exactly that
loudness. Whether or not Zophar meant to pick up Job's animal, the book sets the two side by side
six chapters apart.

**And in chapter 24 the image falls on the poor.** *"As wild donkeys in the desert, they go out to
their work, seeking diligently for food"* (24:5) — the people driven off their land live like the
*pereʾ*, not because they are free but because they have been made homeless. It is the bleakest use,
and it is Job's.

## Significance

- One animal carries four valuations within Scripture's wisdom and narrative — liberty (Genesis 16),
  need (Job 6), incorrigibility (Job 11), dispossession (Job 24) — and God's own word on it (Job 39)
  sides with the first.
- It is a small instance of the book's larger pattern: the friends' proverbs are drawn from the same
  world God's speeches are, and God's speeches see that world differently. Zophar looks at the wild
  donkey and sees a fool; God looks at it and sees something he set free.
- For Genesis, it supports the reading of 16:12 as something other than a curse. The only other
  *pereʾ ʾādām* is an insult, but the animal itself, in the mouth of God, is a boast.
