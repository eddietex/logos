## Analysis

**Proverbs 17:17 sets the friend and the brother side by side, and the wisdom collection does not
keep them in one order.** The friend (*rēaʿ*) loves *bəkol-ʿēt*, *at every time*; the brother is born
*ləṣārâ*, *for distress*. Read as a synonymous couplet, the brother is what the friend becomes in
trouble; read as a progression, kinship is proved by trouble the way friendship is proved by time.
The Hebrew supports both and this page does not choose. Proverbs then complicates the brother: 18:24
has a lover *dābēq mēʾāḥ*, *clinging closer than a brother* — *dābēq*, the verb of the man who
*"will join with his wife"* (Genesis 2:24, *wədābaq*) and of Ruth who *"joined with"* Naomi (Ruth 1:14,
*dābqâ*) — and 27:10 tells the son not to go to his brother's house *bəyôm ʾêdekā*, *in the day of
your disaster*, because a near neighbour is better. *ʾÊd*, *disaster*, is the calamity of 17:5, at
which the gloater rejoices. The book holds both claims: the brother is born for adversity, and in
adversity the brother may be the wrong door. Chapter 18 adds a third claim, five verses before its
friend: *"A brother offended is more difficult than a fortified city"* (18:19). The chapter's brother
can be the one who will not be reached, and its friend the one who clings; Genesis twice shows the
offended brother reached anyway (Genesis 33:4; 50:17). See [[A Strong Tower]]. (Hebrew of Proverbs
17:17; 18:19, 24; 27:10; Genesis 2:24; 50:17; Ruth 1:14 read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the
Masorah*, 2026-10-25.)

**Job is the wiki's test of the saying, and the saying fails in his friends.** They came to comfort
him (2:11). By chapter 6 he is asking for exactly what Proverbs 17:17 promises — *ḥesed*,
*kindness*, *mērēʿēhû*, *from his friend*, the same noun *rēaʿ* (Hebrew from Sefaria) — and saying he
has not received it: *"My brothers have dealt deceitfully as a brook"* (6:15) — brooks swollen with
ice and snow that *"In the dry season … vanish"*, so that the caravans that counted on them perish
(6:16–18). Chapter 19 lists the brothers, acquaintances, relatives, and
*"familiar friends"* who have gone, and ends in the plea *"Have pity on me … you my friends"* (19:21).
The friends were not absent; they spoke through most of the book. What failed was the love *at all
times* — they could not keep loving a man they had decided was guilty.

**The ending reverses the direction.** At 42:10 the restoration comes *"when he prayed for his
friends"*: the one who was owed kindness gives it, by intercession, to the men who withheld it. And
42:11 brings back the *brothers* and *sisters* who had been estranged at 19:13, to eat bread and
comfort him — after the adversity, not during it. Proverbs 17:17's brother born for adversity
arrives late.

**Jonathan is the narrative instance the wiki has not yet reached.** 1 Samuel 18 has the friend who
loves David *"as his own soul"* (18:1, 3; WEB at bible-api.com), across the line of a father's
enmity; the friendship will be read in full when [[David]]'s story is ingested.

### Proverbs 19 in context

Proverbs 19:4, 6–7 shows friends multiplied by wealth and gifts, then absent when the poor person
pleads. This is the social opposite of 17:17's constancy and 18:24's clinging friend. The remedy
supplied within chapter 19 is pity for the poor, valued by Yahweh (17), not advice to acquire wealth
to become lovable. The comparison is ethical and thematic. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 17:17; 18:24;
19:4, 6–7, 17.

### Proverbs 20 in context

Proverbs 20:6 distinguishes many claims to unfailing love from the difficulty of finding a
faithful person. It does not say that no faithful person exists: the question makes constancy
something to establish rather than accept on assertion. Verse 19 adds a concrete test of
companionship, avoiding the teller of secrets; verse 28 makes love and faithfulness sustaining
qualities of kingship too. This qualifies the ideal of 17:17 from within Proverbs itself,
without replacing the duty of loyalty with distrust of everyone.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Sources: stored WEB Proverbs 20 and the local comparisons documented on that
passage page; no new source-language claim.

### Proverbs 27 in context

Chapter 27 gathers the book's friendship sayings more densely than any chapter since 17–19. Its friend
is proved by what he is willing to cost: *"Better is open rebuke than hidden love"* (5), and *"Faithful
are the wounds of a friend; although the kisses of an enemy are profuse"* (6), where the enemy is
*śônēʾ*, *one who hates*, the disguised hater of 26:24. The friend's counsel is sweet (9); the father's
friend is not to be forsaken (10); and *"Iron sharpens iron"* (17) makes friendship a friction that
improves both. Verse 10's near neighbour over the distant brother is the saying already set against
17:17 above; its Hebrew has a written-and-read variant on *your father's friend* (written *wrʿh*, read
*wərēaʿ*). Job's friends, who wounded him, are the test case the wiki keeps in view: Proverbs 27:6
praises the wound that is faithful, and Job's complaint was that theirs were not. See
[[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow]]. Sources: stored WEB Proverbs 26:24; 27; Hebrew from Sefaria's *Miqra
according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-26.

## Significance

Proverbs makes friendship a matter of constancy rather than feeling, and keeps it next to the
sayings about speech: the friend is lost by repetition (17:9) and whispering (16:28), kept by
covering. Job shows what it costs when the friends' speech, not their absence, is the failure. See
[[Love]], [[Brotherhood]], [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]], and
[[Shunned by His Neighbor - Proverbs 14.20 and Job 19.13-19]].
