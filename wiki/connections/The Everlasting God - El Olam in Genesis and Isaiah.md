# The Everlasting God - El Olam in Genesis and Isaiah

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 21:33** — *"Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and called there on the name of
  Yahweh, the Everlasting God."* *ʾĒl ʿÔlām*, and the phrase's only occurrence in the Hebrew Bible.
  See [[The Treaty at Beersheba]].
- **Genesis 14:18–22** — *ʾĒl ʿElyôn*, God Most High, from Melchizedek and then from Abram. See
  [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].
- **Genesis 16:13** — *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, the God who sees, named by Hagar. See [[Hagar and Ishmael]].
- **Genesis 17:1** — *ʾĒl Šadday*, God Almighty, from God of himself. See
  [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] and
  [[God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus]].
- **Genesis 17:7, 8, 13, 19** — *bərît ʿôlām* and *ʾăḥuzzat ʿôlām*: an **everlasting** covenant, an
  **everlasting** possession, four times in one chapter.
- **Genesis 33:20; 35:7** — *ʾĒl ʾĕlōhê Yiśrāʾēl* and *ʾĒl Bêt-ʾĒl*, Jacob's two.
- **Isaiah 40:28** — *"Haven't you known? Haven't you heard? The everlasting God, Yahweh, the Creator
  of the ends of the earth, doesn't faint. He isn't weary."*
- **Psalm 90:2; 93:2; 103:17** — *"from everlasting to everlasting, you are God."*
- **Romans 16:26; 1 Timothy 1:17** — *tou aiōniou theou*, the eternal God, in doxology.

## Analysis

### What ʿôlām means, and what it does not

***ʿÔlām* is not the Greek philosophical *eternity*.** It is a noun of duration — time running out of
sight, past the point where anyone can see the end of it, and it points backward as readily as
forward. The same word gives *"the ancient paths"* (Jeremiah 6:16), the mighty men *"who were of
old"* (Genesis 6:4), and *"from everlasting to everlasting"* (Psalm 90:2). Hebrew makes its claim about God with a word
about **horizons**, not about timelessness; *ʾĒl ʿÔlām* is the God whose duration you cannot see the
end of from where you are standing, which is a different and more concrete assertion than
*the God outside time*.

The Septuagint renders 21:33 *theos aiōnios*, and that is the phrase the New Testament doxologies
inherit; the Latin *Deus aeternus* completes the drift toward the philosophical sense, and English
*Everlasting* sits somewhere between the two.

### Where Abraham got the word

**He had heard it four times, in one speech, four chapters earlier.**

Genesis 17 is the covenant of circumcision, and its adjective is *ʿôlām*: an *everlasting covenant*
(17:7, 13, 19) and the land of Canaan as an *everlasting possession* (17:8). **Abraham then uses that
adjective as a name for the God who said it** — and he uses it at the exact moment the promise is
least visible. He has just signed a non-aggression treaty with the current occupant of the
*everlasting possession*, acknowledging by the act that the land is not presently his; he has one
son, aged about three; and he has, in the next verse, nothing but the standing of a resident alien:
*"Abraham lived as a foreigner in the land of the Philistines many days."*

**So the name is a statement about timescale made against the evidence.** The word that had described
the covenant is turned on its author, by a man whose visible situation contradicts it, at a well he
had to buy the right to be believed about.

### The ʾĒl names, and who does the naming

Genesis is full of compound *ʾĒl* names, and the striking thing is how few of them God supplies.

| name | verse | who says it |
|---|---|---|
| *ʾĒl ʿElyôn* | 14:18–22 | Melchizedek, a Canaanite priest — then Abram, adopting it |
| *ʾĒl Rŏʾî* | 16:13 | Hagar, an Egyptian slave |
| *ʾĒl Šadday* | 17:1 | God, of himself |
| *ʾĒl ʿÔlām* | 21:33 | Abraham |
| *ʾĒl ʾĕlōhê Yiśrāʾēl* | 33:20 | Jacob, at an altar |
| *ʾĒl Bêt-ʾĒl* | 35:7 | Jacob, at a place |

**Four of the six are coined by human beings, and two of those four by people outside the covenant
line.** Each is attached to a place — Salem, Beer Lahai Roi, Beersheba, Shechem, Bethel — and most
are attached to an altar or an act of invocation. The pattern is that **names for God in Genesis come
out of encounters and get fixed to ground**, rather than being delivered as doctrine.

*ʾĒl* is also, outside the Bible, the proper name of the head of the Canaanite pantheon at Ugarit,
and Ugaritic texts use epithets structurally identical to these. Two readings of that fact are
current and both are old: that Israel's ancestors worshipped under the regional high god's titles and
identified him with Yahweh, which is roughly what Exodus 6:3 says happened; or that *ʾēl* is simply
the common noun *god* and these are ordinary descriptive compounds. See [[The Names of God]].

### Isaiah 40:28

The phrase does not recur, but the idea does, and it recurs in the one place in the prophets that
most sounds like a commentary on Abraham's situation.

> *"The everlasting God, Yahweh, the Creator of the ends of the earth, doesn't faint. He isn't
> weary."*

Isaiah 40 is addressed to exiles — people out of the land, holding a promise about it, with no
visible prospect. **Isaiah's argument is Abraham's gesture: that the God in question operates on a
scale that outlasts the situation you can see.** Isaiah says it with *ʾĕlōhê ʿôlām* rather than *ʾĒl
ʿôlām*, and adds *Creator of the ends of the earth* — space to Genesis's time — but the word and the
use are the same.

## Significance

- **One occurrence, and it is a man's coinage.** The Bible's only *ʾĒl ʿÔlām* is not a revelation and
  not a title God claims. It is what Abraham decided to call him, out loud, at a well, next to a tree
  he had just planted.
- **It is the vocabulary of the covenant, redirected.** Genesis 17 said *everlasting* four times
  about an agreement and a piece of land. Genesis 21:33 says it once, about the person on the other
  end of both.
- **And it is said at the moment of least evidence.** Abraham names God *everlasting* in the same
  breath as the narrator calls him a *foreigner* in the land he has been promised forever. The
  pericope's last two verses hold the name and the contradiction side by side and do not comment.
