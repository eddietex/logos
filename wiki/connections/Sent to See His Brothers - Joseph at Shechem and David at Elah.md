## Analysis

**The errand.** In both stories a father sends a younger son from home to the brothers who are away, the flock in
Genesis and the army in 1 Samuel, to learn how they are and report back. The shared word is *šālôm*: Jacob says *rəʾēh
ʾeṯ-šəlôm ʾaḥeyḵā*, *see the welfare of your brothers* (Genesis 37:14), and Jesse *ʾeṯ-ʾaḥeyḵā tip̄qōḏ ləšālôm*, *your
brothers you shall visit for welfare* (1 Samuel 17:18); David then *asked his brothers for šālôm*, WEB *greeted* (17:22)
(WLC). Genesis 37:14 is the only verse in the Hebrew files checked where *šālôm* stands directly before *your brothers*.
Both fathers want word back: Jacob's *hăšiḇēnî dāḇār*, *bring me back word*, and Jesse's *ʿărubbāṯām tiqqāḥ*, *take
their pledge*, whatever token that was (WLC; the sense of *ʿărubbâ* recalled, not checked).

**The setting.** Both sons are shepherds. Joseph was *feeding the flock with his brothers* (Genesis 37:2); David
*went back and forth from Saul to feed his father's sheep* (1 Samuel 17:15) (stored WEB). Joseph is sent *out of the
valley of Hebron* (37:14), David to *the valley of Elah* (17:19), and both words are *ʿēmeq* (WLC); the wiki notes
that and makes nothing of it.

**The brothers.** Joseph's brothers *hated him* and *envied him* before he set out (Genesis 37:4, 11), and at Dothan
they plot to kill him (37:18–20). Eliab's anger is not explained, and comes after David has been anointed *in the
middle of his brothers* (1 Samuel 16:13), Eliab first among those passed over (16:6–7). Joseph's brothers mock *this
dreamer*; Eliab charges David with *pride*. Joseph is stripped and thrown into a pit (Genesis 37:23–24); David turns
away *toward another* and goes on talking (1 Samuel 17:30).

**The beasts.** The brothers plan to tell Jacob *an evil animal has devoured him* (Genesis 37:20), and Jacob believes
it: *Joseph is without doubt torn in pieces* (37:33). Ten verses after Eliab's rebuke, David tells Saul how he took a
lamb back from the lion and the bear (1 Samuel 17:34–35). The text draws no line between the two.

## Significance

Neither text refers to the other, and the parallel is one of shape, not quotation. What the two share is a pattern the
wiki has followed since Genesis: the younger brother chosen over the elder, and the elder's hostility to him (see
[[Brotherhood]] and [[Birthright and the Firstborn]]). Joseph's errand ends in Egypt with the brothers bowing; David's
ends, within the chapter, with the brothers' enemy dead. Both men are sent by a father with food or news and become, by
the story's end, the one who saves the family's people. The wiki records the parallel and leaves it there.
