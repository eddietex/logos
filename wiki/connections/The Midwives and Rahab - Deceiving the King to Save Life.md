# The Midwives and Rahab - Deceiving the King to Save Life

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 1:15–21** — the midwives fear God, save the boys, and answer the king that the Hebrew
  women *"are vigorous, and give birth before the midwife comes to them"*; *"God dealt well with the
  midwives"* — [[The Oppression in Egypt]]
- **Joshua 2:1–7** — Rahab hides the spies from the king of Jericho: *"Yes, the men came to me, but I
  didn't know where they came from … Where the men went, I don't know"* (2:4–5), *"But she had brought
  them up to the roof"* (2:6) *(not yet ingested)*
- **Hebrews 11:31** — *"By faith, Rahab the prostitute, didn't perish with those who were
  disobedient, having received the spies in peace"*
- **James 2:25** — *"wasn't Rahab the prostitute also justified by works, in that she received the
  messengers, and sent them out another way?"*
- **Acts 5:29** — *"We must obey God rather than men"*

## Analysis

**Two scenes with the same parts**: a king demands that women hand over Israelite lives; the women
refuse; they tell the king's men something untrue; and Scripture rewards them.

| | the midwives | Rahab |
|---|---|---|
| the king | of Egypt, unnamed | of Jericho, unnamed |
| the demand | kill the sons at birth | *"Bring out the men"* (Joshua 2:3) |
| the ground | *"the midwives feared God"* (1:17) | her confession of Yahweh *(Joshua 2:9–11, to be read when ingested)* |
| the words | the Hebrew women give birth before the midwife comes | the men left at dusk; *"I don't know"* |
| the reward | *"he gave them families"* (1:21) | her household spared *(Joshua 6)* |
| the New Testament | — | commended for her act, by faith (Hebrews) and by works (James) |

**In both cases the commendation attaches to the act and the fear, and the words are passed over.**
Exodus gives the midwives' fear as the reason God dealt well with them, twice (1:17, 21), and never
mentions their answer in the verdict. Hebrews and James commend Rahab's *receiving* and *sending* the
spies, not what she told the king's messengers.

**The interpretive question is therefore the same for both, and the wiki records the three answers
without choosing**:

1. **The deception was right** — a king seeking innocent blood is owed no truth that would deliver
   it, and the women's words are part of their righteous act.
2. **The act was rewarded and the lie forgiven** — the reading usually associated with Augustine,
   for whom no lie is righteous, and for whom the reward is for mercy.
3. **It was not, or not wholly, a lie** — the midwives may have spoken a partial truth, or delayed on
   purpose.

**Both cases also put women outside the line of power against a king, and both locate the women's
courage in the fear of God**, the principle Acts 5:29 states outright. See [[Deception]],
[[The Fear of God]], and [[Shiphrah and Puah]].

## Significance

These are the two passages every discussion of whether deception can be right returns to, because
in both Scripture rewards the deceiver and in neither does it address the deceit. Genesis's
deceptions (see [[Deception]]) are mostly within the family and mostly paid back; these are the
first in which the deceived party is a king trying to kill, and neither is paid back.
