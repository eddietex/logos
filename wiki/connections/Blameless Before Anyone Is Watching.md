# Blameless Before Anyone Is Watching

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 6:9** — *"Noah was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time. Noah walked
  with God."*
- **Job 1:1** — *"That man was blameless and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from
  evil."*
- **Job 1:8; 2:3** — God repeating the verdict of Job 1:1, twice, word for word.
- **Genesis 7:1** — *"for I have seen your righteousness before me in this generation."*
- **Ezekiel 14:12–20** — Noah, Daniel, and Job named together.
- **James 5:11** — *"you have heard of the perseverance of Job."*
- **Hebrews 11:7** — Noah *"became heir of the righteousness which is according to faith."*

## Analysis

Two books that share almost no vocabulary open the same way. Before Noah does anything, Genesis
6:9 declares him righteous and blameless. Before Job does anything, Job 1:1 declares him blameless
and upright. In both cases the verdict is the **narrator's**, delivered in the man's introductory
sentence, and in both cases it is confirmed by God directly afterwards — Genesis 7:1, *"I have seen
your righteousness before me"*; Job 1:8 and 2:3, where God quotes his own narrator verbatim.

**The shared move is that the assessment precedes the evidence.** This is not how the Old
Testament usually introduces a man. Genesis's standard opening is descent — *these are the
generations of* — and character emerges from what happens next. Here the character is given first
and functions as a premise, not a conclusion. Neither man is shown earning the description; both
are shown living under it.

**The vocabulary is close but not identical, and the difference is worth keeping.** Noah is
*ṣaddîq* (righteous) and *tāmîm* (blameless — the sacrificial word, used of an animal without
defect, and the word God gives Abraham at Genesis 17:1). Job is *tām* — the same root as *tāmîm*,
one letter shorter, and the word Genesis 25:27 uses of Jacob as *"a quiet man"* — paired with
*yāšār*, upright, straight. Both pairs mean integrity, wholeness, a life without a seam in it.
Neither pair means sinlessness, and both books know it: Noah is drunk and exposed two chapters
later, and Job spends thirty chapters insisting on innocence of *this*, not innocence in general.

**Where the two stories diverge is what the verdict buys.**

For Noah the verdict is protective, and the narrative logic is intact. He is righteous; the world
is destroyed; he is spared. Genesis 7:1 states the connection explicitly — *because* I have seen
your righteousness, come into the ark. The reader who wants the world to make moral sense finds it
making moral sense.

For Job the verdict is the **cause** of the catastrophe. God volunteers Job's name to the accuser
precisely because *"there is no one like him in the earth"* (1:8). The blamelessness is not what
saves him from the disaster; it is what qualifies him for it. And the accuser's counter-question —
*"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* — turns Noah's world inside out: in a world where righteousness
reliably protects, no one can tell whether righteousness is loved or bought. See
[[The Fear of God]].

Read in sequence, the two passages are the wisdom tradition's before and after. Genesis 6–9 states
the correlation between character and outcome. Job 1 states the same character and then breaks the
correlation on purpose, using an identical opening sentence so that the break is unmistakable.

**Ezekiel puts the two men in the same sentence.** Ezekiel 14:14 and 14:20 name *"Noah, Daniel, and
Job"* as three men whose righteousness *"would deliver only their own souls"* — the prophet's
argument being that individual righteousness is non-transferable, so Jerusalem cannot be spared on
anyone else's account. Three observations follow.

First, it is the **earliest evidence outside the book of Job that Job was a known figure**, and it
places him in the company of the proverbially righteous rather than in Israel's history. Second,
the grouping is pointedly non-Israelite in flavour — Noah is pre-Israelite, Job is a foreigner, and
the *Danel* named between them is more likely the ancient righteous king of the Ugaritic Aqhat
legend than the exile of the book of Daniel, given the spelling and the antiquity of the other two.
Third, and most sharply: **Ezekiel denies for these three men exactly what Job 42:8 grants to
Job.** In Ezekiel these are men whose righteousness saves nobody else; in the epilogue of Job, God
tells the three friends to bring their offerings and have Job pray for them, *"for I will accept
him, that I not deal with you according to your folly."* The book gives its blameless man an
intercessory office that the prophet's argument explicitly rules out.

## Significance

- **A narrator's verdict is a piece of information, not a character trait.** Both books hand the
  reader knowledge that the characters lack, and in Job that gap is the entire engine. Everything
  the friends say is wrong for a reason the friends have no access to.
- **Righteousness is not protection.** The two passages together are the Old Testament's clearest
  statement that the connection between being right with God and being safe is real enough to be
  narrated in Genesis and unreliable enough to be dismantled in Job. Neither book cancels the
  other, and the canon keeps both.
- **The word *blameless* has to be read as integrity, not perfection**, or both stories collapse —
  Noah's into hypocrisy at Genesis 9:21, Job's into the friends' case against him.
- **Job is a foreigner and the Bible does not qualify the praise.** Ezekiel ranks him with Noah,
  James holds him up to a Christian congregation, and God calls him *my servant* four times in the
  epilogue — the title given to Moses, David, and the Servant of Isaiah. See [[Job (person)]] and
  [[Noah]].
