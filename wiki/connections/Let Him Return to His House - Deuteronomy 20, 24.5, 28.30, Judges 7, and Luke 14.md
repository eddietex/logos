# Let Him Return to His House - Deuteronomy 20, 24.5, 28.30, Judges 7, and Luke 14

## Analysis

**Deuteronomy 20 sends four kinds of men home.** Three have begun something and not yet enjoyed it — a
house not dedicated, a vineyard not yet eaten from, a wife pledged and not taken — and each is sent home
*"lest he die in the battle, and another man"* have it (20:5–7). The fourth is the man who is *"fearful
and faint-hearted"*, sent home *"lest his brother's heart melt as his heart"* (20:8). The first three are
exempted for their own sake. The fourth is exempted for the army's.

**Deuteronomy 24:5 extends the third.** The newly married man does not go out with the army for a year,
and is not given *"any business"*; he is *"free at home"* to *"cheer his wife"*. 20:7 covered the man
betrothed; 24:5 covers the man just married. The two laws, read together, protect the beginning of a
marriage from war at both ends of the wedding. 24:5 goes further than 20:7 in two ways. The exemption
covers *"any business"*, not only war, and the reason is not *"lest he die … and another man take her"*
but *"shall cheer his wife"*, the piel *śimmaḥ*, *make glad* (WLC). 20:7 protects what the man would
lose; 24:5 gives the wife something. It opens with the same words as the divorce law just before it,
*kî-yiqqaḥ ʾîš ʾiššâ*, *when a man takes a wife* (24:1, 5, WLC); see [[Divorce, Remarriage, and the Newlywed]].

**Deuteronomy 28:30 reverses all three.** The covenant curses take the three exemptions and turn each
into its loss, with the same Hebrew verbs: *ʾāraś*, *betroth*; *bānâ*, *build*; *nāṭaʿ*, *plant*; and
*ḥillēl*, *use the fruit* (WLC 20:5–7; 28:30). What the law of war refuses to let *"another man"* take,
the curse says another man will take. The exemptions describe the life a faithful Israel keeps; the
curse describes the same life lost.

**Judges 7 applies the fourth.** Gideon is told by Yahweh that his army is too large, *"lest Israel brag
against me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me'"* (7:2), and is told to send home *"whoever is fearful
and trembling"* (7:3). The word *fearful* is the same adjective as Deuteronomy 20:8, *yārēʾ* (WLC Judges
7:3; Deuteronomy 20:8 has it with the article, *hayyārēʾ*), and the dismissal uses the verb *šûb*,
*return*: Judges has *yāšōb*, Deuteronomy *yēlēk wəyāšōb*, *let him go and return* (WLC). Judges does not cite the
law and gives a different reason: in Deuteronomy the fearful man is sent home to protect his brothers'
courage; in Judges he is sent home so that the victory will plainly be Yahweh's. Twenty-two thousand
leave (7:3). Whether Gideon's proclamation is meant as an application of Deuteronomy 20:8 is a reading;
the verbal agreement is in the text, the dependence is not stated.

**Luke 14 is a commonly drawn parallel, not a quotation.** In the parable of the great supper, three
guests excuse themselves: one has bought a field, one five yoke of oxen, and one has *"married a wife"*
(Luke 14:18–20). Interpreters have often compared the list with Deuteronomy 20:5–7 and 24:5, where a new
property and a new marriage are legitimate grounds for staying home from war. On that reading the guests
offer excuses the law itself would have accepted for a lesser summons, and the parable's point is that
this invitation outranks them. Luke does not cite Deuteronomy, the items do not match exactly — a field
and oxen, not a house and a vineyard — and the Greek of Luke 14 has not been checked here. The wiki
records the comparison as an interpretation.

## Significance

The thread shows the law treating unfinished ordinary life — a house, a crop, a marriage — as worth
protecting even from a war the law itself commands. Deuteronomy 28 measures the curse by the same three
things, so the exemptions and the curse are two halves of one picture. Judges 7 and Luke 14 take up the
dismissals in narrative and parable, each for its own purpose. See [[War]], [[Marriage]], and
[[Laws for Going to War]].
