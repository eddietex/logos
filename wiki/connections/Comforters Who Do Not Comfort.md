# Comforters Who Do Not Comfort

## Analysis

**The book of Job opens and closes its account of human comfort with the same two Hebrew verbs**, and
the pair is the reason this page exists.

At **2:11** the friends set out *lānûd lô ûlənaḥămô* — to condole with him and to comfort him. At
**42:11**, after everything, Job's brothers and sisters and old acquaintances come and *"comforted
him"*: *wayyānudû lô wayənaḥămû ʾōtô*. **The verbs are identical, in the same order, forty chapters
apart.** The first attempt produces the longest argument in the Bible. The second produces a meal, a
piece of money, a gold ring each, and no recorded speech at all.

**Between the two stands Job's verdict on the first**, and it is made of the second verb.
*Mənaḥămê ʿāmāl kullkem* (16:2) is usually rendered *miserable comforters*; literally it is
**comforters of trouble** — *ʿāmāl* being toil, misery, mischief, the word Job uses for his own
condition. The phrase can be read as *comforters who are troublesome* or as *comforters who produce
trouble*, and the grammar does not decide; **on either reading the noun is the job they came to do
and the qualifier is what they made of it.**

**What went wrong is not obscure, because Job says it twice.** *"Oh that you would be completely
silent! Then you would be wise"* (13:5), and *"how can you comfort me with nonsense?"* (21:34). **The
complaint is never that they came, and never that they stayed. It is entirely about what they said.**
For seven days and seven nights they were, by Job's own later standard, perfect comforters. At 21:2
he says what the comfort would be now — *"Listen diligently to my speech. Let this be your
consolation"* — turning Eliphaz's *"consolations of God"* (*tanḥumôt*, 15:11) into the plain request
to be heard; and at 21:5 he asks for the silence back: *"Lay your hand on your mouth."* See
[[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].

**Scripture's other failed comforters divide into two kinds, and the friends are neither.**

**There are the comforters who are absent.** Psalm 69:20, Lamentations 1, and Ecclesiastes 4:1
are related descriptions of missing consolation. Ecclesiastes 4:1 repeats the absence twice,
with the oppressors' power between them. It does not say that the victims are physically
alone. This comparison concerns the situation described; an identical Hebrew formula across
these passages has not been verified here and is not asserted.

**There is the mourner who refuses comfort.** Jacob, told his son is dead, *"refused to be
comforted"* (Genesis 37:35) — *wayəmāʾēn ləhitnaḥēm* — and says he will go down to Sheol mourning.
The comforters there are present and willing; the refusal is his, and it is the right instinct for
the wrong reason, since Joseph is alive.

**Job's friends are the third kind, and the rarest: comforters who are present, willing, competent at
the rite, and destroyed by their own explanation.** Nothing is missing from their performance except
the ability to leave the thing unaccounted for. The book's proposition is that **comfort and
explanation are different acts, and that attempting the second can undo the first** — which is
exactly what 42:11 demonstrates by giving the successful comforters no dialogue.

**And the book has already shown a comforter who succeeded, and it is Job.** The last line of his
memory of the old days is *"as one who comforts the mourners"* (29:25), *ʾăbēlîm yənaḥēm* — the same
*nḥm*, and the mourners of the rite of 2:12–13. **The man the friends came to comfort had been, by his
own account, the town's comforter**, and the chapter says what that consisted of: delivering the poor,
making the widow sing, sitting with the mourner as a man whose counsel others *"waited for … as for the
rain"* (29:23). In the next chapter the position is reversed — *"my harp has turned to mourning"*
(30:31) — and there is no one to do for him what he did.

**Isaiah 40:1 and 2 Corinthians 1:3–4 are here to mark the other end of the word.** *Nāḥam* is one of
the Bible's large theological verbs: it is what God does to his people at the head of Isaiah 40, it is
the root behind Noah's name and the hope attached to it at Genesis 5:29 (see
[[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]]), and 2 Corinthians makes God *"the God of all
comfort"* and human comforting a pass-through of what was received. **Job 2–16 is the same verb
failing in human hands**, and the book does not offer a technique to fix it; it offers a week of
silence that worked and twenty-five chapters of argument that did not.

**One more *nḥm* stands between the two, and it is Job's own** (added on ingesting
[[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]]). His last words are *"Therefore I abhor myself, and repent (niḥamtî) in dust
and ashes"* (42:6). The niphal of *nḥm* can mean *to repent, to change one's mind*, or *to be comforted*,
and the clause is contested (see [[Sitting in Dust and Ashes]]). If it is *comforted*, **the book's comfort
arrives in this verse, from God's speech and not from any human speaker** — after the friends' failure and
before the family's success. If it is *repent*, the verb is still the same one the friends came to perform.
The wiki does not decide the clause; it records that the root is here.

### The healing tongue and the kind word — Proverbs 12

Proverbs 12:18 contrasts rash speech that pierces like a sword with a wise tongue that heals; 12:25
contrasts the weight of anxiety with the gladness a kind word can bring. Job 16:2–5 provides the
failed and imagined successful cases: Job calls his friends miserable comforters, then says that if
their positions were reversed he would strengthen and relieve them with his speech. Comfort is
measured by what the words do to a sufferer, not simply by the speaker’s intention to explain.

The comparison does not turn comforting into agreement with every assertion, or reproof into
cruelty: Proverbs 12:1 commends correction. Job 42:7 nevertheless judges the friends’ speech about
God wrong. Wise correction needs truth, and wisdom’s healing speech cannot be manufactured by
assuming the sufferer’s guilt. A kind word in 12:25 is a real good, not a guarantee that all anxiety
ends through conversation.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]] and [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]]. Sources:
Proverbs 12:1, 18, 25; Job 16:1–5; 42:7 checked in stored WEB.

### Power and no comforter — Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

In [[The Tears of the Oppressed]], consolation is absent within an oppressive distribution of power.
Job 16:2 depicts another failure: people came to comfort and their words make matters worse.
These are related failures, not identical scenes. The Preacher goes on to prefer the dead
and never-existent to those who see this evil (4:2–3); no consolation answers that verdict
within the unit. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:1–3; Job 16:1–5.

## Significance

- **It sets the friends' offence exactly where the text puts it.** They are not condemned for
  indifference, for absence, or for arriving late. They are condemned at 42:7 for what they said
  about God, and Job's own charge against them is that they talked. See
  [[Eliphaz the Temanite]], [[Bildad the Shuhite]], [[Zophar the Naamathite]].
- **It makes 42:11 a deliberate echo and not a coincidence.** The epilogue reuses 2:11's verb pair
  for people who do the same errand and get it right, which is the book's closing comment on the
  difference between sitting with a man and accounting for him.
- **It gives the wiki its standing frame for consolation.** Comfort is a category of act in
  Scripture — see [[Compassion and Mercy]] and [[Mourning and Lament]] — and Job is its most detailed
  case study, from the model week to the catastrophic sequel.
- **It is why *"miserable comforters"* entered the language.** The phrase is quoted far more often
  than it is located, and locating it matters: it is said by a man to three people who walked across
  a desert for him, four chapters after the last of them stopped being silent.

### The gentle answer — Proverbs 15:1, 4, 23

[[A Gentle Answer]] states in general form what the friends of Job failed to do. *"A gentle answer turns
away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger"* (15:1); *"A gentle tongue is a tree of life, but deceit in
it crushes the spirit"* (15:4); *"How good is a word at the right time!"* (15:23). Eliphaz had claimed the
quality for his own speech — *"Are the consolations of God too small for you, even the word that is gentle
toward you?"* (Job 15:11) — and Job answered, *"How long will you torment me, and crush me with words?"*
(Job 19:2), and *"I would strengthen you with my mouth. The solace of my lips would relieve you"* (Job
16:5). **The friends judged their words by their content; Proverbs and Job both judge them by their
effect on the hearer.** The comparison is of subject; whether Job 15:11 and Proverbs 15:1 share Hebrew
vocabulary has not been checked here. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 15:1, 4, 23; Job 15:11; 16:2, 5; 19:2.

### Answering before hearing — Proverbs 18:13, 17

[[A Strong Tower]] states the rule the friends broke: *"He who gives answer before he hears, that is
folly and shame to him"* (18:13), and *"He who pleads his cause first seems right; until another comes
and questions him"* (18:17). Elihu claims to have kept it — *"I waited for your words"* (Job 32:11) — and
his complaint is that the friends' searching produced no answer to Job. See
[[He Who Answers Before He Hears - Proverbs 18.13, 17 and Elihu's Waiting]]. Sources: stored WEB
Proverbs 18:13, 17; Job 32:11.
