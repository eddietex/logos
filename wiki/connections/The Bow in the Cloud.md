# The Bow in the Cloud

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:12–17 — the sign of the covenant, set in the cloud —
  [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- Genesis 21:16, 20; 27:3; 49:24 — *qešet* in its ordinary sense: Ishmael's bow, Esau's, Joseph's
- Psalm 7:12–13 — *"He has bent his bow and made it ready"*, of God
- Lamentations 2:4 — *"He has bent his bow like an enemy"*
- Habakkuk 3:9–11 — *"You uncovered your bow"*: the divine warrior with the weapon drawn
- Ezekiel 1:28 — *"as the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain"*, around
  the glory
- Revelation 4:3 — *"there was a rainbow around the throne, like an emerald to look at"*
- Revelation 10:1 — the mighty angel, *"a rainbow was on his head"*
- *Enuma Elish* IV–VI — Marduk's bow set among the stars after the defeat of Tiamat

## Analysis

**There is no Hebrew word for a rainbow.** The word Genesis 9:13 uses is **qešet**, and in roughly
seventy-five other occurrences across the Hebrew Bible it means a **bow** — the weapon. Ishmael grows
up to be an archer (21:20); Isaac tells Esau to take his quiver and his bow and hunt (27:3); Joseph's
*"bow abides in strength"* (49:24). It is the standard word in every description of a battle, and it
is the standard word when God is the archer: *"He has bent his bow and made it ready"* (Psalm 7:12),
*"He has bent his bow like an enemy"* (Lamentations 2:4), *"You uncovered your bow"* (Habakkuk 3:9).

The meteorological sense exists in the Hebrew Bible in exactly two places — Genesis 9:13–16 and
Ezekiel 1:28 — and in both it is the surrounding words, not the noun, that supply it. Genesis says
*qeštî beʿānān*, **my bow in the cloud**; Ezekiel says *ke-marʾēh haqqešet ʾăšer yihyeh beʿānān
beyôm haggešem*, *"like the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud in the day of rain"*, and has
to specify the rain to be understood.

So the sentence a Hebrew reader meets at Genesis 9:13 is: *I have set my weapon in the cloud.*

**What the reading claims, and what it does not.** The old and widely held inference is that the sign
is a bow laid down — hung in the sky, its arc turned away from the earth and toward heaven, the
warrior's weapon retired at the end of the campaign. Everything in the context supports the sense: the
covenant's content is a promise never to do this again, the bow appears in the presence of the very
clouds that would otherwise be frightening, and God has just spent a chapter as the agent of a flood.

Three honest limits on it. Genesis says only *nātattî*, **I have set** or **given**, and volunteers
nothing about the bow's position, tension, or retirement — the unstringing is a reader's addition.
A single word for both a weapon and a rainbow is unremarkable, since a rainbow is bow-shaped and
several unrelated languages do the same (the English *rainbow* itself, and the French *arc-en-ciel*).
And Ezekiel 1:28, the one canonical parallel, has no martial overtone whatever. The reading is a
strong inference from vocabulary and context; it is not something the text states.

**Ezekiel makes the sign into an attribute of God's glory.** At the end of the throne vision — the
wheels, the living creatures, the crystal expanse, the figure on the sapphire throne — Ezekiel runs
out of comparisons and reaches for this: *"As the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in
the day of rain, so was the appearance of the brightness around. This was the appearance of the
likeness of Yahweh's glory."* The last thing said about the vision of God is that it looked like the
sign of Genesis 9. A prophet whose commission is to announce the destruction of Jerusalem opens his
book with the token of the promise not to destroy the earth.

**Revelation takes it from Ezekiel and puts it around the throne.** John's throne room is Ezekiel's
throne room rewritten, and the rainbow comes with it: *"there was a rainbow around the throne, like
an emerald to look at"* (4:3) — before the seals are opened, before any judgment is poured out. Then
at 10:1, in the middle of the trumpets, the mighty angel comes down *"clothed with a cloud. A rainbow
was on his head."* Both placements do the same work: the book that contains the fiercest judgment
imagery in Scripture keeps the sign of the covenant not to destroy in the frame while it does so.

**And there is a Babylonian bow in a Babylonian sky.** In *Enuma Elish*, Marduk kills Tiamat — the
primeval salt water — with the bow he made for the purpose, splits her carcass to form sky and earth,
and the assembled gods then take the bow, praise it, and set it in the heavens as a constellation, a
permanent token of the victory. A bow, in the sky, after a conflict with cosmic waters, kept as a
memorial. The parallel is close enough to be worth stating and the difference is the usual one:
Marduk's bow commemorates a victory over an enemy who was defeated; God's bow commemorates a promise
made to the survivors of something he did himself, and its stated function is not celebration but
restraint.

## Significance

**The sign faces the wrong way, and that is the point.** Every other covenant sign in Scripture is
borne by the human party and serves as their reminder — circumcision cut into Abraham's household
(Genesis 17:11), the Sabbath kept by Israel (Exodus 31:13, 17), the bread and cup taken *"in memory
of me"* (Luke 22:19). The rainbow is installed in God's field of view, and Genesis states its purpose
three times in his own mouth: *"I will remember my covenant"* (9:15), *"I will look at it, that I may
remember the everlasting covenant"* (9:16). If it is a weapon, it is a weapon hung where its owner
will see it. See [[Remembrance]].

**It puts an image of restraint at both ends of the canon.** Genesis sets the bow in the cloud after
the largest act of judgment in Scripture; Revelation sets it around the throne before the largest
one. In neither place does it prevent judgment — 2 Peter 3:5–7 is explicit that the promise is
specifically *never again by water* — and in both places it is what the judgment is framed by. The
same object, in a sky and around a throne, doing the same work four thousand years apart in narrative
time.

**And it is the reason weather is theological in the Bible.** After Genesis 9 a cloud gathering over
the earth is not a neutral fact. The one thing every human being on the planet can see, and has
always been able to see, is entered into the record as the token of an oath. Isaiah 54:9–10 makes
this explicit — *"as I have sworn that the waters of Noah will no more go over the earth, so have I
sworn that I will not be angry with you"* — and builds the covenant of peace on the credibility of a
promise anyone can check by looking up after it rains.
