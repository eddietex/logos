# The Bow in the Cloud

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 9:12–17** — *"I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between
  me and the earth… I will look at it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant"* —
  [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- **Psalm 7:12** — *"he has bent and strung his bow"*
- **Lamentations 2:4** — *"He has bent his bow like an enemy"*
- **Habakkuk 3:9, 11** — *"You uncovered your bow… at the light of your arrows as they went"*
- **Zechariah 9:10, 13** — the battle bow cut off, and Judah bent as God's bow
- **Ezekiel 1:28** — *"As the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain, so
  was the appearance of the brightness all around"*
- **Isaiah 54:9–10** — *"this is like the waters of Noah to me"*: the Noahic oath as the model of an
  unbreakable promise
- **Revelation 4:3** — *"There was a rainbow around the throne, like an emerald to look at"*
- **Revelation 10:1** — a mighty angel *"clothed with a cloud. A rainbow was on his head"*
- **Enūma Eliš** VI and **Gilgamesh** XI — the Mesopotamian sky-signs after a divine victory and a
  flood

## Analysis

**There is no Hebrew word for "rainbow".** The noun in Genesis 9:13, 14, and 16 is **qešet**, which
occurs some seventy times in the Hebrew Bible and in nearly every other instance means a **war bow**
— Ishmael grows up an archer (Genesis 21:20), Isaac tells Esau to take his quiver and his bow
(27:3), Jonathan strips off his bow for David (1 Samuel 18:4), and God bends his against the wicked
(Psalm 7:12). Genesis 9 is one of only a handful of places where the referent is meteorological, and
Hebrew supplies no separate term to mark the difference. A reader in Hebrew hears *I have set my bow
in the cloud* and has to decide, from context alone, which bow is meant.

The context is a flood that has just ended and a promise not to repeat it. Which makes the natural
reading — old, and found in the medieval Jewish commentators and in Luther — that this is the divine
warrior's weapon, set down. The bow is hung in the cloud where it can be seen: unstrung, drawn by no
one, and pointed away from the earth. What was a weapon becomes a memorandum.

**Two features of Genesis 9's sign follow from that, and they are unusual.**

*It is God's to look at.* Every purpose clause in 9:14–16 has God as its subject: *when I bring a
cloud… I will remember… I will look at it, that I may remember*. Nothing is required of Noah, who is
not even told to watch for it. Compare circumcision, which the human party performs and carries
(Genesis 17:10–14), and the Sabbath, which Israel keeps as its sign (Exodus 31:13, 17). Only one
other covenant sign in Scripture is defined by God's seeing it, and it is also made of blood
withheld: *"When I see the blood, I will pass over you"* (Exodus 12:13).

*It is not created, only assigned.* The verb is *nātan*, to give or set. Genesis does not say that a
rainbow had never appeared before, or that God makes one now. An ordinary optical fact is given a
job — the same move as the mark set on Cain (4:15) and the lights of 1:14 set *"for signs"*. The
sign is the meaning, not the object.

**The prophets keep the ambiguity live.** Habakkuk 3 is the fullest picture of God as an armed
warrior in the Hebrew Bible — *"You uncovered your bow… the sun and moon stood still in the sky at
the light of your arrows as they went"* — and Lamentations 2:4 has him bending it *"like an enemy"*
against his own city. So the weapon is not permanently retired; Genesis 9 retires it from one
specific use. Zechariah 9:10 then imagines the disarmament extended: *"the battle bow will be cut
off."*

**And it becomes the vocabulary of the throne.** Ezekiel, hunting for a comparison for the glory he
has seen above the firmament, reaches for exactly this image — the *qešet* in the cloud on a rainy
day — and stops there; 1:28 is the last line of the vision, and the next verse has him on his face.
John takes the same picture twice in Revelation: a rainbow encircling the throne (4:3), and one on
the head of the angel who comes down clothed in a cloud (10:1). In both, the bow is not a threat but
the frame around the one who judges. Revelation 4 is a throne room from which seals, trumpets, and
bowls will shortly be poured out on the earth, and the first thing said about it is that a rainbow
surrounds it.

## Significance

The Noahic sign is the Bible's first act of divine self-binding made visible, and the form it takes
is a weapon put away. That is the theological content of the image whether or not the narrator
intended the pun: the God who has just used the waters of creation as an instrument declares
publicly that he will not use them so again, and attaches the declaration to something anyone can
see and nobody can manufacture.

The oddity worth keeping is the direction of the sign. Covenant signs in Scripture normally mark the
subordinate party — a cut in the flesh, a day observed, a meal eaten. This one is a reminder to the
superior party, and it exists because the promise is entirely his to keep. Isaiah 54:9–10 draws the
conclusion the rest of the canon works from: if the oath about the waters of Noah holds, then *"the
mountains may depart, and the hills be removed, but my loving kindness will not depart from you"* —
the Noahic covenant is cited not for its content but as the proof that God's promises are the kind
of thing that does not get revoked.
