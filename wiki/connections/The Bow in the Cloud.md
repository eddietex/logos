# The Bow in the Cloud

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:12–17 — *"I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between me
  and the earth"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Deuteronomy 32:23, 42 — *"I will spend my arrows on them… I will make my arrows drunk with blood"*
- Psalm 7:12–13 — *"He has bent his bow, and made it ready… He has made ready for him the
  instruments of death"*
- Psalm 18:14; 144:6 — arrows and lightning together, as one weapon
- Lamentations 2:4; 3:12 — *"He has bent his bow like an enemy… He has bent his bow, and set me as a
  mark for the arrow"*
- Habakkuk 3:9–11 — *"You uncovered your bow… the light of your arrows as they went"*
- Zechariah 9:13–14 — *"Yahweh will be seen over them; and his arrow will go flash like lightning"*
- Ezekiel 1:26–28 — *"As the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain, so
  was the appearance of the brightness all around"*
- Revelation 4:3 — *"There was a rainbow around the throne, like an emerald to look at"*
- Revelation 10:1 — *"a mighty angel… and a rainbow was on his head"*
- *Enūma Eliš* VI 82–91 — Marduk's bow hung in the sky after the defeat of Tiamat

## Analysis

**Hebrew has no word for rainbow.** This is the whole basis of the connection and it is a plain
lexical fact rather than an interpretation. The word at Genesis 9:13 is **qešet**, and *qešet* is the
bow — the weapon. It occurs roughly seventy times in the Hebrew Bible and in every other occurrence
it means an object made of wood and sinew that shoots arrows: Esau's (Genesis 27:3), Jonathan's
(1 Samuel 18:4), Jehu's (2 Kings 9:24), the bows of Elam and Media. Genesis 9 is the only passage in
the Torah where the referent is the arc in the sky, and the language did not acquire a second word
for the purpose. What God says at 9:13, in the words available to say it, is: **I have put my bow in
the cloud.**

**And in Hebrew poetry, God has a bow.** This is the second half of the argument and it is why the
first half matters. The divine warrior armed with bow and arrows is not a stray image; it is a
standing one, and it is old — the arrows are lightning, and the cloud is what he rides. Deuteronomy
32 has him spending his arrows on his enemies. Habakkuk 3, in one of the oldest-sounding poems in
the canon, has him uncovering the bow as he comes with the storm. Psalm 7 has him bending it against
the unrepentant. And Lamentations, most painfully, has him bending it *at Israel*: *"He has bent his
bow like an enemy."*

Put the two facts together and Genesis 9:13 stops being decorative. God has just drowned the world.
He now announces, in the ordinary word for a weapon, that the weapon is going up in the cloud where
he will look at it — and the covenant it signs is a promise never to do that again.

| | The warrior's bow | The bow of Genesis 9 |
|---|---|---|
| Word | *qešet* | *qešet* |
| Where | drawn, uncovered, bent (Hab. 3:9; Lam. 2:4) | *"in the cloud"*, set and left there (9:13) |
| Direction | at an enemy | not stated; nothing is said of arrows at all |
| What follows | arrows, lightning, death | *"I will remember… the waters will no more become a flood"* |
| Who looks at it | the victim | *"I will look at it"* (9:16) |

**The context outside Israel points the same way.** In *Enūma Eliš*, after Marduk kills Tiamat and
makes the world from her body, the assembled gods honor his bow by setting it in the sky as a
constellation. A victorious warrior storing his weapon in the heavens was an image the ancient Near
East already had. Genesis borrows the picture and changes what it means: not a trophy commemorating
a victory, but a weapon laid aside with an undertaking attached. Marduk's bow is in the sky because
he won. God's bow is in the cloud because the war is over and will not resume.

**How far the image can be pushed, and where it should stop.** A very common further step —
medieval in origin and thoroughly at home in modern preaching — has the bow hanging *unstrung*, with
the curve toward the earth and the string toward heaven, so that God has aimed his own weapon at
himself. It is a beautiful reading and it is not in the verse. Genesis says only that the bow is set
in the cloud and that God will look at it. Nothing about strings, arrows, or orientation appears in
the Hebrew, and a page that reported the flourish as exegesis would be inventing evidence for a
conclusion the text already supports without it. The disarmament is in the noun.

**What the sign is actually for.** The other thing usually got backwards. Every popular retelling
has the rainbow as God's reminder *to us*. The text says the opposite twice: *"I will remember my
covenant"* (9:15), *"I will look at it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant"* (9:16). The
audience of the sign is God. That makes it unlike circumcision (Genesis 17:11) and unlike the
Sabbath (Exodus 31:13, 17), both of which are borne and kept by the human party, and like exactly
one other thing in Scripture: the blood on the doorposts at Passover, *"when I see the blood, I will
pass over you"* (Exodus 12:13). The two signs God gives himself are a bow and a bloodstain. See
[[Remembrance]].

**Where the image goes.** The Old Testament reuses it exactly once, and puts it in the most
important place it could. Ezekiel 1 works up through the living creatures, the wheels, the expanse,
the throne, and the figure on it, and then reaches for a comparison for the brightness surrounding
him — *"as the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain."* The vision of
God's glory in exile is described with the sign of the promise not to destroy, offered to a prophet
whose city is about to be destroyed. Revelation 4:3 puts the rainbow around the throne in the same
position, and 10:1 puts it on the head of an angel who then announces that there will be no more
delay. In all three the bow has become part of the furniture of heaven, and in all three it appears
immediately before or during a scene of judgment.

## Significance

The sign of the Noahic covenant is the divine warrior's weapon, named with the ordinary word for it
and placed where the warrior will see it. That reading requires no reconstruction and no allegory —
only the observation that Hebrew has one word for both things and that the Hebrew Bible elsewhere
puts a bow in God's hands.

It also settles what kind of promise Genesis 9 is making. The covenant is not a statement about
weather, and it is not a reassurance that catastrophe will not recur. It is a self-limitation: God
undertakes, unilaterally and with no condition attached, not to use against the world a power he
still has and has already used once. The sign is not evidence that he cannot; it is a standing
reminder, to himself, that he will not. Every later text that leans on this covenant leans on
precisely that feature — Isaiah 54:9–10 argues from it that a greater promise is as unbreakable as
this one, because this one is unbreakable by construction.

And the placement of the image in Ezekiel and Revelation carries the point forward. The last thing
seen around the throne of the God who judges is the weapon he put away.
