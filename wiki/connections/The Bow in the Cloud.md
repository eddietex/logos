# The Bow in the Cloud

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:12–17 — *"I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between me
  and the earth"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Genesis 21:16, 20; 27:3; 48:22; 49:24 — *qešet* in Genesis, always the weapon: Ishmael the archer,
  Esau's hunting bow, Jacob's bow taken in battle, Joseph's bow remaining strong
- Psalm 7:12–13 — *"He has bent his bow, and made it ready… He has prepared for himself the
  instruments of death"*
- Psalm 18:14; 144:6 — arrows and lightning as one image
- Lamentations 2:4; 3:12 — *"He has bent his bow like an enemy… He has bent his bow, and set me as a
  mark for the arrow"*
- Habakkuk 3:8–11 — the divine warrior's theophany: *"You uncovered your bow… The sun and moon stood
  still in the sky at the light of your arrows as they went"*
- Zechariah 9:13–14 — *"I have bent Judah for me. I have filled the bow with Ephraim… Yahweh will be
  seen over them. His arrow will go out as lightning"*
- Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3; Psalm 46:9; Hosea 2:18 — weapons broken or beaten into farm tools as the
  picture of peace
- Ezekiel 1:26–28 — *"As the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain, so
  was the appearance of the brightness all around. This was the appearance of the likeness of
  Yahweh's glory"*
- Revelation 4:3 — *"There was a rainbow around the throne, like an emerald to look at"*
- Revelation 10:1 — a mighty angel *"clothed with a cloud. A rainbow was on his head"*
- Sirach 43:11–12; 50:7 — *"Look at the rainbow, and praise him who made it"*, and the high priest
  in his glory compared to it

## Analysis

**Hebrew has no word for rainbow.** The word at Genesis 9:13 is **qešet**, and it occurs about
seventy-five times in the Hebrew Bible. Everywhere else — every single other occurrence — it is the
weapon: the bow an archer draws. Ishmael becomes *"an archer"*, literally *a shooter of the bow*
(21:20). Isaac tells Esau to take *"your quiver and your bow"* (27:3). Jacob takes a ridge of land
*"with my sword and with my bow"* (48:22). The prophets and the psalms hand the same object to God
when they want him in the field: he bends it, he readies it, he uncovers it, he shoots.

Genesis 9:13 does not say *rainbow*. It says **my bow**, *qaštî*, with the possessive — the same
possessive the warrior texts use. Whatever else the verse is doing, it is not reaching for a nature
word, because there was none to reach for.

**So the sign is a weapon, and the weapon is not in use.** The image the vocabulary produces is a
war-bow hung up in the sky at the end of a campaign — slack, unstrung by the fact of being hung, and
with its arc turned away from the earth. The war in question is the one just fought: God brought the
waters of the deep up and the windows of the sky down against a world *"filled with violence"*, and
the flood is the only conflict in the Bible in which he is unambiguously the attacker and the whole
earth the target. The sign given at the end of it is the instrument put down.

Two features of the passage support the reading rather than merely tolerating it.

1. **The sign is aimed at God, not at us.** *"I will look at it, that I may remember"* (9:16). Every
   other covenant sign in Scripture is something the human party keeps — circumcision cut into the
   body (Genesis 17:11), the Sabbath observed (Exodus 31:13, 16–17). This one is a thing God
   undertakes to look at. A hung-up weapon is exactly the kind of object whose meaning is for its
   owner.
2. **The promise it signs is a self-limitation.** 9:11 is not a promise about what the world will be
   like; it is a promise about what God will not do. A sign for a promise of that shape has to
   represent restraint, and disarmament is the natural picture of restraint by a warrior.

**What cannot be claimed.** No biblical text says *"God laid down his bow"* in so many words, and
the martial sense of *qešet* at 9:13 is an inference from vocabulary rather than a statement of the
verse. It is possible the writer simply used the only available noun for an arc of colour in the
sky and meant nothing military by it. Against that: the noun is possessive (*my* bow), the setting
is the end of a divine assault, and the wider biblical picture of peace is specifically the picture
of weapons decommissioned — swords into ploughshares, spears into pruning hooks, *"the bow, the
sword, and the battle"* broken out of the land (Hosea 2:18, in a verse that is *also* about a
covenant with the animals). The reading is old, it is widely held, and the text does nothing to
resist it. It should be held as a strong reading and not as a translation.

**The bow returns, and it returns around a throne.** Ezekiel, straining to describe what he saw by
the Chebar canal, runs out of nouns and reaches for this one: the brightness around the enthroned
figure was *"as the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain"* (1:28). It
is the last simile in the vision and the one that finally lands the whole thing — the glory of God
looks like the sign of the promise not to destroy. John, seeing the same throne, says it flatly:
*"there was a rainbow around the throne"* (Revelation 4:3), and this is the throne from which the
seals of the book of judgment are about to be opened. In Revelation 10:1 the angel who comes down to
announce that *"there will no longer be delay"* is clothed in a cloud with the rainbow on his head.

The placement is consistent and it is doing something. In all three visions the bow appears at the
moment judgment is about to be described, and in all three it frames the one issuing it. Whatever
comes next, it comes from inside the ring of a promise that a limit is in force.

## Significance

Genesis 9's sign is the Bible's first picture of God constraining his own power, and the picture is
military. That matters for how the covenant is read. The Noahic promise is not an assurance that
God is not dangerous; it is an assurance that he has bound what is dangerous about him, publicly,
with a token he says he will look at. The bow is still his, it is still in the sky, and it is not
drawn.

It also sets the pattern the prophets use whenever they want to say that a covenant cannot fail:
tie it to something in the created order that nobody can interfere with. Jeremiah does it with day
and night (33:20–26), Isaiah with the mountains and the waters of Noah (54:9–10 — see
[[The Waters of Noah - Isaiah 54 and the Covenant of Peace]]), and Genesis does it first with the
weather. A rainbow is not a monument anyone maintains. It appears when a storm does, which is
precisely when the promise is needed, and it appears without being asked for.
