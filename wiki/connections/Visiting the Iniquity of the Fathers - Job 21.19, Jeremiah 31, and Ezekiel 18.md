# Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers - Job 21.19, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 18

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 20:5–6; 34:6–7** — *"visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children, on the third and
  on the fourth generation of those who hate me, and showing loving kindness to thousands"*: the
  principle stated in the Decalogue and in the self-description at Sinai
- **Deuteronomy 24:16** — *"The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, neither shall the
  children be put to death for the fathers. Every man shall be put to death for his own sin."*: the
  principle refused to human courts
- **2 Kings 14:5–6** — Amaziah executes his father's assassins and spares their sons, *"according to
  that which is written in the book of the law of Moses"*, quoting Deuteronomy 24:16
- **Job 5:4** — Eliphaz on the foolish man: *"His children are far from safety"* —
  [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]
- **Job 8:4** — Bildad: *"If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into the hand
  of their disobedience"* — [[Bildad's First Speech]]
- **Job 18:19; 20:10** — the wicked man without son or grandson, and his children begging from the
  poor — [[Bildad's Second Speech]], [[Zophar's Second Speech]]
- **Job 21:19–21** — *"You say, 'God lays up his iniquity for his children.' Let him recompense it to
  himself, that he may know it. Let his own eyes see his destruction … For what does he care for his
  house after him, when the number of his months is cut off?"* — [[Why Do the Wicked Live]]
- **Job 14:21** — *"His sons come to honor, and he doesn't know it. They are brought low, but he
  doesn't perceive it of them"*: the premise of 21:21, stated of every man — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Lamentations 5:7** — *"Our fathers sinned, and are no more. We have borne their iniquities."*
- **Jeremiah 31:29–30** — *"In those days they will say no more, 'The fathers have eaten sour grapes,
  and the children's teeth are set on edge.' But everyone will die for his own iniquity."*
- **Ezekiel 18:1–4, 19–20** — *"What do you mean, that you use this proverb concerning the land of
  Israel … The soul who sins, he shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father."*
- **John 9:1–3** — *"Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus
  answered, "Neither did this man sin, nor his parents."*

- [[The Ten Commandments]] — Exodus 20:5–6, now ingested in its setting of exclusive worship

## Analysis

**Job 21:19 is the third stage of an argument in retreat.** The friends' doctrine began by reading
suffering as the sinner's own payment (4:7–8). When Job pointed at the wicked who do not suffer,
Zophar moved the payment later — *"the triumphing of the wicked is short"* (20:5). When the wicked
man dies at peace anyway, the only place left to collect is his heirs, and the friends have each
gestured there: Eliphaz's children *"far from safety"* (5:4), Bildad's man with *"neither son nor
grandson"* (18:19), Zophar's children begging from the poor (20:10). Job names the move and refuses
it: *"You say, 'God lays up his iniquity for his children.'"*

**His objection has two parts, and both are moral rather than empirical.** The first is that the
wrong person pays — *"Let him recompense it to himself"*. The second, sharper, is that the right
person does not even notice: *"what does he care for his house after him, when the number of his
months is cut off?"* Job has already said that a dead man does not know whether his sons rise or fall
(14:21). If so, deferring the punishment to the children does not punish the father at all. It only
punishes them.

**The Torah holds both sides of this, and knows it.** The Decalogue's God visits iniquity *"on the
third and on the fourth generation of those who hate me"* (Exodus 20:5), and the formula is repeated
as part of the divine name at Sinai (34:7). Deuteronomy 24:16 then forbids exactly that to every human
judge — *"Every man shall be put to death for his own sin"* — and 2 Kings 14:6 shows a king obeying it.
Readers have long reconciled the two by noting the qualifier *"of those who hate me"*: the iniquity
travels where the hatred does, children continuing the fathers' path. But the bare principle, and the
experience of the generation of the exile, outran that reading.

**The exile turned it into a proverb.** *"The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth
are set on edge"* is quoted by Jeremiah (31:29) and by Ezekiel (18:2), and Lamentations 5:7 says it
without the irony: *"Our fathers sinned, and are no more. We have borne their iniquities."* Both
prophets abolish the proverb, Jeremiah as a promise for the days of the new covenant, Ezekiel as a
present ruling and at length: the righteous son of a wicked father lives, the wicked son of a
righteous father dies, and *"the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father"* (18:20).

Job reaches Ezekiel's conclusion by a different road. The prophet argues from God's justice to the
individual; Job argues from the individual's experience to what justice would have to be. And Job's
case is the sharper one, because **the reader knows his own children's deaths were not punishment for
anything** — not for their sins, which the prologue only hypothesises (1:5), and not for their
father's, which the narrator denies (1:1, 8; 2:3). Bildad's 8:4 assumed they died for their own sin;
the doctrine Job attacks at 21:19 would have them dying for someone else's. The truth was neither.

**John 9 rejects both halves of the question at once.** The disciples' *"who sinned, this man or his
parents?"* assumes that the blindness is a payment and only asks whose account it was charged to —
the friends' question exactly, with Bildad's answer and the 21:19 answer as its two options. Jesus
refuses the frame: *"Neither."*

### The warning in its ingested Exodus setting

Exodus 20:5–6 places the generational warning within the command against rival worship. The phrase
about those who hate God is followed by loving kindness to thousands of those who love him and keep
his commands. The raw WEB does not add “generations” after “thousands.” The contrast is substantial,
but it does not supply a full account of how a child's culpability relates to a parent's conduct.

**The tension must remain explicit.** Exodus 20:5 describes iniquity reaching children; Ezekiel
18:19–20 explicitly preserves the life of the righteous son and refuses the transfer of the father's
iniquity. Job 21:19–21 demands recompense to the wrongdoer rather than his children. Continuing the
same hatred, distinguishing shared consequences from individual guilt, or reading a developing
argument across Scripture are possible interpretations. The earlier conclusion here treated the
movement as a settled resolution; that goes beyond what these distinct statements establish on their
own.

## Significance

The texts create a sustained debate about responsibility across generations. The Decalogue's
warning, Deuteronomy's limit on human courts, the exiles' proverb, prophetic insistence on
individual responsibility, Job's objection, and the rejection of the disciples' premise in
John 9 should be read in their own settings. A developmental reading is possible, but **the
tension between Exodus 20:5 and Ezekiel 18:20 is not erased by naming the sequence a conversation**.
Job 21:19 belongs as a protest against charging the wrong person's account.

It also shows the friends' doctrine failing on its own terms. Retribution exists to defend God's
justice. Pushed to the children, it requires God to punish the innocent in order to keep the account
of the guilty balanced, which is the injustice it set out to rule out. Job sees that in one verse.
