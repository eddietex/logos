# Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives

## Analysis

**Job's last question in his first speech to God can be compared with God's
self-description at Sinai.** Exodus 34:6–7 is the nearest thing the Hebrew Bible has to a creed — Yahweh's
name proclaimed by himself to Moses on Sinai, and quoted or echoed more often than any other text
about God's character (Numbers 14:18, Nehemiah 9:17, Psalms 86:15, 103:8 and 145:8, Joel 2:13,
Jonah 4:2, Nahum 1:3). Its central clause is *nōśēʾ ʿāwōn wāpešaʿ wəḥaṭṭāʾâ*: lifting, bearing
away, iniquity and disobedience and sin.

Job 7:21 uses the verb *nāśāʾ* with *pešaʿ*, and pairs it with the hiphil of *ʿābar*, *to cause to
pass*, with *ʿāwōn*. Micah 7:18, the doxology that closes the book of Micah, has the identical pair:
*nōśēʾ ʿāwōn wəʿōbēr ʿal-pešaʿ*, *who lifts iniquity and passes over disobedience*. **Job's two
verbs are the two verbs of Israel's praise of the forgiving God.** He is not inventing a theology of
forgiveness to accuse God with. A deliberate echo is possible; the shared theological language does not establish it by itself.

**And he asks it as a question, conditionally.** *If* he has sinned (7:20). Job never confesses a
specific sin in the book, and two verses earlier he has asked his friends to show him one (6:24).
The question is therefore not *forgive me for what I did* but **why, if there is something, is it
not forgiven — since forgiving is what you said you do?** The logic is that of Moses at Numbers
14:17–19, who quotes Exodus 34 back to God and asks him to act in accordance with it; and of Jonah
4:2, who quotes it back with resentment. Job's version is a third tone: bewilderment. A God who
forgives iniquity is pursuing him as though iniquity were unforgivable, or as though there were
something other than iniquity at stake.

**There is something other at stake**, and the reader knows what it is. Job's suffering is not a
penalty awaiting pardon. It is a test whose subject is precisely whether Job will hold on to God
with nothing to gain (1:9–11; 2:3–5). **Forgiveness cannot end it, because there is nothing it
punishes.** Job's question is the right question for the wrong theology — the friends' theology,
which he partly shares — and the wiki reads it as one of the places where the retribution doctrine
fails from inside: even granting it, the God it describes does not match the God of Exodus 34.

**Two scenes answer the verb.** At 2 Samuel 12:13, after Nathan's parable, *"Yahweh also has put
away (heʿĕbîr) your sin"* — the forgiveness Job asks for, given to a man who had committed adultery
and murder. And at **Zechariah 3:4**, in the Hebrew Bible's only other scene of *haśśāṭān* standing
in God's court, the accusation against the high priest is dismissed and God says ***"I have caused
your iniquity to pass from you"*** — *heʿĕbartî*, the same hiphil of *ʿābar* with the same noun,
*ʿāwōn*. **The accuser's other appearance in a heavenly courtroom ends with exactly what Job asks
for here** — iniquity made to pass — granted not because the defendant was clean (his garments are
filthy) but because God has chosen him. See
[[Ha-Satan - The Accuser in Job, Zechariah, and Chronicles]].

**And the book's ending turns the verb *nāśāʾ* round.** At 42:8 God tells the friends that Job will
pray for them, *"for I will accept him"* — *kî ʾim-pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, literally *I will lift up his
face*. The friends are the ones whose folly needs forgiving, and Job is the one whose face God
lifts. The man who asked why God would not *lift* his disobedience ends the book with his face
lifted and his prayer forgiving others.

### The renewal in its narrative setting — Exodus 34

The proclamation now appears in its narrative setting in [[The Covenant Renewed]]. Moses hears forgiveness
and judgment declared together, bows, and asks for pardon for the people (34:6–9). Job 7:20–21
asks why pardon is withheld; 9:28 and 10:14 fear being held guilty. Comparing these verified WEB
passages shows a real tension in the experience of the forgiving God. **It does not by itself
establish a deliberate quotation of Exodus by Job or the literary direction of dependence.** The
earlier assertion that Job is quoting what God gave was too categorical; a possible echo or
theological comparison is the appropriate claim.

Numbers 14:17–20, by contrast, explicitly frames its appeal as based on what God has spoken. The
parallel with Jonah's different response is now on [[Merciful and Gracious - Exodus 34, Numbers 14, and Jonah 4]]. These additions compare the
English WEB: local Exodus and Job, and Numbers 14:17–23 and Jonah 4:1–3 verified at
bible-api.com on 2026-10-24. They do not independently reverify the older Hebrew analyses
elsewhere on this page.

## Significance

- **Job's question can be read alongside the proclamation.** His complaints are often read as unbelief. 7:21 is
  a theological argument from Israel's most quoted confession about God, and it can be read as expecting
  God to act in the forgiving way Exodus 34 describes. His protest is the gap between that God and what is
  happening to him.
- **It exposes a limit of the retribution framework that both sides of the dialogue use.** If
  suffering is penalty, the remedy is forgiveness, and a forgiving God should have granted it. The
  framework cannot explain why it has not. The book's answer is that the framework is wrong about
  this case, not that God is unforgiving. See [[Retribution]] and [[Forgiveness]].
- **The question is answered later in the canon in a form Job could not have imagined.** Zechariah 3
  is the first scene in which God silences the accuser by removing iniquity, and the New Testament
  builds on it: *"Who could bring a charge against God's chosen ones? It is God who justifies"*
  (Romans 8:33). The wiki records this as the shape of a thread, not as something Job anticipates.
