## Analysis

### Correction from Leviticus 25:23–34

The earlier assertions below that the Levites have no fields or property are **too broad
and contradicted by Leviticus 25:32–34**. Those verses give them city houses with enduring
redemption rights and surrounding fields that may not be sold. The later no-inheritance
references concern tribal allotment and must not be used to deny these explicit holdings.
The older wording is retained below as the interpretation being corrected, not a second
conclusion. Genesis 47:22, 26 protects Egyptian priests from sale; Leviticus protects Levite
holdings differently, but neither group is simply without property.

The reason the older text gives, *because the Levites have no fields*, is also not the
text's: verse 33 says the houses *"are their possession among the children of Israel."*

The general land prohibition (23) also stands beside a permanent walled-city house sale
(30), village-house return (31), and the Levite exception (32–34). Thus neither universal
return of every house nor a blanket prohibition of every Egyptian transaction follows.
See [[Redemption of Land and Houses]]. Sources: stored WEB Genesis 47 and Leviticus 25.


### Qualification from Leviticus 25:1–22

The older formulation below that every element of Joseph's transaction is forbidden is
**too broad**. Genesis 47:18–26 reports crown ownership with a priestly exception;
Leviticus 25:13–16 permits transactions but prices the remaining crops before return.
These are contrasting arrangements, not an explicit verdict on Joseph in Leviticus.
The rhetorical claim below is retained as the earlier interpretation and is qualified
by these passages. Likewise, the later laws preserve distinctions among houses and
kinds of service (25:29–34, 39–46); Jubilee cannot mean that every person receives a
field or that every form of servitude ends.

See [[The Sabbatical Year and the Jubilee]]. Sources for this qualification: stored WEB
Genesis 47:18–26 and Leviticus 25. Leviticus 25 is now ingested through 25:55. Earlier wording here, *sold for debt*, is corrected:
25:39 names poverty and does not mention debt. WEB 25:39 is *to serve as a slave*.


**Genesis 47:20 and Leviticus 25:23 are the same transaction, one performed and one prohibited.**

> **47:20** So Joseph bought all the land of Egypt for Pharaoh, for every man of the Egyptians sold
> his field, because the famine was severe on them, and the land became Pharaoh's.

> **Leviticus 25:23** The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are
> strangers and live as foreigners with me.

**Every element of the Egyptian transaction is named and forbidden by the Israelite law**, and the
law was given to the descendants of the man who conducted it.

| | **Egypt, Genesis 47** | **Israel, Leviticus 25** |
|---|---|---|
| **who owns the land** | Pharaoh, after 47:20 | *"the land is mine"* — God (25:23) |
| **may a field be sold outright?** | it is, by every man in the country | **no** — *"not … in perpetuity"* (25:23) |
| **what happens to a sold field** | it stays sold; the statute is *to this day* (47:26) | it returns at the jubilee (25:13, 28) |
| **the poor man's own person** | *"buy us … we will be servants to Pharaoh"* (47:19) | *"you shall not make him to serve as a slave"* (25:39–40) |
| **why** | because the famine was severe (47:20) | *"for they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt"* (25:42) |
| **the resulting tenure** | tenancy at a fifth, indefinitely (47:24, 26) | possession restored *"to his family"* (25:10) |

**The reason clause in the last row is the connection's whole point.** **Leviticus grounds the
prohibition on the exodus** — *"they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt; they
shall not be sold as slaves"* (25:42). **The condition Israel may not be reduced to is, in so many
words, the condition Genesis 47 reduced Egypt to**, and the ground for the prohibition is that God
took them out of the country where it happened.

### *"Strangers and foreigners with me"*

The two Hebrew phrases are checked against Miqra according to the Masorah (Sefaria), Leviticus
25:23 and Genesis 23:4; the inherited *məgûray* of 47:9 was not rechecked in this pass.

***Gērîm wətôšābîm*** (Leviticus 25:23) **is the patriarchs' own self-description** — *"I am a
stranger and a foreigner living with you"*, Abraham to the Hittites, buying the cave (Genesis 23:4),
**and *"the days of the years of my pilgrimage"*, [[Jacob]] to Pharaoh** (47:9). See
[[A Stranger and a Foreigner - The Sojourner's Confession]].

**The law takes the patriarchs' landlessness and makes it permanent doctrine.** **Israel in its own
country is to hold land on the same terms Abraham held it in someone else's — as a resident alien of
the owner — and the practical consequence is that no Israelite can ever be permanently separated from
a field.** **Egypt is the demonstration of what the alternative looks like.**

### Two exceptions, pointing opposite ways

**Both systems exempt one class, and the exemptions are mirror images.**

- **Egypt exempts the priests** (47:22, 26): they keep their land because the crown already feeds
  them.
- **Israel exempts houses in walled cities** (Leviticus 25:29–30), **which may be sold in perpetuity
  — and then exempts the Levites' city houses from that exemption** (25:32–34), **because the Levites
  have no fields to return to.**

**In Egypt the priesthood is the one group whose property is safe. In Israel the priesthood is the
one group that has none.** See [[The Priests Who Kept Their Land and the Levites Who Got None]].

### Naboth

**The clearest illustration that the law was understood this way is a man refusing a good offer.**
Ahab wants a vineyard next to the palace and offers a better one or its value in money; **Naboth's
answer is not a price** but *"Yahweh forbid it me, that I should give the inheritance of my fathers
to you"* (1 Kings 21:3). **He is not being obstinate; he is citing Leviticus 25.** **The narrative
then shows a king obtaining the field by judicial murder**, because the one thing he cannot do is
buy it — **and a prophet is sent to say that this is the end of his house.**

**Genesis 47 has a king obtaining every field in his kingdom lawfully, by purchase, from willing
sellers, and nobody is sent to say anything at all.**

## Significance

**The pairing is one of the sharpest instances in Scripture of narrative and law standing in direct
opposition, with no cross-reference between them.** **Genesis reports an achievement; Leviticus
outlaws it; and the connective tissue is the exodus that happened in between.**

**It also explains why Genesis 47:13–26 reads uncomfortably to readers who cannot say why.** **The
passage offends a principle the reader has absorbed from elsewhere in the Bible** — that land is not
finally alienable because it is not finally the seller's — **and Genesis has not stated that principle
yet.** **It is stated, for the first time, at Leviticus 25:23**, and the wiki notes that the law
arrives after the narrative rather than before it, which is the usual order in the Pentateuch and not
an accident. See [[Land and Sojourning]], [[Wealth and Possessions]] and [[Kingship and Empire]].
