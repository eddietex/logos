# Care for the Animal - Proverbs 12.10 and Exodus 23

## Passages involved

- [[Whoever Loves Correction]] — Proverbs 12:10.
- [[Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts]] — Exodus 23:4–5, 11–12.

## Analysis

Proverbs 12:10 makes care for an animal a distinguishing act of righteousness:

> A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel.

The contrast judges mercy by its effect on a dependent creature. Ownership does not erase
responsibility for the animal’s life, and calling an act merciful does not make it gentle.

Exodus 23 supplies concrete legal cases alongside this character portrait. An enemy’s straying ox or
donkey must be returned, and the donkey of someone who hates you must be helped under its burden
(4–5). The seventh year leaves food for the poor and then the animals of the field (11); the seventh
day gives rest to the ox and donkey as well as refreshment to dependent people (12). The scope
therefore includes one’s own working animals, an enemy’s animals, and animals feeding on the land’s
unharvested produce.

The texts agree in placing limits on human use of animals, but their forms differ: a proverb
evaluates character, while law directs action and a calendar. Proverbs does not explicitly cite
Exodus, and this comparison establishes no direct borrowing. Nor does either passage state a
prohibition of all animal use or a complete theory of animal rights.

## Significance

[[Righteousness]] and [[Compassion and Mercy]] reach beyond dealings with other humans. [[Work]]
depends on creatures whose needs must be respected, and [[Law and Obedience]] gives that respect
concrete expression in assistance, food, and rest.
