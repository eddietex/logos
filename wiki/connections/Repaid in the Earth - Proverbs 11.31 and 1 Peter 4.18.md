# Repaid in the Earth - Proverbs 11.31 and 1 Peter 4.18

## Passages involved

- [[A False Balance]] — Proverbs 11:31, with 11:8, 21.
- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:3.
- 1 Peter 4:17–19 — WEB verified; not yet ingested.

## Analysis

**A shared argument, different English formulations.** Proverbs 11:31 in the stored WEB says:

> Behold, the righteous shall be repaid in the earth; how much more the wicked and the sinner!

First Peter 4:18 in the verified WEB says:

> “If it is hard for the righteous to be saved, what will happen to the ungodly and the sinner?”

Both reason from what happens to the righteous to the more serious prospect facing the wicked. Yet
the wording must not be flattened: Proverbs speaks of repayment in the earth; First Peter speaks of
difficulty in being saved. The comparison here is established at the level of these verified English
texts. The source-language form and the precise textual route linking the passages have not been
checked in this pass, so no claim of exact Hebrew or Greek correspondence is made.

**First Peter supplies a setting that Proverbs’ isolated saying does not.** Verse 17 begins with
judgment at God’s household and asks about those who do not obey the Good News. Verse 19 addresses
people suffering according to God’s will while doing good, directing them to entrust themselves to a
faithful Creator. Righteousness therefore does not mean exemption from suffering in this context.

Proverbs 11:31 itself also resists a simple picture in which only the wicked face an accounting: the
righteous are repaid too. But it does not explain every sufferer’s circumstances. Job 2:3 explicitly
describes the upright man’s ruin as without cause, and Proverbs 11:8, 21 must not be used to reverse
that verdict. The texts are placed together without making Job’s disaster either hidden guilt or an
instance of the specific judgment addressed in First Peter.

Sources: Proverbs 11 and Job 2:3 checked in stored WEB; 1 Peter 4:17–19 checked through
bible-api.com with `translation=web`, 2026-10-25. Un-ingested status is separate from this
verification.

## Significance

The greater-consequence argument calls the reader to take accountability seriously. It does not
authorize inferring a person’s guilt from suffering. See [[Judgment]], [[Retribution]], and
[[Suffering]].
