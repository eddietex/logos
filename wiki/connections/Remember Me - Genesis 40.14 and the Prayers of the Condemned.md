# Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned

## Passages involved

- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:14, 23: *"remember me when it will be well with you"*, and
  *"didn't remember Joseph, but forgot him"*
- [[Pharaoh's Dreams and Joseph's Rise]] — Genesis 41:9: *"I remember my faults today"* — ***mazkîr***,
  **the causative of 40:14's verb, with the speaker's own offences as its object rather than Joseph**
- Judges 16:28 — Samson: *"Lord Yahweh, remember me, please, and strengthen me, please, only this once"*
- 1 Samuel 1:11 — Hannah: *"if you will… remember me, and not forget your servant"*
- Nehemiah 5:19; 13:14, 22, 31 — *"Remember me, my God, for good"*, four times, closing a book
- Job 14:13 — *"that you would appoint me a set time and remember me"*
- Isaiah 38:3 — Hezekiah: *"Remember now, Yahweh, I beg you, how I have walked before you"*
- Psalm 25:6–7; 106:4 — *"remember your tender mercies"*; *"remember me, Yahweh, with the favor that you
  show to your people"*
- Ecclesiastes 9:15 — *"yet no one remembered that same poor man"*
- Luke 23:42 — *"Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom"*

## Analysis

**The shortest prayers in Scripture are two words long, and the first person to say them is not
praying.**

> **14** But remember me when it will be well with you, and please show kindness to me, and make mention
> of me to Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house. (Genesis 40:14)

***Zəkartanî*** — *remember me* — **and then, three words later, *wəhizkartanî*, the causative: *cause
Pharaoh to remember me*. The verb twice in one verse, in two stems.** Joseph asks a restored courtier
for three things in ascending order: **be remembered, be treated with *ḥesed*, be got out.**

**Every other request to be remembered in the Bible is addressed to God.** Samson at the pillars,
Hannah at Shiloh, Hezekiah on his sickbed, Job wanting to be hidden in Sheol and recalled, Nehemiah
four times over, **and the man dying beside Jesus.** Genesis 40:14 is **the only one aimed at a
human being** — and it is also **the only one that fails.**

**The theme page [[Remembrance]] covers what *zākar* means when God is its subject**: not recollection
but action, the verb that turns the flood at 8:1 and opens Rachel's womb at 30:22, always followed by an
act in the same clause. **This page is about the other end of it — the petition form**, and about what
happens when the verb is asked of someone who cannot supply what God supplies.

### Joseph's request is answered by its own negation

**Genesis sets the request and its failure four verses apart in a chapter of twenty-three**, and states
the failure twice over:

> **23** Yet the chief cup bearer didn’t remember Joseph, but forgot him.

***Lōʾ-zākar … wayyiškāḥēhû.*** **A negative and its positive twin, which Hebrew does not usually
bother with**, and the doubling is the point: this is not an oversight the narrator is covering for.
**And it is the last verse of the chapter**, so the book leaves the reader holding it for the whole of
41:1's *"two full years."*

**Ecclesiastes 9:15 is the same sentence turned into a proverb** — a poor wise man who saved a city by
his wisdom, *"yet no one remembered that same poor man."* Qoheleth states as a general law of the world
what Genesis 40 reports as one thing that happened to one person.

### The Greek makes the link exact

**In the Septuagint, Genesis 40:14 reads *mnēsthēti mou* — and that is, word for word, what the dying
man beside Jesus says.**

*"Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom"* (Luke 23:42) — and the manuscripts differ over
whether the address is *Jesus* or *Lord*, which is the only part of the line that varies.

**Whether Luke is alluding is not decidable and the wiki does not claim it** — *mnēsthēti mou* is the
natural Greek for the natural Hebrew, and any Greek speaker asking to be remembered would say it. **What
is worth recording is the setting, which is not natural at all.**

| | **Genesis 40** | **Luke 23** |
|---|---|---|
| speaker | a prisoner under sentence | a man under sentence |
| addressee | a fellow prisoner about to be released | a fellow condemned man about to die |
| the pair | **two officers, one restored and one hanged** | **two criminals, one who mocks and one who asks** |
| the interval | *"within three more days"* | *"today you will be with me in Paradise"* |
| the answer | **two years, and a forgetting** | **the same day** |

**Both scenes are three men under a death sentence, of whom one is innocent.** In Genesis the innocent
man is the one who interprets and the one who asks. **In Luke he is the one asked.** The request runs
the opposite way, and it is the only one of the two that is granted.

**And in both, the phrase is a request for a verdict to be reversed by someone with access.** Joseph
wants a word spoken to Pharaoh; the thief wants a word spoken in a kingdom. **Neither is asking to be
thought about.**

## Significance

**The thread's use is that it keeps Genesis 40:23 from being read as a moral about Joseph.**

The devotional tradition has long taken 40:14 as a lapse — trusting an Egyptian rather than God — and
**Genesis Rabbah ties the two years of 41:1 to the two forms of *zākar* in the verse**, one year per
stem. **The text supports none of this.** There is no narrator's comment, no divine speech, and no
consequence named as a consequence; **and 40:23's grammatical subject is the cupbearer.** Set beside
Samson, Hannah, Job and Nehemiah, **asking to be remembered is what people in Scripture do when they
have no other leverage** — it is the posture of the powerless, not a failure of faith. Joseph's only
distinctive is that he asked the wrong person, which is a comment on his options and not on his trust.
See [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]], where both readings are set out and neither adopted.

**The second thing the thread shows is how small the vocabulary is.** From a slave in an Egyptian jail
to a man on a Roman cross, **the Bible's request of last resort never changes its words.** What changes
is who can answer it.
