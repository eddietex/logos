# Leave Your Country - The Two Lek-Lekha Commands

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 12:1** — *"Leave your country, and your relatives, and your father's house, and go to the
  land that I will show you."* — [[The Call of Abram]]
- **Genesis 22:2** — *"Now take your son, your only son, whom you love, even Isaac, and go into the
  land of Moriah. Offer him there for a burnt offering on one of the mountains which I will tell you
  of."* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 12:4** — *"So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him."*
- **Genesis 22:3** — *"Abraham rose early in the morning, and saddled his donkey."*
- **Hebrews 11:8, 17–19** — the two obediences, treated as one argument
- **Genesis Rabbah 39:9; 55:7** — the rabbinic pairing of the first trial and the last

## Analysis

**The phrase occurs twice in the Hebrew Bible and both times it is spoken to the same man.**
לֶךְ־לְךָ, *lek-ləkā* — *go, for yourself* — is at Genesis 12:1 and Genesis 22:2 and nowhere else.
The idiom is a *dativus ethicus*, a reflexive pronoun with no work to do in the sentence except to
throw the verb back on the person hearing it; Rashi reads it *"go for your own benefit"*, others
hear an intensifier, *just go*. In Hebrew the doubled two-letter root is audible, and no English
translation carries it. Everything below follows from noticing that a phrase used twice in a
thousand pages is used at the two ends of one life.

**The two commands have the same shape, clause for clause.**

| | Genesis 12:1 | Genesis 22:2 |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | *lek-ləkā* | *lek-ləkā* |
| What is surrendered | your country, your kindred, your father's house | your son, your only son, whom you love, Isaac |
| Structure of the loss | three ablatives, narrowing | four appositions, narrowing |
| Destination | *"the land that I will show you"* | *"one of the mountains which I will tell you of"* |
| Response | *"So Abram went"* — no reply, no question | *"Abraham rose early in the morning"* — no reply, no question |

Both commands strip a man of the thing that makes a future possible: first everything behind him,
then the one thing in front of him. Both withhold the destination and supply it later. Both are
answered by a verb of departure and by no speech at all — which is the more striking in chapter 22,
where Abraham has by then argued God down over Sodom (18:22–33) and is entirely capable of speaking
when he wants to.

**The narrowing is the same rhetorical device both times, and it runs the wrong way on purpose.**
*Your country* → *your kindred* → *your father's house* is geographically a zoom inward but
emotionally a climb: each item is harder to leave than the one before, and the last is the household
he eats in. *Your son* → *your only son* → *whom you love* → *Isaac* does the same thing in four
steps, ending on the name, and refusing every off-ramp — as if the sentence anticipates each
possible evasion (*which son?* — the only one; *the one you can spare?* — the one you love) and
closes it. Genesis is using one technique at both hinges of the story.

**And the second command threatens the first.** The whole content of 12:2 is *"I will make of you a
great nation"*; by 22:2 the nation exists as one boy, and the God who promised it asks for him back.
That is why the pairing is not a curiosity of vocabulary. The two *lek-ləkā* commands mark the
opening and the closing of the promise's exposure: the first asks Abraham to give up the past he
came from, the second to give up the future he was given, and the phrase that introduces both is the
signal that the second is meant to be read as the first one repeated at a higher price.

**Genesis Rabbah makes the pairing explicit** and frames the whole cycle as ten trials running from
*lek-ləkā* to *lek-ləkā* (39:9; 55:7). The reading is old, it is not imposed on the text, and it is
one of the clearest cases in Genesis of a verbal echo carrying an interpretive claim.

**Hebrews 11 treats the two as one argument** without quoting the Hebrew idiom at all. *"By faith,
Abraham, when he was called, obeyed to go out to the place which he was to receive … and he went out,
not knowing where he went"* (11:8), and then *"By faith, Abraham, being tested, offered up Isaac …
concluding that God is able to raise up even from the dead"* (11:17–19). What the letter draws out is
exactly what the parallel structure does: in both cases the destination is unknown and the man goes.
Where Genesis supplies silence, Hebrews supplies a reasoning to fill it — *"concluding that"* — which
is a reading of the gap rather than a report of it.

## Significance

**The Abraham cycle is bracketed rather than sequential.** Genesis rarely marks its own structure by
vocabulary this cleanly. Two verses, ten chapters apart, share a phrase used nowhere else in the
Bible, and each is a command to hand over the thing a life is built on. Everything between them —
Egypt, Lot, the covenant, Hagar, the new name, Sodom, Isaac's birth — sits inside that frame.

**And the second command has no third.** After Moriah, God does not speak to Abraham again in
Genesis except through the angel's oath (22:15–18); the remaining chapters are a burial, a marriage
arranged by proxy, and a death. The two *lek-ləkā*s are the whole of what he is asked, and the
Bible's pattern case of faith is built out of a man who twice said nothing and went.
