## Analysis

**The same two verses, twice.** Proverbs 6:10–11 and 24:33–34 are one saying placed in two
collections. In the Masoretic text (Sefaria, *Miqra according to the Masorah*) the first verse is
letter for letter the same in both places: *məʿaṭ šēnôṯ məʿaṭ tənûmôṯ məʿaṭ ḥibbuq yāḏayim
liškāḇ*. The second differs only in small spellings — *kimhallēḵ*, *like a prowler* (6:11),
against *miṯhallēḵ*, *one who goes about* (24:34); *rēʾšeḵā* against *rêšeḵā*, *your poverty*;
and *maḥsōrəḵā* against the plural-looking *maḥsōreyḵā*, *your want* — and the WEB renders the
two almost identically. Which placement is older is not recoverable, and the wiki claims nothing
about it. The book's editors evidently thought the saying belonged in both collections.

**Two ways to the same lesson.** In chapter 6 the father sends the son to *look at an ant* —
*"Consider her ways"* (*rəʾēh*, *see*). In chapter 24 the speaker reports what *he* saw — *"I saw,
and considered well. I saw, and received instruction"* — and what he saw was a sluggard's
vineyard gone to thorns and a broken wall. **The first lesson is from a creature that works; the
second is from a man who did not.** Between them they give the two halves of Proverbs' method:
wisdom is learned by watching the world, and the world shows both the way and its opposite.

**Agur's ant.** Proverbs 30:25 picks up the same verb and nouns as 6:8: the ants *yāḵînû baqqayiṣ
laḥmām*, *prepare their bread in the summer*, where 6:8 has *tāḵîn baqqayiṣ laḥmāh*. In chapter 6
the ant's point is that she needs no overseer; in chapter 30 it is that she has no strength —
*"not a strong people"* — and is wise anyway. Both sayings make the ant wise by foresight.

**The ant and the officers.** 6:7 says the ant has *"no chief, overseer, or ruler"*, and the
middle word, *šōṭēr*, is the one Exodus uses for the Israelite officers who stood between
Pharaoh's taskmasters and the brickmakers and were beaten when the quota failed (Exodus 5:6, 14).
The wiki does not claim Proverbs alludes to Exodus; the word is an ordinary administrative title.
But it sets the two pictures of work side by side: the work of Israel in Egypt was compelled from
above, and the ant's is self-moved. The sluggard needs a *šōṭēr* and has none.

**Work before the curse.** The ant is not an image of toil. Genesis puts the man in the garden
*"to cultivate and keep it"* (2:15) before the fall, and the curse of 3:17–19 falls on the ground,
not on work as such. The sluggard refuses the first vocation, not the penalty. See [[Work]].

### The sentence collection — Proverbs 10:4–5, 26

The summer gathering and harvest sleep of 10:5 bring the ant’s lesson into a household. Verse 26
adds the experience of those who send the sluggard: idleness frustrates people depending on him.
Verse 15 acknowledges poverty’s destructive force without assigning its cause, so the work sayings
do not diagnose every poor person.

### Proverbs 22 in context

Proverbs 22:13 gives the sluggard's excuse: *"There is a lion outside! I will be killed in the
streets!"* The danger is invented to avoid going out, and the saying is a joke at his expense.
Proverbs 26:13 ([[Answer a Fool]]) repeats it: *"There is a lion in the road! A fierce lion
roams the streets!"*

### The field of the sluggard — Proverbs 24:27, 30–34

The doublet's second placement is now ingested. The observation is framed by an unwritten contrast
three verses earlier: *"Prepare your work outside, and get your fields ready. Afterwards, build your
house"* (24:27). The sluggard's field is the field that was never readied. Its owner is *"the man void
of understanding"*, *ʾāḏām ḥăsar-lēḇ* — the phrase of the *"young man void of understanding"* at 7:7,
the other scene Proverbs narrates in the first person — and the speaker's response, *"I saw, and
considered well. I saw, and received instruction"* (24:32), is the observational method of 6:6's
*"Consider her ways"* applied to a ruin. The thorns and broken wall are taken up on
[[The Vineyard Gone to Thorns - Proverbs 24.30-34, Genesis 3.18, and Isaiah 5.1-7]].

### The sluggard's four sayings — Proverbs 26:13–16

Chapter 26 gathers four sayings on the sluggard, and two of them repeat earlier ones. The lion
(26:13) varies 22:13: it gives two Hebrew words for the beast, *šaḥal* and *ʾărî*, and drops the
sluggard's *"I will be killed"*. The hand buried in the dish (26:15) repeats the first line of 19:24
exactly in Hebrew, *ṭāman ʿāṣēl yāḏô baṣṣallāḥaṯ*; the second line now gives a reason — he is
*nilʾāh*, *weary*, *"too lazy to bring it back to his mouth"*. New are the door turning *"on its
hinges"*, as he turns on his bed and goes nowhere (26:14), and the verdict on his self-esteem: *"The
sluggard is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who answer with discretion"* (26:16). The hand he is
too weary *to bring back* (*lahăšîḇāh*) and the answers the seven *bring back* (*məšîḇê ṭāʿam*) share
the causative of *šûḇ*. The last portrait of the sluggard therefore adds no new laziness. It adds
self-deception: he is also a man *"wise in his own eyes"*. See
[[Wise in His Own Eyes - Proverbs 3.7, 26.12, Isaiah 5.21, and Romans 12.16]].

See [[Answer a Fool]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19:24; 22:13; 26:13–16; Hebrew of 19:24, 22:13,
and 26:13–16 read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-26.

## Significance

The sluggard is one of Proverbs' recurring types (10:26; 24:30–34; 26:13–16), and this is the
first sketch of him. The doublet shows something about the book's making: a single saying could
be placed twice, in an instruction (chapter 6) and in an observation (chapter 24), and the editors
let both stand. The ant appears twice as well. Taken together, the passages ask the reader to see
diligence as wisdom and laziness as folly — and to notice that the poverty in both doublets is
described as an intruder, *"a robber"* and *"an armed man"*, not as a slow decline. Proverbs'
sayings about poverty and laziness are general; the book also knows *"Better is the poor who walks
in his integrity"* (19:1), and the wiki keeps the sluggard sayings from becoming a diagnosis of every
poor man, as it kept Bildad's sayings from becoming a diagnosis of Job. See [[Retribution]] and
[[Wealth and Possessions]].
