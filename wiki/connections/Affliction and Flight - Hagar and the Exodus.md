# Affliction and Flight - Hagar and the Exodus

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 15:13** — *"Know for sure that your offspring will live as foreigners in a land that is
  not theirs, and will serve them. They will afflict them four hundred years."*
- **Genesis 16:6** — *"Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled from her face."*
- **Genesis 16:7–11** — the angel finds her in the wilderness at a spring; *"Yahweh has heard your
  affliction."*
- **Exodus 1:11–12** — *"Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens
  … But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied."*
- **Exodus 2:15, 23–25** — Moses *"fled from the face of Pharaoh"* and sits down by a well in Midian;
  *"God heard their groaning … God saw the children of Israel, and God was concerned about them."*
- **Exodus 3:7** — *"I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and have heard
  their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows."*

## Analysis

One chapter after Abram is told that his offspring will be afflicted in a land that is not theirs,
his household afflicts an Egyptian.

**The verb is the same and it is not a common one in this sense.** *ʿĀnâ* in the piel — to humble,
oppress, afflict — is what Genesis 15:13 says Egypt will do to Israel (*wəʿinnû ʾōtām*), what Exodus
1:11–12 says Egypt does do (*ləʿannōtô bə-siblōtām*), and what Genesis 16:6 says Sarai does to Hagar
(*wattəʿannehā*). The command at 16:9 is its reflexive: *hitʿannî taḥat yādêhā*, **afflict yourself
under her hands**. Genesis 16 is the only place in the patriarchal narratives where the exodus's word
for slavery is used of something a patriarch's household does.

**And the escape route matches.**

| | Genesis 16 | Exodus 1–3 |
|---|---|---|
| who is afflicted | one Egyptian, by Hebrews | Hebrews, by Egyptians |
| the verb | *ʿānâ* (16:6) | *ʿānâ* (Exodus 1:11–12) |
| the flight | *she **fled** from her face* — *bāraḥ* (16:6) | Moses *"**fled** from the face of Pharaoh"* — *bāraḥ* (Exodus 2:15) |
| where to | the wilderness, toward Egypt | the wilderness of Midian, away from Egypt |
| what is there | a spring, and the angel of Yahweh (16:7) | a well (Exodus 2:15), and later a bush and the angel of Yahweh (Exodus 3:2) |
| what God does | *"Yahweh has heard your affliction"* (16:11) | *"I have surely seen the affliction … and have heard their cry"* (Exodus 3:7) |
| the promise | offspring beyond counting (16:10) | a land, and a people brought out |
| the outcome | **she is sent back** (16:9) | **they are brought out** |

The pairing is close enough at the level of vocabulary that it cannot be accidental, and the sequence
is close enough that Genesis 15:13 reads like the setup: the reader is told what affliction in
servitude looks like, and then immediately shown a household doing it.

### The inversion, and what it costs to state it carefully

The neat version — *Israel afflicts an Egyptian before Egypt afflicts Israel* — is worth having, and
worth two qualifications.

First, **Genesis makes nothing of it explicitly.** No narrator's aside connects the two, no later text
in the Old Testament cites Hagar when Israel is enslaved, and Exodus never looks back at Genesis 16.
The link lives entirely in shared words.

Second, **the direction of the parallel changes what it means.** In Exodus, God hears the affliction
and *breaks the oppressor* to end it. In Genesis 16, God hears the affliction and *sends the afflicted
back to it*. That asymmetry is the honest content of the comparison, and softening it — by treating
16:9 as an interim measure or by reading the promise as compensation — is doing work the text does not
do. What the text does supply is that Hagar goes back holding a promise, and that Genesis 21 releases
her from the arrangement altogether without her having to run.

### The place of the meeting

Both stories put the divine encounter at water in the wilderness, at the moment of flight, before
anything has been resolved. Hagar at a spring on the road to Shur; Moses at a well in Midian, and then
at Horeb. Israel, coming out, will walk into the wilderness of Shur itself (Exodus 15:22) and find no
water for three days. The Bible's God is met at the edge of the desert by people who are running, and
it happens for the first time to a slave nobody is looking for.

## Significance

- **It makes Genesis 16 the exodus in miniature and in reverse**, which is a strong claim to hang on
  vocabulary — so this page hangs it on exactly the vocabulary and no more: *ʿānâ*, *bāraḥ*, the
  wilderness water, and *God heard the affliction*.
- **It complicates the exodus's moral geometry before the exodus happens.** Israel's founding memory
  is *we were slaves and God brought us out*, and the humane laws are grounded in it (Deuteronomy
  15:15; 24:18). Genesis has already recorded the founding household on the other side of the
  transaction, without comment, and Deuteronomy 23:15–16 — which forbids returning a runaway slave —
  is the Torah eventually legislating against what Genesis 16:9 commands.
- **It is why the word *ʿŏnî* in Ishmael's naming matters.** *"Yahweh has heard your affliction"*
  (16:11) uses the noun that Exodus 3:7 will use of Israel in Egypt. The boy's name is a permanent
  record that God heard a slave's affliction four hundred years before he heard a nation's.
