## Analysis

**Exodus 29:14 is the first sin offering in Exodus burned outside the camp.** The bull's blood and
fat belong to the altar. Its meat, skin, and dung are taken away and burned. Exodus does not give the
reason. It does show that this offering is made for the priests themselves, who lay their hands on
it before they have served (29:10).

**Hebrews reads the location as a pattern.** Hebrews 13:11 speaks of animals *"whose blood is brought
into the holy place by the high priest as an offering for sin"*, whose bodies are burned *"outside of
the camp"*. Hebrews 13:12 continues: *"Therefore Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people
through his own blood, suffered outside of the gate."* Then 13:13 calls readers to *"go out to him
outside of the camp, bearing his reproach"*.

**The closest Torah analogue for Hebrews' wording is not Exodus 29.** The ordination bull's blood is
put on the altar's horns and at its base (Exodus 29:12; Leviticus 8:15), not brought into the holy
place. Hebrews' description fits offerings whose blood enters the sanctuary, which Exodus has not yet
narrated. Exodus 29 shares the disposal outside the camp and the category of sin offering. It is
not the specific rite Hebrews describes, and this page does not claim that it is.

**Hebrews 7:27 contrasts priests who need offerings for themselves.** Exodus 29 begins the priests'
service with a sin offering on their behalf and then establishes daily offerings (29:38–42). Hebrews
contrasts that with one who *"did this once for all, when he offered up himself"* (7:27).

### The sanctuary blood and the clean ash site — Leviticus 4:1–21

The now-ingested priestly and congregational offerings supply both features together:
blood enters the Tent of Meeting (5–7, 16–18), and the bull's body is burned outside the
camp (11–12, 21). This is a closer ritual parallel to Hebrews 13:11 than the ordination
bull of Exodus 29 alone. The sevenfold sprinkling is before the veil; Leviticus 4 does
not describe entry behind it or the annual Day of Atonement.

The place outside must be clean (12). Hebrews' application to suffering and reproach
(13:12–13) therefore should not be turned into a claim that Leviticus calls this disposal
site unclean. The typological connection concerns blood, bodies, and location; it does
not erase the distinct purposes and settings of the texts.

### Ruler and individual — a distinct procedure

**Limit on the category:** Leviticus 4:22–35 prescribes blood on the outer burnt-offering
altar and does not command disposal outside the camp. The preceding bulls’ treatment
cannot be generalized to every sin offering. Leviticus 6:24–30, now ingested in
[[The Priests’ Portions of Sin and Trespass Offerings]], distinguishes priestly eating from the burning required when blood enters
the tent. This reinforces the importance of the specific blood-and-disposal pattern
already discussed above, rather than adding a new claim about Hebrews’ wording.

See [[The Sin Offering for Ruler and Individual]]. Source for this addition: stored WEB Leviticus
4:22–35 and 6:24–30. No new quotation from an unstored book or source-language claim.

### Priestly portions — Leviticus 6:24–7:10

Leviticus 6:26, 29 permits priestly eating of sin-offering flesh, while 6:30 forbids
it when blood is brought into the tent for atonement in the Holy Place. This now-ingested
rule expressly distinguishes the cases described in Leviticus 4. Verse 6:30 commands
burning but does not itself repeat the outside-camp location supplied in 4:12, 21.
Calling every sin offering either eaten or burned would contradict one of these clauses.
The addition establishes the Leviticus distinction; it introduces no further claim about
Hebrews’ wording.

Source for this addition: stored WEB Leviticus 2; 4; 6:24–7:10.

### The consecration now ingested — Leviticus 8

Leviticus 8:15–17 now narrates the ordination bull’s blood on the altar and the burning
of its body outside the camp. The blood is not reported as entering the tent. Thus the
prohibition on eating sanctuary-blood sin offerings in Leviticus 6:30 does not state the
only circumstance in which sin-offering flesh must burn. Installation has its own command
(Exodus 29:14). This preserves the distinction from the closer blood-and-body combination
in Leviticus 4 already compared with Hebrews, without adding a new claim about Hebrews.

## Significance

**The typology rests on a place and a category, not on a single verse.** Exodus 29 is where the sin
offering and its disposal outside the camp first appear together in Exodus. Hebrews applies that
pattern to Jesus' death outside the city and to his followers' leaving of the camp. Readers can trace
the Exodus contribution without collapsing the differences between ordination, later sin offerings,
and Hebrews' argument. See [[Sin]], [[Sacrifice and Offering]], [[Blood]], and [[Priesthood]].
