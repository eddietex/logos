## Analysis

**The law.** Deuteronomy 25:4 is four Hebrew words, *lōʾ-taḥsōm šôr bǝdîšô*. The verb *ḥāsam*,
*muzzle*, occurs nowhere else in Genesis–Deuteronomy (WLC lemma search). The ox that threshes may eat
from the grain it treads. In context it sits between the cap on the beating of *"your brother"*
(25:3) and the brother-in-law's duty (25:5–10), and no reason is given for it.

**Deuteronomy's working animal.** The ox already rests on the Sabbath with the household (5:14), and a
brother's fallen ox must be lifted up (22:4). The nearer parallel is human. The neighbor may eat
*"grapes your fill"* in another man's vineyard, but put none in a container (23:24). The
hired man is paid *"in his day"* (24:15). The worker eats from the work, and the ox is included.

**Paul's first use (1 Corinthians 9).** The quotation answers a question about Paul's right to be
supported by the churches he serves. He cites *"the law of Moses"* (9:9) and then asks, *"Is it for
the oxen that God cares, or does he say it assuredly for our sake? Yes, it was written for our sake,
because he who plows ought to plow in hope"* (9:9–10). The reading moves from the ox to the worker.
Whether Paul denies that the law concerns oxen, or says that it does not concern oxen *only*, is a
long-standing question, and the wiki adopts no answer. His Greek was not checked.

Paul's reading has a basis in the passage itself. Deuteronomy's surrounding laws are about persons,
and the ox's verse is surrounded by them. That observation does not settle what Paul meant.

**The second use (1 Timothy 5).** Here the verse is paired with a saying: *"The laborer is worthy of
his wages"* (5:18). The same words stand in Luke 10:7, among Jesus' instructions to those he sends
out (WEB). 1 Timothy introduces both with *"the Scripture says"*. Whether the second saying is cited as
Scripture, and from what source, is disputed; the wiki records the question and does not take a
side. The application is to *"the elders who rule well"*, who are *"worthy of double honor"* (5:17).

## Significance

The verse is the clearest case so far of a Torah law about animals being read in the New Testament as
a law about workers. Deuteronomy 25:4 itself already stands among laws about persons. Paul's use
extends what is in the passage, and both of his citations apply it to support for those who teach. See
[[Work]], [[Law and Obedience]], and [[Justice, Family Duty, Honest Weights, and Amalek]].
