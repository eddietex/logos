# Like All the Nations - Deuteronomy 17.14 and 1 Samuel 8.5, 20

## Passages involved

- [[The Law for the King]] — Deuteronomy 17:14: *"I will set a king over me, like all the nations
  that are around me"*.
- 1 Samuel 8:5 *(not yet ingested; WEB checked)* — the elders to Samuel: *"Now make us a king to
  judge us like all the nations."*
- 1 Samuel 8:19–20 *(not yet ingested; WEB checked)* — *"No; but we will have a king over us, that
  we also may be like all the nations, and that our king may judge us, and go out before us, and
  fight our battles."*
- [[Kings Will Come Out of You]] — the promise of kings to Abraham, Sarah, and Jacob, before any
  request.

## Analysis

**The same request, foreseen and then made.** Deuteronomy 17:14 puts words in Israel's mouth for a
time after the conquest. 1 Samuel 8 has the elders say almost the same words. In the WLC the two
share the verb *śîm*, *set*, with *melek*, *king*, and the phrase *kəkol-haggôyim*, *like all the
nations*:

| | Hebrew (WLC) | WEB |
|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomy 17:14 | *ʾāśîmâ ʿālay melek kəkol-haggôyim ʾăšer səbîbōtāy* | *"I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me"* |
| 1 Samuel 8:5 | *śîmâ-lānû melek ləšopṭēnû kəkol-haggôyim* | *"make us a king to judge us like all the nations"* |
| 1 Samuel 8:20 | *wəhāyînû gam-ʾănaḥnû kəkol-haggôyim* | *"that we also may be like all the nations"* |

The phrase is the link; whether 1 Samuel was written with Deuteronomy in view, or the reverse, is a
dating question the wiki does not settle.

**The two answers differ.** Deuteronomy grants the request with conditions and does not call it
sin: the king must be chosen by Yahweh, an Israelite, not a multiplier of horses, wives, or gold,
and a reader of the law. In 1 Samuel 8:7 Yahweh tells Samuel that the people have rejected him
(paraphrased; not quoted here), and Samuel warns of what a king will take (8:11–18). The king is
given anyway. Readers have joined the two in different ways: Deuteronomy permits what 1 Samuel
regrets; or 1 Samuel condemns the motive (to be like the nations) and not the office; or the two
reflect different views of kingship in Israel. None is adopted here.

**What the people add.** In 1 Samuel 8 the people say what they want the king for: *"to judge us"*
(8:5) and to *"go out before us, and fight our battles"* (8:20). Deuteronomy gives the king neither
job. Judging belongs to the judges in the gates and the court at the chosen place (16:18–17:13), and
the speech before battle to the priest and officers (20:2–9). The king of 17:14–20 reads the law;
the king of 1 Samuel 8:20 is wanted for war.

**"Like the nations" elsewhere in Deuteronomy.** The same chapters warn Israel not to *"learn to
imitate the abominations of those nations"* (18:9) and not to ask how *"these nations serve their
gods"* so as to do likewise (12:30). The request of 17:14 is the one place where wanting to be like
the nations is allowed, and the law at once makes the king unlike theirs.

## Significance

The phrase is the hinge of [[Kingship and Empire]] in Israel: the request Deuteronomy anticipates is
the one that brings Saul. It also bears on [[The Nations]], since the desire to be like them is both
granted and limited. See [[The Law for the King]].
