# As It Was in the Days of Noah

## Passages involved

- Genesis 6:1–8 — [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]
- Genesis 6:9–7:24 — [[Noah and the Flood]]: the ark, the waiting, and the flood
- Genesis 8:20–22 — [[The Waters Recede]]: the promise never to do it this way again
- Isaiah 54:9–10 — *"this is like the waters of Noah to me"*: the promise used as a guarantee
- Matthew 24:37–39 *(not yet ingested)* — *"as the days of Noah were, so will the coming of the Son
  of Man be"*
- Luke 17:26–27 *(not yet ingested)* — the same saying, paired with the days of Lot
- Hebrews 11:7 *(not yet ingested)* — Noah warned about things not yet seen
- 1 Peter 3:20 *(not yet ingested)* — God's patience waiting while the ship was being built
- 2 Peter 2:5; 3:5–7 *(not yet ingested)* — Noah as a preacher of righteousness, and the flood as
  the pattern for a coming judgment

## Analysis

**Jesus reads Genesis 6 as a description of ordinariness.** This is the surprise of Matthew
24:37–39, and it is easy to miss because the reader arrives from a chapter about Nephilim and
universal wickedness. *"As the days of Noah were, so will the coming of the Son of Man be. For as in
those days which were before the flood they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in
marriage, until the day that Noah entered into the ship, and they didn't know until the flood came
and took them all away."* Not violence, not corruption, not the sons of God — **eating, drinking,
and marrying**. The four activities named are all innocent. What condemns the generation in Jesus'
telling is that *"they didn't know"*, and the emphasis falls on the suddenness of the division: two
in a field, two grinding at the mill, one taken and one left.

That is a genuine and deliberate narrowing of Genesis 6, and it is worth resisting the urge to
harmonize it away. Genesis says the earth was filled with violence; Jesus says they were having
dinner. Both are describing the same generation, and the point of the second description is that the
first was not visible from inside it. The flood came on a world that was going about its business.
Luke's version puts this beyond doubt by pairing it with Lot: *"they ate, they drank, they bought,
they sold, they planted, they built; but in the day that Lot went out from Sodom, it rained fire"* —
a list of six ordinary occupations attached to the most notoriously wicked city in the Bible.

**The elements the New Testament actually takes from this passage.**

| Genesis 6:1–8 | Reused as |
|---|---|
| A generation under an unannounced verdict (6:5) | A world that *"didn't know"* (Matthew 24:39) |
| A period before the sentence falls (6:3, on the countdown reading) | *"the patience of God waited"* (1 Peter 3:20) |
| A total judgment (6:7) | The pattern of a judgment still to come (2 Peter 3:5–7) |
| One man who found favor (6:8) | *"Noah, with seven others, a preacher of righteousness"* (2 Peter 2:5) |
| The division between that man and everyone else | One taken, one left (Matthew 24:40–41) |

**Where 1 Peter and 2 Peter differ from Jesus.** Where Matthew's use is about *unawareness*, Peter's
is about *patience* and *rescue*: the ship being built is a period of divine forbearance, and the
eight persons *"saved through water"* become, in 1 Peter 3:21, a figure of baptism. 2 Peter 3
supplies the argument that Genesis 6:1–8 most naturally underwrites — that the world's continuance
is not evidence that judgment will not come, because it has come once already, by water, on a world
that had every reason to think it would not.

**A note on 2 Peter 2:5's "preacher of righteousness".** Genesis says nothing about Noah preaching.
The tradition that he warned his generation during the ark's construction is Second Temple Jewish
(Josephus has it, and *Jubilees* and the Sibylline Oracles develop it), and 2 Peter reflects it.
Genesis 6:1–8 gives Noah no speech at all — in fact he speaks for the first time at 9:25, and what
he says is a curse.

## Significance

**It settles what kind of text Genesis 6 is.** Jesus does not treat the flood generation as a
curiosity of prehistory but as the standing analogy for the present age, which means the primeval
history is being read in the New Testament as a description of how the world characteristically is,
not only of how it once was. Genesis 6:5's verdict on the human heart is quoted nowhere in the New
Testament, and yet the flood generation is invoked repeatedly — the *situation* travels even where
the sentence does not.

**And it puts the weight in an unexpected place.** If the analogy Jesus draws is to a world eating
and drinking and marrying, then the warning is not primarily to the notably wicked. It is to the
people in Genesis 6:1 — *"when men began to multiply"* — who were doing exactly what they were
blessed to do, and who, as far as the narrative lets us see, noticed nothing at all until the man
with the strange boat went inside and the door was shut.

**A note now that Genesis 7 is on the page.** Jesus' phrase is *"until the day that Noah entered
into the ship"*, and Genesis gives that day twice — 7:7, and again at 7:13 with the date attached,
*"In the same day Noah, and Shem, Ham, and Japheth … entered into the ship"*. The shutting of the
door, which the parable of the ten virgins will make its own image (Matthew 25:10), is Genesis 7:16,
and Genesis assigns it to God rather than to Noah: *"then Yahweh shut him in."* That detail sharpens
the analogy rather than softening it. Nobody in the account is shown refusing the ark or being
refused it; the text simply records that the door closed and the water came, and the New Testament's
use of the scene depends on exactly that silence. See [[Noah and the Flood]], and
[[The Flood and Baptism]] for the other, opposite thing the New Testament does with the same eight
people.

**And a note now that Genesis 8 is on the page: the promise is why the next judgment is fire.**
2 Peter 3:5–7 is the most argued-out of these passages, and its logic runs straight through
[[The Waters Recede]]. The world that then was *"being flooded with water, perished"*; the present
heavens and earth are *"reserved for fire"*. The change of element is not rhetorical escalation. It
is required, because at Genesis 8:21 God said *"I will never again strike every living thing, as I
have done"* — and Isaiah 54:9 treats that as a sworn oath: *"as I have sworn that the waters of Noah
will no more go over the earth, so have I sworn that I will not be angry with you."* The New
Testament's use of the flood as the pattern for a coming judgment therefore has to keep the pattern
and change the means, and 2 Peter does exactly that. The typology depends on the promise it works
around.
