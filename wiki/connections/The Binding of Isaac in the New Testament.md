# The Binding of Isaac in the New Testament

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 22:1–19** — the test, the four-fold *"your son, your only son, whom you love, even
  Isaac"*, the ram, and the oath. See [[The Binding of Isaac]].
- **Hebrews 11:17–19** — *"By faith, Abraham, being tested, offered up Isaac … concluding that God is
  able to raise up even from the dead"*
- **James 2:21–23** — *"Wasn't Abraham our father justified by works, in that he offered up Isaac his
  son on the altar?"*, quoting Genesis 15:6 alongside it
- **Romans 8:32** — *"He who didn't spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all"*
- **John 3:16** — *"God so loved the world, that he gave his one and only Son"*
- **Genesis 22:2, 12, 16 LXX** — *τὸν υἱόν σου τὸν ἀγαπητόν*, *the son you love*; *οὐκ ἐφείσω*, *you
  did not spare*

## Analysis

**Three New Testament writers use this chapter for three different arguments, and none of them reads
it the same way.**

### Hebrews: it was about resurrection

> By faith, Abraham, being tested, offered up Isaac. Yes, he who had gladly received the promises was
> offering up his one and only son, to whom it was said, "Your offspring will be accounted as from
> Isaac," concluding that God is able to raise up even from the dead. Figuratively speaking, he also
> did receive him back from the dead. *(Hebrews 11:17–19)*

**Hebrews fills the chapter's central silence with a syllogism.** Genesis reports what Abraham did
and nothing about why; Hebrews supplies a *λογισάμενος*, a **reckoning**: the promise runs through
Isaac, God has told him so (quoting 21:12), therefore if Isaac dies God must raise him. **It is
presented as an inference from the promise, not as a report of Abraham's state of mind**, and it is
the only account of Abraham's reasoning anywhere in Scripture.

It also makes sense of the one line in Genesis that would otherwise be a lie: *"We will worship, and
come back to you"* (22:5), first person plural. **Hebrews is the earliest surviving attempt to
reconcile 22:5 with 22:2**, and every later reading of that verse is downstream of it.

*"Figuratively speaking"* — *ἐν παραβολῇ* — is the letter conceding that nobody died. **Isaac is
received back from a death that did not happen**, which is as far as Hebrews will push it. See
[[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].

### James: it was about works

> Wasn't Abraham our father justified by works, in that he offered up Isaac his son on the altar?
> You see that faith worked with his works, and by works faith was perfected. So the Scripture was
> fulfilled which says, "Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him as righteousness."
> *(James 2:21–23)*

**James does something Paul never does: he puts Genesis 15:6 and Genesis 22 in the same sentence.**
Romans 4 quotes 15:6 at length and does not mention Moriah at all. James quotes 15:6 *as fulfilled
by* Moriah — the belief of chapter 15 completed by the act of chapter 22, some thirty years and
seven chapters later.

**And James is reading a seam that is genuinely in Genesis.** The chapter itself grounds the promise
in an act: *"because you have done this thing"* (22:16), *"because you have obeyed my voice"* (22:18),
and afterwards *"because Abraham obeyed my voice"* (26:5) — against Genesis 15, where God passes
between the pieces alone and Abram undertakes nothing. **The New Testament's sharpest internal
argument is anchored in a tension the Old Testament put there first.** See
[[Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James]] and
[[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].

### Paul: it was about God

**Paul never retells the story, and uses one word of it.** Romans 8:32's *οὐκ ἐφείσατο τοῦ ἰδίου
υἱοῦ* — *"He who didn't spare his own Son"* — is the Septuagint's verb from Genesis 22:16, *οὐκ
ἐφείσω τοῦ υἱοῦ σου τοῦ ἀγαπητοῦ*, where it is what God says to Abraham. **Paul takes the sentence
God spoke to Abraham and says it about God**, without naming Abraham, without naming Isaac, and
without an argument — as though the allusion would carry itself.

The rest of the vocabulary works the same way. *"Your son, your only son, whom you love"* is rendered
in the Septuagint as *τὸν ἀγαπητόν*, **the beloved**, which is the word spoken over Jesus at the
Jordan and on the mountain (Mark 1:11; 9:7); John 3:16's *μονογενής*, **only-begotten**, translates
the other half of Hebrew *yāḥîd*. **The New Testament's stock phrase for what God gave up is
assembled entirely out of Genesis 22:2.**

### What none of them says

**No New Testament writer calls Isaac a type of Christ.** The identification is patristic — Melito of
Sardis, Irenaeus, Tertullian, and after them the standard reading — and it is built from the picture:
a beloved only son, carrying the wood up a hill outside Jerusalem, on the third day. **The New
Testament uses the chapter's language for the *Father* and its example for *Abraham*, and leaves the
son alone.** Where the ram is read typologically instead — the substitute that dies so the son does
not — the logic is nearer to the New Testament's own. See
[[The Ram Instead of the Son - Substitution at Moriah]].

## Significance

- **It is the clearest case in Scripture of one chapter supporting three incompatible arguments.**
  Hebrews reads it as faith reasoning past death; James as works completing faith; Paul as a
  description of God. All three are legitimate readings of the same nineteen verses, and the wiki
  records them as three rather than harmonizing them into one.
- **It shows the Septuagint doing theological work.** *ʾĀhab* → *ἀγαπητός* and *ḥāśak* → *φείδομαι*
  are ordinary translation choices in 200 BC that become load-bearing New Testament vocabulary two
  centuries later. **The bridge from Moriah to Golgotha is built out of a Greek translator's word
  choices**, which does not make it illegitimate but does make it traceable.
- **And it is where the wiki's rule about filling silences is easiest to see.** Genesis withholds
  Abraham's reasoning; Hebrews supplies it and marks it as an inference. That is the honest version
  of what every reader of this chapter does.
