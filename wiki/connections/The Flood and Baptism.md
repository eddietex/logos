# The Flood and Baptism

## Passages involved

- Genesis 6:9–7:24 — [[Noah and the Flood]]
- Genesis 8:6–12 — the dove sent out over the water — [[The Waters Recede]]
- 1 Peter 3:18–22 — *"eight souls were saved through water. This is a symbol of baptism"*
- Matthew 3:16 — the Spirit descending *"as a dove"* at Jesus' baptism
- 1 Corinthians 10:1–2 — Israel *"baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea"*
- Romans 6:3–4 — baptism as burial into a death
- Exodus 2:3, 5 — the other *tēbâ* in the Bible: the pitch-sealed basket that carries Moses through
  the water

## Analysis

**The New Testament makes the connection once, explicitly, and in a passage that is difficult for
other reasons.** 1 Peter 3:20–21: *"…when the patience of God waited in the days of Noah, while the
ship was being built. In it, few, that is, eight souls, were saved through water. This is a symbol of
baptism, which now saves you — not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a
good conscience toward God, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."*

The Greek word behind *"symbol"* is **antitypon** — the *antitype*, the thing the pattern was a
pattern *of*. Peter is not offering an illustration. He is saying the flood was the shape and baptism
is the substance.

**Where the correspondence is exact, and where it is strained.** Both are worth stating, because
the passage has been made to carry more than it says.

| The flood | Baptism, in 1 Peter |
|---|---|
| A world under judgment | The old life under judgment |
| Water as the instrument of that judgment | Water as the instrument of salvation |
| Eight people brought through it alive | The baptized *"saved through water"* |
| The ark as what carries them through | Christ's resurrection as what saves |
| A new world on the far side | *"the answer of a good conscience toward God"* |

The strain is in the second row and Peter knows it. In Genesis the water is what kills; the ark is
what saves. In baptism the water is the saving thing. Peter's phrase is *diesōthēsan di' hydatos* —
*saved through water*, where *through* carries both senses at once, *by means of* and *by passing
through*. The eight were not saved *from* the water but *through* it: the same flood that destroyed
the world carried the ark. That double sense is the whole hinge of the typology, and it is why Paul
can say in Romans 6 that baptism is a **burial**. The water is not a cleaning. It is a death you come
out the other side of.

Peter then does something careful that is easy to miss. Having said baptism *"now saves you"*, he
immediately rules out the mechanism a reader would assume: *"not the putting away of the filth of the
flesh"* — not washing, not the rite operating on its own — *"but the answer of a good conscience
toward God, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."* The saving thing is located in the
resurrection, and the sentence has been read as both the strongest baptismal-regeneration text in the
New Testament and as its own qualification of that reading. It is genuinely both, and the argument
between sacramental and memorialist traditions runs straight through this verse.

**The other elements later readers have drawn on.**

- **The eight.** *Oktō psychai*. Early Christian architecture took it literally: baptistries were
  built octagonal from the fourth century onward, and Ambrose's inscription for the baptistry at
  Milan says so outright — eight sides for the eight saved, and for the eighth day, the day of
  resurrection. This is one of the clearer cases of a typology leaving the page and becoming a
  building.
- **The dove.** Genesis 8:8–12 has Noah send a dove out over the water three times; it returns with
  an olive leaf and then does not return at all. All four Gospels have the Spirit descend *"as a
  dove"* at Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16; Mark 1:10; Luke 3:22; John 1:32), and the patristic reading
  connects them without hesitation. The New Testament never makes the link explicit, and the dove of
  Genesis 8 is a bird sent to test the water rather than a descent from heaven — but the pairing of a
  dove, water, and a new beginning is in both scenes, and the iconography has never let go of it.
  Traced out at length, with the *rûaḥ* of Genesis 1:2 and 8:1 alongside it, in
  [[The Dove and the Spirit]].
- **The ark as the church.** Cyprian's *"outside the church there is no salvation"* is argued in part
  from the ark, and the reading becomes standard in the West: one vessel, one door, everyone inside
  it saved and everyone outside it not. It is worth noting how much of that is drawn from the
  structure rather than from Peter, who says nothing about the ark being anything.
- **The *tēbâ*.** Genesis uses a word for the ark that appears in only one other place in Scripture:
  the basket of Exodus 2:3, 5, sealed with pitch, carrying the infant Moses through the water that is
  killing every other Hebrew boy. Two vessels, one word, both waterproofed the same way, both
  carrying a rescued figure through a judgment by water, and the second of them opening the exodus
  that 1 Corinthians 10:2 then calls a **baptism** — *"baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the
  sea"*. Genesis, Exodus, and Paul are working the same figure in three registers.

## Significance

**It is the New Testament's own answer to what the flood was for.** Genesis narrates the flood and
declines to interpret it; 1 Peter reads it as the pattern of how anyone is saved at all — not
extracted from judgment but carried through it. That is the same shape the wiki keeps finding in
Genesis and it is stated most sharply here: Noah found favor and the flood still came (see
[[Favor and Grace]]). He was not spared the water. He was given something to be in while it fell.

**And it puts the flood in the present tense.** Peter's typology and Jesus' *"as it was in the days
of Noah"* are doing opposite things with the same passage and both are in the canon. Jesus uses the
flood generation as a warning to a world that does not know what is coming (see
[[As It Was in the Days of Noah]]); Peter uses the same flood as a description of what has already
happened to the reader. One makes the reader the drowning generation, the other makes them the eight
in the boat. The New Testament does not choose, and the passage supports both because Genesis put
the door in the side of the ark and had God shut it (7:16) without saying anything about who was
where when he did.
