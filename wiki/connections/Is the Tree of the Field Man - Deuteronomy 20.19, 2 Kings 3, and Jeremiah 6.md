# Is the Tree of the Field Man - Deuteronomy 20.19, 2 Kings 3, and Jeremiah 6

## Passages involved

- [[Laws for Going to War]] — Deuteronomy 20:19–20; the fruit trees of a besieged city may not be cut
  down; only trees *"that you know are not trees for food"* may be used for siege works.
- [[Holy Living in Field and Community]] — Leviticus 19:23–25; *"all kinds of trees for food"*, whose
  fruit is forbidden three years and holy in the fourth.
- 2 Kings 3:19, 25 — Elisha's word against Moab: *"shall fell every good tree, and stop all springs of
  water, and mar every good piece of land with stones"*, and Israel does so. *(WEB, bible-api.com; not yet
  ingested)*
- Jeremiah 6:6 — *"Cut down trees, and cast up a mound against Jerusalem."* *(WEB, bible-api.com; not yet
  ingested)*
- Deuteronomy 28:52–53 — the siege turned on Israel, *"in the siege and in the distress with which your
  enemies will distress you"*. *(stored WEB, not yet ingested)*

## Analysis

**The law.** Deuteronomy 20:19 forbids taking an ax to the fruit trees of a city under long siege, and
gives two reasons: *"for you may eat of them"*, and *"is the tree of the field man, that it should be
besieged by you?"* The tree is not the enemy, and it will feed Israel after the siege. 20:20 permits
cutting trees *"that you know are not trees for food"* to build siege works. The phrase *ʿēṣ maʾăkāl*,
*tree for food* (20:20, WLC), is the phrase of Leviticus 19:23 (WLC), where newly planted food trees are
protected by a different rule: their fruit is not eaten for three years.

**2 Kings 3 does the opposite, at a prophet's word.** In the campaign of Israel, Judah, and Edom against
Moab, Elisha says Yahweh will give Moab into their hand and that they *"shall fell every good tree"*
(3:19), and the narrator reports that *"they … felled all the good trees"* (3:25). The Hebrew is *kol-ʿēṣ
ṭôb tappîlû* (WLC 3:19), *every good tree you shall fell* — not the phrase *ʿēṣ maʾăkāl* of Deuteronomy
20:20, and not its verb *kārat*, *cut down*. Readers have explained the conflict in more than one way:
that *good tree* need not mean a fruit tree; that Deuteronomy 20 governs a siege intended to take and
hold a city, whereas 2 Kings 3 describes a punitive campaign against a land Israel was not to possess;
or that the narrator simply records a practice the law forbade. 2 Kings does not mention the law. The
wiki records the tension and does not resolve it.

**Jeremiah 6 turns the siege on Jerusalem.** *"Yahweh of Armies said, 'Cut down trees, and cast up a mound
against Jerusalem'"* (6:6). Here the besiegers are the enemy and the besieged city is Israel's own. The
verse does not say which trees, and its Hebrew has not been checked here, so whether it echoes the
vocabulary of Deuteronomy 20 is left open. It matches the reversal Deuteronomy itself predicts: the siege
Israel lays in chapter 20 is laid against Israel in the curses of chapter 28, *"in the siege and in the
distress"* (28:53), with the same noun *māṣôr* (WLC).

**What the law does not say.** It does not extend the protection to every useful thing, or to the land in
general; later Jewish law built a broad principle against needless destruction, *bal tašḥît*, on this
verse, but that extension is rabbinic, not Deuteronomy's. It also does not say that fruit trees may never
be cut in any war — only in a siege, and only as a means of taking the city.

## Significance

Deuteronomy 20:19 is the law code's one limit on what war may destroy that is not about people: it
protects the trees that will feed the land after the siege, not the army during it. The sieges narrated later in Kings and threatened
in Jeremiah stand beside it unreconciled, and the wiki will revisit this page when those books are
ingested. See [[War]] and [[Laws for Going to War]].
