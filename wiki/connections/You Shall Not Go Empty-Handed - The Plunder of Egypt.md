# You Shall Not Go Empty-Handed - The Plunder of Egypt

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 15:14** — *"I will also judge that nation, whom they will serve. Afterward they will come
  out with great wealth."* — [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]]
- **Exodus 3:21–22** — *"I will give this people favor in the sight of the Egyptians, and it will
  happen that when you go, you shall not go empty-handed. But every woman shall ask of her neighbor,
  and of her who visits her house, jewels of silver, jewels of gold, and clothing; and you shall put
  them on your sons, and on your daughters. You shall plunder the Egyptians."* — [[The Burning Bush]]
- **Exodus 11:2–3** — *"let every man ask of his neighbor, and every woman of her neighbor, jewels of
  silver, and jewels of gold." Yahweh gave the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians.* — [[The Last Plague Announced]].
  **Men now ask as well as women** (*rēʿēhû*, *rəʿûtāh*; WLC), and the favour is reported as already
  given, in a speech about what is still to come
- **Exodus 12:35–36** — *"they asked of the Egyptians jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and
  clothing. Yahweh gave the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians, so that they let them have what
  they asked. They plundered the Egyptians."* — [[The Death of the Firstborn and the Departure]]
- **Psalm 105:37** — *"He brought them out with silver and gold."*
- **Deuteronomy 15:13–14** — the released Hebrew slave: *"When you let him go free from you, you shall
  not let him go empty. You shall furnish him liberally …"*

## Analysis

**A promise made to Abram in a vision is specified at the bush, repeated before the last plague, and
reported done on the night of the exodus — in the same words each time.** *Great wealth* (Genesis
15:14) becomes *not empty-handed* (Exodus 3:21), becomes *jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and
clothing* (3:22; 11:2; 12:35), and is closed with the narrator's *"They plundered the Egyptians"*
(12:36), repeating God's *"You shall plunder the Egyptians"* (3:22) almost exactly.

**The verb is the exodus's own.** *"You shall plunder"* is ***wəniṣṣaltem***, and *"they plundered"*
***wayənaṣṣəlû*** — the intensive of ***nāṣal***, whose causative is God's *"I have come down to
deliver them"* (*ləhaṣṣîlô*, Exodus 3:8). **Israel strips Egypt in the same root by which God rescues
Israel.** In Genesis–Deuteronomy the piel occurs only at Exodus 3:22 and 12:36 (WLC lemma search, read by
stem), **and the children's answer in the same chapter uses the hiphil — *"he … spared (hiṣṣîl) our
houses"* (12:27) — nine verses before *wayənaṣṣəlû***. The root's one other use in Exodus outside the
hiphil is the hitpael of 33:6, after the calf: *"The children of Israel stripped themselves of their
jewelry from Mount Horeb onward"* (WLC; `raw/bible/`; not yet ingested).

**Favor, not force.** Each of the three Exodus texts says *Yahweh gave the people favor in the sight of
the Egyptians*. **The goods are asked for and given**, by neighbours and by the woman *"who visits her
house"* — *gārat bêtāh*, the one sojourning there. *Plunder* is the outcome described as if it were
the spoil of a war Israel never fought.

**The moral question is old.** Readers from antiquity have asked whether *asking* for goods one does
not intend to return is theft. **The standing answers**: that it is wages, the back pay of four hundred
years of unpaid service (an argument made in Jewish and early Christian apologetic); that the goods
were freely given, as the text insists three times, by people who had seen the plagues; and that it is
the judgment Genesis 15:14 announced, executed by gift instead of by sword. **The law of release sides
with the first**: an Israelite master must not send his own released slave away *"empty"* (*rêqām*, the
word of Exodus 3:21) but furnish him from flock, floor, and press (Deuteronomy 15:13–14). **Israel is
commanded to do for its slaves what Egypt was made to do for Israel.** The wiki records the answers and
adopts none.

**And the gold has a future.** What is put on the sons and daughters (3:22) will be given twice more in
Exodus: **once for the golden calf** (32:2–4) **and once for the tabernacle** (35:22). Those uses are
recorded for when the pericopes are reached.

## Significance

- **It is one of the clearest prophecy-fulfillment chains inside the Torah**, with its terms set in
  Genesis 15, fixed at the bush, and closed in Exodus 12.
- **It ties the exodus to the law of the slave's release**, by the shared *rêqām*.
- **It sets up the book's double use of the plunder** — the calf and the tabernacle — as a test of what
  the delivered do with what they were given.
