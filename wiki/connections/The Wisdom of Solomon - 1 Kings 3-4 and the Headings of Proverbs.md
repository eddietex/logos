# The Wisdom of Solomon - 1 Kings 3-4 and the Headings of Proverbs

## Analysis

**Kings tells how Solomon got wisdom; Proverbs is offered as a specimen of it.** 1 Kings 3 gives the
origin — a request at Gibeon for a heart *"to judge"*, granted beyond asking — and 1 Kings 4 the
reputation: wisdom measured against *"the children of the east"* and Egypt, and an output of three
thousand proverbs on trees, animals, birds, creeping things, and fish. Proverbs 1:1 attaches the whole
book to that reputation in its first words. The connection is the reason the book reads as royal
wisdom: its first purpose clauses include *"righteousness, justice, and equity"* (1:3), which is the
competence Solomon asked for.

**The headings claim less than the tradition does, and more carefully.** Proverbs names Solomon three
times, but the book also gives collections to anonymous *"wise"*, to Agur, and to Lemuel's mother, and
says one Solomonic collection was copied out by men of Hezekiah's court (25:1). The book's own headings
therefore describe an anthology assembled over time, with Solomon as its fountainhead and patron rather
than its sole writer. **The wiki follows the headings**: a passage is attributed to Solomon where its
heading does so, and to whoever else its heading names otherwise.

**The subjects do not match exactly, and that is worth noticing.** 1 Kings 4:33 lists nature —
plants and animals. Proverbs does use creatures (the ant, the eagle, the dog, the leech), but the book is
mostly about speech, work, households, kings, and the fear of Yahweh. Kings describes the breadth of a
royal reputation; Proverbs preserves a moral curriculum. The two are related, not identical.

**"The children of the east" ties Solomon to Job's world.** 1 Kings 4:30 measures Solomon against *"all
the children of the east"*, the phrase Job 1:3 uses for the people among whom Job was greatest. Both books
locate Israel's wisdom inside an international tradition, and the wiki's Job pages have treated that
territory — [[Uz]], [[Teman]] — as wisdom's home ground. Solomon is the Israelite king who excels it;
Job is the man from inside it whom the tradition could not account for.

**Kings and Nehemiah also record that the wisest king fell.** *"When Solomon was old, his wives turned
away his heart after other gods"* (1 Kings 11:4); Nehemiah 13:26 uses him as the cautionary case. The
book that bears his name opens with the fear of Yahweh as the beginning of knowledge (Proverbs 1:7), and
its lectures will warn at length against the strange woman. The wiki records the tension rather than
resolving it: the headings attach the book to Solomon's wisdom, and the histories attach to Solomon the
failure the book warns against.

**Proverbs 8 states as Wisdom's claim what 1 Kings 3 narrates as Solomon's story.** *"By me kings
reign"* (8:15) is the general form of a king's request for *"an understanding heart to judge"*
(1 Kings 3:9); and *ʿōšer wəḵāḇôḏ*, *riches and honour*, which Wisdom says are *"with me"* (8:18), is
the pair God gave Solomon unasked (*gam-ʿōšer gam-kāḇôḏ*, 1 Kings 3:13; Hebrew read from Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25). Proverbs does not name Solomon in chapter 8, and the wiki
does not claim the speech was written with him in view; the two passages share a theory of rule, and
the book's heading puts them under one name.

### The sentence collection — Proverbs 10:1

The heading now stands over an ingested specimen: household joy and grief, work at harvest, speech,
and divine blessing. The wise son of 10:1 is an unnamed type, not an identified event in Solomon’s
biography. The collection attribution and its teaching characters must be kept distinct.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Job comparisons checked
in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### The words of the wise — Proverbs 22:17

The first heading that names someone else is now ingested. *"Turn your ear, and listen to the words of the
wise"* (22:17) changes speaker and form at once — direct commands with reasons, not two-line
observations — and its preface aims at *"trust … in Yahweh"* (22:19). The WEB's *"thirty excellent
things"* (22:20) rests on a disputed Hebrew word; see the passage's notes. The book's attributions are
kept as the book gives them: this collection is not Solomon's.

See [[Thirty Excellent Things]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 22:17–21.

### The men of Hezekiah — Proverbs 25:1

The third heading to name Solomon is now ingested. *"These also are proverbs of Solomon, which the men
of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out"* (25:1) is the only heading in the book that names a later
stage of the book's making: it credits the sayings to Solomon and their copying to unnamed men of
[[Hezekiah]]'s court. In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read 2026-10-26) the verb is
*heʿtîqû*; *"copied out"* is the WEB's reading of it. The heading therefore dates this collection's
compilation no earlier than Hezekiah's reign and keeps its attribution to Solomon at the same time.
The chapter it introduces opens with kings — *"the glory of kings is to search out a matter"* (25:2)
— a line that sits easily under a royal heading, though no king in 25:2–7 is named.

See [[The Glory of Kings]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 25:1–7.

### Agur’s words — Proverbs 30:1–3

The new heading names [[Agur]] , son of [[Jakeh]] , speaking to [[Ithiel]] and [[Ucal]] in the
stored WEB. It marks a distinct attribution after the collection copied by Hezekiah’s men. Agur
begins by confessing deficient understanding rather than claiming Solomon’s reputation. No date,
country, court position, or identification with Solomon is supplied by this heading.

See [[The Words of Agur]]. Sources: stored WEB and the comparisons documented on that page;
no new source-language identity is claimed.

### Lemuel and his mother — Proverbs 31:1–9

The heading at 31:1 is now ingested in [[The Words of King Lemuel]]. It names [[Lemuel]] and
credits [[Lemuel's Mother]] as the teacher. The transmitted words belong to a king who is
instructed, not a king presumed exempt from learning. The warning about lost strength and
impaired judgment leads to speaking for the poor. No identity with Solomon is supplied; the
book’s multiple attributions remain distinct. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 31:1–9.

### The Preacher's heading — Ecclesiastes 1:1–11

[[Vanity of Vanities]] now introduces [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] in [[Jerusalem]]. The similarity to
Proverbs 1:1 is real at the level of the English headings: both identify a speaker through
David and kingship. The difference is equally explicit: Proverbs names Solomon; Ecclesiastes
names a title. The latter's royal association is not a new proof of historical authorship.
The opening also changes the question: the benefits of wisdom announced in Proverbs 1:2–6
stand beside the Preacher's inquiry into labor's gain, satisfaction, and remembrance.
Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–11; Proverbs 1:1–7.

### Wisdom above all before him — Ecclesiastes 1:12–18

[[The Burden of Wisdom]] adds a claim close to 1 Kings 3:12: the Preacher has *"great wisdom above
all who were before me in Jerusalem"* (Ecclesiastes 1:16). The resemblance is in the comparison of
one king's wisdom with others'. The differences are also explicit. 1 Kings 3:12 presents the
wisdom as God's gift and compares Solomon with all before and after him; Ecclesiastes 1:16 says
*"I have obtained for myself"* and compares only with predecessors in Jerusalem, implying more than
one. And where 1 Kings 3–4 describes wisdom's success, Ecclesiastes 1:18 reports its grief. The
royal resemblance is real; it does not settle the identification. Source: stored WEB
Ecclesiastes 1:12–18; 1 Kings 3:12 as quoted above.

### Riches and honor tested — Ecclesiastes 2:1–11

1 Kings 3:13, quoted above, adds *"riches and honor"* to the wisdom Solomon asked for.
[[The Trial of Pleasure]] catalogues riches of a royal kind — houses, vineyards, servants, flocks,
*"silver and gold … the treasure of kings and of the provinces"* (Ecclesiastes 2:8) — and keeps
wisdom beside them: *"My wisdom also remained with me"* (2:9). Where 1 Kings presents the riches as
God's gift, Ecclesiastes 2:4–8 presents them as acquired (*"I made myself … I bought … I gathered"*)
and judges them without profit (2:11). The resemblance to Solomon's splendor in 1 Kings 4–10
(paraphrased; not stored) is real; Solomon is still not named, and 2:9's predecessors in
Jerusalem remain the same difficulty as 1:16's. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:1–11; 1 Kings
3:13 as quoted above.

### A song under Solomon's name — Song of Solomon 1:1–4

[[Love Better Than Wine]] adds the Song's heading to the ingested attribution evidence.
Proverbs 1:1 names Solomon and identifies his father and kingship; Song 1:1 associates
the song with Solomon without that extended identification. Ecclesiastes 1:1 gives a
royal and Davidic description without his name. Each heading retains its own wording.

The song that follows its heading opens with desire, fragrance, and rejoicing rather
than a program for instruction. Neither the heading nor the mention of the king's
rooms makes every speaker Solomon or identifies the woman as a named historical wife.
The attribution does not by itself establish a composition date or a detailed account
of authorship. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 1:1–4; Proverbs 1:1–7;
Ecclesiastes 1:1. These comparisons add no new quotation from an unstored book.

## Significance

The link explains why a collection of sayings is canonical wisdom and not merely folk wisdom: it is
anchored to a king to whom God gave wisdom by request, and it begins with Israel's God by name. It also
sets the standard by which Matthew 12:42 measures Jesus — *"someone greater than Solomon"* — so that the
New Testament's claim that Christ is *"made to us wisdom from God"* (1 Corinthians 1:30) is made against
the figure the book of Proverbs opens with. See [[Solomon]], [[Wisdom]], and
[[The Purpose of the Proverbs]].
