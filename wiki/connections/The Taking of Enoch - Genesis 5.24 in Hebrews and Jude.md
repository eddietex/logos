# The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 5:21–24** — *"Enoch walked with God, and he was not found, for God took him"* —
  [[From Adam to Noah]]
- **2 Kings 2:1–12** *(not yet ingested)* — Elijah taken up, the Bible's only comparable departure
- **Psalm 49:15; 73:24** — the psalmist's hope that God will *take* him, using the same verb
- **Sirach 44:16; 49:14** — the earliest surviving comment on the verse, in a Jewish text of c. 180
  BC
- **1 Enoch** — the apocalyptic corpus built entirely around him, from the third century BC onward
- **Hebrews 11:5** *(not yet ingested)* — *"By faith Enoch was taken away, so that he wouldn't see
  death"*
- **Jude 14–15** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied about these…"*

## Analysis

Genesis gives Enoch four verses, and the fourth is one of the strangest sentences in the Torah.

**What the Hebrew says.** *Wayyithallēk ḥănôk ʾet-hāʾĕlōhîm wəʾênennû kî-lāqaḥ ʾōtô ʾĕlōhîm* —
literally *"And Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him."* Two things are doing the
work. The first is the flat negative *ʾênennû*, "and he was not" (WEB: *"he was not found"*), which
is not the language of dying; the chapter has a perfectly good formula for that, and uses it eight
times. The second is *lāqaḥ*, "took" — an ordinary verb made extraordinary by its object. The same
verb carries Elijah up in 2 Kings 2, and it is the verb the psalmist uses when he hopes God will
*"receive"* or *"take"* him from the power of Sheol (Psalm 49:15) and *"afterward receive me to
glory"* (Psalm 73:24). Those psalms are the closest the Hebrew Bible comes to reading Genesis 5:24
as a statement about anyone else's prospects.

**The minority reading, and why it lost.** Nothing in the grammar forbids taking *"God took him"* as
an unusually delicate way of reporting an early death — he was 365 in a chapter where everyone else
lives past 890, so *God took him young* is a natural English idiom and a possible Hebrew one. This
reading appears occasionally in modern criticism and has essentially no footing in the reception
history: from Sirach onward, every ancient reader takes the verse as a departure without dying. The
deciding argument is structural rather than lexical — Genesis 5 is a machine for saying *"then he
died"*, and it declines to say it here.

**The Septuagint's two decisions.** The Greek translators made two choices that determined how the
verse was read in the New Testament and after.

1. They rendered *"walked with God"* as *euērestēsen tō theō*, **"he pleased God"** — replacing a
   description of manner with a verdict on character.
2. They rendered *"God took him"* as *metethēken auton ho theos*, **"God translated/transferred
   him"** — a verb of relocation, which removes the ambiguity the Hebrew leaves open.

Hebrews 11:5 uses both, in order, and its argument depends on them: *"By faith Enoch was taken away
(metetethē), so that he wouldn't see death… For he has had testimony given to him that before his
translation he had been well pleasing to God."* The writer then draws the general principle in the
next verse — *"without faith it is impossible to be well pleasing to him"* — so the whole of Hebrews
11:6, one of the New Testament's most quoted sentences about faith, is built on the Septuagint's
gloss of Genesis 5:22. This is worth stating plainly rather than either hiding or over-claiming: the
Hebrew does not say Enoch had faith, the Greek says he pleased God, and Hebrews reads the second as
evidence of the first. That is a legitimate inference and it is an inference.

**Jude quotes a book that is not in anyone's Bible.** Jude 14–15 attributes a prophecy of judgment to
*"Enoch, the seventh from Adam"*, and the words are a close rendering of *1 Enoch* 1:9, a Jewish
apocalypse from the last centuries BC. Three observations, all of which matter and none of which
settles the others:

- The epithet *"the seventh from Adam"* shows that the generation-count in Genesis 5 was being made
  and used in the first century, which is direct evidence that the symmetry with the seventh
  generation of Cain's line (Lamech, at 4:23–24) is ancient rather than a modern reading. See
  [[Two Lines from Adam - Cain's Genealogy and Seth's]].
- Jude quotes the book as a source for what Enoch said. Whether that implies he regarded *1 Enoch*
  as Scripture is a genuinely open question — Paul quotes Greek poets without canonizing them
  (Acts 17:28, Titus 1:12) — and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, alone among Christian communions,
  does include *1 Enoch* in its canon.
- The prophecy Jude quotes has no basis in Genesis whatever. Genesis records nothing Enoch said.

**What the tradition built on four verses.** Because Genesis leaves the content of the walking
entirely blank, later Judaism filled it in on a scale out of all proportion to the source. *1 Enoch*
makes him a heavenly scribe, a visionary of the divine throne, the accuser of the fallen watchers,
and the authority behind a solar calendar; *2 Enoch* and *3 Enoch* go further, the latter identifying
him with the angel Metatron. *Jubilees* 4:17–23 makes him the inventor of writing and the first
keeper of the calendar. Some of this is plainly generated by details of the text — the 365 years for
the calendar, the name of his father [[Jared]] (*"descent"*) for the watchers' descent — which is a
useful demonstration of how much an ancient reader could get out of a name.

**The pattern the verse establishes.** Two people in the Hebrew Bible leave without dying: Enoch and
Elijah. Both become figures of expectation — Elijah explicitly (Malachi 4:5), Enoch by the weight of
the literature attached to him — and both are invoked in Christian readings of the two witnesses of
Revelation 11, on the reasoning that they are the two candidates who have not yet died. That is
inference stacked on inference; the connection worth keeping is simpler. Genesis 5 establishes death
as the rule of human life in the most relentless way available to it, and in the same chapter records
one exception. Everything the Bible later says about resurrection and about not seeing death has this
verse behind it as the first precedent.

## Significance

Genesis 5:24 is the Old Testament's earliest hint that the sentence of Genesis 3 is not the last word
on a human life, and it is a hint rather than a doctrine: four verses, no explanation, one man. The
New Testament does not build its resurrection hope on Enoch — it builds it on Christ — but Hebrews 11
puts him in the roll of faith as the case that proves God *"is a rewarder of those who seek him"*,
and does so by reading a Greek translation of a Hebrew idiom about walking. Tracing that chain is a
good exercise in how much of the Bible's later theology travels through its own translation history.

It is also the origin of the theme the wiki now tracks separately: see [[Walking with God]].
