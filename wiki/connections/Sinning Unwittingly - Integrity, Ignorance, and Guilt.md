# Sinning Unwittingly - Integrity, Ignorance, and Guilt

## Passages involved

- [[The Trespass Offering for Holy Things and Unknown Guilt]] — Leviticus 5:14–19.

- [[Confession and Offerings According to Means]] — Leviticus 5:1–13.

- [[The Sin Offering for Ruler and Individual]] — Leviticus 4:22–35.

- **Leviticus 4:1–21** — [[The Sin Offering for Priest and Congregation]]; priestly and communal bull offerings.

- **Genesis 20:3–7** — a death sentence for an act done on false information, a plea of integrity
  accepted, and a remedy that is still required — [[Abraham and Abimelech]]
- **Genesis 20:9** — *"you have brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin"* — the guilt described
  as something done *to* the innocent party
- **Genesis 12:17** — the same structure without the conversation: a household plagued for a marriage
  it was misled into — [[Abram in Egypt]]
- **Leviticus 4:1–35; 5:1–19** — the sin offering and the guilt offering for the *šəgāgâ*, the sin
  committed unwittingly, for the individual, the ruler, and the whole assembly
- **Numbers 15:22–31** — the sharp line between what is done *unwittingly* and what is done *with a
  high hand*, which has no offering at all
- **Numbers 35:9–34; Deuteronomy 19:1–13; Joshua 20** — the cities of refuge: the unintentional
  killer is not a murderer and is still not simply free
- **Deuteronomy 21:1–9** — a corpse in open country, and a town that must clear itself of blood it
  did not shed
- **1 Samuel 14:24–45** — Jonathan eats the honey without knowing his father's oath, and the lot
  falls on him anyway
- **2 Samuel 6:6–7** — Uzzah steadies the ark, and dies for it
- **Job 6:24** — *"Teach me, and I will hold my peace. Cause me to understand wherein I have erred
  (šāgîtî)"*: the verb of the *šəgāgâ*, from a man who will not confess a sin he cannot name but is
  willing to be shown one he did not know of — [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]
- **Psalm 19:12–13** — *"Who can discern his errors? Forgive me from hidden errors. Keep back your
  servant also from presumptuous sins"*
- **Luke 23:34; Acts 3:17; 1 Timothy 1:13** — *"they don't know what they are doing"*, and Paul
  saying he was shown mercy *"because I did it ignorantly in unbelief"*

## Analysis

**Genesis 20 is the first place in the Bible where somebody does a thing that carries death, could
not have known, says so, and is believed — and is still not simply told that nothing happened.**

The exchange is four verses long and every clause of it matters.

> **3** *"Behold, you are a dead man, because of the woman whom you have taken. For she is a man's
> wife."*
>
> **5** *"In the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands I have done this."*
>
> **6** *"Yes, I know that in the integrity of your heart you have done this, and I also withheld you
> from sinning against me. Therefore I didn't allow you to touch her."*
>
> **7** *"Now therefore, restore the man's wife. For he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and
> you will live."*

**Three separate things are true at once**, and later Israelite law will be built on the fact that
they are compatible:

1. **The state of the heart is real, and God grants it.** *Tom lēbāb*, integrity of heart, is not
   a plea the text treats as special pleading; it is conceded in the first word of the reply.
2. **The act was still lethal.** *"Behold, you are a dead man"* was not a bluff, and 20:7 repeats the
   sanction in the alternative: *"If you don't restore her, know for sure that you will die, you, and
   all who are yours."* Nothing in the passage says that a sincere man could not have died of this.
3. **What kept it from happening was not the man's integrity but God's prevention.** *"I also
   withheld you from sinning against me"* — the credit for the innocence goes somewhere other than
   the innocent.

### The category the law makes of it

Leviticus 4 opens by naming the situation Genesis 20 dramatizes: *if anyone sins unwittingly*
(*bišgāgâ*, from *šāgag*, to stray or err) *in any of the things which Yahweh has commanded not to be
done*. The chapter then works through the anointed priest, the whole congregation, the ruler, and the
common person, and gives each an offering. **The law's assumption is exactly Abimelech's situation:
that a person can incur real liability without intending anything, and that the remedy is a
procedure rather than an argument.**

Numbers 15:22–31 draws the boundary from the other side. Offerings cover the unwitting sin; the
person who acts *"with a high hand"* — deliberately, in contempt — *"blasphemes Yahweh"* and *"shall
be cut off"*, with nothing provided. **The earlier claim here that the whole sacrificial apparatus is for the first case is
corrected below in light of Leviticus 1–4 and 6:1–7.** That
is worth holding beside how often the Bible's narratives are read as though guilt tracked intention
exactly.

The cities of refuge (Numbers 35:9–34) apply the same logic to killing. The man who kills without
enmity is explicitly not a murderer and explicitly not free: he flees, he is tried, and he stays in
the city until the high priest dies. **Innocence of intent changes the category and does not dissolve
the consequence** — which is precisely what Genesis 20 does, in narrative, with a household that is
healed by a prayer rather than acquitted by an argument.

### Where the Bible does not soften it

Two narratives make the point uncomfortably.

- **Jonathan (1 Samuel 14:24–45)** eats honey in the field because he did not hear his father's rash
  oath. The lot still falls on him, Saul still pronounces the sentence, and it is the army's
  intervention — *"shall Jonathan die, who has worked this great salvation in Israel?"* — that saves
  him, not the fact of his ignorance.
- **Uzzah (2 Samuel 6:6–7)** reaches out to steady the ark when the oxen stumble, and dies. The text
  gives no bad motive and does not suggest one. David's reaction is anger and then fear.

Neither passage is comfortable, and the wiki does not attempt to make them so. What they establish is
that the Bible's world does not treat holiness as inert in the presence of good intentions — which is
the background against which Genesis 20:6's *"I also withheld you"* is not a technicality but a
mercy: the danger was live, and something interposed.

### And where it does soften it

Psalm 19 asks for exactly the thing Abimelech was given. *"Who can discern his errors? Forgive me
from hidden errors"* — *šəgîʾôt*, from the same root as *šəgāgâ* — *"Keep back your servant also from
presumptuous sins"* (19:12–13). **The prayer is for both halves of Genesis 20:6**: cleansing for what
was done unknowingly, and prevention for what would be done knowingly.

The New Testament keeps the category and puts weight on it. *"Father, forgive them, for they don't
know what they are doing"* (Luke 23:34); *"I know that you did this in ignorance, as did also your
rulers"* (Acts 3:17); *"I obtained mercy because I did it ignorantly in unbelief"* (1 Timothy 1:13).
**In each case the ignorance is the ground of the appeal and not the end of the matter** — forgiveness
is still asked for, repentance is still called for, mercy is still described as mercy. That is the
Genesis 20 shape exactly: *your integrity is real, and you still need the prophet to pray.*

### The priest and congregation — Leviticus 4:1–21

The now-ingested unit distinguishes the anointed priest whose sin brings guilt on the
people (3) from the assembly whose violation was hidden and becomes known (13–14).
The elders lay hands on the communal bull; the priest handles its blood and makes
atonement, with forgiveness explicitly promised (15–20). Ignorance and innocence are
not interchangeable categories here.

**Correction to the earlier claim about the whole sacrificial apparatus:** the assertion
above that it is all for unwitting sin is too broad. Leviticus 1–3 regulates offerings
without making every case an unintentional offense; Leviticus 6:1–7 provides restitution
and an offering for fraud, robbery, and false swearing. The particular scope of Leviticus
4 must not become a universal definition of sacrifice. Those later Leviticus verses were
checked in stored WEB for this correction and remain in their own ingestion queue.

**Qualification of the Genesis comparison:** Genesis 20:6 describes God preventing
Abimelech from sinning by touching Sarah; Leviticus 4:13 describes commandments already
violated. Both require a response to revealed danger or liability, but the earlier claim
that they are exactly the same situation overlooks that distinction. The analogy does
not prove that Abimelech performed the offense this statute addresses.

Source for this addition: stored WEB Genesis 20; Leviticus 1–4 and 6:1–7. These conclusions
rest on the English translation; no new source-language claim is made.

### Ruler and individual — a distinct procedure

The ruler and common person incur guilt through unwitting violations (22, 27), and the
remedy follows the offense becoming known (23, 28). Different animals are specified, but
all three procedures conclude with forgiveness (26, 31, 35). This extends the chapter’s
account beyond priest and assembly without making ignorance an acquittal. No named ruler
is accused and no spoken confession is prescribed in this unit.

See [[The Sin Offering for Ruler and Individual]]. Source for this addition: stored WEB Leviticus
4:22–35. No new quotation from an unstored book or source-language claim.

### Specific confession and affordable remedy

Leviticus 5:2–4 emphasizes hiddenness and awareness, while 5:1 describes a witness’s
failure to report without explicitly calling that failure inadvertent. The earlier
grouping of all Leviticus 5:1–19 under unwitting sin is therefore too sweeping. Verse 5
requires specific confession; verses 7–13 provide birds or flour according to means.
Knowledge calls for response, but cost does not exclude the poor from forgiveness.

See [[Confession and Offerings According to Means]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 5:1–13;
this qualification concerns the English text, not a verified Hebrew lexical equation.

### Holy things and wrongdoing without knowledge

[[The Trespass Offering for Holy Things and Unknown Guilt]] makes the distinction explicit: Leviticus 5:17–19 describes
liability despite lack of knowledge, followed by a ram offering and forgiveness. Verses
15–16 separately require restitution plus a fifth for unwitting wrong in holy things.
The repayment provision should not be silently extended to the second case, which does
not repeat it. Neither case says that a subjective fear of guilt proves an actual offense.

Source for this addition: stored WEB Leviticus 5:14–19; no new source-language claim.

## Significance

The Bible's first sustained treatment of guilt makes it a matter of what was done as well as of what
was meant, and refuses to let either swallow the other. Abimelech is neither a sinner who is let off
nor an innocent to whom nothing happened. He is a man who was in danger, was prevented, and was
healed by somebody else's prayer.

**And the reason the case is put through a foreigner is worth noticing.** Genesis could have raised
the question of unwitting guilt through anyone. It raises it through the one man in the chapter with
no covenant, no law, and no instruction — which means the category is presented as universal before
Sinai exists to codify it, in the same way that [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]] and
[[The Noahic Commandments and the Council of Jerusalem]] present the killing of a human being as
answerable everywhere. See [[Sin]] and [[Righteousness]].
