# Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 29:30** — *"He went in also to Rachel, and he loved also Rachel more than Leah, and served
  with him yet seven other years"* — [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]]
- **Genesis 29:31–33** — *"Yahweh saw that Leah was hated, and he opened her womb … 'Because Yahweh
  has heard that I am hated'"*
- **Deuteronomy 21:15–17** — *"If a man has two wives, the one beloved, and the other hated, and they
  have borne him children, both the beloved and the hated; and if the firstborn son is hers who was
  hated; then it shall be, in the day that he causes his sons to inherit that which he has, that he
  may not give the son of the beloved the rights of the firstborn before the son of the hated, who is
  the firstborn: but he shall acknowledge the firstborn, the son of the hated, by giving him a double
  portion of all that he has."*
- **Genesis 35:22; 49:3–4** — Reuben, the hated wife's firstborn, and what he loses
- **1 Chronicles 5:1–2** — *"his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph"*, stated outright
- Compare **Genesis 37:3–4** — the same construction one generation on: *loved more*, then *hated*
- Compare **Malachi 1:2–3; Romans 9:13** — *"I loved Jacob; but Esau I hated"*, the same idiom applied
  to God

## Analysis

**Deuteronomy 21:15–17 describes one household in the Bible, and it is Jacob's.**

Every term in the law is on the page in Genesis 29–30. *A man has two wives* — Leah and Rachel. *One
beloved and the other hated* — 29:30 says *loved more*, and 29:31 says *hated*, which is the same
statement in the two registers Hebrew uses for it. *Both have borne him children.* *The firstborn son
is hers who was hated* — Reuben, Leah's, the first of the twelve. **The law then forbids the one thing
that household goes on to do.**

### Loved, hated, and what the idiom means

***Śānēʾ*** is *to hate*, and in this construction it is **comparative rather than emotional.** The
proof is Genesis 29 itself, where the two verses run consecutively:

| | |
|---|---|
| **29:30** | *"he **loved also Rachel more than** Leah"* |
| **29:31** | *"Yahweh saw that Leah was **hated**"* |

**The narrator says *loved less* and then says *hated*, about the same situation, one verse apart.**
Nothing in the passage reports Jacob doing anything hostile to Leah; what it reports is a ranking.
The same idiom lets Malachi say *"I loved Jacob; but Esau I hated"* about a choice between two
peoples, and lets Jesus say a disciple must *"hate"* his father and mother (Luke 14:26) — **a
comparative, in a language that states preference by naming both poles.** See [[Love]].

**The law is written in that idiom, which is why it names the wives by rank rather than by conduct.**
Deuteronomy 21 is not legislating about a man who mistreats a wife. It is legislating about a man who
has one favourite, and it protects the son of the other one **precisely because the preference is
real and is expected to persist.**

### What Jacob's household does

**The law is broken, twice, and the second time by Jacob himself in his own last speech.**

- **Reuben, Leah's firstborn, loses the double portion.** *"Unstable as water, you shall not excel,
  because you went up to your father's bed"* (49:4) — the disinheriting is for the incident at 35:22,
  which Genesis reports in a single verse and then breaks off from mid-sentence.
- **The birthright goes to the loved wife's son.** [[Rachel]]'s firstborn is Joseph, and Joseph gets
  the double portion in the only form a double portion can take in this book: **two tribes**, Ephraim
  and Manasseh, blessed with crossed hands and the younger deliberately put first (48:13–20).
  **1 Chronicles 5:1–2 states the transfer without euphemism** — *"Reuben … he was the firstborn; but,
  because he defiled his father's couch, his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph"* — and then
  adds the correction the law never got to make: *"the birthright was Joseph's"*, but *"Judah
  prevailed above his brothers, and of him came the prince."* **Judah is Leah's.**

**So the outcome is split**, and the Chronicler is the one who notices: **the double portion goes to
the loved wife's line and the kingship to the hated wife's**, and the Bible's royal and messianic
descent runs through the woman Jacob did not choose.

### What the connection does not claim

- **Deuteronomy never mentions Jacob**, and Genesis never mentions the law. **No biblical writer
  connects them.** The correspondence is between the law's terms and the narrative's facts, and it is
  the reader who holds both — the same posture the wiki takes at
  [[Two Sisters - The Marriage Genesis Records and Leviticus Forbids]].
- **Genesis gives a reason for Reuben's loss that the law would not accept as one.** 49:4 disinherits
  him for his own act, not for his mother's standing — and Deuteronomy 21:15–17 addresses only the
  case of preference, saying nothing about a firstborn who forfeits by conduct. **The two texts are
  not in direct contradiction**, and the wiki says so rather than manufacturing one. What is true is
  that the portion lands where the law says it must not.
- **Nobody in Genesis is condemned for any of it.** The narrator states the ranking at 29:30 and 37:3
  and reports what followed. **The verdicts are all in the plot.** See [[Retribution]].

## Significance

- **It is the second law in the Torah written against this family's practice**, after the one that
  forbids marrying two sisters, and the pair of them cover the whole of Genesis 29–30 between them.
  **The Torah narrates a household and then legislates it out of existence**, without a word of
  cross-reference in either direction. See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]].
- **It explains why Genesis 29:31 reads the way it does.** *"Yahweh saw that Leah was hated"* is not a
  psychological note; it is the same legal-idiomatic word Deuteronomy uses, and **the sentence puts God
  on the side the law will later take** — the un-preferred wife's — four hundred years before the law
  says so.
- **And it is the seam where the two halves of Israel's future part.** Priesthood and kingship come out
  of the hated wife; the double portion comes out of the loved one. **Genesis arranges that in a
  chapter with no divine speech in it at all**, and then opens the next verse with *"Yahweh saw."*
