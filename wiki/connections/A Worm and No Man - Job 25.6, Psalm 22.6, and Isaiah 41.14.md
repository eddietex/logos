# A Worm and No Man - Job 25.6, Psalm 22.6, and Isaiah 41.14

## Passages involved

- **Job 25:5–6** — Bildad: *"Behold, even the moon has no brightness, and the stars are not pure in
  his sight; How much less man, who is a worm, the son of man, who is a worm!"* —
  [[Bildad's Third Speech]]
- **Job 7:5** — *"My flesh is clothed with worms and clods of dust"* — [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]
- **Job 17:14** — *"If I have said to corruption, 'You are my father;' to the worm, 'My mother,' and
  'my sister'"* — [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]]
- **Job 21:26** — *"They lie down alike in the dust. The worm covers them."* —
  [[Why Do the Wicked Live]]
- **Job 24:20** — *"The worm shall feed sweetly on him"* — [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]]
- **Psalm 22:6** *(not yet ingested)* — *"But I am a worm, and no man; a reproach of men, and despised
  by the people."*
- **Psalm 22:1** *(not yet ingested)* — *"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"*, quoted from the
  cross at Matthew 27:46
- **Job 30:9–10, 20** — *"Now I have become their song … don't hesitate to spit in my face"*, and *"I
  cry to you, and you do not answer me"*: the psalm's *"a reproach of men"*, *"All those who see me mock
  me"* (22:6–7) and *"I cry in the daytime, but you don't answer"* (22:2), without the worm and without
  the rescue — [[Now They Mock Me]]
- **Isaiah 41:14** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Don't be afraid, you worm Jacob, and you men of Israel. I
  will help you," says Yahweh, "and your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel.*"
- **Isaiah 66:24; Mark 9:48** *(not yet ingested)* — *"their worm will not die, nor will their fire be
  quenched"*: the worm of judgment
- **Acts 12:23** *(not yet ingested)* — Herod, *"because he didn't give God the glory … was eaten by
  worms and died"*

## Analysis

**Three times the Hebrew Bible calls a living human being a worm, and each time someone different is
speaking.** Bildad says it of humanity, in the third person, to diminish. The psalmist says it of
himself, in the first person, in a lament. God says it to Israel, in the second person, in a promise.

| | Speaker | Of whom | Word | What follows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Job 25:6** | Bildad | *man*, *the son of man* — everyone | *rimmâ* and *tôlēʿâ* | nothing; the friends stop speaking |
| **Psalm 22:6** | the sufferer | *I* | *tôlaʿat* | *"a reproach of men, and despised by the people"* — then rescue (22:21–24) |
| **Isaiah 41:14** | Yahweh | *you*, Jacob | *tôlaʿat* | *"Don't be afraid … I will help you … your Redeemer"* |

**Bildad's is the only one in which the worm is a category.** The psalmist's worm is a description of
how he has been treated — scorned, mocked, surrounded, the next verses say — and the verse is a
complaint against the people who reduced him. Isaiah's worm is how small and exposed Israel is among
the nations, and the line exists to be contradicted by help. In both, the speaker or the hearer is
*made* a worm by circumstances, and someone objects. Bildad's worm is not made by anything. It is what
*ʾĕnôš* and *ben-ʾādām* are, set beneath moon and stars as the bottom of a scale, and nobody objects.

**Job has been using the word all along — for death.** *Rimmâ* is the maggot of decay, and in Job's
mouth it is what happens to a body: his own sores (7:5), the grave he claims as kin (17:14), the
common end of the prosperous and the bitter (21:26), the end of the wicked (24:20). Job's worm is
honest about mortality and makes no judgment about worth. Bildad moves it from the grave to the
living, and from what death does to what a man deserves. It is the same move the friends make with
everything Job gives them — his *tear* (16:9 against 18:4), his *net* (18:8 against 19:6), his
*dread* (23:15 against 25:2) — taken from his experience and returned as his verdict.

**The worm of Psalm 22 belongs to the man who asks why God has forsaken him.** Psalm 22 opens *"My
God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"*, and its sixth verse is the worm. That is Job's question
without the lawsuit, and its speaker's worm is a confession of how low he has been brought, said to
God and not about him. Matthew 27:46 puts the psalm's first line on the cross, and Christian reading
has long applied the whole psalm, the worm included, to the crucifixion. **The Bible's fullest use of
*worm* for a human being is the lament of a sufferer whom God hears** (22:24), not the doctrine of a
friend who has stopped listening.

**Isaiah 41:14 is the answer Bildad does not give.** It grants the smallness entirely — *"you worm
Jacob"* — and draws the opposite inference: not *how much less*, but *don't be afraid*. It also
supplies the word Job reached for at 19:25: *gōʾēl*, Redeemer. The worm in Isaiah has a Redeemer, and
says so in the same verse.

**The worm of judgment is a different image.** Isaiah 66:24 and its quotation at Mark 9:48 (*"their
worm will not die"*) and Herod's death in Acts 12:23 use the worm as Job does at 24:20 — the decay
that falls on the wicked — not as a name for the living human being. They belong to Job's usage and
not to Bildad's.

## Significance

- **The same word carries contempt, lament, and comfort**, and the difference is not the word but the
  grammatical person and the direction of address. A worm spoken *about* everyone, to a man who asked
  for a hearing, is the friends' theology at its end. A worm spoken *by* the sufferer to God is prayer.
  A worm spoken *by* God to the sufferer is a promise.
- **It completes the record of the friends' anthropology.** See
  [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]], where Bildad's worm is the bottom step of a three-speech
  descent from Eliphaz's *"houses of clay"* (4:19). This page records where the Bible puts the worm
  when it is not being used to close an argument.
- **Psalm 22 and Isaiah 41 are not yet ingested.** Their analysis here is provisional and should be
  revisited when the Psalms and Isaiah are reached.
