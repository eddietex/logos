# Seventy Nations - Genesis 10 in Deuteronomy 32 and Luke 10

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 10:1–32** — the Table of Nations: fourteen from Japheth, thirty from Ham, twenty-six from
  Shem. See [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 46:27; Exodus 1:5; Deuteronomy 10:22** — *"all the souls of the house of Jacob, who came
  into Egypt, were seventy."*
- **Exodus 24:1, 9; Numbers 11:16–25** — seventy elders on the mountain, and seventy who receive the
  spirit.
- **Numbers 29:12–34** — the bulls of Sukkot: thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven — the
  descending series that totals seventy.
- **Deuteronomy 32:8** — *"When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he separated
  the children of men, he set the bounds of the peoples according to the number of the children of
  Israel."*
- **Luke 10:1, 17** — seventy (or seventy-two) sent out ahead of Jesus, two by two, into every city he
  was about to enter.

## Analysis

**The count.** Genesis 10 yields seventy names below Noah: Japheth's fourteen, Ham's thirty, Shem's
twenty-six. It is not a mechanical total — Sheba and Havilah each appear twice (10:7 and 10:28–29),
several entries are plural peoples rather than persons, and *Calneh* at 10:10 may be a common noun.
Different reckonings are possible, and the ancient ones differ: the Septuagint's Table comes to
seventy-two, having added Elisha to Japheth's line and Cainan to Shem's. What is stable is that the
number is round and that no ancient reader appears to have arrived at a different total by accident.

**Seventy is Scripture's number for a complete set held in order.** Jacob's household is seventy going
down into Egypt. The elders who eat before God on Sinai are seventy, and the seventy who receive a
share of Moses' spirit are the same institution. Sukkot's week of bull offerings totals seventy, which
the Talmud reads as an offering on behalf of the seventy nations (*Sukkah* 55b) — a Jewish festival
sacrificing, in the temple, for everyone else. The number does not mean *approximately many*; it means
*all of them, counted*.

**Deuteronomy 32:8 puts the two seventies in one sentence, and the text of that sentence is disputed.**
The Masoretic reading, which the WEB follows, is *"according to the number of the children of Israel"*
— the nations divided to match the seventy of Jacob's house, so that the two lists correspond and
Israel's own size is the measure of the world's. The Septuagint reads *"according to the number of the
angels of God"*, and the Qumran manuscript 4QDeut^j reads *bənê ʾĕlōhîm*, **sons of God** — which
makes the verse a division of the nations *among* heavenly beings, each people allotted one, with
Israel kept by Yahweh himself in the next verse. The variant is old and the manuscript evidence for
the second reading is strong; most modern critical editions prefer it as the harder and better-attested
text, and the Masoretic reading as an adjustment away from a sentence that could be misread
polytheistically.

Both readings depend on Genesis 10. The first needs the number seventy to be there. The second needs
the division of the nations to be an act with a fixed roster, which is what the Table supplies. And
the second reading connects onward to Deuteronomy 4:19, Psalm 82, and Daniel 10:13, 20 — where the
nations have princes, and the princes can be opposed.

**Luke 10 sends out seventy.** Twelve were sent to Israel in Luke 9:1–6, one per tribe. Then seventy
are sent *"into every city and place where he was about to come"* — and the number, against the
background above, is not arbitrary: it is the number of the nations. The manuscripts are split between
**seventy** and **seventy-two**, and they are split along precisely the line that separates the
Masoretic Table from the Septuagint's. The textual history of Luke 10:1 inherits the textual history of
Genesis 10.

Luke does not comment on the number, and the mission of Luke 10 is inside Israel, not outside it. So
the connection is a resonance rather than a stated fulfilment — but Luke is the evangelist who traces
Jesus' genealogy back past Abraham to Adam (Luke 3:38), who quotes *"all flesh will see God's
salvation"* (3:6), and who writes the second volume in which the nations arrive. He is unlikely to
have chosen the number carelessly.

## Significance

The thread is one of the clearest cases in Scripture of a bare list doing theological work centuries
later. Genesis 10 records no promise, no command, and no divine speech; it counts. And the count is
then picked up as a way of saying three different things:

1. **That the world is complete and known** — there are this many peoples, and God set their bounds.
2. **That Israel is proportioned to it** — seventy nations, seventy in Jacob's house, seventy elders.
   Israel is not the world's exception but its scale model.
3. **That the mission is addressed to all of it** — seventy sent out, into every place.

It also shows how a textual variant can travel. The seventy/seventy-two split in Genesis 10 (Masoretic
against Septuagint) reappears in Deuteronomy 32:8 (children of Israel against sons of God) and again in
Luke 10:1 (seventy against seventy-two), and in each case the two readings are theologically live
rather than trivial. Whichever is original, the ancient readers were clearly counting Genesis 10
against the rest of the canon and expecting the numbers to line up.
