# The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest

## Passages involved

- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — Genesis 9:25–27, the curse and its threefold refrain
- Genesis 10:15–19 *(not yet ingested)* — Canaan's eleven descendants and the border of the land
- Genesis 15:16 — *"the iniquity of the Amorite is not yet full"*
- Leviticus 18:24–30; 20:22–24 — the land vomiting out its inhabitants for what they did
- Deuteronomy 7:1–5; 9:4–5; 20:16–18 — the conquest commanded, and its stated reason
- Joshua 9:22–27 — the Gibeonites made *"cutters of wood and drawers of water"*
- Judges 1:28–35; 1 Kings 9:20–21 — Canaanites under forced labour
- Ezra 9:1; Nehemiah 9:8 — the peoples of the land named again after the exile

## Analysis

Genesis 9:25 is the primeval history's only statement about a specific nation's status, and the nation
it names is the one Israel will spend six books taking a land from. The obvious reading is that the
episode exists for that purpose — an **etiology**, a story told to account for a relation that already
holds. Three features of the text support it.

**The gloss is doubled.** *"Ham is the father of Canaan"* is stated at 9:18 and again at 9:22, and
nobody else's paternity is noted anywhere in the passage. The narrative works hard to get Canaan into
a scene he takes no part in.

**The curse is repeated three times in three verses** (9:25, 26, 27) and is the only element common to
all three lines of the oracle. Whatever else the oracle does, it is about Canaan.

**The offence is displaced.** Ham sees; Canaan is cursed. A story constructed to explain a present
relation would put the sentence where the relation is; a story reporting an incident would put it
where the guilt is.

### What the conquest texts actually appeal to

Here is the striking part, and it is easy to miss because the assumption runs the other way: **the
Bible never uses Genesis 9:25 to justify anything.** Not once. No conquest narrative, prophet, psalm,
or epistle cites Noah's curse.

When the Torah gives a reason for dispossessing the Canaanites, it gives a moral one and grounds it in
their conduct, not their ancestry:

- **Genesis 15:16** delays the possession of the land for four generations *"for the iniquity of the
  Amorite is not yet full"* — which presumes a threshold that has not been crossed, and therefore a
  people not yet liable. A curse pronounced by Noah would already have settled the matter centuries
  earlier.
- **Leviticus 18:24–30** grounds the expulsion in the practices just listed and warns Israel that the
  land *"vomits out"* whoever does them — *"that the land not vomit you out also, when you defile it,
  as it vomited out the nation that was before you"*. The threat is portable, which is exactly what a
  hereditary curse is not.
- **Deuteronomy 9:4–5** is explicit on both sides: *"Don't say in your heart… 'For my righteousness
  Yahweh has brought me in to possess this land'… but for the wickedness of these nations Yahweh your
  God does drive them out from before you."* Neither Israel's merit nor Canaan's ancestor is the
  ground.

The nearest thing in Scripture to 9:25 being enacted is **Joshua 9**, where the Gibeonites — Hivites,
and therefore Canaanites by Genesis 10:17 — become *"cutters of wood and drawers of water for the
congregation"* in perpetuity. Even there the text gives the reason as their deception and the oath
Israel swore, not as Noah's word; the servitude is the price of a treaty they obtained by fraud.
Judges 1:28–35 and 1 Kings 9:20–21 then report Canaanites put to forced labour under the monarchy as a
matter of policy and of Israel's failure to drive them out, again without citing Genesis.

### What follows

The wiki records the etiological reading as the most economical account of why the episode is shaped
as it is, and records equally that **the canon itself declines to use it**. Those two facts sit
together and are both worth having. Genesis 9:25 explains, in the manner of ancient origin stories,
how the storyteller's world came to be arranged as it is; the legal and prophetic material that
actually adjudicates Israel's relation to the Canaanites argues from behaviour, from covenant, and
from a warning that the same land will do the same thing to Israel — as it eventually does.

## Significance

Two consequences, one interpretive and one moral.

**Interpretive.** A curse pronounced by a drunk patriarch, unratified by God, uncited by any later
writer, and superseded in every text that addresses the same question, is a weak foundation for a
doctrine of anything. Reading Genesis 9:25 as the theological ground of the conquest inverts the
canon's own priorities, which put the reason in Leviticus 18 and Deuteronomy 9 and leave Noah's oracle
standing as narrative.

**Moral.** The verse's actual career as a warrant happened entirely outside Scripture, and it was not
aimed at Canaanites. Its use to sanction the enslavement of African peoples — for roughly fifteen
centuries, and most consequentially in the Atlantic slave trade and its nineteenth-century American
apologetic — rests on transferring the curse from Canaan to Ham and from the Levant to a continent,
against the plain sense of a text that names the target six times. The six textual counts against that
reading are set out on [[Ham]] and on [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]]; this page adds the
seventh, which is that the Bible's own writers, who had every occasion to use the verse, never did.
