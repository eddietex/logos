# Entertaining Angels Unawares

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 18:1–8** — *"Yahweh appeared to him by the oaks of Mamre … three men stood opposite
  him … He stood by them under the tree, and they ate."* See
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Genesis 19:1–3** — *"The two angels came to Sodom at evening. Lot sat in the gate of Sodom …
  he made them a feast, and baked unleavened bread, and they ate."*
- **Judges 6:11–24** — Gideon brings a kid and unleavened cakes to a stranger under the oak, who
  touches them with a staff and vanishes in the fire: *"Alas, Lord Yahweh! Because I have seen
  Yahweh's angel face to face!"*
- **Judges 13:2–23** — Manoah offers a kid, the visitor tells him to offer it to Yahweh instead,
  refuses to give his name, and goes up in the altar flame: *"Manoah didn't know that he was
  Yahweh's angel."*
- **Tobit 12:19** *(deuterocanonical)* — Raphael, at the end: *"All these days I did appear unto
  you; but I did neither eat nor drink, but ye did see a vision."*
- **Luke 24:13–35** — two disciples press a stranger to stay the night: *"He was known by them in
  the breaking of the bread."*
- **Hebrews 13:2** — *"Don't forget to show hospitality to strangers, for in doing so, some have
  entertained angels without knowing it."*

## Analysis

**Hebrews 13:2 is a single clause and it names no one, which is why it works.** *Mē epilanthanesthe
tēs philoxenias, dia tautēs gar elathon tines xenisantes angelous* — *some, without knowing it, have
hosted angels*. The verb *elathon* is the same root as *lanthanō*, to escape notice, and it is doing
the argument's whole job: the point is not that angels are worth hosting, it is that **you cannot
tell**, and therefore the rule has to be applied to everyone at the door.

The *tines*, *some*, is almost certainly Abraham and Lot. Genesis 18–19 is the only pair of scenes
in Scripture where hosts feed messengers, do not know it, and are told later; Judges 6 and 13 have
the recognition inside the scene, and in Judges 13 the visitor declines the meal. Hebrews has just
used Abraham as its long example of faith (11:8–19), and Genesis 18 supplies both the meal and the
not-knowing.

### What the guests eat, and whether they eat

Scripture is not consistent here, and the inconsistency is the interesting part.

| | The meal | What happens to it |
|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 18:8** | curds, milk, veal, cakes | *"and they ate"* — flat, unexplained |
| **Genesis 19:3** | a feast, unleavened bread | *"and they ate"* |
| **Judges 6:19–21** | a kid and cakes on a rock | touched with a staff, consumed by fire from the rock |
| **Judges 13:15–16** | a kid | refused: *"I will not eat your bread. If you prepare a burnt offering, you must offer it to Yahweh"* |
| **Tobit 12:19** | — | eating denied outright: *"ye did see a vision"* |
| **Luke 24:30, 43** | bread; and later broiled fish | he takes, blesses, breaks, gives — and in 24:43 eats |

Genesis says the visitors ate and offers no gloss. Judges converts the meal into a sacrifice, which
is the older text's problem being solved. Tobit — Second Temple, and self-conscious about it —
denies the eating altogether. Later Jewish and Christian commentators mostly followed Tobit's
instinct: Josephus has the visitors only *seem* to eat (*Antiquities* I.197), and Genesis Rabbah 48
canvasses several ways to avoid the plain sense, including that they appeared to eat as a courtesy
to the host. **The wiki records Genesis's own reticence: it says they ate, and it is the same book
that will have Jacob wrestle a man all night and come away limping.**

The other detail Genesis notices without comment is the menu. Curds, milk, and a calf on the same
table is a combination that later halakhah forbids, and the rabbinic literature discusses the verse
at length for exactly that reason. Genesis is describing a meal, not a kitchen.

### The recognition, and where it sits

The scenes divide by *when the host finds out*.

- **Genesis 18** — Abraham is never told. The narrator tells the reader in verse 1; Abraham behaves
  the same way either side of the announcement, and by 18:23 he is arguing with the guest.
- **Genesis 19** — Lot is not told either, and the guests end up rescuing the host.
- **Judges 6 and 13** — recognition inside the scene, and both hosts are terrified by it: *"We shall
  surely die, because we have seen God."*
- **Luke 24** — recognition at the table and in the act of hospitality itself, and the guest becomes
  the host: *"he took the bread and gave thanks. Breaking it, he gave it to them."* The Emmaus meal
  reverses the roles at the exact moment of recognition, which is what John 13:1–17 does with the
  foot-washing.

**Hebrews takes the first case and makes it the rule.** Its ethic depends on the host not knowing —
if you can identify the angel, the command is about angels; because you cannot, it is about
strangers.

### Reception: the icon

Because Orthodox practice does not depict the Father, Genesis 18 became the standard visual subject
for the Trinity in Eastern Christianity, under its proper name — **the Hospitality of Abraham**,
*Philoxenia*, the same Greek word as Hebrews 13:2. Andrei Rublev's icon of about 1425 strips the
scene of Abraham, Sarah, the tent, and the calf, and leaves three figures at a table with a cup;
it is the best-known image of the doctrine in existence and it is a picture of a meal in Genesis.

The reading behind it goes back at least to Ambrose's *tres vidit, unum adoravit* — *he saw three
and worshipped one* — and is argued at length in Augustine's *De Trinitate* II, though Augustine is
notably careful not to assign the persons. The wiki records this as reception. Genesis's own concern
is with a host who did not know; the New Testament quotes this chapter at Luke 1:37, Romans 9:9,
Hebrews 13:2 and 1 Peter 3:6, and none of the four reads the three visitors that way. See
[[The Angel of Yahweh]] and [[Let Us Go Down - The Divine Plural in Genesis]].

## Significance

- **The ethic is built on ignorance, not on discernment.** Hebrews does not tell its readers to look
  out for angels; it tells them that the people who hosted angels could not tell, so the only safe
  policy is to receive everyone. That is a stronger command than *be kind to visitors* and it is
  derived entirely from a narrative detail — that Genesis 18:1 tells the reader something Abraham
  is never told.
- **It is the clearest case in the canon of a New Testament ethic argued from a Genesis scene rather
  than from a Genesis law.** No commandment about hospitality is quoted; the story is the argument.
- **And it sets up the judgment texts.** If the guest may be more than a guest, then a town that
  refuses one has done something worse than be rude — which is what Genesis 19, Judges 19, and Luke
  10:10–12 all say, each of them measuring a place by the same meal. See [[Hospitality]] and
  [[Sodom]].
