# Sacrifice Before the Priesthood

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 4:3–5** — Cain and Abel bring offerings, uncommanded, *"as time passed"*.
- **Genesis 8:20–21** — Noah builds the first altar in Scripture and offers burnt offerings of
  every clean animal and bird.
- **Job 1:5** — Job sanctifies his children and offers burnt offerings for each of them, after
  every round of their feasting, *"continually"*.
- **Genesis 12:7–8** — Abram's altars at Shechem and by Bethel, with no offering mentioned at either
  — [[The Call of Abram]]
- **Genesis 13:18** — Abram's third altar, at the oaks of Mamre, again with nothing said to be
  offered on it — [[Abram and Lot Separate]]
- **Genesis 22:9, 13** — the altar on Moriah, and the ram offered on it *"instead of his son"*: the
  first burnt offering in Genesis since Noah's, and the only one the Abraham cycle completes —
  [[The Binding of Isaac]]
- **Genesis 31:54; 35:1–7** *(not yet ingested)* — the rest of the patriarchs' altars.
- **Exodus 24:5** — *"young men of the children of Israel"* offer the covenant sacrifices, before
  any priesthood exists.
- **Exodus 28–29; Leviticus 1–9** — the priesthood instituted and the offerings regulated.
- **Job 42:7–9** — Job's intercession for the three friends, and the last sacrifice in the book.
- **Genesis 14:18–20** — Melchizedek, the first man in Scripture called a priest, in a scene with no
  altar and no offering — [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]]
- **Hebrews 7:1–28** — a priesthood that does not descend from Levi.

## Analysis

Every sacrifice in the Bible before Sinai is offered by a man who has not been told to offer it,
at an altar nobody authorised, with no priest present, under no rule that says what is acceptable.
This thread follows that pattern to its clearest instance, which is Job.

**The pattern in Genesis.** Cain and Abel bring their gifts on their own initiative — no command,
no altar, no ritual language, and no stated basis for the difference in reception (see
[[Sacrifice and Offering]] and [[Cain and Abel]]). Noah builds the first *altar* and offers the
first *burnt offering* immediately on leaving the ark, again unprompted, and the response he gets
is not addressed to him at all: *"Yahweh said in his heart"* (see [[The Waters Recede]]). In both
cases the offering is response rather than transaction, and in both the initiative is entirely
human.

**Abram's altars change the pattern's shape without breaking it.** At 12:7 he builds *"to Yahweh, who
had appeared to him"*, and at 12:8 he builds and *"called on Yahweh's name"* — still uncommanded,
still with no priest, and now with **no offering named at all**. Genesis reports the construction and
says nothing about a victim, a burning, or an aroma, and it goes on doing this at 13:18, 26:25, and
33:20. So the patriarchal altar is a different instrument from Noah's: Noah's produced a smell that
God responded to, while Abram's mark places and declare a name in a land he does not own. The
uncommandedness is the constant; what is offered, and whether anything is, drops out of view for six
chapters. See [[The Altar at Shechem and the Covenant at Shechem]].

**Job 1:5 is the pattern's outer limit**, and it is worth stating exactly how far it goes.

> **1:5** It was so, when the days of their feasting had run their course, that Job sent and
> sanctified them, and rose up early in the morning, and offered burnt offerings according to the
> number of them all. For Job said, “It may be that my sons have sinned, and renounced God in their
> hearts.” Job did so continually.

Job is his own priest — he *sanctifies* his children himself, using *qādaš*, the verb of ritual
preparation that Exodus and Leviticus will reserve for consecrated persons and objects. He offers
a whole burnt offering (*ʿōlâ*), the same category as Noah's, one per child. And the occasion is
**a sin that may not have occurred**: not a transgression observed, confessed, or even suspected on
evidence, but a hypothesis — *"It may be"* — about an interior act, *"in their hearts"*.

That is a sacrifice for a possible, invisible offence committed by someone else, offered
repeatedly, forever. Nothing in the later Levitical system does this. Leviticus 4–5 provides for
unintentional sin, and the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16) covers the sins of the people
collectively and including those unknown — but both operate on the assumption that sin has in fact
happened, and both run through a priest. Job's practice runs ahead of the offence and through
nobody.

**Why the absence of law matters here.** The book of Job has no covenant, no Torah, no sanctuary
and no priesthood anywhere in it, and this verse is one of the strongest internal signals that it
is set — or means to appear set — in a patriarchal world before Sinai. See [[Uz]] and [[Job]]. But
it does something more than date the story. **It shows the friends' theology operating in a world
with no rule book.** Retribution is not presented in Job as a misreading of Leviticus; it is
presented as what people conclude on their own, from watching how life goes, when no revelation has
told them otherwise. That makes the book's critique broader than a critique of Israelite legalism.

**The epilogue closes the loop.** At 42:8 God tells Eliphaz to take seven bulls and seven rams,
offer a burnt offering — the same *ʿōlâ* — and have Job pray for them, *"for I will accept him,
that I not deal with you according to your folly."* The book's first and last sacrifices are both
Job interceding for others. The first is for children who may have sinned in their hearts; the last
is for three friends who certainly have sinned with their mouths, and this time God says outright
that the intercession works. The man whose scrupulousness the accuser called mercenary in chapter 1
is the man God will only be approached through in chapter 42.

## Significance

- **The priesthood arrives without a sacrifice attached, which is the mirror image of the rest of
  this thread.** Every case above is a sacrifice with no priest; Genesis 14:18 is a priest with no
  sacrifice — *kōhēn*'s first occurrence in the Bible, attached to a Canaanite king who brings out
  bread and wine, blesses, and receives a tenth. The office and the act enter Scripture separately
  and neither waits for the law. See [[Priesthood]] and [[Melchizedek]].
- **Sacrifice is older than the law that regulates it**, and the Bible does not treat the earlier
  instances as irregular. Noah's altar sets the vocabulary — *ʿōlâ*, *rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ* — that Leviticus
  then inherits, and Job's practice is reported without a hint of criticism.
- **The head of a household acts as its priest.** Cain and Abel act for themselves, but Noah offers
  on behalf of a household and a whole surviving creation, and Job offers on behalf of his ten
  children by name and number. The office is real before it is institutional, which is part of what
  Hebrews 7 is arguing when it locates a priesthood outside Levi's line.
- **Job 1:5 makes the accuser's question sharp.** *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* has force
  precisely because verse 5 shows a man whose piety exceeds any known requirement. Extraordinary
  scrupulousness looks like either extraordinary devotion or an extraordinarily careful investment,
  and nothing observable distinguishes them. See [[The Fear of God]].
- **An offering for a sin of the heart is a problem the sacrificial system never solves.** Job's
  intuition — that the offence that matters most is interior and undetectable — is the same
  intuition that drives Psalm 51:16–17, Jeremiah 31:33, and the Sermon on the Mount, and it is why
  the burnt offerings have to be repeated *continually*.

- **And the first anointing has no office behind it either.** Genesis 28:18 has Jacob pour oil on a
  stone at Bethel — **the first *šemen* in Scripture** — centuries before any priest, king, or vessel
  is anointed, and God himself later refers to the act without qualification: *"where you anointed a
  pillar"* (31:13). **Nothing is killed and nothing is burned**, which makes it the theme's cleanest
  case: a cultic act performed correctly by a man who holds no position and has been given no rite.
  See [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] and
  [[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]].
