# With Harshness - Exodus 1.13-14 and Leviticus 25

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 1:13–14** — *"The Egyptians ruthlessly made the children of Israel serve … all their
  service, in which they ruthlessly made them serve"* — [[The Oppression in Egypt]]
- **Exodus 1:17, 21** — *"the midwives feared God"*
- **Leviticus 25:43** — *"You shall not rule over him with harshness, but shall fear your God"*
- **Leviticus 25:46** — *"over your brothers the children of Israel you shall not rule, one over
  another, with harshness"*
- **Leviticus 25:53** — *"He shall not rule with harshness over him in your sight"*
- Compare **Leviticus 25:42, 55** — Israel as God's servants whom he brought out of Egypt *(by
  reference; to be quoted when Leviticus is reached)*

## Analysis

***Bəpārek***, *with rigour* — the WEB's *ruthlessly* in Exodus and *with harshness* in Leviticus —
**occurs in the Torah only in these two places**: twice in Exodus 1:13–14 and three times in the
jubilee law of Leviticus 25. (Ezekiel 34:4 uses it of Israel's shepherds, outside the Torah.)

**The law of the Hebrew servant is written in the vocabulary of Egypt.** An Israelite who sells
himself to a fellow Israelite may be made to work, but not *bəpārek* — not as Egypt made Israel
work. And the alternative the law gives is the midwives' virtue: *"but shall fear your God"*
(25:43). **Exodus 1 sets *harshness* and *the fear of God* side by side as the oppressor's method
and the thing that resisted it; Leviticus 25 makes them the two options open to every Israelite
master.**

**The reasoning is stated in the same chapter** (25:42, 55, by reference): the Israelites are God's
servants, brought out of Egypt, and so may not be sold or treated as slaves are. **The Exodus makes
Israel Yahweh's servants and therefore no one else's** — the same *ʿābad* that Exodus 1 uses five
times for Egypt's service.

## Significance

**Israel's law remembers Exodus 1 as a prohibition.** What Egypt did to Israel, Israel may not do to
itself. The thread runs through the Torah's social law — the stranger, the servant, the poor — and
is usually given its explicit reason: *you were slaves in Egypt*. See [[Slavery and Servitude]],
[[The Fear of God]], and [[Remembrance]].
