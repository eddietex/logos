# Why Do You Ask My Name - Genesis 32, Exodus 3, and Judges 13

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 32:29** — Jacob: *"Please tell me your name."* The wrestler: *"Why is it that you ask
  what my name is?"* He blesses him there and gives nothing else. — [[Wrestling at the Jabbok]]
- **Exodus 3:13–15** — Moses: *"they ask me, 'What is his name?' What should I tell them?"* God: *"I
  AM WHO I AM," and he said, "You shall tell the children of Israel this: 'I AM has sent me to you.'"*
  — [[The Burning Bush]]
- **Judges 13:17–18** — Manoah: *"What is your name?"* The angel of Yahweh: *"Why do you ask my
  name, since it is incomprehensible?"*

## Analysis

**Three men ask a divine figure for a name, and none of them gets a proper noun back.**

The two closest in wording are Genesis and Judges. **Jacob's *"tell me your name"* and the wrestler's
refusal are, almost word for word, Manoah's question and the angel's answer** — Judges 13:18's
***pilʾî***, *incomprehensible* or *wonderful*, is a stronger refusal than Genesis supplies (which
gives only the question thrown back, with no adjective attached), but the shape of the exchange —
*ask, deflect with a counter-question, give nothing, then act* — is identical in both. **Both men are
then told, or conclude, that they have seen God and are alive**: Jacob names the place for it
(*"I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved"*, 32:30); Manoah tells his wife *"we shall
surely die, because we have seen God"* (Judges 13:22), and is corrected only by his wife's plainer
logic — a God who wanted them dead would not have accepted their offering (13:23).

**Exodus is the odd one out, and it is odd on purpose.** Moses does not merely ask and get refused; he
is given an answer, and the answer is a sentence rather than a noun — ***ʾehyeh ʾăšer ʾehyeh***, *I
will be what I will be* (or *I am what I am*), which folds back on itself grammatically so that no
name is actually transmitted. [[Names and Naming]] already calls this *"the theme meeting its
limit"* — the one place a request for a name is answered with a name that is also, functionally, a
refusal. **Genesis and Judges withhold outright; Exodus withholds by complying.**

**What all three share is the moment the question is asked.** In each case a human has just been
addressed, commanded, or physically overpowered by a figure who has not offered a name voluntarily,
and in each case the human's *next* move — after the blessing, after the burning bush, after the
angel's ascent in the altar flame (Judges 13:20) — is the one the text records, not the name. **The
withheld name is not an oversight the narrative regrets; in every instance the encounter concludes
successfully without one.**

### What the pairing does not say

**It does not identify the three figures with each other.** Genesis 32's wrestler, Exodus 3's voice
from the bush, and Judges 13's *malʾak YHWH* are treated as three separate instances of the same
narrative pattern on [[The Angel of Yahweh]], not as three appearances of one continuous character —
the wiki's standing practice for every comparable scene in that page. **What is shared is the
question and its refusal, not necessarily the one being asked.**

## Significance

- **It supplies the closest verbal parallel in the Hebrew Bible to Genesis 32:29**, and dates the
  pattern at both ends of Israel's early history — a patriarch before the nation exists, and a judge
  once it has judges.
- **It sharpens what Exodus 3 is actually doing.** Read beside two flat refusals, *I AM WHO I AM*
  looks less like an evasion and more like the one time the question is answered without being
  satisfied — the same reticence, delivered as content instead of as a rebuff.
- **It gives the wiki's non-identification of Genesis 32's wrestler a second data point.** Whatever he
  was, the text's reluctance to name him belongs to a pattern this family and this nation meet more
  than once, and the reluctance itself, not the identity behind it, is the thing Scripture keeps
  returning to.

### Exodus 3 read in place

**Ingesting [[The Burning Bush]] sharpens the Exodus side of the comparison.** Two verses before the
name, God has already said ***ʾehyeh*** to Moses as a promise — *"Certainly I will be with you"*,
*kî-ʾehyeh ʿimmāk* (3:12) — and he says it twice more afterwards, to Moses' mouth (4:12, 15). **So the
reply that withholds by complying also gives something Jacob and Manoah never receive: a name that is
first heard as a pledge of company.** The reticence reading recorded above is one of three on the
passage page; the others — self-existence, after the Septuagint's *egō eimi ho ōn*, and faithful
presence, after 3:12 — are set out there, and the wiki adopts none. And unlike Genesis 32 and Judges
13, **the question in Exodus is not the human's own**: Moses asks on behalf of the people who will ask
him (*"they ask me"*), and the answer is given for them — *"This is my name forever"* (3:15).
