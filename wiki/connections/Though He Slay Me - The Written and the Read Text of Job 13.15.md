# Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15

## Passages involved

- **Job 13:15** — *"Behold, he will kill me. I have no hope. Nevertheless, I will maintain my ways
  before him."* (WEB, following the written text) — *"Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him: but
  I will maintain mine own ways before him."* (KJV, following the read text) — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Job 13:13–14** — *"Let come on me what will. Why should I take my flesh in my teeth, and put my
  life in my hand?"* — the verse's run-up
- **Job 13:16** — *"This also shall be my salvation, that a godless man shall not come before him."*
- **Job 6:11; 7:6; 14:7, 19; 17:15; 19:10** — Job's other statements about hope — see
  [[The Arrows of the Almighty]], [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]],
  [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Psalm 100:3** — *"It is he who has made us, and we are his"* (Qere *lô*) / *"and not we
  ourselves"* (Ketiv *lōʾ*, KJV)
- **Isaiah 9:3** (9:2 Hebrew) — *"You have multiplied the nation. You have increased their joy"*
  (Qere *lô*) / *"and not increased the joy"* (Ketiv *lōʾ*, KJV)
- **Isaiah 63:9** — *"In all their affliction he was afflicted"* (Qere *lô*) / *"he was not
  afflicted"* (Ketiv *lōʾ*)
- **Job 9:33** — *"There is no umpire between us"* (MT *lōʾ*) / *"would that there were"* (LXX,
  reading *lû*) — [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]]

## Analysis

**Two words that sound the same.** Hebrew *lōʾ*, *not*, is spelled *L-ʾ*; *lô*, *to him* or *for
him*, is spelled *L-W*. Spoken, they are indistinguishable. The Masoretes, who preserved the
consonantal text (*Ketiv*, *what is written*) exactly as they received it, marked a number of places
where the reading tradition (*Qere*, *what is read*) pronounced the other word, and in Job 13:15 the
written text has *lōʾ* and the margin directs the reader to say *lô*.

- **Ketiv:** *hēn yiqṭəlēnî lōʾ ʾăyaḥēl* — *Behold, he will slay me; I will not wait* / *I have no
  hope.*
- **Qere:** *hēn yiqṭəlēnî lô ʾăyaḥēl* — *Though he slay me, for him I will wait.*

Both continue identically: *ʾak-dərākay ʾel-pānāyw ʾôkîaḥ*, *but my ways to his face I will argue.*

**The Qere has the older and wider attestation in the versions.** The Targum, the Vulgate (*etiam si
occiderit me in ipso sperabo*), and through them the whole Western devotional tradition read *for
him*, and the KJV's *"yet will I trust in him"* made it one of the best-known sentences in English.
It also has a strong contextual case: Job has just said *"Let come on me what will"* (13:13), and a
declaration of hope in God *even unto death* fits a man about to take his life in his hand. The
Qere also supplies a theological arc the book seems to want — the faith of 1:21 and 2:10, still
present in the middle of the protest.

**The Ketiv fits the sentence better.** *ʾak*, *but* or *nevertheless*, wants a contrast: *I have
no hope, but I will argue my case* is a contrast; *I will hope in him, but I will argue my case* is
much weaker. *Yāḥal* means *to wait*, not *to trust*, and Job has consistently refused to wait — *"What
is my strength, that I should wait (ʾăyaḥēl)?"* (6:11), the same verb in the same form. And the run-up
in 13:14 is about risking death, which the Ketiv states plainly: *he will kill me.* For these reasons
the RSV, NRSV, NJPS (*"He may well slay me; I may have no hope"*), and the WEB follow the Ketiv, and so
does the wiki.

**What does not change.** Read either way, the verse's point is the second half. Job's determination
to argue his case to God's face does not depend on whether he expects to survive it. The Ketiv makes
that determination starker — integrity without hope — and the Qere makes it more devotional —
integrity *as* hope. Neither makes Job a man who has stopped protesting, and the KJV's
*"yet will I trust in him"* has often been quoted as though the sentence stopped there, which it does
not.

**The pattern elsewhere.** The *lōʾ/lô* confusion is one of the commonest Ketiv–Qere pairs in the
Hebrew Bible, and in almost every case the choice changes the meaning from negation to relation.
Psalm 100:3's *"and not we ourselves"* (Ketiv, KJV) against *"and we are his"* (Qere, almost all
modern versions) is the most familiar. Isaiah 63:9 is the most theologically charged: *"In all their
affliction he was afflicted"* is the Qere; the Ketiv, *"he was not afflicted"*, is usually joined to
the next clause — *it was no messenger or angel, but his own presence that saved them*. Job 9:33 is a
cousin: the Masoretic *lōʾ*, *there is no umpire*, against the Septuagint's apparent *lû*, *would
that there were*. **In Job the choice between *no* and *O that* or *for him* recurs at exactly the
places where the book is deciding how much hope its hero has.**

## Significance

- One of the most-quoted verses in the book rests on a reading the modern consensus rejects, and the
  wiki's page title keeps the famous phrase because it is how the speech is known. See the Notes on
  [[Though He Slay Me]].
- The Ketiv corrects a mistake made earlier in this wiki: [[Hope]] had described the marginal
  reading as the one that says *"I have no hope"*. It is the written text that says so; the margin
  says *"for him"*. Corrected in the same ingest.
- The verse is the hinge between 13:3 (*"I desire to reason with God"*) and 13:18 (*"I know that I am
  righteous"*), and its verb *ʾôkîaḥ* is the root of the missing *mōkîaḥ* of 9:33. See
  [[Mediation and Advocacy]].
