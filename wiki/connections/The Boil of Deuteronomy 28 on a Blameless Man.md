# The Boil of Deuteronomy 28 on a Blameless Man

## Passages involved

- **Job 2:7** — *"So Satan went out from the presence of Yahweh, and struck Job with painful sores
  from the sole of his foot to his head."* — [[The Second Test]]
- **Deuteronomy 28:35** — *"Yahweh will strike you in the knees, and in the legs, with a sore boil,
  of which you can't be healed, from the sole of your foot to the crown of your head."*
- **Deuteronomy 28:27** — *"Yahweh will strike you with the boil of Egypt, with the tumors, with the
  scurvy, and with the itch, of which you can't be healed."*
- **Exodus 9:8–11** — the sixth plague: *šəḥîn* breaking out in boils on man and beast throughout
  Egypt, so that the magicians could not stand before Moses
- **Leviticus 13:18–23** — the priest's examination of a *šəḥîn* that has healed and broken out
  again, and the verdict *clean* or *unclean*
- **2 Kings 20:7 · Isaiah 38:21** — Hezekiah's *šəḥîn*, and the cake of figs
- **Job 7:5 · 30:30** — the disease described from inside it, both times by Job
- **Job 42:7** — *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has"*

## Analysis

**Job's disease is named, and the name is a legal one.** *Šəḥîn rāʿ* — a malignant or evil boil — is
not a general word for illness. It occurs thirteen times in the Hebrew Bible, and every occurrence
outside Job sits in one of three settings: **a plague on Egypt** (Exodus 9), **a priestly diagnosis
of uncleanness** (Leviticus 13), or **the covenant curse list** (Deuteronomy 28). It is the disease
the law knows how to interpret.

**And Job 2:7 quotes the curse list.** Deuteronomy 28:35 threatens the covenant-breaker with a
*šəḥîn rāʿ* that cannot be healed, *"from the sole of your foot to the crown of your head"*. Job is
struck with a *šəḥîn rāʿ*, *"from the sole of his foot to his head"*. **Same disease, same two
anatomical limits, same idiom for totality.** Whether the author of Job is citing Deuteronomy, or
both are drawing on a shared formula for a whole-body affliction, the effect on a reader who knows
the curse list is identical and cannot be avoided: **the man the narrator called blameless in the
first verse of the book, and whom God has now called blameless three times, is wearing the mark of
a man under judgment.**

**This is what makes the friends' case, and it makes it before anyone speaks.** Eliphaz, Bildad and
Zophar arrive four verses later. They have a doctrine — [[Retribution]] — that says circumstances
are a readable index of standing with God, and they have in front of them a symptom that the Torah
itself designates as the sign of a curse. **They are not being obtuse. They are reading correctly by
every rule available to them, and the rules return a false verdict.** Bildad will say at 18:13 that
*"the firstborn of death devours his limbs"* to a man whose limbs are visibly going, and he is
applying a principle, not inventing a slander.

**The book therefore does not attack the doctrine by denying that suffering is ever a curse.** It
attacks it by producing one counter-instance whose cause the reader has been shown and the
interpreters have not. Deuteronomy 28 says: if you break the covenant, this will happen to you. It
never said the converse. **Job is the book that notices the difference between a rule and its
inverse, and spends forty-two chapters on the gap.**

**Two further details sharpen the irony.**

**The boil of Egypt was a plague on the oppressor.** Exodus 9:11 has the magicians unable to stand
because of it. The same affliction, in Job, lands on a man whose only recorded religious activity
is offering burnt offerings on behalf of his children in case they sinned in their hearts
([[Job of Uz]]).

**The boil of Leviticus 13 is diagnosable, and diagnosis is the whole problem.** The priest looks,
measures the hair and the depth, and pronounces *clean* or *unclean*. **The book of Job has no
priest, no altar-law, and no Israel** — the friends improvise a diagnosis with no authority to make
one, and God overrules it at 42:7. See [[Clean and Unclean]] and
[[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

**And the curse says *of which you can't be healed*.** Deuteronomy offers no remedy; Hezekiah's
*šəḥîn* is healed by God through a poultice and a promise, in a narrative that pointedly reverses
the curse. **Job is healed by neither — the epilogue restores his fortunes and never mentions his
skin.** The one thing the text says about the disease at the end of the book is nothing at all.

## Significance

- **It supplies the book's engine without a word of argument.** One clause of description puts the
  entire retribution doctrine on the page as an unstated premise, so that the debate can begin
  already lost.
- **It shows the friends as competent rather than wicked**, which is what makes 42:7 hard. God does
  not rebuke them for stupidity; he rebukes them for not having *"spoken of me the thing that is
  right"* — and what they said was, in the ordinary case, right.
- **It is the clearest evidence that the book is written from inside Israel's scriptures** while
  being set outside Israel. Job has no law, no covenant, and no Sinai, and his affliction is
  described in the exact vocabulary of the covenant curses. See [[Uz]] and [[Job (person)]].
- **It marks the point where suffering becomes legible.** The losses of chapter 1 were catastrophe,
  which invites pity. The disease of chapter 2 is a **verdict written on a body**, which demands
  explanation — and every explanation available requires a sin. See [[The Second Test]].
