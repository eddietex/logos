# Rachel Weeping - Her Grave, Ramah, and Bethlehem

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 35:19–20** — *"Rachel died, and was buried on the way to **Ephrath** (also called
  **Bethlehem**). Jacob set up a pillar on her grave. The same is the Pillar of Rachel's grave to this
  day."* See [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]].
- **Genesis 48:7** — Jacob's own retelling, to Joseph: *"Rachel died by me in the land of Canaan in
  the way, when there was still some distance to come to Ephrath, and I buried her there in the way to
  Ephrath (also called Bethlehem)"* *(not yet ingested)*.
- **1 Samuel 10:2** — Samuel to Saul: *"When you have departed from me today, then you will find two
  men by **Rachel's tomb, in the border of Benjamin at Zelzah**."*
- **Jeremiah 31:15** — *"A voice is heard in **Ramah**, lamentation and bitter weeping, Rachel weeping
  for her children. She refuses to be comforted for her children, because they are no more."*
- **Jeremiah 31:16–17** — the two verses nobody quotes: *"Refrain your voice from weeping … your work
  will be rewarded … your children will come again to their own border."*
- **Jeremiah 40:1** — Ramah as the staging camp for the deportation to Babylon.
- **Matthew 2:16–18** — Herod kills the infants *"in **Bethlehem** and in all the surrounding
  countryside"*, and Matthew quotes Jeremiah 31:15 over it.
- **Ruth 4:11** — the elders at the gate of Bethlehem: *"Yahweh make the woman who has come into your
  house like **Rachel and like Leah**, which two built the house of Israel."* See
  [[Like Rachel and Like Leah - The Builders of the House of Israel]].

## Analysis

### Two locations, about ten miles apart, on opposite sides of Jerusalem

| text | where Rachel's grave is | whose territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 35:19; 48:7** | *"on the way to Ephrath (also called Bethlehem)"* | Judah, ~5 miles S of Jerusalem |
| **1 Samuel 10:2** | *"in the border of Benjamin at Zelzah"* | Benjamin, N of Jerusalem |
| **Jeremiah 31:15** | weeping heard *"in Ramah"* | Benjamin, ~5 miles N of Jerusalem |

**Genesis puts her south and the other two put her north, and the two groups cannot both be describing
the same hillside.** This is not a subtle tension; it is one of the clearest geographical
disagreements in the Hebrew Bible, and it has been noticed for as long as there have been
commentators.

**The northern siting also fits the story better, which is what makes it hard to dismiss.**
[[Benjamin]] is the son whose birth killed her, and *son of the south* or not, the tribe's land is
north of Jerusalem. **A mother buried on the border of her own son's inheritance is a legible
arrangement.** A mother buried in Judah, twenty miles from the family tomb at [[Machpelah]] and in the
territory of her sister's son, is not — which is exactly the oddity the Genesis account leaves
standing.

### The readings, and the wiki adopts none

1. **The gloss is a later identification.** *"(also called Bethlehem)"* is an explanatory aside of the
   kind this narrator makes constantly — *"Luz (that is, Bethel)"*, *"Kiriath Arba (which is
   Hebron)"*, both in the same chapter — and on this reading an editor identified an *Ephrath* he knew
   (the Bethlehem one) with an *Ephrath* the text meant differently. **The strength of this reading is
   that the gloss is formally separable**; the weakness is that it appears twice, at 35:19 and 48:7.
2. **Two Ephraths.** 1 Chronicles 2:19, 24, 50 has *Ephrath/Ephrathah* as a **person** — Caleb's wife,
   mother of Hur, *"father of Bethlehem"* — and clan names travel with clans. An Ephrathite settlement
   in Benjamin and another at Bethlehem would make both texts correct about different places with the
   same name. **This is the harmonization with the most textual support**, and it still requires the
   Genesis gloss to be attaching the wrong one.
3. **Zelzah is the problem, not Bethlehem.** *Ṣelṣaḥ* occurs once in the Bible and nobody knows what
   it is; some read it as a place, some as a common noun. If the 1 Samuel phrase is corrupt or means
   something other than a location, the northern evidence reduces to Jeremiah's *Ramah* — which is
   about where a voice is heard, not where a grave is.
4. **Jeremiah is not giving a location at all.** *Ramah* means *height*, and the prophet may be
   speaking of a cry going up rather than of a town — though Jeremiah 40:1 uses Ramah as a real place
   in the deportation, which makes the geographical sense the natural one here.

**What is checkable**: Genesis says it twice, 1 Samuel says the opposite once, Jeremiah is compatible
with 1 Samuel, and the traditional tomb on the Bethlehem road — shown to pilgrims since at least the
fourth century and still shown — follows Genesis.

### Why Jeremiah reaches for her

**Jeremiah's Rachel is not mourning her own death; she is mourning a deportation six hundred years
later.** Ramah is where Nebuzaradan assembles the captives before marching them to Babylon (40:1), and
the prophet puts the matriarch's voice over that assembly ground. **She is chosen because of who her
children are**: [[Joseph]] and [[Benjamin]], and so Ephraim, Manasseh, and Benjamin — **the northern
tribes, the ones already lost to Assyria.** The chapter's surrounding verses are explicit about it:
*"I will be the God of all the families of Israel"* (31:1), *"Is Ephraim my dear son?"* (31:20).

**And the passage is not a lament.** Jeremiah 31 is the most hopeful chapter in the book — it contains
the new covenant (31:31–34) — and the weeping of verse 15 is answered in verses 16 and 17: *"Refrain
your voice from weeping… your children will come again to their own border."* **The verse everyone
knows is the setup for a promise, and it is almost always quoted without it.**

### What Matthew does with it

> **17** Then that which was spoken by Jeremiah the prophet was fulfilled, saying, **18** "A voice was
> heard in Ramah, lamentation, weeping and great mourning, Rachel weeping for her children; she
> wouldn't be comforted, because they are no more." (Matthew 2:17–18)

**Matthew moves the verse from a northern deportation to a southern massacre**, and the move only
works if Rachel is understood to be buried at Bethlehem — **that is, if the Genesis gloss is the
operative geography.** By the first century it plainly was.

**Note what Matthew does not say.** This is the one formula quotation in his infancy narrative
introduced with *"was fulfilled"* rather than *"that it might be fulfilled"* — a difference many
readers have thought deliberate — and **there is no prediction here to fulfil.** Jeremiah 31:15 is not
a prophecy of anything; it is a description. **Matthew is doing typology, not proof-texting a
forecast**: the mother of the tribes weeps at the second catastrophe as she wept at the first, and the
reader who knows the chapter knows what verses 16–17 say next. See
[[The Protoevangelium - Genesis 3.15 and the Seed of the Woman]] for the wiki's standing treatment of
how a New Testament writer reads an Old Testament verse forward.

## Significance

- **The one matriarch left outside the family tomb becomes the one the tradition comes back to.**
  Sarah, Rebekah, and Leah are at [[Machpelah]] and are never invoked over a later disaster. **Rachel
  is buried on a roadside, under a pillar the narrator says is still standing, and is the only
  ancestor in Scripture summoned to weep over her descendants six hundred years after her death.**
  See [[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].
- **Her grave's location is one of the Bible's clearest internal geographical disagreements**, and the
  wiki records it rather than resolving it — the same treatment it gives the two accounts of Jacob's
  flocks and the two namings of Israel.
- **The verse's afterlife runs opposite to its plain sense.** Jeremiah wrote it as the dark half of a
  promise about return; Matthew uses it as the dark half of a nativity; and it is quoted almost
  everywhere as unrelieved grief, with verses 16 and 17 left off. **Both prophets who use her know
  she is the mother who did not live to raise her son.** See [[Hope]] and [[Suffering]].

**Genesis 49:31 is now ingested**, in [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]] — **the other half of 48:7.** *"And
there I buried Leah"* is the only report of [[Leah]]'s burial in the Bible, **and it places the wife
Jacob was tricked into in the cave he is asking to be carried to, while [[Rachel]] stays beside a road
to Ephrath.** **Genesis states both facts, one chapter apart, in Jacob's own voice, and comments on
neither.**
