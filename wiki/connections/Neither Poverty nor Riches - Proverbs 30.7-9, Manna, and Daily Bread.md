# Neither Poverty nor Riches - Proverbs 30.7-9, Manna, and Daily Bread

## Passages involved

- [[Remember the Wilderness and the Giver of Wealth]] — Deuteronomy 8:1–20.

- [[Do Not Forget When You Are Full]] — Deuteronomy 6:10–25.

- [[The Words of Agur]] — Proverbs 30:7–9.
- [[Manna and Quail]] — Exodus 16:16–21.
- [[Wisdom Calls at the Gates]] — Proverbs 8:18–21, the contrasting promise of wealth.
- Matthew 6:9–13 — prayer for God’s name, bread, forgiveness, and deliverance; verified WEB, not yet

## Analysis

### Fullness and the giver of wealth

Deuteronomy 8:10–18 makes the danger more explicit: abundance can lead the heart to credit its own power. Agur fears fullness leading to denial of Yahweh (Proverbs 30:7–9). The difference remains: Moses commands gratitude within anticipated abundance, while Agur asks not to receive riches. Sources: stored WEB Deuteronomy 8 and Proverbs 30:7–9.


### Fullness in Deuteronomy 6:10–25

Deuteronomy 6:10–12 anticipates eating to fullness and warns against forgetting Yahweh.
Proverbs 30:8–9 asks for enough, fearing that fullness might lead to denial of Yahweh.
The shared danger is abundance severed from acknowledgment of its giver.

**Difference retained:** Deuteronomy tells a people how to live faithfully with inherited
abundance; Agur asks not to receive riches. These are different responses to a common danger,
not identical economic instructions. This is a thematic comparison, not an assertion of
literary dependence. Sources: stored WEB Deuteronomy 6:10–25 and Proverbs 30:7–9.

Agur’s two requests remove falsehood and ask for provision fitted to need. Neither poverty nor
riches is the desired state: fullness could lead him to deny Yahweh, while poverty could tempt
him to theft and dishonor of God’s name. These are first-person fears, not diagnoses of every
person in either economic condition. The prayer gives no universal income threshold.

Exodus 16:16–21 narrates measured sufficiency: each gathers according to eating, and neither
excess nor lack remains at measurement. Unauthorized overnight keeping spoils. It is a concrete
comparison for receiving enough, but Agur does not mention manna or impose its gathering rules
on every household. The narrative comparison is thematic, not an asserted allusion.

Matthew 6:9–13 places the request for daily bread within a prayer for the holiness of God’s
name, his reign and will, forgiveness, and deliverance from temptation and evil. This resembles
Agur’s joining of provision to fidelity and the honor of God’s name. Agur’s petition is personal
and explicitly excludes riches and poverty; Matthew’s is communal and does not repeat those
two exclusions. The texts need not be identical to illuminate one another.

**Explicit tension within Proverbs:** 8:18–21 presents riches and filled treasuries among
Wisdom’s gifts, whereas 30:8 asks not to be given riches. Treating wealth as capable of being
both a gift and a temptation is a synthesis of these claims, not a reason to suppress either
one. The desired good in Agur’s prayer is faithfulness sustained by enough.

## Significance

[[Prayer]], [[Wealth and Possessions]], and [[Famine and Plenty]] converge on provision that
serves fidelity. The comparison resists equating the greatest accumulation with the greatest
good, while leaving both hunger and others’ vulnerability morally serious.
