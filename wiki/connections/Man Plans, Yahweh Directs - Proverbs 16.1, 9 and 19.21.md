## Analysis

**Proverbs 16 frames itself with the claim.** The chapter's first saying gives the heart its
*arrangements* (*maʿarkê-lēb*) and Yahweh the tongue's *answer* (16:1); its ninth gives the heart its
plans and Yahweh the steps (16:9); its last gives the hand the throw and Yahweh the decision (16:33).
None of the three denies that people plan. Each puts the plan on one side of a line and the outcome on
the other.

**The vocabulary is shared with Jeremiah.** Proverbs 16:9's second half is *wa-YHWH yākîn ṣaʿădô*; the
second half of Jeremiah 10:23 is *wəhākîn ʾet-ṣaʿădô* — the same verb (*kûn*, to make firm or direct)
and the same noun (*ṣaʿad*, step), read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*. Proverbs
states the division of labour as an observation about everyone. Jeremiah turns it into a confession
addressed to Yahweh, and in the next verse into a plea: the prophet knows his steps are not his to
direct, and asks, *"Yahweh, correct me, but gently; not in your anger"* (10:24, WEB at bible-api.com).
Which text knew the other is not claimed here.

**Proverbs 16:3 is the reconciliation.** *"Commit your deeds to Yahweh, and your plans shall
succeed"* — the verb *gōl*, *roll*, as in Psalm 37:5's *gôl ʿal-YHWH darkekā* (Hebrew from Sefaria). The
plans are *established* (*yikkōnû*, *kûn* again) when the works are handed over. So the chapter does not
leave planning and providence simply opposed: the plan that is committed is the one that stands.

**Proverbs 19–21 restates it three ways.** *Many plans* against Yahweh's *counsel* (19:21); a man's
steps from Yahweh, so that he cannot understand his own way (20:24); and no counsel *against* Yahweh,
with the horse prepared and the victory his (21:30–31). The last is the frame applied to war: the
preparation is human and necessary, the outcome is not in it.

**Genesis has the narrative.** Joseph's brothers planned evil and carried it out; Joseph's reading is
that God *"meant it for good"* (Genesis 50:20). That is Proverbs 16:9 told across thirteen chapters,
with one addition the proverb does not make: the human plan was wicked, and its wickedness is not
excused by the outcome — *"you meant evil"*. Compare
[[What Profit Is It - Joseph's Brothers and the Ambush of Proverbs 1]].

**James 4 turns it into speech.** The merchant's plan — this city, a year, trade, profit — is not
condemned for being a plan, but for being said without the condition: *"If the Lord wills."* That is
Proverbs 16:1 brought to the tongue: the plan is man's, the answer is not.

**Job is the case where the frame is the problem.** If Yahweh directs the steps, then Job's losses are
not outside the frame either, and Job says so: *"I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose
of yours can be restrained"* (Job 42:2). See
[[No Purpose of Yours Can Be Restrained - Job 42.2 and Genesis 11.6]]. The proverb gives comfort to the
one whose plans are committed; Job shows the same truth from the side of the man who cannot understand
his way (Proverbs 20:24).

### Proverbs 19 in context

Proverbs 19:20 commands receiving counsel immediately before 19:21 says Yahweh's counsel prevails.
Human plans are plural and limited, but listening is still commanded. The juxtaposition makes divine
sovereignty a limit on self-sufficiency rather than a reason to stop learning. Verse 3 also keeps
the fool responsible for his ruined way. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 19:3, 20–21.

### Proverbs 20 in context

Proverbs 20:24, now ingested, turns the statement about Yahweh directing steps into a question
about human understanding of one's own course. Yet verse 18 explicitly commends advice in
planning, including warfare. Human limitation therefore does not abolish deliberation. The
chapter places responsible preparation beside an outcome and path that the planner cannot fully
comprehend; it does not promise that counsel makes every plan succeed.

See [[Wine Is a Mocker]]. Sources: stored WEB Proverbs 20 and the local comparisons documented on that
passage page; no new source-language claim.

### Proverbs 21 in context

Proverbs 21 frames itself with the theme: the king's heart turned like watercourses (1), and no
counsel against Yahweh while the prepared horse awaits a victory that is his (30–31). The horse is
still prepared, so the chapter keeps preparation beside a limit on its outcome. See
[[Victory Is with Yahweh - Proverbs 21.31, Exodus 15, and Psalm 20.7]].

See [[The King's Heart]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 21; no new source-language claim.

## Significance

The connection is the sentence collection's statement of [[Providence]], and it holds [[Wisdom]]'s
planning and [[Faith]]'s committing together rather than setting them against each other. It gives
[[God]] the outcome without taking the plan away from the planner, and it explains why the wiki reads
Proverbs' promises as general: if the steps are Yahweh's, then the outcome of any one life is his to
assign, which is the ground on which [[Retribution]] cannot be read backwards.
