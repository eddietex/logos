# The Two Flights of Hagar

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 16:1–16** — Sarai gives Hagar to Abram, resents the pregnancy, afflicts her; Hagar flees
  toward Shur; the angel of Yahweh finds her at a spring, sends her back, and promises innumerable
  offspring; Ishmael is born.
- **Genesis 21:8–21** — Sarah sees Ishmael *"mocking"* at Isaac's weaning feast and demands that both
  be cast out; Abraham complies at God's word; Hagar and the boy run out of water in the wilderness of
  Beersheba; *"God heard the voice of the boy"*; a well is opened; they settle in Paran. See
  [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].
- **Genesis 25:12–18** — Ishmael's twelve princes and their territory *"from Havilah to Shur"*, in
  a genealogy whose heading still calls her *"Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's servant"*. See
  [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]].

## Analysis

Genesis tells the same story twice, and the second telling changes everything that matters.

| | Genesis 16 | Genesis 21:8–21 |
|---|---|---|
| the divine name used | **Yahweh** throughout | **God** (*ʾĕlōhîm*) throughout |
| what starts it | Hagar's pregnancy, and Sarai's loss of standing | Ishmael *"mocking"*, and Isaac's inheritance |
| Sarah's demand | none — she acts herself | *"Cast out this servant and her son!"* (21:10) |
| Abraham | one sentence, no reaction | *"The thing was very grievous in Abraham's sight"* (21:11), and God tells him to comply |
| how Hagar leaves | **she flees** (*bāraḥ*, 16:6) | **she is sent away** (*šālaḥ*, 21:14) |
| where | toward Shur — toward Egypt | the wilderness of Beersheba — nowhere |
| the crisis | affliction | thirst, and a child about to die |
| who is heard | *"Yahweh has heard your **affliction**"* (16:11) | *"God has heard the **voice of the boy**"* (21:17) |
| the messenger | *Yahweh's angel* **finds** her at a spring | *the angel of God* calls **out of the sky** |
| the water | a spring she is already at, which she names | a well *"God opened her eyes"* to see (21:19) |
| the promise | *"I will greatly multiply your offspring"* (16:10) | *"I will make him a great nation"* (21:18) |
| the outcome | **"Return to your mistress, and submit"** (16:9) | she stays; God is with the boy; she takes him an Egyptian wife (21:20–21) |

**Four things the second telling changes.**

1. **She does not go back.** The command of 16:9 is not repeated, and nothing in Genesis 21 asks her
   to submit to anything. The story that began with a return ends with a household of her own in the
   wilderness of Paran.
2. **The child is the one heard.** In 16 the affliction heard is hers and the name given to the son
   records it; in 21 it is the boy's own voice, and the name is being cashed. Ishmael does not speak
   in either chapter — 21:17 says God heard him without reporting a word.
3. **She acts.** In 16 she is the object of every verb until the wilderness. In 21 she puts the child
   down, walks a bowshot away so as not to watch him die, lifts up her voice and weeps, fills the
   bottle, gives him drink, and finally chooses his wife — the only mother in Genesis to do a father's
   job.
4. **Sarah gets what she asked for and it is not a victory.** Genesis reports her demand, God's
   endorsement of it, and Abraham's grief, and then follows Hagar out into the desert and stays with
   her for eight verses. The narrative's sympathy is not where the plot's authority is.

### The doublet question

Classical source criticism reads the two as variants of one tradition, assigning Genesis 16 mainly to
J (which uses *Yahweh*, has Hagar pregnant, and has an angel of *Yahweh*) and 21:8–21 to E (which uses
*ʾĕlōhîm*, has a small child, and has an angel *of God* calling from the sky). The strongest evidence
is internal and awkward: in 21:14–15 Abraham puts the boy on Hagar's shoulder with the bread and
water, and she *"put the child under one of the shrubs"* — which reads like a toddler, while the
chronology of 16:16, 17:25 and 21:5 makes Ishmael at least fourteen at Isaac's weaning.

The wiki records the difficulty and does not need it resolved. Read as one narrative, the sequence
works and works well: the first episode ends with a woman sent back into servitude holding a promise,
and the second ends with the promise keeping her alive outside it. The second is the answer to the
first, and Genesis lets it be one without ever saying so.

### What survives of her in the land

Nothing of Hagar remains in Abraham's household, and two things remain in the country. The name she
gave God, *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, is fixed to a well between Kadesh and Bered — and Isaac, the son who displaced
her son, lives at that well for the rest of his life (24:62; 25:11) — and Genesis 25:11, the verse
that makes it his address, is the last verse before her son's genealogy. And her son's descendants
range *"from Havilah to Shur"* (25:18), [[Shur]] being the road she was on when she was turned back:
**the territory of Ishmael's twelve princes is bounded, at its western end, by the point where his
mother was stopped.**

## Significance

- **It is the wiki's reason for not reading Genesis 16:9 as the last word.** The command to return is
  real, unmitigated, and not repeated; Genesis itself ends the arrangement five chapters later, and
  the Torah eventually legislates against returning runaway slaves at all (Deuteronomy 23:15–16).
- **It shows Genesis's method with its own doublets.** The book does not harmonize; it lays the second
  version beside the first and lets the differences do the work. The same technique produces the three
  wife-sister episodes (see [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]]) and the two accounts of creation.
- **It is where Ishmael stops being a problem and becomes a people.** *"I will make him a great
  nation"* (21:18) is the promise of 12:2 in the same words, given to the wrong son, and Genesis keeps
  both promises without ever explaining how they sit together.
