# Listening to the Voice - Genesis 3 and Genesis 16

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 3:6** — *"When the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight
  to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took some of its fruit, and
  ate; and she gave some to her husband with her, and he ate it, too."*
- **Genesis 3:17** — *"To Adam he said, 'Because you have listened to your wife's voice, and ate from
  the tree, about which I commanded you … the ground is cursed for your sake.'"*
- **Genesis 16:2** — *"Sarai said to Abram, 'See now, Yahweh has restrained me from bearing. Please
  go in to my servant. It may be that I will obtain children by her.' Abram listened to the voice of
  Sarai."*
- **Genesis 16:3** — *"Sarai, Abram's wife, took Hagar the Egyptian, her servant … and gave her to
  Abram her husband to be his wife."*
- **Genesis 21:12** — *"God said to Abraham, 'Don't let it be grievous in your sight because of the
  boy … In all that Sarah says to you, listen to her voice.'"* See
  [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].
- **Genesis 3:16** and **Genesis 16:10** — *harbâ ʾarbeh*, *I will greatly multiply*, of a woman's
  pain and of a slave's offspring.

## Analysis

Genesis 16:2 ends with a clause that occurs in only one other place in the book, and the other place
is the sentence in which the ground is cursed.

**The clause.** *Wayyišmaʿ ʾabrām lə-qôl śāray* — *and Abram listened to the voice of Sarai*.
Genesis 3:17: *kî šāmaʿtā lə-qôl ʾištekā* — *because you have listened to the voice of your wife*.
Same verb, same preposition, same construct: *listen to the voice of* + wife. It is the only stated
ground of the curse on Adam that is not the eating itself, and it is the last thing said about Abram
before he takes a second wife.

**And the clause does not travel alone.** Four verbs run in the same order in both scenes:

| | Genesis 3 | Genesis 16 |
|---|---|---|
| see | *the woman **saw** that the tree was good* (3:6) | *"**See** now"* (16:2); *she **saw** that she had conceived* (16:4) |
| take | *she **took** some of its fruit* (3:6) | *Sarai … **took** Hagar the Egyptian* (16:3) |
| give | *she **gave** some to her husband* (3:6) | *and **gave** her to Abram her husband* (16:3) |
| listen | *you have **listened to** your wife's **voice*** (3:17) | *Abram **listened to** the **voice** of Sarai* (16:2) |

In both, the woman initiates and the man complies without recorded speech; in both, *her husband* sits
in the same syntactic slot; in both, the thing taken and given is something the couple was not
supposed to reach for on their own terms.

**A fifth link is in the promises.** The infinitive-absolute *harbâ ʾarbeh*, *I will greatly
multiply*, occurs three times in Genesis: 3:16 (the woman's pain in childbirth), 16:10 (Hagar's
offspring), and 22:17 (Abraham's offspring after the binding). Two of the three are in these two
chapters, and the first is a sentence of pain over the second's promise of children.

### What the parallel is not saying

Three qualifications, because this connection is easy to misuse.

- **Genesis 16 pronounces no curse and no rebuke.** Nobody is sentenced, God criticizes nobody, and
  the narrator offers no evaluative word. Whatever judgment the chapter contains is carried entirely
  by imported vocabulary. That is a different literary act from Genesis 3, which states its verdict
  at length.
- **Genesis 3:17 does not condemn listening to one's wife**, and Genesis proves it: at **21:12** God
  himself commands Abraham, in the identical idiom, *"in all that Sarah says to you, listen to her
  voice"* — about the very household this chapter created. The idiom is neutral; what matters is
  whose word is being obeyed *instead of* what.
- **The surrogacy arrangement is not itself the target.** Genesis narrates it twice more, at 30:3–13,
  and four of the twelve tribes come out of it without a word of censure.

What the parallel does say is narrower and sharper: **Genesis is signalling that this is the second
time the promise has been reached for by a shortcut, and that the shortcut has the same shape as the
first.** In Eden the couple takes a good thing on their own timing; in Hebron they take a legally
available solution to a promise God has not yet kept. Both times the man is passive, both times the
outcome is real and irreversible, and both times a third party pays.

### The one who pays

The difference is who. In Genesis 3 the consequences fall on the pair who acted and on the ground.
In Genesis 16 the pair who act are untouched — Abram gets a son, Sarai gets no punishment — and the
whole cost falls on a woman who was not consulted, who is never addressed by either of them, and who
is afflicted with the verb the covenant used one chapter earlier for slavery in Egypt.

## Significance

- **It is how Genesis passes judgment without a verdict.** The chapter's refusal to evaluate anyone is
  a technique, not an oversight; the evaluation is smuggled in as vocabulary and the reader is left to
  find it. This is the same method as 13:10's *"like the garden of Yahweh, like the land of Egypt"*,
  and as 6:11's *ḥāmās* reappearing in Sarai's mouth at 16:5.
- **It puts the Abraham cycle's crisis in the primeval history's terms.** Genesis 1–11 is about
  humanity reaching for what it was going to be given; Genesis 12–25 is about a family waiting for
  what it has been promised. Genesis 16 is the point where the second story briefly becomes the first
  one again.
- **It should not be turned into a rule about wives.** The history of preaching on Genesis 16:2 has
  regularly used the echo of 3:17 to make Sarai a second Eve and the moral *do not listen to women*,
  and Genesis 21:12 flatly contradicts it. The parallel is about the shape of an action, not the sex
  of the person proposing it.
