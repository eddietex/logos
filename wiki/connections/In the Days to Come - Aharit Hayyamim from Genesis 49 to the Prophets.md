# In the Days to Come - Aharit Hayyamim from Genesis 49 to the Prophets

## Passages involved

- Genesis 49:1 — *"that I may tell you that which will happen to you **in the days to come**"* —
  **the first occurrence in the Bible**
- Numbers 24:14 — Balaam: *"what this people shall do to your people in the latter days"*, just
  before the star out of Jacob
- Deuteronomy 4:30; 31:29 — Moses, twice, of Israel's apostasy and its return
- Isaiah 2:2 // Micah 4:1 — *"in the latter days… the mountain of Yahweh's house shall be
  established"*
- Jeremiah 23:20; 30:24; 48:47; 49:39 — of the end of wrath and of restorations for Moab and Elam
- Ezekiel 38:16 — Gog comes against the land *"in the latter days"*
- Hosea 3:5 — *"afterward the children of Israel shall return… and David their king"*
- Daniel 2:28; 10:14 — *"what shall be in the latter days"*, of the dream and of the vision
- Acts 2:17; Hebrews 1:2; 2 Peter 3:3; 2 Timothy 3:1 — the Greek *en tais eschatais hēmerais*

## Analysis

***Bəʾaḥărît hayyāmîm*** is **not *at the end of time*.** ***ʾAḥărît*** is the *after-part*, the
*latter end*, the *remainder* of a thing — **it is what a life has at 49:1 and what a harvest has at
Proverbs 23:18.** **The phrase means *later on*, and its weight comes entirely from context.**
**Nothing in the Hebrew makes it eschatological.**

**Genesis 49:1 is the first of about thirteen occurrences in the Old Testament, and the plainest.**
[[Jacob]] means the tribal future — what will happen to the twelve when they are peoples rather than
men — **and everything the poem then says is territorial, military and political.** [[Zebulun]] gets
a coastline, [[Issachar]] gets a corvée, [[Gad]] gets raiders. **There is no world to come in it.**

**Two features of the Torah's use are worth holding onto.** **First, it is rare**: three occurrences
outside Genesis (Numbers 24:14; Deuteronomy 4:30; 31:29), **all of them in speeches and none in
narration.** **Second, all four Torah occurrences introduce something the speaker is about to say**
— it is an announcement formula, not a description of a period.

**The prophets keep the formula and change what it holds.** **Isaiah 2:2 and Micah 4:1 are the
turn**: the same three words now introduce a mountain lifted above the hills, nations streaming to
it, and swords beaten into ploughshares. **Hosea 3:5 adds a returning people and *David their
king*.** **Daniel 2:28 and 10:14 use it of a sequence of empires and of a conflict at the end of
one.** **By the time of Daniel the phrase carries what English means by *the last days*; in Genesis
it carries no such thing**, and the wiki records the development rather than reading the later sense
back into 49:1.

## Significance

**The New Testament's *en tais eschatais hēmerais* is the Septuagint's rendering of this phrase, and
it is used to say that the last days have begun.** Peter at Pentecost quotes Joel — *"it will be in
the last days, says God, that I will pour out my Spirit"* (Acts 2:17) — **and Hebrews opens by
saying God *"has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son"* (1:2).** **What was a formula
for *later on* has become a claim about the present.**

**The wiki's position is that Genesis 49:1 is the vocabulary's starting point and not its content.**
A dying man in Egypt telling twelve sons what is coming is the first use of a phrase that will end
up meaning the consummation of history — **and the distance between the two is the whole distance
this thread measures.** **Nothing in Genesis 49 requires the later sense, and nothing in the later
texts disowns the earlier one.**
