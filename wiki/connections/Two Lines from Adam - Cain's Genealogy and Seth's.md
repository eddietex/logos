# Two Lines from Adam - Cain's Genealogy and Seth's

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 4:17–26** — the line of Cain, seven generations to Lamech; then Seth and Enosh —
  [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]]
- **Genesis 5:1–32** *(not yet ingested)* — the line of Seth, ten generations to Noah, with ages
  and deaths

## Analysis

Genesis gives two genealogies from Adam back to back, and they share most of their names.

In generational order they are:

- **Cain's line (4:17–18)**, seven from Adam — Adam, **Cain**, **Enoch**, **Irad**, **Mehujael**,
  **Methushael**, **Lamech**.
- **Seth's line (5:3–32)**, ten from Adam — Adam, Seth, Enosh, **Kenan**, **Mahalalel**, **Jared**,
  **Enoch**, **Methuselah**, **Lamech**, Noah.

The two are different lengths and the orders do not correspond, so the correspondence has to be read
as a set of names rather than as a row-by-row alignment:

| Cainite name | Sethite name | Relation |
|---|---|---|
| Cain (*Qayin*) | Kenan (*Qênān*) | same consonantal root |
| Enoch (*Ḥănôk*) | Enoch (*Ḥănôk*) | identical |
| Irad (*ʿÎrād*) | Jared (*Yered*) | same consonants, different first letter |
| Mehujael (*Meḥûyāʾēl*) | Mahalalel (*Mahălalʾēl*) | close, both ending in *-ʾēl* |
| Methushael (*Metûšāʾēl*) | Methuselah (*Metûšelaḥ*) | same first element, different second |
| Lamech (*Lemek*) | Lamech (*Lemek*) | identical |

Six of Cain's seven names have a counterpart, two of them exact. Only Seth, Enosh, and Noah are
unique to the Sethite list.

**Three accounts of why.**

1. **Two versions of one list.** The standard critical reading: a single ancient genealogy of the
   first men, preserved in two branches of the tradition (conventionally J for Genesis 4 and P for
   Genesis 5), with names drifting in transmission and the order shuffled. The evidence for it is
   the density of the overlap and the fact that the divergences are mostly phonetic. Against it:
   the two lists differ in more than spelling — 4:17–24 is a narrative with a poem in it and 5:1–32
   is a formulaic register with ages and a repeated *"and he died"* — and Genesis clearly wants them
   read as two families, since 4:25 goes out of its way to restart from Adam.
2. **Deliberate literary counterpoint.** The names are reused *because* the comparison is the
   point. This has the merit of accounting for what the two lists actually do with their shared
   names, which is the opposite thing in each case (below), and for their placement, immediately
   adjacent, with 4:25–26 as the hinge.
3. **Coincidence in a small name-pool.** Ancient Semitic naming drew on a limited stock, and
   families reused names constantly — the Bible itself has several Enochs, several Lamechs, dozens
   of Zechariahs. On its own this cannot carry the whole overlap, but it lowers how much any single
   repetition proves.

These are not mutually exclusive. A shared traditional list can be *deployed* for contrast, and
readings 1 and 2 are held together by many commentators.

**What the shared names are made to do.** Whatever the origin, the finished text sets the pairs
against each other, and the two most loaded ones are exact inversions:

- **The two Enochs.** In Cain's line, Enoch is the man a city is named after — the founder's son,
  the first name in the world's first urban record, otherwise silent. In Seth's line, Enoch is the
  man who *"walked with God, and he was not found, for God took him"* (5:24), the only person in the
  chapter who does not die. One Enoch has a place named for him; the other leaves the place
  entirely.
- **The seventh generation in each.** Cain's Lamech is seventh from Adam, and what the seventh
  generation of that line produces is the Song of the Sword: *"I have slain a man for wounding me…
  truly Lamech seventy-seven times."* Seth's seventh from Adam is Enoch, who is taken by God without
  dying. Jude 14 calls him *"the seventh from Adam"* by name, so the count was being made in
  antiquity and the symmetry is unlikely to be modern invention.
- **The two Lamechs.** Seth's Lamech comes ninth rather than seventh, and speaks the only other poem
  in these chapters — over his newborn son: *"This one will comfort us in our work and in the toil
  of our hands, caused by the ground which Yahweh has cursed"* (5:29). He names Noah for **rest**
  and for relief from the curse of 3:17; his namesake named himself for revenge. The Sethite
  Lamech's recorded lifespan is 777 years (5:31), which is either a coincidence or a very dry joke
  at the expense of the man who claimed seventy-seven.

**The endings match the beginnings.** Cain's line runs to a song about murder and then stops — the
narrative simply abandons it, and the flood ends it without a word. Seth's line runs to Noah, and
through him to Abraham, David, and (in Luke 3:36–38) Jesus. Genesis 4 puts the two endings four
verses apart: the boast at 4:24, and *"At that time men began to call on Yahweh's name"* at 4:26.
See [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].

**A caution.** The tidy "line of the serpent / line of the promise" scheme that later theology
builds on these chapters gets more from them than they say. Genesis never labels either line, never
condemns Cain's descendants for their descent, and credits them with the tent, the harp, and the
forge — real goods, held by the wrong family (see [[City and Civilization]]). And the flood account
will make clear that the corruption was universal, not confined to one branch: Noah, a Sethite, is
saved by grace and not by pedigree. The contrast Genesis draws is real; the genetic determinism
sometimes read out of it is not in the text.

## Significance

This is the Bible's first use of a device it will rely on constantly: two lines set side by side so
that the reader draws the conclusion the narrator declines to state. Genesis will do it again with
Ishmael and Isaac, Esau and Jacob, and the sons of Leah and Rachel. Here it establishes the pattern
in its simplest form — nearly the same names, opposite outcomes — and hands the reader the job of
noticing.

It also settles the wiki's naming: the two Enochs and the two Lamechs are distinct pages, qualified
by parentage. See [[Enoch (son of Cain)]] and [[Lamech (son of Methushael)]]; their Sethite
namesakes get pages when Genesis 5 is ingested.
