# Sitting in Dust and Ashes

## Passages involved

- **Job 2:8** — *"He took for himself a potsherd to scrape himself with, and he sat among the
  ashes."* — [[The Second Test]]
- **Job 42:6** — *"Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes."* —
  [[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]]
- **Job 13:12** — *"Your memorable sayings are proverbs of ashes (mišlê-ʾēper)"*: the friends'
  wisdom made of the ash Job is sitting in — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Job 30:19** — *"He has cast me into the mire. I have become like dust and ashes."* —
  *keʿāpār wāʾēper*, the pair's first appearance in the book — [[Now They Mock Me]]
- **Genesis 18:27** — *"See now, I have taken it on myself to speak to the Lord, although I am dust
  and ashes"* — [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]]
- **Genesis 3:19** — *"for you are dust, and to dust you shall return"* — [[The Fall]]
- **Esther 4:1–3** — Mordecai in sackcloth and ashes in the middle of the city, and *"many lay in
  sackcloth and ashes"* in every province
- **Jonah 3:6** — the king of Nineveh rises from his throne, puts on sackcloth, and sits in ashes
- **2 Samuel 13:19** — Tamar puts ashes on her head and goes away crying
- **Jeremiah 6:26 · Ezekiel 27:30** — *"roll yourself in ashes"*; the mariners of Tyre casting dust
  on their heads and wallowing in ashes
- **Daniel 9:3** — *"I set my face to the Lord God, to seek by prayer and petitions, with fasting
  and sackcloth and ashes"*
- **Isaiah 61:3** — *"to give to those who mourn in Zion a garland for ashes"*
- **Matthew 11:21 · Luke 10:13** — *"they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes"*

## Analysis

**Ashes are the Bible's standing material for grief, and *ʾēper* does three separate jobs.**

**Mourning.** Tamar, Mordecai, the mariners of Tyre, and the whole province-wide reaction of Esther
4:3 use ashes as the public sign of a loss. It is not a private gesture: the point of sitting in
ashes is to be seen sitting in them, which is why Mordecai does it *"in the middle of the city"* and
why the king of Nineveh does it after getting off his throne.

**Repentance.** Jonah 3, Daniel 9, and Jesus's *"in sackcloth and ashes"* use the same material for
turning rather than for loss. **The two uses are not distinguished by any external feature** — the
posture, the substance and the sackcloth are identical — which is part of why Job 2:8 and Job 42:6
can be the same image doing different work.

**Mortality.** Genesis 18:27's *ʿāpār wāʾēper*, dust and ashes, is an assonant pair — Abraham naming
what he is made of in order to say what he has no standing to do. It reaches back to Genesis 3:19's
*dust*, and it is the only use in the list that is about a **category** rather than an occasion.

**Job uses all three, and the book's shape is the distance between the first use and the last** —
with a middle step at 30:19 that the wiki's first draft of this page missed.

At **2:8** he sits *bətôk-hāʾēper*, among the ashes, and every marker says mourning: he has buried
ten children, he is diseased, and the friends who arrive four verses later will sit down in the same
dirt with him and sprinkle dust on their own heads (2:12–13). **Nothing in chapter 2 suggests
repentance, and the narrator explicitly denies that there is anything to repent of.** See
[[Seven Days of Silence]] for the arrival, and [[Mourning and Lament]] for the rite in full.

At **30:19**, in the last chapter of his defence before the oath, he says *"He has cast me into the mire.
I have become like dust and ashes"* — ***keʿāpār wāʾēper*, Abraham's pair, and its first use in the
book.** **The pair occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible — Genesis 18:27, Job 30:19, and Job 42:6 —
and Job says two of them.** In between the ashes he sat in (2:8) and the ashes he names at the end
(42:6) is this: not a rite and not a repentance, but a description of what God has made of him. He no
longer sits in the material; *"I have become"* it (*wāʾetmaššēl*), the mortality category of Genesis
18:27 imposed instead of confessed. The next verse turns to God — *"I cry to you, and you do not answer
me"* (30:20) — which is Abraham's situation exactly: dust and ashes, speaking to the Lord, uncertain of
any standing to do so.

At **42:6** he says *"I repent in dust and ashes"* — *ʿāpār wāʾēper*, Abraham's pair and his own of
30:19, not chapter 2's single word. **The substance has not changed and the act has.** And the change is not a confession
of the sin the friends spent twenty-five chapters looking for: God says four verses later that Job
spoke of him *"the thing that is right"* and the friends did not. **Whatever Job retracts at 42:6, it
is not the innocence he swore to at 27:5.**

**The Hebrew of 42:6 is, in fact, the most contested clause in the book**, and it is worth stating
why. The verb *ʾemʾas* has no object — *I reject*, *I melt away*, *I despise* — and *niḥamtî ʿal*
can mean *I repent of*, *I am comforted concerning*, or *I am consoled about*. The preposition
before *dust and ashes* can mean *in*, *concerning*, or *upon*. **Serious translations therefore
range from "I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes" (the traditional reading, and WEB's) to
"I am comforted concerning dust and ashes" — that is, concerning the human condition Abraham named
at Genesis 18:27.** The wiki flags the range and does not decide it, and records that the decision
changes what the book's ending means: an abasement, or a consolation.

**Added on ingesting [[I Repent in Dust and Ashes]].** *Niḥamtî* is from the same root, *nḥm*, as the
friends' errand — *"to comfort him"*, *ûlənaḥămô* (2:11) — and as Job's *"miserable comforters"* (16:2)
and the family who *"comforted him"* at 42:11. So the consolation reading is not only a possibility of
grammar: it would mean that the comfort the friends came to give and could not is what Job finds,
alone with God, in the verse before they are rebuked. The verb is also God's own *was sorry* at Genesis
6:6. Neither fact decides the clause. See [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]] and
[[Divine Grief and Relenting]]. **And Job says it before the restoration begins (42:10)** — still on the
ash-heap, still with nothing given back.

**What is not in doubt is the frame.** Job enters his suffering sitting in ashes and leaves it
speaking of dust and ashes, and in between he has been answered out of a whirlwind. **The book
begins and ends a man in the same material, and the reader is required to notice that it means
something different the second time.**

**The king of Nineveh is the closest external parallel to 2:8's staging**, and it is instructive.
Jonah 3:6 has a monarch leave his throne, remove his robe, and sit in ashes — **the same descent
from the highest place in a society to its refuse that Job 1:3's *"greatest of all the children of
the east"* undergoes in one verse.** Nineveh's king does it to avert a sentence. Job does it without
a sentence to avert.

**The Septuagint puts him on a dung-heap.** Where the Hebrew has ashes, the Greek of Job 2:8 has
*epi tēs koprias exō tēs poleōs*, on the dung-heap outside the city — an **exclusion** rather than a
mourning rite. This is the source of the dunghill of Christian art and of the *Testament of Job*,
and it makes the friends' failure to recognise Job at 2:12 a matter of where he is as well as of what
he looks like. **The wiki follows the Hebrew and records the Greek**; the two readings differ on
whether Job is a mourner or an outcast, and the dialogue can be read either way.

## Significance

- **It brackets the book.** Job 2:8 and Job 42:6 are its first and last images of the man, and
  ashes are in both. (They are not the only places the word appears: Job calls the friends' maxims
  *"proverbs of ashes"* at 13:12, and says *"I have become like dust and ashes"* at 30:19. This page
  earlier said the two bracketing verses were the only ones; that was wrong, and is corrected here.)
  Any reading of the ending has to account for the fact that he does not get up out of the ashes to
  say it.
- **It puts Job in Abraham's phrase.** *Dust and ashes* is said by exactly two people in the Hebrew
  Bible, three times, and every time while addressing God about something the speaker has no standing
  to address him about — Abraham arguing about Sodom, Job crying unanswered at 30:19–20, and Job
  answering the whirlwind at 42:6. See [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] and
  [[Now They Mock Me]].
- **It shows how little the gesture settles.** Mourning and repentance are indistinguishable from
  outside, which is exactly the friends' problem: they arrive, find a man in the posture of a
  penitent, and conclude there is something to be penitent about. See [[Retribution]].
- **It is the Bible's image for a reversal of status**, held in common by a king who steps down to
  it, a queen's uncle who sits in it in public, a sacked city's sailors who wallow in it, and the
  greatest man of the east who is put in it by two sentences of permission.
