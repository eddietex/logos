# Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 18:23–25** — *"Will you consume the righteous with the wicked? … Be it far from you to
  do things like that, to kill the righteous with the wicked, so that the righteous should be like
  the wicked. May that be far from you. Shouldn't the Judge of all the earth do right?"* See
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Genesis 20:4** — Abimelech, two chapters later and from outside the covenant: *"Lord, will you
  kill even a righteous nation?"* — the same appeal, made by a foreigner about his own kingdom, and
  conceded in the first clause of the reply. See [[Abraham and Abimelech]].
- **Exodus 32:9–14** — Moses at Sinai: *"Why should the Egyptians speak, saying, 'He brought them
  out for evil…'? Turn from your fierce wrath"* — and *"Yahweh repented of the evil which he said he
  would do to his people."*
- **Numbers 14:13–20** — Moses again, arguing from God's own self-description, and *"I have
  pardoned according to your word."*
- **Numbers 16:22** — *"Shall one man sin, and will you be angry with all the congregation?"* —
  Abraham's question in one clause.
- **Amos 7:1–6** — the prophet twice stopping a judgment: *"Lord Yahweh, forgive, I beg you! How
  could Jacob stand? For he is small."* Twice: *"It shall not be."*
- **Jeremiah 5:1** — *"if you can find a man, if there is anyone who does justly, who seeks truth,
  then I will pardon her."*
- **Ezekiel 14:12–20** — Noah, Daniel, and Job in a condemned land: *"they would deliver neither
  sons nor daughters; they only would be delivered themselves."*
- **Ezekiel 18:1–32** — *"The soul who sins, he shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of
  the father."*
- **Job 8:3; 9:22–24; 34:10–19** — the question asked at book length: *"Does God pervert justice?"*
  and *"He destroys the blameless and the wicked."*
- **Romans 3:5–6** — *"Is God unrighteous who inflicts wrath? … For then how will God judge the
  world?"*
- **Genesis 22:1–3** — the same man, four chapters later, given a command about his own son and
  saying nothing at all. **The Bible's first advocate does not argue when the case is his**, and
  Genesis offers no account of the difference. See [[The Binding of Isaac]].

## Analysis

**Genesis 18:25 is the first time in Scripture that a human being holds God to a standard**, and it
does it in a form that assumes the answer: not *are you just?* but *shouldn't you be?* The
grammatical shape matters, because it means Abraham is not testing God — he is appealing to
something he takes to be settled, and using it to argue.

Two things are unprecedented in the sentence.

**God is called a judge.** He has judged — in the garden, at the flood, at Babel — but *šōpēṭ* is
not a title Genesis has used of him. Abraham coins it, and he attaches it to *"all the earth"*,
which makes the standard universal rather than covenantal. He is arguing about a Canaanite city he
has no obligation to, in front of the God who has just promised him the land it stands in.

**And the standard is one God supplied four verses earlier.** *"That they may keep the way of
Yahweh, to do righteousness and justice"* (18:19) — *ṣədāqâ ûmišpāṭ*, the pair's first occurrence in
Scripture, given as the content of what Abraham is chosen to teach his household. He then uses
*mišpāṭ* on God in 18:25. The chapter hands over the measure and is immediately measured by it, and
nothing in the text treats this as impudence: God concedes six times without a word of rebuke.

### The two problems, and how the Bible separates them

Genesis 18 runs together two questions that later Scripture pulls apart.

| | The question | Where the Bible answers it |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual liability** | May the innocent be killed along with the guilty? | Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18 say no, flatly and as law |
| **Vicarious deliverance** | May the guilty be spared *because of* the innocent among them? | Genesis 18:26 says yes, to fifty and down to ten — and Ezekiel 14:14, 20 says no, by name |

The first has a straight line through Scripture and ends in legislation. The second does not.
Abraham gets his concession, Jeremiah 5:1 lowers the number to one and still offers pardon, and then
Ezekiel 14 puts three of the most righteous men imaginable inside a condemned land and says
explicitly that they would save nobody but themselves. **The wiki records this as a real tension and
does not dissolve it.** The two texts are addressed to opposite situations — Genesis to a man asking
whether God can be trusted, Ezekiel to a people counting on other people's merit — but they say
opposite things about the same mechanism, and Ezekiel names Noah as one of the three, which is
pointed: Noah's righteousness did save his household (Genesis 7:1).

### The one person who is not a prophet, and who wins fastest

**Two chapters after Abraham invents the argument, a Canaanite king uses it and gets his answer in
one clause.**

> **18:23** *"Will you consume the righteous with the wicked?"*
>
> **20:4** *"Lord, will you kill even a righteous nation?"*

Abimelech has had no call, no covenant, and no prior word from God; he is being told in a dream that
he is a dead man for a marriage he was deceived into. And his reply is the same appeal in the same
form — *would you really kill the righteous?* — with his own kingdom as the righteous party.
**Abraham had to work six rounds down from fifty to ten and came away with a conditional. Abimelech
is conceded in the first word of the response**: *"Yes, I know that in the integrity of your heart
you have done this"* (20:6).

The comparison cuts two ways and the wiki lets both stand. It suggests that the standard Abraham
appealed to is genuinely universal — available to anyone, argued successfully by an outsider on his
own behalf. And it suggests that Genesis 18's long negotiation was hard not because God was
reluctant but because Sodom's case was bad. **Nobody in Sodom ever made this argument. The first
person outside the covenant line who does gets it granted immediately.** See
[[Abraham and Abimelech]].

### Who does the arguing

The office Abraham invents here is filled repeatedly and always by the same kind of person: not a
priest and not a king, but someone God has spoken to.

- **Moses** does it twice at scale, and Exodus 32:14 is the Torah's most direct statement that God
  *"repented of the evil"* — the verb *nāḥam*, which Genesis 6:6 used of the flood. Moses argues
  from reputation (*"why should the Egyptians speak?"*), from the promise to the patriarchs, and
  finally from his own standing: *"blot me, I pray you, out of your book"* (32:32). He offers to be
  the ten.
- **Amos** does it as a prophet's ordinary work, and the two visions he stops are followed by two he
  cannot.
- **Job** does it from the other side, as the innocent party rather than the advocate, and gets no
  concession at all — which is what makes his book the hardest test of Abraham's premise. *"He
  destroys the blameless and the wicked"* (9:22) is Genesis 18:25 denied.
- **Paul** uses the sentence as a premise rather than a plea. *"For then how will God judge the
  world?"* (Romans 3:6) assumes that God's judging the world is fixed and that anything implying he
  is unjust must therefore be false — the same move Abraham makes, in a syllogism instead of a
  bargain.

See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

### What Genesis 18 does not say

It is routinely summarized as Abraham talking God down, and the text will not support it. God gives
the first concession in full and unasked (18:26), never objects, never sets a floor, and never
displays the anger Abraham twice apologizes for pre-emptively. Abraham is the one who stops, at ten.

And the outcome is not the one negotiated. The city is destroyed — so there were not ten — and
*"God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the middle of the overthrow"* (19:29). **The
intercession is answered, but by the deliverance of the righteous man out of the city rather than by
the sparing of the city for his sake.** Which is to say: the flood's arrangement, not Abraham's. See
[[Noah and the Flood]] and [[Sodom]].

## Significance

- **It establishes that God's justice is arguable in advance**, not merely acknowledged in
  retrospect. Abraham does not wait to see what happens and then justify it; he says what would and
  would not count as right, and God accepts the terms.
- **It puts the standard in a human mouth before it is put in a law.** *Righteousness and justice*
  enters Scripture as something Abraham is to teach his children and immediately becomes something
  he can hold God to. The prophets who use the pair for the next four centuries are working the
  same verse from both ends.
- **It is the Bible's clearest case of the tension between corporate and individual judgment**, and
  the tension is never resolved so much as distributed: the law protects the individual, the
  prophets ask for the one righteous man, Ezekiel forbids counting on him, and the New Testament
  puts one man in the position Abraham could not find ten to fill.
- **And Job is its counter-witness.** Any reading of Genesis 18:25 that makes God's justice
  self-evidently legible from events has to answer Job 9:22, and the wiki keeps both texts in view
  rather than letting the confident one absorb the other.
