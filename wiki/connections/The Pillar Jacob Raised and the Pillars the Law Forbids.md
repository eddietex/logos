# The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids

## Passages involved

- Genesis 28:18, 22 — the stone at Bethel set up and anointed
- Genesis 31:45–52 — the pillar and the heap at Galeed, as a boundary witness
- Genesis 35:14, 20 — the pillar at Bethel again, with a drink offering; and the one over Rachel's
  grave
- Exodus 24:4 — Moses builds an altar and **twelve pillars** at Sinai
- Exodus 23:24; 34:13; Deuteronomy 7:5; 12:3 — smash the Canaanite pillars
- Leviticus 26:1 — *"you shall not rear up for yourselves … a standing stone"*
- Deuteronomy 16:21–22 — *"neither shall you set yourself up a pillar, which Yahweh your God hates"*
- Joshua 4:1–9 — twelve stones out of the Jordan
- Joshua 24:26–27 — a great stone under an oak, *"it shall be a witness against us"*
- 1 Samuel 7:12 — Ebenezer
- 1 Kings 14:23; 2 Kings 17:10; 18:4; 23:14 — the pillars Israel keeps putting up and the kings who
  break them
- Hosea 3:4; 10:1–2 — pillars as both loss and indictment
- Isaiah 19:19 — *"a pillar at its border to Yahweh"*, in Egypt

## Analysis

**The Hebrew word is the same in the commendation and in the prohibition.**

***Maṣṣēbâ***, from ***nāṣab***, *to stand* — a **standing stone**, set upright and left there. It is
what Jacob makes at Bethel out of his pillow, what Moses makes twelve of at Sinai, and what
Deuteronomy 16:22 says **Yahweh hates**. No translation difference hides the problem; the noun is
identical.

| | **raised, with approval or without comment** | **forbidden or destroyed** |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis | 28:18, 22; 31:45; 35:14, 20 | — |
| Exodus | 24:4 — twelve, at Sinai | 23:24; 34:13 — the Canaanites' |
| Leviticus / Deuteronomy | — | Leviticus 26:1; Deuteronomy 7:5; 12:3; **16:22** |
| Joshua | 4:9; 24:26–27 (*ʾeben*, a stone, set up as witness) | — |
| Kings / Prophets | Isaiah 19:19 | 2 Kings 18:4; 23:14; Hosea 10:1–2 |

### The readings, and what each one costs

**1. The prohibition is about Canaanite cult objects, not about stones.** In Exodus 23:24 and 34:13
the pillars to be smashed are always *theirs*, standing beside an ***ʾăšērâ*** — a wooden pole or
tree associated with a goddess — and Deuteronomy 16:21–22 pairs the two in a single prohibition,
which suggests the target is a package rather than a shape. **On this reading Jacob's stone is not
what the law bans**, and Deuteronomy's *"for yourselves"* is doing the work. **The cost:**
Deuteronomy 16:22 says the pillar itself is *hated*, without qualification, and Leviticus 26:1 lists
it beside idols and carved images.

**2. It is a change in the law over time.** What was permissible in the patriarchal period and even
at Sinai became forbidden once worship was centralised, because a standing stone at a local site is
by definition a rival to the one place Yahweh chooses. **On this reading Genesis and Deuteronomy do
not agree and were not meant to** — the historical-critical version assigns them to different periods
and different hands. **The cost:** it is a solution outside the text rather than in it, and Isaiah
19:19 still wants a pillar afterwards.

**3. The distinction is memorial against cultic.** Jacob's stones mark **where something happened** —
a dream, a treaty, a grave — and are never sacrificed at; the forbidden ones are places where
offerings are made. Joshua 24:27 is explicit about the memorial function: *"it shall be a witness
against us, lest you deny your God."* **The cost:** Jacob **anoints** his with oil (28:18) and pours
a drink offering on it (35:14), which is cultic by any ordinary definition, and he calls it *God's
house*.

**The wiki records all three and adopts none.** What is not in doubt is that Genesis knows nothing of
a prohibition, that the law states one flatly, and that neither text acknowledges the other.

### What the stones are for in Genesis

**Jacob is the only patriarch who raises them, and he raises four.** Abraham and Isaac build
**altars** — at Shechem, at Bethel, at Hebron, at Moriah, at Beersheba — and an altar is a thing you
use. **A pillar is a thing you leave.** Jacob's four are set up at the four hinges of his life: the
night he leaves (28:18), the boundary with Laban (31:45), the night he comes back (35:14), and the
grave of the wife he crossed the country for (35:20). **Three of them are markers of a departure or a
return, and one is a headstone.**

**And at Galeed the function is spelled out.** The pillar and the heap are set between two men who do
not trust each other: *"This heap is witness, and the pillar is witness"* (31:52). **A standing stone
is testimony that outlives the people who agreed to it** — which is exactly Joshua's use of one, and
exactly what makes it dangerous once nobody remembers what it was testifying to.

## Significance

**The tension is not really about stones. It is about what happens to a place where God actually
showed up.**

Bethel is the case that proves it. Jacob's stone is set up because something happened there, and God
himself later refers to it approvingly — *"I am the God of Bethel, where you anointed a pillar"*
(31:13). **Eight hundred years later the same site has a golden calf in it** (1 Kings 12:28–29), and
Hosea's verdict on the northern kingdom is that *"according to the abundance of his fruit he has
multiplied his altars … he has made goodly pillars"* (10:1). **The law's flat prohibition looks
excessive from Genesis 28 and looks like foresight from 2 Kings 17.**

Which is the ordinary reading offered for the whole tension, and the wiki gives it as a reading
rather than a resolution: **a monument to a real revelation is the most reusable object in the world,
and Deuteronomy would rather lose Jacob's stone than keep Jeroboam's.**
