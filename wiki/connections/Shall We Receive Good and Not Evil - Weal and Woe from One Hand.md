# Shall We Receive Good and Not Evil - Weal and Woe from One Hand

## Passages involved

- **Job 2:10** — *"What? Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?"*
  — [[The Second Test]]
- **Job 1:21** — *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away. Blessed be Yahweh's name."* —
  [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]]
- **Isaiah 45:7** — *"I form the light and create darkness. I make peace and create calamity. I am
  Yahweh, who does all these things."*
- **Lamentations 3:37–38** — *"Who is he who says, and it comes to pass, when the Lord doesn't
  command it? Doesn't evil and good come out of the mouth of the Most High?"*
- **Amos 3:6** — *"Does evil happen to a city, and Yahweh hasn't done it?"*
- **Deuteronomy 32:39** — *"I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal. There is no one who can
  deliver out of my hand."*
- **1 Samuel 2:6–7** — *"Yahweh kills and makes alive. He brings down to Sheol and brings up. Yahweh
  makes poor and makes rich."*
- **Ecclesiastes 7:14** — *"In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity
  consider; yes, God has made the one side by side with the other."*
- **Genesis 50:20** — *"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good"* —
  [[You Meant Evil Against Me - The Two Intentions at Genesis 50.20]]

## Analysis

**Job's question is rhetorical, and the Bible answers it the same way every time it is asked.**
*Gam ʾet-haṭṭôb nəqabbēl mēʾēt hāʾĕlōhîm wəʾet-hāraʿ lōʾ nəqabbēl* — shall we accept the good from
God's hand and not accept the *raʿ*? The word is not *sin*; it is **calamity, harm, the bad thing
that happens**, and the Old Testament uses it of God's action without embarrassment in at least six
places that share a single grammatical habit: **a pair of opposites, both governed by one divine
verb.**

| Passage | The pair | The verb |
|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomy 32:39 | kill / make alive · wound / heal | *I* |
| 1 Samuel 2:6–7 | kill / make alive · poor / rich | *Yahweh* |
| Job 2:10 | good / evil | *receive from the hand of God* |
| Isaiah 45:7 | light / darkness · peace / calamity | *form* and *create* |
| Lamentations 3:38 | evil / good | *come out of the mouth of* |
| Amos 3:6 | — | *has Yahweh not done it* |

**The construction is called a merism, and its purpose is exhaustiveness**: naming the two ends in
order to include everything between them. What makes these particular merisms hard is that one end
is a thing nobody wants attributed to God, and the texts attribute it anyway, in the same clause as
the thing everybody does.

**Isaiah 45:7 is the strongest of them and the most often softened.** *ʿŌśeh šālôm ûbôrēʾ rāʿ* — the
verb is *bārāʾ*, the verb of Genesis 1:1, used nowhere in the Hebrew Bible with a human subject. It
is spoken to Cyrus, in a passage insisting that Yahweh and no other is behind the rise and fall of
empires, and English versions have rendered *raʿ* as *evil* (KJV), *calamity* (WEB, NASB),
*disaster* (NIV, ESV) and *woe* (JPS). **The range is real** — *raʿ* covers moral evil and sheer
misfortune both, and the context here is plainly political catastrophe rather than sin. But the
softening is doing work, and the honest statement is that **the Old Testament declines to place
calamity outside God's agency**, whatever it thinks about moral evil.

**Job's version is the only one spoken by a sufferer.** Deuteronomy, Samuel, Isaiah, Lamentations
and Amos are all assertions *about* God, made by prophets or narrators. **Job 2:10 is a man with
incurable sores on an ash-heap saying it about his own case, to his wife, about their children.**
That is why the verse carries weight the doctrinal statements do not, and why the narrator certifies
it in the next clause — *"In all this Job didn't sin with his lips."*

**And the book does not let it stand as a resolution.** Six verses earlier, God has said *"you
incited me against him, to ruin him **without cause**"* — conceding an external mover and keeping
the act. Job, who has not heard that and never will, attributes the whole of it to God's hand
directly. **Both statements are in the same passage, and the text reconciles neither.** Thirty-one
chapters later Job is still saying it and no longer saying it calmly: *"the arrows of the Almighty
are within me"* (6:4), *"he multiplies my wounds without cause"* (9:17). **What changes between 2:10
and chapter 9 is not Job's theology of agency but his willingness to be quiet about it**, and at
42:7 God says he spoke rightly.

**Genesis 50:20 is the same problem solved in the other direction.** Joseph's *"you meant evil
against me, but God meant it for good"* keeps two intentions in one event without collapsing them —
the brothers' *raʿ* and God's *ṭôb*. **Joseph gets to say that because he is standing at the end of
the story and can see what it was for.** Job is standing in the middle of his and is given no such
sentence; the whirlwind, when it comes, offers him wild goats and storehouses of snow rather than a
purpose. **The two books are the Bible's two treatments of the same question, and only one of them
supplies an outcome to read the suffering by.**

**Ecclesiastes states the epistemology behind Job's line.** *"God has made the one side by side with
the other, to the end that man should not find out anything after him"* (7:14) — prosperity and
adversity are deliberately interleaved **so that the pattern will not be readable**. That is a
direct denial of [[Retribution]] as a diagnostic, and it is the wisdom tradition conceding what the
book of Job dramatises.

**The New Testament keeps the substance and changes the emphasis.** It does not repeat the merism —
*"God is light, and in him is no darkness at all"* (1 John 1:5) runs the other way, and James 1:13
denies outright that God tempts anyone. What it retains is the refusal to put affliction outside
God's reach: Paul's thorn is *"a messenger of Satan"* and is nonetheless *given* (2 Corinthians
12:7), in a passive whose agent is not in doubt, and is answered not by removal but by *"my grace is
sufficient for you"*. **That is Job's structure exactly — an affliction delegated, permitted, and
not explained — with the permission disclosed to the sufferer, which is the one thing Job never
gets.**

## Significance

- **It is the fullest statement in Scripture of a single divine agency**, made at the worst possible
  moment by the worst-treated man in the Bible, and certified as not a sin.
- **It sets the floor for everything Job says afterwards.** Every accusation in chapters 3–31 rests
  on the same premise as 2:10: that there is one hand, and it is God's. **Job's later violence is
  not a loss of faith in that premise; it is the premise taken seriously.**
- **It is the reason the friends and Job are not arguing about agency.** Both sides agree God did it.
  They differ about what may be inferred, and [[Retribution]] is the name of that inference.
- **It marks where the Bible declines a defence it could have made.** Attributing calamity to a
  second power would relieve the pressure; the Old Testament will not do it, and the book of Job —
  which has a second power available on stage at 2:6 — **refuses it most pointedly of all**, since
  the accuser is gone by verse 7 and the epilogue at 42:11 credits the whole affair to Yahweh.
  See [[Satan]] and [[Providence]].
