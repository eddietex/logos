# I Put On Righteousness - Job 29.14 and the Garments of Justice

## Passages involved

- **Job 29:14** — *"I put on righteousness, and it clothed me. My justice was as a robe and a
  diadem."* — [[The Months of Old]]
- **Job 19:9** — *"He has stripped me of my glory, and taken the crown from my head."* —
  [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Job 22:6** — Eliphaz: *"you have … stripped the naked of their clothing."* —
  [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]

- **Isaiah 11:5** — of the shoot of Jesse: *"Righteousness will be the belt of his waist, and

- **Isaiah 59:16–17** — of Yahweh, who *"saw that there was no man"*: *"He put on righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation

- **Colossians 3:12** — *"Put on therefore … a heart of compassion, kindness, lowliness, humility, and

- **Revelation 19:8** — *"the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints."*

## Analysis

**Scripture regularly describes character as clothing**, and the figure has two forms. In one, a
virtue or a vice is something a person puts on: the wicked man of Psalm 109:18 wraps himself in
cursing until it soaks *"into his inward parts like water"*, and Colossians 3:12 tells believers to put
on compassion and kindness. In the other, God is the one who dresses someone: Isaiah 61:10's *"robe of
righteousness"*, Psalm 132's priests clothed with salvation, and Zechariah 3, where an angel strips the
high priest of his filthy garments and a clean turban is set on his head. The first form is ethics;
the second is grace.

**Job 29:14 sits at the point where the two forms meet.** *Ṣedeq lābaštî* — *I put on righteousness* —
is the first form, a man dressing himself in what he did. But the second half turns the verb round:
*wayyilbāšēnî*, *and it clothed itself with me.* The garment becomes the agent and the man the thing
worn. The idiom is rare and strong: the only close parallels make the subject the Spirit of God, who
*clothes himself* with Gideon (Judges 6:34) and with others in Chronicles. Job does not claim that
much, but the grammar puts his righteousness in the position the Spirit holds there — something that
took hold of him rather than something he wore for show.

**The two items named are official dress.** *Məʿîl*, the robe, is the garment of rank — the high
priest's robe of the ephod is a *məʿîl*, and so are the robes of kings and of Samuel. *Ṣānîp*, the WEB's
*diadem*, is a wound headdress, a turban: it is the *"clean turban"* set on Joshua in Zechariah 3:5 and,
in a related form, the *"royal diadem"* of Isaiah 62:3. **Job is saying that his justice was his office**
— that what marked him out at the gate, where 29:7–10 seats him, was not his wealth but the visible
fact of his judging rightly, as a priest is known by his robe and a king by his crown.

**And it was taken off him.** *"He has stripped me of my glory, and taken the crown from my head"*
(19:9) is the same image undone: *ʿăṭārâ*, the crown, removed — and the *kābôd* of 19:9 is the *"My
glory is fresh in me"* of 29:20. In between, Eliphaz charged him with stripping others: *"you have …
stripped the naked of their clothing"* (22:6). **The man accused of stripping the naked was himself
stripped, and what he lost was a garment of justice.**

**The prophets give the same garment to God and to his king.** Isaiah 11:5 belts the shoot of Jesse
with righteousness and faithfulness; Isaiah 59:17 dresses Yahweh himself in righteousness as a
breastplate, because *"there was no man"* to do justice and he must do it himself. Ephesians 6:14 then
hands that divine armour to the church, *"the breastplate of righteousness"*. Read in that line, Job
29:14 is a human being wearing in miniature what God wears in Isaiah 59 — the righteousness that acts
for the oppressed.

**God's answer will put the question back to him.** At 40:10, in the second whirlwind speech, Yahweh
says *"Now deck yourself with excellency and dignity. Array yourself with honor and majesty"* — the
royal garments of God, offered to Job as a challenge: if you can wear these and humble the proud, *"then
I will also admit to you that your own right hand can save you"* (40:14). The robe Job remembers in
29:14 is genuine; the robe of 40:10 is the one no man can put on.

## Significance

- It names the shape of Job's defence. In chapters 29–31 his righteousness is not a doctrine and not a
  ritual status but the list of deeds toward the needy that Scripture uses everywhere to define the
  righteous man (see
  [[Bread to the Hungry and Clothing to the Naked - Job 22.6-9, Job 31, and Matthew 25]]); 29:14 is that
  list summed up as a garment.
- It links the clothing images of the book together — the robes torn in mourning (1:20; 2:12),
  sackcloth sewn on the skin (16:15), the crown stripped (19:9), the naked stripped (22:6), the robe of
  justice (29:14), and the majesty Job cannot wear (40:10). See [[Nakedness and Covering]] and
  [[Five Changes of Clothing - The Coat Given Again]] for the same figure in Joseph's story.
- It is the bridge between the Old Testament's ethical garment and the New Testament's given one.
  Revelation 19:8 joins them: the bride is *given* fine linen to wear, and the linen *is* *"the
  righteous acts of the saints."*
