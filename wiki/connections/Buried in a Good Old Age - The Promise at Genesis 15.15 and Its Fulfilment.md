# Buried in a Good Old Age - The Promise at Genesis 15.15 and Its Fulfilment

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 15:15** — *"but you will go to your fathers in peace. You will be buried at a good old
  age."*
- **Genesis 25:7–10** — *"one hundred seventy-five years. Abraham gave up his spirit, and died in a
  good old age, an old man, and full of years, and was gathered to his people. Isaac and Ishmael, his
  sons, buried him in the cave of Machpelah"*
- Genesis 15:13–14 — the four hundred years of affliction the promise is embedded in
- Genesis 35:29; 47:9 — Isaac's death notice, and Jacob's verdict on his own life
- Job 42:17 — *"So Job died, being old and full of days"*

## Analysis

**A promise made in the dark during a covenant ceremony, and kept sixty years and eleven chapters
later in the same three words.**

*Śêbâ ṭôbâ*, **a good old age**, occurs in Genesis exactly twice, and the two occurrences are these.
It is not a stock phrase the book reaches for when someone dies: [[Sarah]] gets a lifespan and a
funeral (23:1–2), [[Terah]] gets *"died in Haran"* (11:32), [[Lot]] gets nothing. **The phrase is
reserved, used once as a commitment and once as a discharge**, and Genesis marks the second by
reusing the first's words and saying nothing at all about it.

### The promise clause by clause

| Genesis 15:15 | Genesis 25:7–10 |
|---|---|
| *"you will go to your fathers"* | *"was gathered to his people"* (25:8) |
| *"in peace"* | — *not stated in words*; supplied by the scene: two sons at one grave, an estate settled, an heir blessed |
| *"You will be buried"* | *"Isaac and Ishmael, his sons, buried him"* (25:9) |
| *"at a good old age"* | *"died in a good old age"* (25:8) |

**Three of the four are matched in the text and the fourth is matched in the arrangement.** The
promise is the only one in the Abraham cycle that is entirely about the man himself rather than about
his offspring, his land, or the nations — and it is the only one he lives to see kept.

### Where it sits in Genesis 15, which is the point

**The verse is a parenthesis inside a prophecy of slavery.**

> **13** … Know for sure that your offspring will live as foreigners in a land that is not theirs,
> and will serve them. They will afflict them four hundred years.
> **14** I will also judge that nation, whom they will serve. Afterward they will come out with great
> wealth,
> **15** but you will go to your fathers in peace. You will be buried at a good old age.
> **16** In the fourth generation they will come here again …

**God tells Abram that his descendants will be enslaved for four centuries, and then, in the middle of
it, that he personally will die peacefully at a great age.** The word order is the point: the
covenant's long horizon and the covenant-bearer's short one are stated in consecutive verses, and the
short one is a comfort offered inside a sentence of very bad news. See
[[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] and [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].

**Genesis 25:8 is therefore a down payment.** The four-hundred-year clause is still outstanding when
Abraham is buried; the one clause the reader can check has been checked. **The book's method here is
the same one [[A Wife for Isaac]] introduced** — put the fulfilment on the page in the promise's own
vocabulary and decline to point at it.

### The counter-case in the same book

**Jacob, asked directly, gives the opposite verdict on his own life.**

> **47:9** The days of the years of my pilgrimage are one hundred thirty years. Few and evil have
> been the days of the years of my life, and they have not attained to the days of the years of the
> life of my fathers in the days of their pilgrimage.

**A patriarch, standing in front of Pharaoh, says his life was short and bad and worse than his
father's and grandfather's.** Genesis records both verdicts and ranks neither. Abraham dies
*śābēaʿ* — **satisfied**, with no object attached to the word, which is unique in Scripture; Jacob
dies having said in advance that he was not. See [[Life and Death]] and [[Suffering]].

**And Job gets the phrase's near-twin.** *"So Job died, being old and full of days"* (42:17) — the
same closing formula, at the end of a book about a man who lost everything and argued with God about
it for thirty-five chapters. **The Bible gives the formula to the man who was promised it and to the
man who was never promised anything**, which is roughly what [[The Prosperity of the Wicked]] and
[[Job (person)]] are already about.

## Significance

**It is the smallest promise in the Abraham cycle and the only one fulfilled inside it.**

Everything else God swore to Abraham is outstanding when he is buried: the land is a cave he paid four
hundred shekels for, the nation is one son of seventy-five with a barren wife of thirty-five years'
standing, and the blessing to *"all the families of the earth"* has not begun. **Hebrews 11:13 states
the balance without softening it — *"These all died in faith, not having received the promises"* —
and Acts 7:5 adds that God gave him *"no inheritance in it, no, not so much as to set his foot on."***

What he did receive was fifteen words at Genesis 15:15, and Genesis 25:8 pays them in full. **The
cycle ends by keeping the one promise that was about the man and not about the future**, which is
either a very small thing or the only kind of thing a person actually gets to see. Genesis does not
say which. See [[Faith]] and [[Hope]].
