# The First Man and Wisdom Before the Hills

## Passages involved

- **Job 15:7–8** — Eliphaz: *"Are you the first man who was born? Or were you brought out before the
  hills? Have you heard the secret counsel of God? Do you limit wisdom to yourself?"* —
  [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]]
- **Proverbs 8:22–31** — Wisdom personified: *"Before the mountains were settled in place, before
  the hills, I was brought forth… When he established the heavens, I was there."*
- **Job 38:4, 21** — God, from the whirlwind: *"Where were you when I laid the foundations of the
  earth? … Surely you know, for you were born then, and the number of your days is great!"*
- **Genesis 2:7** — the forming of the first man — [[The Garden of Eden]]
- **Job 28:12–28** — *"Where shall wisdom be found? … God understands its way"*
- **Ezekiel 28:12–15** — the figure in Eden, on the holy mountain, *"full of wisdom"*

## Analysis

Eliphaz's taunt is a compressed double allusion, and both halves matter.

**"Are you the first man who was born?"** reaches back to a widespread ancient conception — attested
in Israel and beyond it — of a primeval human who, because he was there at the beginning, possessed
knowledge unavailable to anyone since. The Bible's version of that figure is [[Adam]], formed
directly by God, placed in the garden, and set to name the animals. Ezekiel 28:12–15 preserves a
related tradition of a being in Eden, on God's holy mountain, *"full of wisdom and perfect in
beauty"*.

**"Or were you brought out before the hills?"** is the sharper half. The verb is *ḥûl*, to be
brought forth in labour, and it is the verb Proverbs 8:24–25 uses twice of Wisdom: *"When there were
no depths, I was brought forth … before the hills, I was brought forth."* Even the noun is shared —
*hills*, *gəbāʿôt*. Eliphaz is asking whether Job imagines he is Wisdom herself, present before the
landscape existed, watching the world being laid out.

The two halves converge on verse 8's question, which is what the taunt is for: *"Have you heard the
secret counsel of God?"* — the *sôd*, the divine council. Only a being present at the founding could
have. Job is not, and therefore, Eliphaz concludes, has no standing to contradict the tradition.

**And then God asks the identical question, in earnest, and it means the opposite.** *"Where were
you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if you have understanding"* (38:4), followed
by the flattest sarcasm in Scripture: *"Surely you know, for you were born then, and the number of
your days is great!"* (38:21). This is Eliphaz's rhetorical figure, word for word in substance,
placed in God's mouth twenty-three chapters later — and God immediately proceeds to spend four
chapters describing exactly the founding of the world Job did not witness.

The reversal is the connection's point, and it cuts in two directions at once.

**Eliphaz was right about the fact and wrong to be the one saying it.** Job really has not heard the
council and really was not there when the foundations were laid. But Eliphaz has not either, and he
is using a limit that applies to both of them to disqualify one of them. Only the being who *was*
there can press the argument, and when he does, he presses it against Job and the friends
indifferently — and then tells the friends that Job spoke rightly and they did not (42:7). **The
same question is legitimate from God and illegitimate from Eliphaz**, because Eliphaz is using it as
a debating move and God is using it as a description of the actual situation.

There is a second irony inside verse 8, noted on the passage page and worth repeating here: **the
reader has attended the council.** Job 1:6–12 and 2:1–6 are set in it. The book's structure makes
Eliphaz's rhetorical question answerable in a way he cannot imagine — someone has heard the secret
counsel of God, and it is us, and what was said there destroys his argument.

## Significance

- **It ties Job to the wisdom tradition's own self-portrait.** Proverbs 8 is the tradition's highest
  claim: Wisdom present at creation, delighting in the inhabited world. Job 15:7 shows a
  representative of that tradition wielding the claim as a weapon, and Job 28 shows the tradition
  concluding that wisdom is not findable by the living at all — *"It can't be gotten for gold"*.
  Between the two, the book has walked its own inheritance to the edge.
- **It gives the whirlwind speeches a target.** God's questions in Job 38–41 are often read as
  overwhelming Job with irrelevance. Read against 15:7–8, they are precise: God is taking up the
  friends' rhetorical question and making it real, thereby dissolving the credential the friends
  claimed to hold. Nobody in the book is qualified to say what God is doing, which is why the one
  who kept saying he did not understand is the one commended.
- **It supplies one of the wiki's links from Job back to Genesis.** Job's cast is non-Israelite and
  its poetry knows no covenant, but its argument keeps touching the primeval history: the first man
  here, the crying blood of Abel at 16:18 (see [[Do Not Cover My Blood]]), and the anthropology of
  Genesis 6:5 behind 15:14–16 (see [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]).
- **A caution.** The Proverbs 8 link rests on shared vocabulary — *ḥûl* and *gəbāʿôt* — in a
  formulation that may simply have been a stock way of saying *before the world began*. It is strong
  enough to note and too slight to date either book by, and this wiki draws no conclusion about
  which text is earlier.
