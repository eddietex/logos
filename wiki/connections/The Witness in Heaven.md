# The Witness in Heaven

## Passages involved

- **Job 9:32–33** — *"For he is not a man, as I am, that I should answer him … There is no umpire
  between us, that might lay his hand on us both."*
- **Job 16:19–21** — *"Even now, behold, my witness is in heaven. He who vouches for me is on high …
  that he would maintain the right of a man with God, of a son of man with his neighbor!"* —
  [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]]
- **Job 17:3** — *"Now give a pledge, be collateral for me with yourself. Who is there who will
  strike hands with me?"* — same passage
- **Job 19:25–27** — *"But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives."*
- **Job 33:23–24** — Elihu: *"If there is beside him an angel, an interpreter, one among a
  thousand…"*
- **Job 1:6–12; 2:1–6** — the heavenly court, containing an accuser and no advocate
- **Zechariah 3:1–5** — Joshua the high priest, the accuser at his right hand, and the Angel of
  Yahweh rebuking him
- **1 Timothy 2:5** — *"one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus"*
- **Hebrews 7:25; 9:24** — *"he always lives to make intercession for them"*; *"to appear before the
  face of God for us"*
- **Romans 8:26–27, 34** — the Spirit interceding within, and Christ *"at the right hand of God,
  who also makes intercession for us"*
- **1 John 2:1** — *"we have a Counselor with the Father, Jesus Christ, the righteous"*

## Analysis

Across four speeches Job reaches for a figure he cannot name, and the reaching escalates. At 9:33
the figure is an **absence**: there is no umpire, no one with standing over both parties. At
16:19 the figure is asserted as **present**: *my witness is in heaven*. At 17:3 Job demands a
**guarantor** and finds no one in the room to be one. At 19:25 he calls him a **living Redeemer**.
The book never introduces this figure, never confirms him, and never has him act.

**What Job wants is precise, and it is not what most readers assume.** He does not want rescue, and
he does not want relief; he asks for those far less often than he asks to be heard. He wants a
*proceeding* — someone who can compel a hearing between a man and God and give a verdict, because
his own testimony has been ruled inadmissible by the only people present. The specific offices are
legal and commercial: *môkîaḥ* (arbitrator), *ʿēd* and the Aramaic *śāhēd* (witness), *ʿārab* (to
stand surety), *gōʾēl* (kinsman-redeemer). See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

**Who is the witness?** Three readings, all defensible.

**(1) God himself.** Job appeals from the God he is experiencing to the God he has known — from
God-as-assailant to God-as-he-truly-is. This is the majority view among modern commentators. It
fits the book's monotheism, it explains why the figure needs no introduction, and it makes sense of
17:3, where Job explicitly asks God to be his surety *with God*. Its difficulty is 16:21, where the
figure *"maintain[s] the right of a man with God"*, which reads more naturally of a third party.

**(2) A distinct heavenly advocate.** An angel or intercessor, the office Elihu describes at
33:23–24. This fits the legal language and the escalation from umpire to redeemer, and it fits a
book that has already shown a populated heavenly court. Its difficulty is that Job elsewhere
insists no such figure exists — 9:33 is a complaint about an absence — and the book never confirms
one.

**(3) Job's cry, personified.** Verse 18 has just asked that his cry *"have no place to rest"*, and
verse 19 may report that it has arrived and now stands as his witness. This follows the verse order
exactly and is the least popular; against it, *"he who vouches for me"* is personal.

**The irony the reader carries is unavailable to Job.** The heavenly court in this book really does
contain a figure with an interest in his case — and that figure is the prosecution. Job asks, blind,
for the counterpart of a being he does not know exists. Zechariah 3 is the one place in the Hebrew
Bible where that configuration is completed: the accuser stands at the right hand of a man in filthy
garments, and someone rebukes him and reclothes the defendant.

## Significance

**The Christian reading, and its limits.** From at least the patristic period these verses have been
read as reaching toward Christ, and the New Testament supplies exactly the offices Job named:
mediator (1 Timothy 2:5), intercessor appearing before God's face (Hebrews 7:25, 9:24), and
advocate — *paraklētos*, the one called alongside — with the Father (1 John 2:1). Job 19:25 in
particular has been read this way for so long that Handel's setting has made it hard to hear
otherwise.

Three qualifications keep the reading honest.

- **Job asks for an advocate *against* God.** The New Testament's advocate stands *with the Father*,
  on the side of a guilty party, in a configuration where God is not the adversary. That is a
  different problem, differently solved. Reading 16:19 as a straightforward prophecy of 1 John 2:1
  loses the thing Job is actually saying.
- **The book does not endorse the figure.** No character confirms him, God does not mention him in
  the whirlwind speeches, and the resolution at 42:7–10 comes by an entirely different route.
- **The Hebrew of 19:25–27 is badly damaged** — *"after my skin is destroyed, then in my flesh shall
  I see God"* renders a text whose preposition is ambiguous between *from* and *without* my flesh,
  and the following verses are among the hardest in the Bible. Whatever weight the thread carries,
  19:25 is the weakest link in it, not the strongest.

**What the connection does establish** is that the Hebrew Bible contains a demand for something it
does not supply, articulated by its most extreme sufferer, in legal terms, four times, with
increasing confidence. The New Testament writers use that vocabulary. Whether Job is prophesying or
whether the New Testament found in an old vocabulary the only words adequate to a new fact is a
question the wiki leaves open, and the difference matters less than it looks: on either account,
the question Job asked is the one the answer is shaped to fit.

**And the book's own resolution is stranger than either.** Job spends thirty chapters asking for
someone to intercede for him, and the last thing he does before his fortunes turn is intercede for
the friends: *"my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him"* (42:8). The advocate never
arrives. Job becomes one.
