# Two Sons Nearly Lost - The Sending of Ishmael and the Binding of Isaac

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 21:8–21** — Sarah demands the expulsion; God tells Abraham to comply; Abraham rises early,
  loads Hagar, and sends her; the water gives out; the boy is laid under a bush; the angel of God
  calls out of the sky; God opens her eyes to a well; the boy lives and becomes a nation. See
  [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].
- **Genesis 22:1–19** — God demands the offering; Abraham rises early, loads the donkey, and goes;
  the wood is laid on Isaac; the knife is raised; Yahweh's angel calls out of the sky; Abraham lifts
  his eyes and sees a ram; the boy lives and the promise is sworn.

## Analysis

**Genesis puts these two chapters back to back, and they are the same chapter told twice.**

| | Genesis 21:8–21 | Genesis 22:1–19 |
|---|---|---|
| the son | *"the son of this servant"* — never named in the episode | *"your son, your only son, whom you love, even Isaac"* |
| who demands it | **Sarah**: *"Cast out this servant and her son!"* (21:10) | **God**: *"Now take your son … and go"* (22:2) |
| how Abraham takes it | *"The thing was very grievous in Abraham's sight"* (21:11) | nothing is reported at all |
| God's part | he overrules the grief and endorses the demand (21:12) | he makes the demand (22:1–2) |
| the morning | *"Abraham rose up early in the morning"* (21:14) | *"Abraham rose early in the morning"* (22:3) — the same Hebrew clause |
| the loading | bread and a skin of water put on Hagar's shoulder | the split wood laid on Isaac's back (22:6) |
| the journey | wandering, until the water runs out | three days, to a place named in advance |
| the child's danger | thirst | a knife |
| who cannot look | *"Don't let me see the death of the child"* (21:16) | Abraham looks, and lifts the knife (22:10) |
| the intervention | *"The angel of God called to Hagar out of the sky"* (21:17) | *"Yahweh's angel called to him out of the sky"* (22:11) |
| what the voice says first | *"Don't be afraid"* | *"Don't lay your hand on the boy"* |
| the seeing | *"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water"* (21:19) | *"Abraham lifted up his eyes, and looked, and saw … a ram"* (22:13) |
| the promise after | *"I will make him a great nation"* (21:18) | *"I will multiply your offspring greatly like the stars"* (22:17) |
| where it ends | the wilderness of Paran, and a wife from Egypt | **Beersheba** (22:19) |

**Abraham loses both sons in two consecutive chapters and gets one of them back.** He is silent in
both. He is given an instruction he does not argue with in both — the man who haggled six times over
Sodom (18:23–33) says nothing to either demand. And in both, the thing that saves the child is not
Abraham: it is a voice from the sky and a provision that was already there.

### The three firsts they share

**A divine voice calling *out of the sky*** — *min-haššāmayim* — occurs in Genesis at 21:17, 22:11
and 22:15, and nowhere else. In every earlier scene the messenger is on the ground: he *finds* Hagar
at a spring (16:7), he sits under a tree and eats (18:8), he takes Lot by the hand (19:16). **The two
near-losses of Abraham's sons are the only occasions in the book on which heaven has to be reached
into.**

**And both turn on eyes.** Neither rescue creates anything. The well in 21:19 was there; the ram in
22:13 was there. What changes is that somebody sees it — *God opened her eyes* in the first case,
*Abraham lifted up his eyes* in the second. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

**And both are the making of a name.** Hagar had already named God *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, *a God who sees*, at
the well of the first flight (16:13); Abraham names the mountain *Yahweh Will Provide* (22:14), which
in Hebrew is *Yahweh yirʾeh*, ***Yahweh sees***. The two episodes end with the same verb turned into
two divine names, one given by a slave and one by a patriarch.

### Why chapter 21 has to come first

*"your son, your only son, whom you love, even Isaac"* (22:2) is a sentence that could not be spoken
one chapter earlier, and Genesis knows it. *Yāḥîd*, **only**, is exact and it is only true from 21:14
onward. The expulsion is what makes the binding possible as a sentence, which is the strongest
argument for reading the two as a designed pair rather than as neighbours.

Rabbinic tradition noticed the joint and made something of it: *Genesis Rabbah* 55:7 has God and
Abraham negotiate over which son *"your only son"* means, precisely because two are alive. The
midrash is answering a real feature of the text.

### Where they differ, and it is the whole point

**The tests are not the same test.** Chapter 21 asks Abraham to give up a son to somebody else's
demand, for a reason he thinks is wrong, and God tells him the demand is right. Chapter 22 asks him
to give up a son to God's own demand, for no reason at all, and there is nobody to appeal to.

**And the narrator's attention goes opposite ways.** In 21 the camera leaves Abraham at the gate and
follows the woman for eight verses; in 22 it stays with Abraham for the whole three days and never
tells us what Isaac thought. **The chapter that is theologically central gives us less of the child's
interior than the chapter that is theologically peripheral gives us of the slave's.**

**One son gets a nation; the other gets the covenant.** Both promises are made by the same voice from
the same sky within thirty verses of each other, and Genesis keeps both. See [[Call and Election]].

## Significance

- **It is the clearest evidence that Genesis composes in pairs.** The wife-sister episodes, the two
  creation accounts, the two genealogies from Adam, and the two flights of Hagar all work this way,
  and this pair is the tightest of them: same opening clause, same rescue mechanism, same voice, same
  outcome, opposite emotional weight. See [[The Two Flights of Hagar]] and
  [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].
- **It is where the Abraham cycle empties out.** Lot leaves at 13:11, Eliezer is set aside at 15:4,
  Ishmael is sent at 21:14, and Isaac is asked for at 22:2. By the end of chapter 22 the promise has
  been reduced to one son and then handed back, and the next thing that happens in the book is a
  funeral.
- **It is the reason later readers have found Ishmael's story hard to file.** Christian tradition
  reads Genesis 22 as the type of everything and Genesis 21 as a domestic embarrassment, but the two
  are built out of the same parts, and the God who speaks from the sky over the bush speaks first.
