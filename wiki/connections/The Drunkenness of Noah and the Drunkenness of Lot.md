# The Drunkenness of Noah and the Drunkenness of Lot

## Passages involved

- [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] — Genesis 9:20–27
- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:30–38
- [[Noah and the Flood]] — Genesis 6:9–7:24, the judgment behind the first scene
- [[The Destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah]] — Genesis 19:1–29, the judgment behind the second
- Genesis 10:15–19; Deuteronomy 2:9, 19; 23:3–6 — what becomes of the peoples each scene explains

## Analysis

**Scripture records exactly two men getting drunk before the law is given, and both of them are the
sole survivor of a world-ending judgment.** The scenes are eleven chapters apart and built the same
way, beat for beat, down to the etiology hung on the end.

| | Genesis 9:20–27 | Genesis 19:30–38 |
|---|---|---|
| **The survivor** | Noah, the one household carried through the flood | Lot, the one household carried out of the overthrow |
| **The judgment behind it** | the earth drowned | the plain burned |
| **The first thing he does after** | *"Noah began to be a farmer, and planted a vineyard"* (9:20) | *"Lot went up out of Zoar… He lived in a cave"* (19:30) |
| **Where** | *"within his tent"* (9:21) | *"in a cave with his two daughters"* (19:30) |
| **How the wine reaches him** | he plants it, presses it, drinks it | *"They made their father drink wine"* (19:33), and again (19:35) |
| **The exposure** | *"He drank of the wine and got drunk. He was uncovered within his tent"* (9:21) | *"the firstborn went in, and lay with her father"* (19:33) |
| **The child who acts** | Ham, who *"saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brothers outside"* (9:22) | the two daughters, who plan it aloud and report to each other (19:31–34) |
| **What the father knows** | *"Noah awoke from his wine, and knew what his youngest son had done to him"* (9:24) | *"He didn't know when she lay down, nor when she arose"* (19:33, and again 19:35) |
| **The father's response** | a curse and two blessings, in his own mouth (9:25–27) | nothing; he never speaks again in Scripture |
| **The result** | Canaan cursed; the peoples of the land (10:15–19) | Moab and Ammon, *"to this day"* (19:37–38) |
| **The narrator's verdict** | none | none |

**Three of Israel's neighbours have their origin explained by a scene in which a drunk father is
uncovered.** Canaan comes out of the tent, Moab and Ammon out of the cave. In both cases Genesis
reports the act, names the people, and declines to grade anyone in the room — which is the
primeval history's habit turned up in the middle of the Abraham cycle, where it is much harder to
tolerate.

### The one difference that matters

**Noah wakes up.** *"Noah awoke from his wine, and knew what his youngest son had done to him"*
(9:24) — the verb is *yādaʿ*, and the whole second half of that scene depends on it: because he
knows, he speaks, and because he speaks, there is a curse, a blessing, and a line drawn through the
human race that the Table of Nations then follows.

**Lot never knows.** Genesis says so twice, in almost identical words, and the repetition is the
point: *"He didn't know when she lay down, nor when she arose"* (19:33); *"He didn't know when she
lay down, nor when she got up"* (19:35). Same verb, negated, twice. **There is no waking scene, no
speech, no curse, no blessing, and no reaction of any kind.** The one thing Noah does that gives
his scene a second half is precisely the thing Lot is denied.

The consequence is structural. Genesis 9 ends with a man deciding what his sons' descendants will
be; Genesis 19 ends with a narrator's footnote about two nations, spoken over the head of a man who
is still asleep. **The father who knows becomes an actor in the etiology. The father who does not
know becomes a name in it.**

### The wine, and who supplies it

The second difference is agency, and it runs the other way. **Noah's drunkenness is entirely his
own** — he plants the vineyard, he drinks, and the reflexive form at 9:21 (*wayyitgāl*, *he
uncovered himself*) puts the exposure on him as well. **Lot's is done to him.** The daughters
propose it (*"Come, let's make our father drink wine"*, 19:32), administer it, and repeat it the
following night after comparing notes.

So the two scenes distribute the blame in opposite directions and Genesis grades neither
distribution. It is worth stating plainly what that means for the standard readings: the case for
Noah's innocence in chapter 9 rests on the text's silence, and the same silence covers Lot's
daughters eleven chapters later. **A reader who wants Genesis to exonerate
Noah cannot get it from the text without also getting silence about the cave**, and a reader who
wants the cave condemned has to explain why the same narrator says nothing about the vineyard.
See [[The Vine and the Cup]], which sets out what the rest of the canon does with the state both
men are in.

### The third member of the group

This pair is not free-standing. [[Naked and Ashamed - Genesis 3 and Genesis 9]] shows that Genesis
9:20–27 is already built on Genesis 3 — plant, eat, exposure, covering, curse — and Genesis
19:30–38 joins the same series at the same beats, minus the covering. **Nobody covers Lot.** The
garment that Shem and Japheth carry in backwards (9:23), and the coats of skins God makes at 3:21,
have no counterpart in the cave. It is the one element of the pattern that the third scene drops,
and what fills the gap is a pregnancy.

## Significance

**The parallel is the reason Genesis 19:30–38 is not an isolated scandal.** Read on its own it is a
lurid appendix to the destruction of Sodom. Read against Genesis 9 it is the second instance of a
form the book has already used — *the survivor of a total judgment, undone in private immediately
afterwards, and a neighbouring people explained by it* — and the form is making an argument.

The argument is the flood's own conclusion, restated. God undertakes never to curse the ground
again, and the reason he gives is not that people have improved — *"because the imagination of
man's heart is evil from his youth"* (8:21) — and the very next paragraph proves the premise on the
best man alive. Genesis 19 does the same work on a smaller scale: **a household is
carried out of a burning city by angels, and within nine verses it produces the two nations
Deuteronomy will bar from the assembly.** Rescue changes the location and not the material.

There is one more thing the pair does, and it is why the wiki keeps it as a parallel rather than a
theme. **It explains Genesis's method by repetition.** A reader can dismiss the missing verdict in
chapter 9 as the awkwardness of an old story badly joined. Two of them, eleven chapters apart, in
the same shape, with the same silences in the same places, is not an accident of transmission. It
is how this book tells you where a people came from: **it gives you the scene and refuses you the
judgment**, and then leaves the canon four hundred years to decide — which, in the case of Moab, it
never entirely does. See [[Moab and Ammon - The Two Nations Born in the Cave]].

### A third scene that shares the vocabulary

**Genesis 29:22–25 is not a drunkenness story and it is built out of the same three parts.** Laban
*"made a feast"* — ***mišteh***, from ***šātâ***, *to drink*, the noun this book uses for Isaac's
weaning banquet (21:8) and nothing else before it — then *"in the evening, he took Leah his
daughter"*, and then *"in the morning, behold, it was Leah!"* **A drinking feast, a night, and a man
who did not know which woman he was with.**

**The text never says Jacob was drunk**, and the wiki does not say it either; what it records is that
this is the second time Genesis assembles a *mišteh*, a night, and an unrecognised woman, and that
the first time it did so it stated the not-knowing outright, twice — *"He didn't know when she lay
down, nor when she arose"* (19:33, 35). **The parallel is in the furniture and the sequence, not in a
verdict**, and the pericope page keeps it at exactly that width. See [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]] and
[[Deception]].
