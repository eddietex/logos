# The Sons of God and the Angels That Sinned

## Passages involved

- Genesis 6:1–4 — [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]
- Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7 *(not yet ingested)* — the phrase's other Hebrew Bible occurrences, in
  [[Job]]
- Job 4:18; 15:15 — *"he charges his angels with error"* and *"he puts no trust in his holy ones.
  Yes, the heavens are not clean in his sight"*: the Hebrew Bible's other statements of fallibility
  among heavenly beings, both spoken by [[Eliphaz the Temanite]]. See
  [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] and [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]].
- Deuteronomy 32:8 *(not yet ingested)* — the nations divided by the number of the sons of God
- *1 Enoch* 6–16 — the Watchers narrative (not canonical in most traditions; see below)
- Jude 6 *(not yet ingested)* — the angels who did not keep their first domain
- 2 Peter 2:4 *(not yet ingested)* — the angels who sinned, cast into Tartarus
- Matthew 22:30 *(not yet ingested)* — angels in heaven neither marry nor are given in marriage

## Analysis

**The reading came first.** It is easy to assume the identification of Genesis 6's *sons of God*
with angels is a later fancy grafted onto a plain text. The evidence runs the other way. Every
surviving pre-Christian Jewish treatment of Genesis 6:1–4 takes them as heavenly beings: *1 Enoch*,
the Book of Jubilees, the Genesis Apocryphon and the Damascus Document from Qumran, Josephus, Philo.
The Septuagint tradition is divided — some manuscripts of Genesis 6:2 read *angeloi tou theou*,
*angels of God*, outright. And the earliest Christian writers to comment — Justin Martyr, Athenagoras,
Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria — inherit it without argument. The Sethite reading
(*sons of God* = the godly line of Genesis 5) is first attested in Julius Africanus in the third
century, is pressed by Chrysostom, and becomes standard in the West only through Augustine's
*City of God* XV. What is now the traditional interpretation in most Protestant commentary is the
younger of the two by several centuries.

**What *1 Enoch* built.** The Watchers narrative (*1 Enoch* 6–16) takes Genesis 6:1–4 and supplies
everything Genesis withheld: two hundred angels who descend on Mount Hermon and bind themselves by
oath, led by Shemihazah and Asael; wives taken; giants born who devour the earth's produce and then
its people; forbidden knowledge taught to humanity — metalwork and weapons, cosmetics, sorcery,
astrology; the archangels' appeal; the Watchers bound in darkness until the judgment; and the
spirits of the dead giants left roaming as unclean spirits. This is not exegesis of Genesis so much
as a mythology constructed on its silences, and it was enormously influential in the Second Temple
period — which is the fact that makes the New Testament passages hard to assess.

**Jude and 2 Peter.** Jude 6: *"Angels who didn't keep their first domain, but deserted their own
dwelling place, he has kept in everlasting bonds under darkness for the judgment of the great day."*
2 Peter 2:4, in a passage that parallels Jude closely: *"God didn't spare angels when they sinned,
but cast them down to Tartarus, and committed them to pits of darkness to be reserved for
judgment."* Three observations, in order of how much weight they bear:

1. **Neither text quotes or names Genesis 6.** They speak of angels who sinned and are imprisoned;
   they do not mention the daughters of men, the Nephilim, or Noah's generation.
2. **The sequence is suggestive.** Jude lists three examples of judgment in order: the unbelieving
   exodus generation, the angels, and Sodom and Gomorrah. 2 Peter's list is angels, then **the flood
   and Noah**, then Sodom. In 2 Peter the angels stand immediately before the flood, which is where
   Genesis 6:1–4 stands.
3. **Jude 14–15 quotes *1 Enoch* 1:9 by name**, three verses after the angels notice. Jude plainly
   knew the book. Whether he endorses its Watchers narrative, or simply shares its vocabulary the
   way any writer draws on the imagery current in his time, is the interpretive question — and the
   language of *"deserted their own dwelling place"* is closer to *1 Enoch* than to anything in
   Genesis. See [[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]] for the canon question
   this raises.

**The strongest objection.** Matthew 22:30 — *"in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given
in marriage, but are like God's angels in heaven"* — is the verse most often held to settle it, and
it is a real difficulty for the angelic reading. Two qualifications, neither decisive. The saying is
about resurrected humans and uses angels as the comparison, so its subject is not angelic capacity
as such. And it specifies angels *in heaven*, which on the reading in question is precisely what
these are not. A second objection is stronger for being internal to Genesis: if the offense is
angelic, the sentence in 6:3 and the flood in 6:7 both fall on the wrong party. Genesis names no
punishment for the sons of God at all.

**Why the question stays open.** The lexical argument for the angelic reading is the best single
argument anyone has in this debate: the phrase *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* means heavenly beings everywhere
else it occurs in Hebrew. The narrative argument for the Sethite reading is the best contextual one:
two chapters of contrasted genealogies immediately precede, and the judgment falls on humanity. Both
are good arguments about different kinds of evidence, and neither has ever dislodged the other.

## Significance

Three things hang on this that are worth more than the puzzle itself.

**What the flood answers.** If the sons of God are divine beings, the last transgression before the
flood is the boundary between heaven and earth failing, and the primeval history's arc runs:
humanity crosses a boundary with a tree, a man crosses a boundary with his brother's blood, and
finally the orders of creation themselves stop holding their places. The flood then answers a
cosmological breach, and Genesis 1's whole logic of separation and kind is what is at stake. If they
are Sethites, the arc is more moral and less cosmic, and the flood answers the loss of the last
uncorrupted human line.

**How much Genesis withholds, and why.** However the identity question is settled, the contrast
between the four verses Genesis gives this and the eleven chapters *1 Enoch* gives it is itself the
lesson. Genesis has a story available in which the world goes wrong because something invaded it
from outside, and it will not tell that story. Instead it moves in one verse from the sons of God to
*"Yahweh saw that the wickedness of **man** was great in the earth"*. Whatever happened in 6:1–4,
Genesis insists the flood is about the human heart.

**The reception history as a case study.** This is one of the clearest instances in Scripture where
the earliest interpretation is not the traditional one, and where the traditional one arose for
reasons that were partly theological (discomfort with angelic procreation, and with the standing of
*1 Enoch*) rather than purely exegetical. It is a useful check on the instinct that older readings
are always the plainer ones — and equally on the instinct that the majority view must be the oldest.
