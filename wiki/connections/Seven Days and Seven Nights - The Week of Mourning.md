# Seven Days and Seven Nights - The Week of Mourning

## Passages involved

- **Job 2:13** — *"So they sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one
  spoke a word to him, for they saw that his grief was very great."* —
  [[Seven Days of Silence]]
- **Genesis 50:10** — *"there they lamented with a very great and severe lamentation. He mourned for
  his father seven days."* — [[The Deaths of Jacob and Joseph]], at [[Abel Mizraim]]
- **Genesis 50:3** — the seventy days of Egyptian embalming and mourning that precede it
- **1 Samuel 31:13** — the men of Jabesh Gilead bury Saul's bones under the tamarisk *"and fasted
  seven days"*
- **Ezekiel 3:15** — *"I sat there overwhelmed among them seven days"*, the prophet among the exiles
  by the river Chebar
- **Sirach 22:12** — *"Mourning for the dead lasts seven days"*, stated as a rule
- **Judith 16:24** — the house of Israel mourns Judith seven days
- **Job 3:1** — *"After this Job opened his mouth"*: the week ends

## Analysis

**Seven days is the Hebrew Bible's standing term for mourning a death**, and the wiki now has it
three times: Joseph for Jacob, Jabesh Gilead for Saul, and three friends for a man who is not dead.

**Genesis 50:10 is the first.** Jacob's cortege stops at the threshing floor of Atad, *beyond the
Jordan*, and keeps seven days there — set inside the seventy days of Egyptian mourning at 50:3, so
that the chapter carries **an Egyptian term and a Hebrew one in the same funeral**. The Canaanites
who watch name the place *ʾĒbel Miṣrayim*, mourning of Egypt, for what they saw.

**1 Samuel 31:13 is the clearest that the week is a term and not an emotion.** The men of Jabesh
Gilead have just walked all night to take Saul's body off the wall at Beth Shan, burned it, and
buried the bones. The seven-day fast that follows is what you do, in order, after a burial.

**Ezekiel 3:15 borrows it for something else.** The prophet sits seven days among the exiles
*"overwhelmed"* — *mašmîm*, stunned, desolate — and then, on the eighth, the word of Yahweh comes to
him. **The week is a term of speechlessness that a commission ends**, which is structurally what
happens in Job 2:13–3:1, with the difference that in Job the man who speaks on the far side of it is
the sufferer and not God.

**Job 2:13 is the one that does not fit, and that is the point.**

**It is the longest.** Genesis and Samuel say seven days; Job says *seven days and seven nights*,
and the doubling is not decorative — it is the same emphatic construction the book uses at 2:7 for
the sores *"from the sole of his foot to his head"*. Whatever the term normally is, this one is kept
to the letter.

**It is held over a living man.** Every other instance in the list follows a death, and most follow a
burial. **Here there is no corpse.** The friends arrive, fail to recognise him, perform the death
rites — torn robes, raised voices, dust on the head — and then keep the funeral week beside a man who
can hear them do it. **The book's judgment on the scale of Job's suffering is delivered in the form
of a customary term applied to someone still breathing**, and it is delivered by three men who will
later be wrong about everything else.

**And it is silent.** Genesis 50:10 has *"a very great and severe lamentation"*; Job 2:13 has *"no
one spoke a word to him."* The two are not in conflict — loud lament at a funeral and silence in
front of a sufferer are different obligations — but the contrast is why Job's week became the model
text for the practice rather than Jacob's.

**That practice is Jewish *shivah*** — *šibʿâ*, seven — and the rule most often derived from this
verse is not the length but the silence: **a visitor does not open the conversation; the mourner
speaks first** (b. *Moʿed Qatan* 28b), with Job 3:1 read as the mourner opening. On that reading the
friends keep the whole law of comforting perfectly, and the book's catastrophe begins one verse after
it releases them.

## Significance

- **It dates the friends' failure precisely.** Whatever is wrong with Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar
  begins at 4:1 and not at 2:11. For seven days and seven nights they do the thing correctly, at
  cost, having travelled to do it. See [[Seven Days of Silence]].
- **It measures Job's condition without describing it.** The book never tells us what Job looks
  like. It tells us that three men who knew him did not recognise him and then kept the mourning term
  for the dead in front of him, and lets the reader do the arithmetic.
- **It links the wiki's two mourning weeks across an enormous distance.** Genesis 50 and Job 2 are
  the Bible's two extended treatments of the rite, and they sit at the two ends of what mourning is
  for: a son burying a father at the proper time in great old age, and three strangers to grief
  burying a man who is going to be alive for another forty chapters. See [[Mourning and Lament]].
- **It sets up the book's structural joke about speech.** The wisest thing the three wisest men in
  the east ever do lasts a week and contains no words. See [[Wisdom]] and [[Language and Speech]].
