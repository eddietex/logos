# Folly in Israel - Nebalah from Genesis 34 to 2 Samuel 13

## Passages involved

- Genesis 34:7 — *"he had done folly in Israel in lying with Jacob's daughter; a thing ought not to
  be done"*
- Deuteronomy 22:21 — the unchaste bride, *"because she has done folly in Israel"*
- Joshua 7:15 — Achan, *"because he has… done a disgraceful thing in Israel"*
- Judges 19:23–24 — the old man at Gibeah, *"don't do this folly"*
- Judges 20:6, 10 — the Levite and the assembly, *"they have committed lewdness and folly in
  Israel"*
- 2 Samuel 13:12 — Tamar to Amnon, *"don't do this folly… no such thing ought to be done in Israel"*
- Jeremiah 29:23 — the false prophets in Babylon, *"they have done foolish things in Israel, and have
  committed adultery with their neighbors' wives"*

## Analysis

**Genesis 34:7 is the Bible's first *nĕbālâ bĕyiśrāʾēl*, and it is an anachronism on its face.**
There is no Israel yet. Jacob was renamed two chapters earlier (32:28), his household numbers
thirteen people, and they are camped outside a town that is not theirs. **The narrator judges an act
against a family by the standard of a nation that does not exist**, and never explains the leap.

***Nĕbālâ*** is not stupidity. The root's noun ***nābāl*** is the fool of Psalm 14:1 and the
namesake of the man in 1 Samuel 25 whose wife says *"folly is with him"* — **a moral category, not
an intellectual one: the person who acts as though the community's obligations do not bind him.**
**Every use of the phrase in the Hebrew Bible is an outrage that is also a betrayal of the covenant
community from inside it**, and six of the seven are sexual.

**The formula's career is unusually tight.**

| text | the act | who says it | what follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 34:7** | Shechem and Dinah | the narrator, of Jacob's sons' reaction | a massacre, and a curse fifteen chapters later |
| **Deuteronomy 22:21** | a bride found not to be a virgin | the law | stoning |
| **Joshua 7:15** | Achan's theft from the devoted things | Yahweh | Achan burned in the valley of Achor |
| **Judges 19:23** | the men of Gibeah | the old man of the house | a concubine handed out, and dismembered |
| **Judges 20:6, 10** | the same, recounted | the Levite, then the assembly | a civil war that nearly destroys Benjamin |
| **2 Samuel 13:12** | Amnon and Tamar | Tamar herself | Absalom's two-year silence, then Amnon killed |
| **Jeremiah 29:23** | prophets lying in Yahweh's name | Yahweh | roasted in the fire by the king of Babylon |

**Three things run right through the list.**

1. **It is always said, never merely narrated.** The phrase is a speech-act inside the story — a
   character or God naming an act as beyond the community's bounds. Genesis 34:7 is the nearest
   thing to an exception, and even there it is reported as what Jacob's sons understood.
2. **It always precedes violence, and the violence is always disproportionate to any stated
   penalty.** Deuteronomy 22:21 is the only entry where the response is a legal sentence. Everywhere
   else — Shechem, Gibeah, Amnon — the naming of the *nĕbālâ* opens onto a killing the law never
   prescribed.
3. **Genesis 34 and 2 Samuel 13 share more than the phrase.** Both use ***ʿānâ*** for the act, both
   add *"a thing ought not to be done"* (*wĕkēn lōʾ yēʿāśeh* / *lōʾ yēʿāśeh kēn*), both have a silent
   father, and both end with a brother taking the penalty into his own hands. See
   [[Dinah and Tamar - Two Rapes and Two Avenging Brothers]].

**And Judges 19–20 is the case that reads Genesis 34 back.** It is the other *nĕbālâ* whose answer
is the destruction of a town, and the wiki already tracks the chapter as a deliberate retelling of
Sodom — see [[Gibeah and Sodom - Judges 19 as a Retelling]]. **Judges thus stands at the junction of
both of Genesis's atrocity stories**, quoting Sodom's plot and Shechem's vocabulary in the same
chapter.

## Significance

**The phrase is how the Hebrew Bible marks an act it will not accommodate.** It names no penalty,
belongs to no code outside Deuteronomy 22, and appears where the community has to decide what it is
willing to be — which is why it clusters in Judges and in the Court History rather than in the legal
corpora.

**For Genesis 34 specifically it cuts both ways, and the wiki adopts neither side.** The narrator's
use of it at 34:7 endorses the brothers' judgment of what was done. **It does not endorse what they
did about it** — Genesis 49:5–7 is the book's verdict on that, and it is a curse. **The chapter's
one clear moral statement and its one clear moral condemnation land on opposite parties**, fifteen
chapters apart. See [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] and
[[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]].
