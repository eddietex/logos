## Analysis

**What she has at home.** The peace offering, *zeḇaḥ šəlāmîm*, was the sacrifice the worshipper ate:
part went to the altar, part to the priest, and the rest was a meal for the offerer and his
household. Leviticus 7:16 sets the time limit for a vow offering — the day of sacrifice and the
next. So the woman's *"Sacrifices of peace offerings are with me"* is an invitation to a meal, and a
meal that cannot wait: the meat must be eaten *today* or tomorrow. Her *"Today"* (*hayyôm*) and her
*"until the morning"* (7:18) fit the law's clock. The Hebrew of Proverbs 7:14 and Leviticus 7:15–16
is read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*. Leviticus 7:16 is cited here as the rule
for a vow offering; Proverbs does not cite Leviticus, and the wiki does not claim the writer had
that text in view.

**The pun.** *Šəlāmîm*, *peace offerings*, and *šillamtî*, *I have paid*, share the root *šlm*. Her
first sentence is a play on completed obligations: the vow is paid, the offering made, the account
square. Everything owed to God is settled, she says — now there is the feast.

**Piety or pretext.** The text does not say whether the vow was sincere. Either way, the lecture's
point stands: the vocabulary of the sanctuary is available to the strange woman, and a religious act
can be the opening line of a seduction. The wiki does not read the verse as evidence of cultic
prostitution; Proverbs 7 names no god and no shrine, and the woman speaks of her own house and her
own husband (7:19).

### Proverbs 20 in context

Proverbs 20:25 supplies another warning about religious commitment: rash dedication followed by
belated consideration of vows becomes a snare. Proverbs 7:14 concerns vows claimed as already
paid in a seduction; 20:25 concerns the timing of deliberation before commitment. Both prevent
treating religious language as an automatic guarantee of wisdom. Verse 25 does not specify the
vowed object, victim, or sacrifice, and should not be turned into a retelling of a particular
rash-vow narrative.

### The peace offering’s altar portion — Leviticus 3

Leviticus 3 prescribes the altar portion and blood handling for cattle, sheep, and goats;
it does not itself describe the offerer's meal or distinguish vow offerings from thanksgiving.
Leviticus 7:11–21 supplies those distinctions and eating rules; 7:28–34 assigns priestly
portions. The ritual that lies behind the wisdom comparison therefore has several legal
parts. Reading the meal back into chapter 3 as though it were stated there obscures that
division of instruction.

### The eating law now ingested

[[Thanksgiving, Vows, and the Peace Offering]] distinguishes thanksgiving from the vow category named by
Proverbs 7:14. Its longer eating window supports the meal inference, but neither establishes
her sincerity nor makes that meal a thanksgiving sacrifice. Leviticus 7:19–21 also makes
cleanliness a condition of eating; Proverbs does not report her ritual state. Religious
claims cannot by themselves establish compliance with either ritual or moral obligations.

### The general peace-offering rule — Leviticus 19:5–8

Leviticus 19 permits eating on the offering day and the next, then requires third-day
remains to be burned. Its general peace-offering wording matches the vow/freewill
window of 7:16–18 but does not repeat the thanksgiving exception of 7:15. This scope
tension must remain visible: chapter 19 does not explicitly cancel thanksgiving’s
same-day deadline. It also explicitly assigns cutting off to the third-day eater
(19:8); 7:18 names iniquity, while 7:20–21 attaches cutting off to unclean eating.

See [[Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 7:11–21; 19:5–8.

### The vow victim — Leviticus 22:17–33

Leviticus 22:21 requires a vow or freewill peace offering to be "perfect to be accepted", and
22:23 allows a deformed or incomplete bull or lamb for a freewill offering "but for a vow it shall
not be accepted." A paid vow therefore had a stricter standard for the animal than a freewill
gift. Verses 29–30 also restate the same-day rule for thanksgiving, so the 7:15 exception that
chapter 19 did not repeat is expressly reaffirmed. Proverbs 7:14 says nothing about the animal's
condition; the law does not make her claim more or less credible.

See [[Acceptable Victims and the Holy Name]]. Source: stored WEB Leviticus 7:15–16; 22:21–23, 29–30.

## Significance

The verse brings [[Sacrifice and Offering]] and [[Oaths and Vows]] into the wisdom literature by a
negative example: worship that settles its accounts with God and goes straight on to adultery. The
prophets will make the same complaint at the level of the nation; Proverbs makes it at the level of
one house.
