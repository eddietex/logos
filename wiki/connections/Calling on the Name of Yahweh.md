# Calling on the Name of Yahweh

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 4:26** — *"A son was also born to Seth, and he named him Enosh. At that time men began
  to call on Yahweh's name."* — [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]]
- **Genesis 12:8; 13:4; 21:33; 26:25** *(not yet ingested)* — Abram and Isaac building altars and
  calling on the name
- **Exodus 6:2–3** *(not yet ingested)* — *"I am Yahweh. I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to
  Jacob, as God Almighty; but by my name Yahweh I was not known to them."*
- **Joel 2:32** — *"whoever will call on Yahweh's name shall be saved"*
- **Acts 2:21; Romans 10:13** — Peter at Pentecost and Paul in Romans, both quoting Joel

## Analysis

Genesis 4 ends on eleven words that are doing more work than their length suggests, and all three
of their difficulties are worth keeping.

**1. "Began" or "profaned"?** The verb is *huḥal*, a hophal form. Read from *ḥālal* I, "to begin",
it gives the standard translation: *then men began to call on the name of Yahweh* — the first note
of anything like public worship in the Bible, placed deliberately three verses after Lamech's boast
so that Cain's line ends in a song about killing and Seth's begins in prayer. Read from the
identically spelled *ḥālal* II, "to profane", it gives *then men began to profane the name of
Yahweh*, making 4:26 the origin of idolatry. That second reading is not a curiosity: it is Genesis
Rabbah 23:7, followed by Rashi and much of the medieval Jewish tradition, and Maimonides builds his
account of the origin of idol worship on it. Nearly all modern translations, WEB included, take
"began". The narrative context favours them — the verse is placed as a counterweight to 4:24, and
the same phrase is unambiguously positive everywhere else it appears.

**2. What "calling on the name" means.** *Qārāʾ bešēm YHWH* is broader than petition. Its other
Genesis occurrences all attach it to an altar — Abram at Bethel (12:8), Abram returning to Bethel
(13:4), Abraham at Beersheba (21:33), Isaac at Beersheba (26:25) — so it covers public invocation
and proclamation as much as private prayer: naming God aloud, in a place, in a way other people can
hear. By the time Joel uses it, it has become the shorthand for the whole act of turning to God for
rescue, and that is the sense the New Testament inherits.

**3. The problem with Exodus 6:3.** Genesis 4:26 has the name *Yahweh* in public use ten generations
before Abraham. Exodus 6:2–3 has God tell Moses that to the patriarchs *"by my name Yahweh I was not
known to them."* These cannot both be simple statements about when the syllables were first heard.
Two long-standing resolutions:

- **Source-critical.** The two verses come from different sources — a strand (conventionally J) that
  uses the divine name from Genesis 2 onward, and one (P) that reserves its revelation for the
  burning bush and uses *ʾĕlōhîm* or *ʾĒl Šadday* before it. Genesis 4:26 is then J's own account of
  when the name entered use, and it simply does not agree with P. This is one of the observations
  the documentary hypothesis was originally built on, and it is a real feature of the text
  regardless of what one makes of the theory.
- **Semantic.** *Yādaʿ*, "know", regularly means know *by experience*, and "the name" regularly
  stands for the character behind it. On this reading Exodus 6:3 says the patriarchs never saw the
  name Yahweh *demonstrated* — as the God who keeps covenant by delivering a nation — which is
  exactly what the exodus is about to supply. The name was spoken; it had not yet been shown. The
  Hebrew supports this comfortably, and the reading has a long history.

Both are serious, and the wiki keeps both. It is worth noticing that the choice is not between a
faithful reading and a critical one: the semantic answer is old, and the source-critical observation
is about a genuine discontinuity in the text that the semantic answer explains rather than denies.

**The thread itself.** What begins in Enosh's generation becomes the patriarchs' standing practice,
then a prophetic promise, and finally the New Testament's shortest description of how anyone is
saved. Joel 2:32 — *"whoever will call on Yahweh's name shall be saved"* — is quoted twice in the
New Testament, and both times something is added to it. Peter quotes it at Pentecost (Acts 2:21) to
explain what has just happened to a crowd. Paul quotes it at Romans 10:13, and the surrounding verses
make explicit what he is doing with it: the *Lord* whose name is called on is Jesus (10:9), so a
sentence that in Joel means Yahweh is applied without argument to Christ. The line from Genesis 4:26
to Romans 10:13 is one of the clearest cases in Scripture of a phrase keeping its exact wording
across three thousand years while its referent is being pressed.

## Significance

Genesis 4 has spent twenty-four verses on what human beings can build without God — a city, a
livelihood, an art, a technology, and a song about revenge — and then closes on the one human
activity that is not a construction. The final verse of the chapter is a report that people started
saying a name. It is placed there as the alternative to everything preceding it, and it is the
beginning of a thread that runs to the last books of the Bible.
