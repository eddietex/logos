# A Glutton and a Drunkard - Proverbs 23.20-21, Deuteronomy 21.20, and Matthew 11.19

## Passages involved

- [[Do Not Envy Sinners]] — **Proverbs 23:19–22** — *"Listen, my son, and be wise, and keep your
  heart on the right path! Don't be among ones drinking too much wine, or those who gorge themselves
  on meat: for the drunkard and the glutton shall become poor; and drowsiness clothes them in rags.
  Listen to your father who gave you life, and don't despise your mother when she is old."*
- **Deuteronomy 21:18–21** — *"If a man has a stubborn and rebellious son, who will not obey the
  voice of his father or the voice of his mother, and though they chasten him, will not listen to
  them; then his father and his mother shall take hold of him and bring him out to the elders of his
  city … They shall tell the elders of his city, 'This our son is stubborn and rebellious. He will not
  obey our voice. He is a glutton and a drunkard.'"* (WEB verified at bible-api.com; Deuteronomy not
  yet in `raw/`)
- **Matthew 11:19** — *"The Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, 'Behold, a gluttonous
  man and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!' But wisdom is justified by her
  children."* (WEB verified at bible-api.com)
- **Luke 7:34** — the same saying, with *"you say"* (WEB verified at bible-api.com)

## Analysis

**The same pair of words, in the same setting.** In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*
(read 2026-10-26), Proverbs 23:21 has *sōḇēʾ wəzōlēl*, *drunkard and glutton*, after 23:20's
*sōḇəʾê-yāyin* and *zōləlê ḇāśār*; Deuteronomy 21:20 has *zōlēl wəsōḇēʾ*, the same two participles
in the reverse order. Both texts set the pair inside a household: Proverbs between *"Listen, my son"*
(23:19) and *"Listen to your father … don't despise your mother"* (23:22), Deuteronomy in a son who
*"will not obey the voice of his father or the voice of his mother"* (21:18). The wiki notes the
shared vocabulary and setting; it does not claim that either text was written with the other in view.

**Advice and law.** Proverbs addresses the son before the fact, and its sanction is natural: the
drunkard and the glutton *"shall become poor"*. Deuteronomy addresses the parents after the fact,
when chastening has failed, and its sanction is the city's: the elders hear the case and the men
of the city stone him. The two describe the same character at two stages — the son who might still
listen, and the son who has stopped. Deuteronomy requires both parents to bring the charge and the
elders to hear it, so the father alone does not hold the power of life and death; the law is
discussed when Deuteronomy is ingested.

**The charge turned on Jesus.** Matthew 11:19 and Luke 7:34 report that Jesus's critics called him
*"a gluttonous man and a drunkard"*. The Greek of those verses was not checked in this pass, and
whether the Gospel writers or the critics they quote meant to evoke Deuteronomy 21:20 is a matter of
interpretation; the wiki records the shared pair of English words and the scholarly suggestion that
the charge echoes the rebellious-son law, without asserting it. What the Gospel text itself adds is
the answer: *"But wisdom is justified by her children"* — the saying appeals to wisdom against a
charge that the wisdom literature would have treated as the mark of the foolish son.

## Significance

Proverbs 23:20–21 is the gentlest form of a judgment the canon makes three ways: as a father's
warning, as a law of last resort, and as a slander answered by wisdom. The thread shows how one
pair of words, *glutton and drunkard*, carried the full weight of a son's rejection of his parents.
