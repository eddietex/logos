# An Abomination to the Egyptians — Separate Tables in Genesis and Exodus

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 43:32** — [[The Second Journey]]; *"because the Egyptians don't eat bread with the
  Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians"* — ***tôʿēbâ***, **the word's first
  occurrence in Scripture**
- **Genesis 46:31–34** *(not yet ingested)* — *"every shepherd is an abomination to the Egyptians"*
- **Exodus 8:26** — *"we shall sacrifice the abomination of the Egyptians before their eyes"*
- **Leviticus 18:22–30; Deuteronomy 7:25–26; 14:3** — the word turned round
- **Genesis 39:14, 17; 40:15; 41:12** — *"Hebrew"* as the Egyptians' word for Joseph

## Analysis

### The Bible's first abomination is a dining custom

**[[Joseph]] throws a feast and cannot sit at it.**

> **32** They served him by himself, and them by themselves, and the Egyptians, that ate with him, by
> themselves, because the Egyptians don't eat bread with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to
> the Egyptians. (43:32)

**Three tables in one room.** The governor alone; his Egyptian household apart; eleven Hebrews apart.
**The man at the solitary table belongs to both of the other two and can admit to neither**, which is
the verse's literary work — but its lasting work is the noun.

***Tôʿēbâ*** will become one of the most heavily loaded words in the Hebrew Bible: **the term for what
Yahweh detests**, applied in Leviticus and Deuteronomy to idolatry, sexual transgression, dishonest
weights, child sacrifice, and the practices of the nations Israel dispossesses. **Its first occurrence
in Scripture is none of that.** It is Egyptian table manners, **reported by the narrator in a neutral
explanatory clause, with no suggestion that anybody is at fault.**

**The word enters the Bible meaning *what somebody else won't touch*.**

### "Hebrew" is not a word Israelites use of themselves

**The verse's other noun matters as much.** ***ʿIbrî*** occurs in the Joseph cycle only in Egyptian
mouths or in speech addressed to Egyptians: **[[Potiphar's Wife]]** — *"he has brought a Hebrew in to
us to mock us"* (39:14), *"the Hebrew servant, whom you have brought to us"* (39:17); **Joseph
himself, explaining his situation to the cupbearer** — *"For indeed, I was stolen away out of the land
of the Hebrews"* (40:15); **the cupbearer to Pharaoh** — *"a young man, a Hebrew"* (41:12).

**It is an outsider's category**, and 43:32 is the narrator adopting it for one clause in order to
report an Egyptian point of view. See [[Abram the Hebrew - The First Use of the Name]]. **The family
is being described, in its own book, in the vocabulary of the people who will enslave it.**

### The custom hardens into a policy

**Genesis uses *tôʿēbâ* twice, and the second time [[Joseph]] weaponises it.**

> **34** … that you shall say, 'Your servants have been keepers of livestock from our youth even until
> now, both we, and our fathers:' that you may dwell in the land of Goshen; for every shepherd is an
> abomination to the Egyptians. (46:34)

**He coaches his family to declare the precise occupation that will make Egyptians want them kept at
a distance**, so that Pharaoh will settle them in Goshen — **separate land on the strength of the same
revulsion that produced separate tables.** The prejudice of 43:32 becomes, three chapters later,
**the instrument by which Israel is given a territory of its own inside Egypt.**

**This is the mechanism Exodus depends on.** A family of seventy that assimilates does not come out
four hundred years later as a nation. **Genesis names the thing that keeps them distinct, and it is
not a commandment and not a covenant sign — it is somebody else's distaste.** See [[The Nations]] and
[[Land and Sojourning]].

### Moses turns it into an argument

**Exodus 8:26 is the word's hinge.** Pharaoh offers to let Israel sacrifice inside Egypt, and Moses
refuses on the grounds established in Genesis:

what Israel must sacrifice is, Moses says, *"the abomination of the Egyptians"*, and to do it in their
sight would get the people stoned (Exodus 8:26).

**The genitive has flipped.** In Genesis a *tôʿēbâ* is what Egyptians find repellent **about
Israelites**; here it is a thing Israelites do that Egyptians find repellent, named as such, **and
used as a legal reason why the two peoples cannot share a country.** Moses argues from the table
custom of Genesis 43 to the necessity of the Exodus. **The separateness that began as a seating
arrangement becomes the case for leaving.**

### And then it is turned round

**Leviticus and Deuteronomy take the word away from the Egyptians.** *"You shall not eat any
abominable thing"* (Deuteronomy 14:3), governing Israel's own diet; the sexual prohibitions of
Leviticus 18, which close by saying the land vomited out its previous inhabitants for *tôʿēbôt*;
Deuteronomy 7:25–26 on idols. **Israel acquires its own list**, and the list is presented as God's
rather than a nation's taste.

**The trajectory is the point:**

| | **who is disgusted** | **at what** | **on whose authority** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 43:32** | Egyptians | eating with Hebrews | **custom** — no authority cited |
| **Genesis 46:34** | Egyptians | shepherds | custom, **used by Joseph** |
| **Exodus 8:26** | Egyptians | Israel's sacrifice | custom, **used by Moses** |
| **Leviticus 18; Deuteronomy 14** | **Yahweh** | idolatry, sexual sin, unclean food | **command** |

**Scripture's most severe word for wrongness begins as a description of what one nation finds
distasteful about another, and is taken over.** See [[Clean and Unclean]].

## Significance

**Genesis 43:32 is a throwaway explanatory clause doing two large jobs at once.**

**It isolates [[Joseph]] at the moment of his greatest apparent success.** He has the grain, the
office, the house, the servants, and his whole family under his roof — **and he eats alone, because
the Egyptians will not sit with Hebrews and he cannot say that he is one.** The three tables are the
cost of [[Zaphenath-Paneah - Renaming at a Foreign Court]] rendered as furniture.

**And it plants the fact that makes Exodus possible.** [[Hospitality]] in Genesis has been the book's
reliable virtue — [[Abraham]] under the oaks (18:4–8), [[Laban]] receiving a servant (24:31–32), and
in this same chapter water, washed feet and fodder (43:24). **Here the formula is executed perfectly
and then breaks at the last step.** Genesis does not moralise about it. **It simply notes that these
two peoples cannot share a meal, and four hundred and thirty years later that is the only reason
there is still an Israel to bring out.**
