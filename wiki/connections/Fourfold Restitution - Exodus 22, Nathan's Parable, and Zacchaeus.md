# Fourfold Restitution - Exodus 22, Nathan's Parable, and Zacchaeus

## Passages involved
- **Exodus 22:1** — *"If a man steals an ox or a sheep, and kills it, or sells it; he shall pay five
  oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep"* — [[Laws of Property and Restitution]]
- **Exodus 22:4** — a stolen animal *"found in his hand alive"* is repaid double
- **2 Samuel 12:5–6** — David, hearing Nathan's parable of the rich man who took a poor man's lamb:
  *"As Yahweh lives, the man who has done this deserves to die! He must restore the lamb fourfold,
  because he did this thing, and because he had no pity!"*
- **Luke 19:8** — Zacchaeus: *"Behold, Lord, half of my goods I give to the poor. If I have
  wrongfully exacted anything of anyone, I restore four times as much."*
- **Proverbs 6:30–31** — [[The Lamp of the Commandment]]: *"Men don't despise a thief, if he steals to satisfy himself when he is
  hungry: but if he is found, he shall restore seven times. He shall give all the wealth of his
  house."*

## Analysis

**Exodus 22:1 sets a rate, and two later stories use it.** The ordinance's case is a stolen animal
that has been slaughtered or sold — that is, one that can no longer be returned. For a sheep the
rate is four.

**Nathan's parable fits the ordinance's case closely.** The rich man takes a poor man's lamb and
slaughters it for a guest; it cannot be given back. David's sentence has two parts. *"He must
restore the lamb fourfold"* is the Exodus rate for a sheep. *"The man who has done this deserves to
die"* is not: Exodus 22 prescribes no death for theft. David's own words supply the reason he goes
beyond the law — *"because he had no pity"*. **He sets the legal remedy beside an outrage the law
does not measure.** The parable, of course, is about David, and the case it stands for is not
theft at all.

**Zacchaeus applies the rate to himself, outside any court.** His wrong is *"wrongfully exacted"*,
not stolen livestock, and nobody has charged him. The fourfold figure goes beyond what the Torah
requires for many kinds of wrong. Leviticus 6:1–5 and Numbers 5:6–7, on a voluntarily confessed
wrong, are commonly cited as requiring the principal plus a fifth; **those texts were not fetched
in this pass, so that comparison is recorded as unverified.** What Luke 19:8 does show is a man
reaching for the heaviest figure the theft law gives for a sheep, and adding half his goods to the
poor.

**Proverbs goes higher.** A thief who steals because he is hungry is not despised, yet *"if he is
found, he shall restore seven times"* (6:31). The figure is sevenfold, not the Exodus rate; it may
be a proverbial number for full repayment rather than a legal tariff, and the second line,
*"all the wealth of his house"*, points the same way. **The wiki does not resolve how Proverbs'
figure relates to Exodus 22.** The context, now ingested at [[The Lamp of the Commandment]], is an
argument from lesser to greater: the hungry thief is the excusable case, and even he must pay; the
adulterer of 6:32–35 has taken what no payment returns, and the husband *"won't regard any
ransom"* (6:35). The figure *šiḇʿāṯāyim* is the number of Genesis 4:15, *"vengeance will be taken on
him sevenfold"*; the verb, *yəšallēm*, is Exodus 22's (Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according
to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25).

## Significance

The ordinance gives a figure, and the figure outlives the ordinance. **In both narrative uses the
number appears where no judge is enforcing it**: once as a king's verdict on a fictional case
that convicts the king himself, and once as a tax collector's vow after Jesus comes to his house.
In 2 Samuel the rate is a measure the king cannot escape; in Luke it is a standard a converted man
holds himself to.
