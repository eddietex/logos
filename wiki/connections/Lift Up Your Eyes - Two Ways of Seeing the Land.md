# Lift Up Your Eyes - Two Ways of Seeing the Land

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 13:10–11** — *"Lot lifted up his eyes, and saw all the plain of the Jordan, that it was
  well-watered everywhere … So Lot chose the Plain of the Jordan for himself."*
- **Genesis 13:14–15** — *"Yahweh said to Abram, after Lot was separated from him, 'Now, lift up your
  eyes, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward, for
  all the land which you see, I will give to you, and to your offspring forever.'"*
- **Genesis 3:6** — *"When the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight
  to the eyes … she took some of its fruit, and ate."*
- **Genesis 6:2** — *"God's sons saw that men's daughters were beautiful, and they took any that
  they wanted for themselves as wives."*
- **Deuteronomy 34:1–4** — Moses on Nebo: *"Yahweh showed him all the land … 'I have caused you to
  see it with your eyes, but you shall not go over there.'"*
- **Matthew 4:8–10** — *"the devil took him to an exceedingly high mountain, and showed him all the
  kingdoms of the world and their glory. He said to him, 'I will give you all of these things, if you
  will fall down and worship me.'"*

## Analysis

Genesis 13 puts the same gesture in two mouths four verses apart, and everything hangs on who does
the lifting.

**Lot lifts his own eyes.** Nobody tells him to. What he sees is real and accurate — the *kikkar* of
the Jordan genuinely is the best-watered ground within reach — and what he does with the sight is
choose *for himself* (*wayyibḥar-lô Lôṭ*, with the reflexive pronoun the Hebrew does not need). The
narrator surrounds his look with two pieces of information he does not have: that the plain is *like
the garden of Yahweh, like the land of Egypt* (two places whose distinguishing feature is that people
were expelled from them), and that this is all *before Yahweh destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah*.

**Yahweh lifts Abram's.** *Śāʾ nāʾ ʿênêkā* — the same verb, in the imperative, with the softening
particle *nāʾ*, and with a stated starting point: *from the place where you are*. Abram is not
prospecting; he is being shown. Where Lot's look covers one valley bounded by a road sign (*as you go
to Zoar*), Abram's is given four directions and no boundary at all, and it comes with a grant rather
than a decision: *I will give it to you, and to your offspring forever*. He is then told to walk it —
the legal gesture of taking possession — and he answers by pitching a tent and building an altar.

### The seeing formula in Genesis

The verb *rāʾâ*, to see, followed by an assessment and then a taking, is one of Genesis's most
reliable warning signs.

| | What is seen | The judgment made | What follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 3:6 | the tree | *good for food … a delight to the eyes* | *she took … and ate* |
| Genesis 6:2 | the daughters of men | *beautiful* | *they took … wives* |
| Genesis 13:10 | the plain of the Jordan | *well-watered everywhere* | *Lot chose … for himself* |

In none of the three is the perception wrong. The fruit was good, the women were beautiful, the plain
was well-watered. The pattern is not about mistaken sight but about sight as a sufficient basis for
taking, and Genesis 13:14 answers it not by forbidding the look but by supplying someone else to
initiate it.

### Moses on Nebo

Deuteronomy 34:1–4 is the closest verbal parallel in the Torah to Genesis 13:14–15, and it inverts
the outcome. Moses is taken up a mountain and shown the land in every direction, and God says the
words that make the scene unbearable: *"This is the land which I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to
Jacob, saying, 'I will give it to your offspring.' I have caused you to see it with your eyes, but
you shall not go over there."* The promise Abram receives while looking is the promise Moses is
denied while looking, and the text names Abram as its original recipient in the same breath. Both men
die without holding the land; Acts 7:5 and Hebrews 11:9–13 make that the point rather than the
problem.

### The mountain and the offer

Matthew 4:8–10 stages the scene once more with the terms reversed. A high place, a panorama of
territory, and an offer — *"I will give you all of these things"* — from someone with no right to
make it, on a condition. Where Genesis 13:14 has the giver of the land initiating the look and
attaching no condition, and Genesis 13:10 has a man looking on his own and taking what he sees, the
temptation combines the worst of both: an unauthorized look and an unauthorized giver. The answer
Jesus gives is a quotation from Deuteronomy (6:13), the book that ends with Moses on the mountain
being shown a land and told he may not have it.

## Significance

- **It is the interpretive key to Genesis 13.** The chapter has almost no action — two households
  separate — and it is organized entirely around who looks, on whose initiative, at how much, and
  with what result. The wordplay reaches back one verse further than the two lookings: the land could
  not *bear* them (*nāśāʾ*, 13:6), so each man *lifts* (*nāśāʾ*) his eyes.
- **It refuses the easy moral.** Genesis never says Lot sinned by looking, and Yahweh commands Abram
  to do the identical thing. What separates them is not the act but its authorization and its
  horizon — one valley chosen, or a whole land given.
- **Genesis 28:13 does it a third way: with the eyes shut.** Abram is told to lift his eyes and look
  in four directions, and then to walk the land in its length and its width; Lot lifts his eyes and
  picks a valley. **Jacob is given the land lying down, unconscious, and at night** — *"the land
  whereon you lie, to you will I give it"* — and the same speech supplies the four directions from
  the other side, as the promise that he will *spread abroad* to them. **The two ways of seeing
  become three, and the third involves no seeing at all.** See [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]].
- **It sets up the New Testament's reading of Abraham.** Hebrews 11:9–10 has him dwelling in tents in
  the land of promise *"as in a land not his own"* because *"he looked for the city which has
  foundations"*. The epistle keeps the verb and moves the object: the man who was told to lift his
  eyes over a country is described as having looked past it.
