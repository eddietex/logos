# Where Can I Go from Your Spirit - Job 23.8-10 and Psalm 139.7-12

  that I take. When he has tried me, I shall come out like gold."* —
  [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]]
- **Job 23:3** — *"Oh that I knew where I might find him! That I might come even to his seat!"* —

- **Job 13:24** — *"Why hide you your face, and hold me for your enemy?"* — [[Though He Slay Me]]
- **Psalm 139:1–12** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Yahweh, you have searched me, and you know me … Where

- **Jeremiah 23:23–24** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Am I a God at hand … and not a God afar off? Can

- **Isaiah 45:15** *(not yet ingested)* — *"Most certainly you are a God who has hidden yourself,
  God of Israel, the Savior."*

## Analysis

**The same map, walked in opposite directions.** Psalm 139 and Job 23 both take a man to every
point of the compass — the psalm vertically (heaven, Sheol) and to the horizon (*"the wings of the
dawn"*, *"the uttermost parts of the sea"*), Job around the four quarters of his own body: ***qedem***
before him, ***ʾāḥôr*** behind, ***śəmōʾl*** to the left, ***yāmîn*** to the right. In the psalm the
point of the journey is that God is at every stop; the speaker could not get away if he tried. In Job
the point is that God is at none of them; the speaker could not get there if he tried.

**And both end in the same sentence.** The psalm's last turn is *"Search me, God, and know my
heart. Try me (bəḥānēnî), and know my thoughts"* (139:23). Job's is *"he knows the way that I take.
When he has tried me (bəḥānanî), I shall come out like gold"* (23:10). The verb is the same —
***bāḥan***, to assay — and so is the claim: **God knows me, and testing will show what I am.** The
psalmist asks for the test; Job is already in it. The psalmist's God is present and therefore knows
him; Job's God is absent and knows him anyway.

**Job has lived both halves.** In chapter 7 Job's complaint was Psalm 139's premise turned to
torment: a God who visits *"every morning"*, tests *"every moment"*, and will not look away long
enough for Job to swallow his spit (7:18–19) — *"you watcher of men"* (7:20). Chapter 10 used the
psalm's other theme, the body knit in the womb, as an accusation (10:8–12; see
[[You Have Fashioned Me as Clay - Job 10.8-12, Psalm 139, and the Potter]]). Chapter 23 is the
inversion's inversion: now God cannot be found. The book does not resolve the two into one another.
**The God who is everywhere watching is nowhere available to be addressed**, and Job suffers from
both at once.

**Amos and Jeremiah give the psalm's theme to the guilty.** For them the inescapable God is a threat:
there is nowhere for the sinner to hide (Amos 9:2–4; Jeremiah 23:24). That is the version Job's
friends would recognize, and it is the version 24:13–17 sets against the criminals who say *"No eye
shall see me"* (24:15) while *"His eyes are on their ways"* (24:23). Job's complaint is not that God
does not see; it is that seeing and answering have come apart.

## Significance

- Job 23:8–10 and Psalm 139 are the two classic texts of divine omnipresence in the Hebrew Bible,
  and they describe it from opposite sides — as a presence that cannot be escaped and as a presence
  that cannot be found. Read together they show that the theological claim (God is everywhere) does
  not settle the experiential question (where is he?). See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].
- The shared *bāḥan* makes the parallel more than thematic: both texts end in a man submitting
  himself, willingly or not, to God's assay. See
  [[I Shall Come Out Like Gold - Job 23.10 and the Refiner's Fire]] and [[Testing]].
- Isaiah 45:15's *"a God who has hidden yourself … the Savior"* holds the two together in one
  line, and is the nearest the Hebrew Bible comes to Job's position stated as confession rather than
  complaint.
