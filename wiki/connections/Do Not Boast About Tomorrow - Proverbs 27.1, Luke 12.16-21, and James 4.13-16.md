## Analysis

**The proverb forbids the boast, not the plan.** Proverbs 27:1's verb is *ʾal-tiṯhallēl*, *do not praise
yourself*, and its reason is ignorance: *mah-yyēleḏ yôm*, *what a day will give birth to* (Hebrew from
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-26). Proverbs elsewhere commends planning: the
heart plans and Yahweh directs (16:1, 9), and the same chapter ends by telling the herdsman to know his
flocks *because* stored wealth does not last (27:23–24). What is ruled out is speaking of tomorrow as
though it were already held.

**James applies the proverb to the merchant's itinerary.** His trader names a city, a year, and a
profit, and James's objection is the proverb's: *"you don't know what your life will be like
tomorrow"*. James supplies what the proverb leaves unspoken, the conditional *"If the Lord wills"*, and
names the fault in the proverb's own terms: *"you glory in your boasting. All such boasting is evil."*
James does not cite Proverbs, and no claim is made here about his Greek wording or about the Septuagint
of Proverbs 27:1, neither of which was checked; the parallel is in the thought and the shared object,
tomorrow.

**Jesus' parable narrates the case.** The rich man of Luke 12 does what Proverbs 27:1 forbids: he talks
to his own soul about *"many years"*. God's reply is one night. The parable's last line — *"So is he who
lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God"* (12:21) — moves from the uncertainty of time
to the use of wealth, the same move Proverbs 27 makes between its first verse and its last five.
Matthew 6:34 turns the same uncertainty from boast to anxiety: tomorrow is not to be counted on, and not
to be feared either.

**In Proverbs, God is not named in 27:1.** The verse states the limit without saying who sets it. 16:1
and 16:9 had said it is Yahweh who directs the steps; James names *the Lord*. The New Testament texts
make explicit what the proverb, in a chapter without the divine name, leaves to the reader.
