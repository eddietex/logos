# Sheep Without a Shepherd - Numbers 27.17, 1 Kings 22.17, and the Gospels

## Passages involved

- [[Joshua Commissioned]] — Numbers 27:17: *"that the congregation of Yahweh may not be as sheep
  which have no shepherd"* (stored WEB).
- **1 Kings 22:17** — *"I saw all Israel scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no
  shepherd. Yahweh said, 'These have no master. Let them each return to his house in peace.'"*
  *(verified WEB, not yet ingested)*.
- **Matthew 9:36** — *"he was moved with compassion for them, because they were harassed and
  scattered, like sheep without a shepherd"* *(verified WEB, not yet ingested)*.
- **Mark 6:34** — *"he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd,
  and he began to teach them many things"* *(verified WEB, not yet ingested)*.

## Analysis

**Moses coins the image as a reason for succession.** In Numbers 27:16–17 the danger is not
enemies or famine but an absence: a congregation with no one to go out and come in before it. The
remedy is a named man, Joshua, appointed by Yahweh and installed in public.

**Micaiah's vision reverses the request.** In 1 Kings 22:17 the scattered flock is what Israel
looks like after its king falls. The verse does not cite Numbers, but it uses the same picture for
the same failure — a people left without the one who leads it out and back. Moses asks that
Israel *not* become such a flock; Micaiah sees that it will.

**The Gospels apply it to crowds.** Matthew and Mark use the phrase in narrator summaries of
Jesus' compassion. Matthew links it to the people being *harassed and scattered*; Mark links it
directly to teaching. Neither verse quotes Numbers by name, and this page does not claim either
evangelist intends a Joshua–Jesus typology. The shared name of Joshua and Jesus is tracked
separately at [[Joshua and Jesus - One Name]]; the connection there rests on the name, not on this
phrase.

**What is not claimed.** Whether the Gospel wording depends on Numbers, on 1 Kings, or on a
common idiom is not settled by the verses themselves; Ezekiel 34 and Zechariah also use shepherd
language and are not yet ingested or checked for this page. The Greek and Hebrew wording of the
later verses was not compared in this pass.

## Significance

The phrase gives the wiki a single thread for leadership seen from below: what a people looks like
without a shepherd. Its first use is a prayer, answered with an appointment. Later uses are
observations — of a defeat, and of crowds — and the response in Mark is teaching. Further shepherd
texts belong here as they are ingested. See also
[[The God Who Has Fed Me - The First Time God Is a Shepherd]], where God is the shepherd of one
man rather than leader of a congregation.
