## Analysis

### The firstling's blood — Deuteronomy 15:23

A firstling with a defect is not sacrificed but eaten at home, and the rule follows it there: *"Only
you shall not eat its blood. You shall pour it out on the ground like water"* (15:23), almost word for
word 12:16 (WLC, morphhb). See [[Consecrate the Firstborn of the Herd and Flock]]. Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 15:21–23.

### Meat at home, blood on the ground — Deuteronomy 12

Deuteronomy 12 lets domestic animals be killed and eaten in the towns, but keeps the limit:
*"you shall not eat the blood. You shall pour it out on the earth like water"* (16, 24), *"for the
blood is the life"* (23). The hunter's procedure of Leviticus 17:13 is in effect extended to
home slaughter, without the covering with dust; sacrificial blood still goes on the altar at the
chosen place (27). See
[[One Place or Every Place - Exodus 20.24, Leviticus 17, and Deuteronomy 12]]. Source: stored WEB
Deuteronomy 12:15–27; Leviticus 17:13.

### The rationale made explicit — Leviticus 17

Leviticus 17:11 explicitly connects blood with life, as Genesis 9:4 does, and adds
God’s gift of blood on the altar for atonement. Verses 10–14 include foreigners and
give hunters a distinct procedure: pour out the blood and cover it with dust.
This is not an altar rite. Thus reserved from food does not mean every animal’s blood
must reach the sanctuary. Sources: stored WEB Genesis 9:3–4; Leviticus 17:10–14.


Genesis 9:3 permits animal food; 9:4 excludes flesh with its life, identified there as its
blood. Leviticus 3 places a restriction on blood within instructions for a particular
sacrifice: the priests sprinkle blood around the altar, and the concluding statute forbids
eating blood or fat in all dwellings through the generations.

The parallel is the limit on consumption, not an identical historical setting or procedure.
Genesis addresses Noah and his sons after the flood and does not prescribe this priestly
altar rite. Leviticus addresses Israel through the continuing speech to Moses (1:1–2) and
also reserves the named fat portions. Its chapter 3 does not repeat Genesis 9:4's explanation
in terms of life. Leviticus 7:23–27 further specifies the fat of cattle, sheep, and goats,
and the blood of birds and animals.

### The prohibition developed — Leviticus 7:22–38

Verse 24 permits other use of fat from animals that die or are torn but forbids eating
it. Thus the restriction is not confined to fat from animals actually sacrificed.
Verses 25 and 27 add cutting off as a sanction; verse 26 explicitly includes bird and
animal blood in all dwellings. The text gives no procedure for implementing that sanction.
The blood prohibition and the permission for priests to receive breast and thigh (31–34)
are complementary limits, not a ban on every animal product.

## Significance

Food permission is bounded in both passages. In Leviticus the same blood excluded from
ordinary eating has a prescribed altar use. This comparison neither makes Noah an Aaronic
priest nor imports every later sacrificial rule into Genesis.
