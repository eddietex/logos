# He Takes the Wise in Their Craftiness - Job 5.13 in 1 Corinthians 3.19

## Passages involved

- **Job 5:12–13** — Eliphaz, in his first speech: *"He frustrates the plans of the crafty, so that
  their hands can't perform their enterprise. He takes the wise in their own craftiness; the counsel
  of the cunning is carried headlong."* — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]
- **1 Corinthians 3:18–20** — *"Let no one deceive himself. If anyone thinks that he is wise among
  you in this world, let him become a fool, that he may become wise. For the wisdom of this world is
  foolishness with God. For it is written, 'He has taken the wise in their craftiness.' And again,
  'The Lord knows the reasoning of the wise, that it is worthless.'"*
- **Psalm 94:11** — *"Yahweh knows the thoughts of man, that they are futile"*, Paul's second
  citation, paired with Job.
- **Genesis 3:1** — the serpent, *ʿārûm*, *"more subtle than any animal of the field"*: the same root
  as Job 5:12's *crafty* and 5:13's *craftiness* — [[The Fall]]
- **Job 42:7** — *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has"*
- **Romans 11:35** — the other New Testament quotation of Job, from Job 41:11: *"Or who has first
  given to him, and it will be repaid to him again?"*

## Analysis

**Paul introduces it with *"it is written"* (*gegraptai*), his standard formula for Scripture.** He
does not hedge it, attribute it, or distinguish it from the Psalm he cites next. A sentence spoken
by Eliphaz — the one character God rebukes by name — is quoted as authority in a letter about the
foolishness of human wisdom.

**The wording is Paul's own, not the Septuagint's.** The Greek Old Testament renders Job 5:13 as *ho
katalambanōn sophous en tē phronēsei*, *who catches the wise in their prudence*. Paul writes *ho
drassomenos tous sophous en tē panourgia autōn*, *who grasps the wise in their craftiness* — a
different verb (*drassomai*, to seize with the hand, found nowhere else in the New Testament) and a
different noun. *Panourgia*, *craftiness* or *cunning*, is the closer match to the Hebrew
*ʿormâ*, and it is the word Paul uses of the serpent at 2 Corinthians 11:3 (*"as the serpent
deceived Eve in his craftiness"*). Either Paul translated from the Hebrew himself or he used a Greek
version closer to it than the one that survives. Romans 11:35, the other quotation of Job, also
departs from the Septuagint toward the Hebrew. In both cases Paul seems to be reading Job directly.

**The Hebrew root links the verse to the garden.** *ʿārûm*, the adjective of Job 5:12 (*the crafty*)
and the root of 5:13's *ʿormâ*, is the word that introduces the serpent at Genesis 3:1. Proverbs
uses the same word positively of the *prudent* (Proverbs 12:16, 23; 14:8), so it is not inherently
pejorative, but in Job 5 it is the wisdom that outwits itself. Paul's *panourgia* lands on the
same pairing.

**Why the citation is less strange than it looks, and why it is still worth flagging.** Three
observations.

1. **The proposition is true.** That God overturns the schemes of the clever is a commonplace of
   the Old Testament (Psalm 33:10; Isaiah 29:14, which Paul has already cited at 1 Corinthians
   1:19). Eliphaz is not wrong about it.
2. **The context of the citation is the right one.** Paul uses the verse against a church dividing
   itself by the wisdom of its teachers — *"I follow Paul", "I follow Apollos"* — and his point is
   that cleverness is not a qualification before God. That is an application Eliphaz's sentence
   supports.
3. **The context of the original is not.** Eliphaz said it to a man in ashes who had not claimed
   to be wise, in support of a diagnosis God had already ruled out. **And the verse turns against its
   speaker.** If God takes the wise in their craftiness, the clearest instance in the book of Job is
   Eliphaz himself, whose wisdom — observation, vision, and consensus — produces a confident,
   orthodox, and false verdict, and who is caught by it at 42:7.

The wiki's standing position, stated on [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] and
[[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]], is that **God's verdict on the friends at 42:7 is a verdict on
what their arguments made of God, not on every sentence in them.** Paul's citation is the clearest
evidence for that reading: an apostle treats a friend's sentence as Scripture. The usual
explanations — that a true proposition stays true in a wrong mouth, and that inspiration attaches
to the book's report rather than to the speaker — are both reasonable, and neither is stated in
either text.

## Significance

- It is the only New Testament quotation of Job drawn from the speeches the book rebukes. Romans
  11:35 quotes God's own speech from the whirlwind.
- It shows how early Christian reading handled Job: as a book whose propositions could be lifted
  and used, without attention to who spoke them. Whether that is a reading strategy to imitate or
  to be careful of is exactly the question Job's friends raise.
- It is an irony the book of Job does not need Paul to make, but Paul sharpens it: the verse about
  the wise caught in their own craftiness is spoken by the man the book catches.
