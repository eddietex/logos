# Washing the Hands of Innocent Blood - Deuteronomy 21, Psalms 26 and 73, and Matthew 27

## Passages involved

- [[An Unsolved Killing and the Broken-Necked Heifer]] — Deuteronomy 21:6–8; the elders of the nearest
  town *"wash their hands over the heifer"* and say, *"Our hands have not shed this blood, neither have our
  eyes seen it."*
- Psalm 26:6 — *"I will wash my hands in innocence, so I will go about your altar, Yahweh"*. *(WEB,
  bible-api.com; not yet ingested)*
- Psalm 73:13 — *"Surely I have cleansed my heart in vain, and washed my hands in innocence"*. *(same)*
- Matthew 27:24–25 — Pilate *"took water, and washed his hands before the multitude, saying, 'I am
  innocent of the blood of this righteous person. You see to it.'"* The people answer, *"May his blood be
  on us, and on our children!"* *(same)*
- [[Refuge for the Unwitting Killer]] — Deuteronomy 19:10, 13; innocent blood that leaves *"blood guilt
  on you"*, and must be purged.

## Analysis

**In Deuteronomy the washing goes with a sworn statement.** The elders do two things over the dead
heifer: they wash their hands, and they speak. The words say what the washing means — *"Our hands have
not shed this blood"* (21:7) — and add what the gesture alone could not, *"neither have our eyes seen
it"*. The rite is not a cleansing from guilt the town has; it is a public denial that it has any. It is
followed by a prayer for the whole people, because the denial does not remove the blood. Only Yahweh's
*kipper* does (21:8).

**In the Psalms the gesture has become a figure of speech.** Psalm 26:6 uses it of the worshipper's fitness
to approach the altar: *"I will wash my hands in innocence"*. Psalm 73:13 uses the same words with bitter
irony: the psalmist has kept his hands clean *"in vain"*, since the wicked prosper anyway. Neither psalm
mentions blood or a killing. Whether they draw on the rite of Deuteronomy 21 or on a wider idiom of clean
hands is not stated in either text. The Hebrew of the psalms was not checked here.

**In Matthew the gesture is made by the wrong man and fails.** Pilate washes his hands and, like the
elders, declares himself innocent of *"the blood"*. He is the one person in the scene with the power to
prevent the death, which the elders of Deuteronomy 21 never had. The crowd then accepts the blood on
itself, the reverse of the elders' prayer *"don't allow innocent blood among your people Israel"*. Matthew
does not cite Deuteronomy, and whether he intends the allusion is debated; some readers take the scene to
draw on the Deuteronomic rite, others on a gesture of innocence known in the wider world. The Greek of
Matthew 27 was not checked here.

## Significance

The three texts trace one gesture across three settings: a legal rite for blood with no known shedder, a
worshipper's claim of integrity, and a judge's attempt to disown a death he authorizes. Deuteronomy is
the only one of the three in which the gesture is commanded, and the only one in which it is followed by a
petition, because Deuteronomy does not suppose that clean hands settle the matter of the blood. The wiki
records the Matthew allusion as possible, not established.
