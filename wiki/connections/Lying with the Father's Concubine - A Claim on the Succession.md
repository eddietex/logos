# Lying with the Father's Concubine - A Claim on the Succession

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 35:22** — *"While Israel lived in that land, Reuben went and lay with Bilhah, his father's
  concubine, and Israel heard of it. Now the sons of Jacob were twelve."* See
  [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]].
- **Genesis 49:3–4** — the deathbed verdict, fourteen chapters later: *"Reuben, you are my firstborn …
  Boiling over as water, you shall not excel; because you went up to your father's bed, then defiled
  it."*
- **1 Chronicles 5:1–2** — the redistribution stated flatly: *"he was the firstborn; but, because he
  defiled his father's couch, his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph."*
- **2 Samuel 3:7–8** — Abner and **Rizpah**, Saul's concubine, and Ish-bosheth's accusation: *"Why
  have you gone in to my father's concubine?"*
- **2 Samuel 16:21–22** — Ahithophel's counsel to **Absalom**: *"Go in to your father's concubines …
  then the hands of all who are with you will be strong"*, performed *"on the roof, in the sight of
  all Israel."*
- **2 Samuel 12:11–12** — Nathan's oracle, which that roof fulfils: *"I will take your wives before
  your eyes, and give them to your neighbour … before all Israel, and before the sun."*
- **1 Kings 2:13–25** — **Adonijah** asks for Abishag, and Solomon reads the request as a coup: *"Ask
  for him the kingdom also"*, and has him killed the same day.
- **Leviticus 18:8; 20:11; Deuteronomy 22:30; 27:20** — the law's treatment of the same act, in a
  different register entirely.
- **1 Corinthians 5:1** — Paul on *"a man has his father's wife"*, and what the assembly is to do
  about it.

## Analysis

### Four men, one act, and it is always about who is in charge

**Taking a ruler's woman is how the Hebrew Bible's politics says *I am taking his place*.** The act is
public, or made public, and in three of the four cases the text has someone state the political
meaning out loud.

| who | whose woman | what it is read as | what it costs him |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reuben** (Gen 35:22) | Bilhah, Jacob's concubine | never stated at the time | the birthright (49:4; 1 Chr 5:1) |
| **Abner** (2 Sam 3:7) | Rizpah, Saul's concubine | Ish-bosheth's accusation is the whole point of the scene | his loyalty to the house of Saul, and then his life |
| **Absalom** (2 Sam 16:22) | ten of David's concubines | Ahithophel says it plainly: *the hands of all who are with you will be strong* | nothing yet; the coup fails later |
| **Adonijah** (1 Kgs 2:22) | Abishag, David's nurse — *and he never touches her* | Solomon: *"ask for him the kingdom also"* | his life, that day |

**Adonijah's case is the proof that the act is about succession and not about sex.** He does nothing;
he asks, politely, through Solomon's own mother, for a woman the text has already told us *"the king
didn't know her"* (1 Kings 1:4). **Solomon executes him for the request.** The claim is made by
proximity to the dead king's household, and everyone in the room understands it immediately.

**Absalom's is the proof that it must be seen.** Ahithophel's advice is not to sleep with the
concubines but to do it *"in the sight of all Israel"*, on a tent pitched on the palace roof — **the
same roof David was walking on when he saw Bathsheba** (2 Samuel 11:2). The point is publicity; the
purpose is to make the breach irreparable so that Absalom's supporters cannot be bought back.

### Which makes Reuben's verse the odd one out, and the wiki says so

**Genesis supplies none of the apparatus.** No witness, no counsellor, no stated motive, no
declaration, no scene — one sentence, and *"Israel heard of it."* The political reading is imported
from the three later cases; **Genesis 35:22 does not offer it.**

**What Genesis does offer is the consequence, and it takes fourteen chapters to arrive.** At 49:3–4
Jacob finally speaks, and **the charge he brings is not usurpation but defilement**: *"you went up to
your father's bed, then defiled it"* — *ḥillaltā*, the verb of profaning something holy. 1 Chronicles
5:1 repeats that word and adds the outcome: the birthright to Joseph's sons. **The succession is
indeed what he loses**, but neither text says that seizing it was what he was after.

**Three readings, and the wiki adopts none.**

1. **A bid for the succession**, on the pattern the later books make explicit — the firstborn asserting
   that the aging patriarch's household is now his. **The strength of this reading is that it explains
   why the loss is specifically the birthright**; its weakness is that it is entirely supplied from
   outside the verse.
2. **An act on [[Rachel]]'s account.** Bilhah is Rachel's slave, and Rachel has been dead for three
   verses. On this reading — old, and found in Jewish commentary — [[Reuben]] is his mother's son
   defending [[Leah]]'s standing against the loved wife's household now that the loved wife is gone.
   It has the merit of explaining the placement: **the verse sits immediately after Rachel's burial
   and nowhere else.**
3. **The narrator recording an offence without a motive**, exactly as it recorded [[Rachel]]'s theft
   of the *teraphim* (31:19) and [[Ham]]'s act in the tent (9:22) without one. **This is the wiki's
   own default reading of Genesis's habit** — see [[The Teraphim - Household Gods in Genesis and After]]
   and [[Naked and Ashamed - Genesis 3 and Genesis 9]] — and it declines to supply the missing clause.

### The sentence nobody finishes

**The Masoretic Text will not complete the verse.** A *piska bĕʾemṣaʿ pasuq* — a section break in the
middle of a verse, one of very few in the Hebrew Bible — falls after *"and Israel heard of it"*, and
the next clause is the unrelated *"Now the sons of Jacob were twelve."* **The Septuagint fills the
gap**: *καὶ πονηρὸν ἐφάνη ἐναντίον αὐτοῦ*, *and it appeared evil before him*.

**The wiki records the Greek as an ancient reading of the break and not as a recovered Hebrew
clause.** What the Hebrew does is what it did at 34:5, where *"Jacob heard … and held his peace"*:
**twice in two chapters it tells us what this man heard and refuses to tell us what he thought.** See
[[Jacob]] and [[Language and Speech]].

### The law's register, which is a different one

**Leviticus and Deuteronomy treat the act as sexual and cultic, not political.** *"You shall not
uncover the nakedness of your father's wife: it is your father's nakedness"* (Leviticus 18:8); *"both
of them shall surely be put to death"* (20:11); *"Cursed is he who lies with his father's wife"*
(Deuteronomy 27:20), one of the twelve curses recited from Mount Ebal.

**Two things follow.** First, **the death penalty the law prescribes is not applied to Reuben** — the
patriarchs precede Sinai, and the wiki has tracked this gap since Jacob's marriage to two sisters
(Leviticus 18:18) and Abraham's to his half-sister (Leviticus 18:9). Second, **the law's framing is
about the father's honour** — *"it is your father's nakedness"* — which is closer to Jacob's *defiled*
at 49:4 than to Ahithophel's calculation.

**Paul inherits the Levitical framing intact.** *"It is actually reported that there is sexual
immorality among you… that one has his father's wife"* (1 Corinthians 5:1) — the same phrase, the same
offence, and a church told to act on it because *"even the Gentiles don't do this."*

## Significance

- **The Bible's first firstborn to be disinherited loses it for an act the book will not explain**,
  and the three later men who do the same thing are given counsellors, audiences, and stated motives.
  **Genesis is the sparest telling of the pattern and the founding one.**
- **The act is legible to everyone in the later narratives without a word of explanation**, which is
  the strongest evidence that it carried a fixed public meaning: Ish-bosheth accuses, Ahithophel
  advises, Solomon executes, and none of the three has to say why the act means what it means.
- **And it is the mechanism by which the twelve tribes get their actual shape.** Reuben's verse is why
  the double portion goes to [[Joseph]]'s two sons, which is why there are thirteen tribes and twelve
  allotments, and why Ephraim and Manasseh are counted separately from Genesis 48 onward. **One
  sentence with no scene attached to it redraws the map of Israel.** See
  [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] and
  [[The Twelve Sons and the Lists That Reorder Them]].
