# Eaten or Burned - The Sin Offering in Leviticus 6, 9, and 10

## Passages involved

- [[The Priests’ Portions of Sin and Trespass Offerings]] — Leviticus 6:24–30; the rule.
- [[The Eighth Day and the Appearing Glory]] — Leviticus 9:8–15; Aaron's calf and the
  people's goat.
- [[The Uneaten Sin Offering]] — Leviticus 10:16–20; Moses' complaint and Aaron's answer.
- [[Aaron and His Sons Consecrated]] — Leviticus 8:14–17; the ordination bull burned outside
  the camp.
- [[Outside the Camp - The Sin Offering and Hebrews 13]] — the wider disposal pattern.

## Analysis

**The rule.** Leviticus 6:26 and 6:29 give sin-offering flesh to the officiating priest and
the male priests, eaten in the court of the Tent of Meeting. Leviticus 6:30 excepts any sin
offering whose blood "is brought into the Tent of Meeting to make atonement in the Holy
Place": it "shall be burned with fire". Blood destination decides between eating and burning.

**Moses applies the rule exactly.** In Leviticus 10:18 he reasons, "Behold, its blood was not
brought into the inner part of the sanctuary: you certainly should have eaten it in the
sanctuary, as I commanded." The people's goat had its blood handled at the altar, so under
6:30 it was food for the priests. Verse 17 adds that the offering was given to them "to bear
the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them before Yahweh".

**Chapter 9 complicates the picture.** Aaron's own calf had its blood put on the altar's horns
and poured at its base (9:9), not brought inside, yet "the meat and the skin he burned with fire
outside the camp" (9:11). That matches the priests' ordination bull (Exodus 29:14;
Leviticus 8:17) rather than the 6:30 test. The goat was then offered "for sin, like the first"
(9:15). If "like the first" includes the burning, the priests followed the calf's pattern; if
it covers only the slaughter and blood, Moses' complaint stands on 6:30 alone. **The text does
not say which,** and Moses' reaction shows he expected the goat to be eaten. Both readings are
recorded here; neither is adopted.

A distinction between the cases is available in the text without resolving that question: the
calf was the priest's own offering (9:8), the goat the people's (9:15). The priest eating an
offering made for his own sin would be a different case from his eating one given him for the
congregation (10:17). Leviticus does not state that principle in these chapters, so it remains
an inference.

**Aaron's answer concedes the rule and appeals beyond it.** He does not claim that the goat
should have been burned. He asks whether, after "such things as these have happened to me",
eating would have been pleasing in Yahweh's sight (10:19), and Moses accepts it (10:20). The
exchange shows the rule being applied by Moses and then set aside in one circumstance, on
Moses' authority; no divine ruling on the case is reported.

## Significance

The sin offering is not one procedure. Blood that enters the tent, the priest's own offering,
and the people's offering at the outer altar are disposed of differently, and Leviticus 10
shows a priest held to the difference. It also shows that the distinction could be questioned
in a day of judgment and grief, and that Moses' acceptance of Aaron's answer is part of the
record rather than a contradiction to be explained away.
