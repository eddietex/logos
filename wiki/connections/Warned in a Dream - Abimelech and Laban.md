# Warned in a Dream - Abimelech and Laban

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 20:3–18** — *"God came to Abimelech in a dream of the night, and said to him, 'Behold,
  you are a dead man'"* — [[Abraham and Abimelech]]
- **Genesis 31:24, 29** — *"God came to Laban, the Syrian, in a dream of the night, and said to him,
  'Be careful that you don't speak to Jacob either good or bad'"* — [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]]
- **Genesis 12:17** — the earlier and wordless version: Yahweh plagues Pharaoh's house, with no
  warning and no dream — [[Abram in Egypt]]
- **Job 33:14–18** — Elihu's general rule: God speaks *"In a dream, in a vision of the night"* to
  *"withdraw man from his purpose, and hide pride from man. He keeps back his soul from the pit"* —
  [[Elihu Speaks in Anger]]
- **Genesis 26:8–11** — Abimelech of Gerar works it out by looking out of a window, with no dream at
  all — [[Isaac in Gerar]]

## Analysis

**Twice in Genesis God speaks directly to a man outside the covenant line, at night, in a dream, to
stop him doing something to this family — and the two scenes are built the same way.**

| | Abimelech, Genesis 20 | Laban, Genesis 31 |
|---|---|---|
| **the opening** | *"God came to Abimelech in a dream of the night"* (20:3) | *"God came to Laban, the Syrian, in a dream of the night"* (31:24) |
| **the words** | *"Behold, you are a dead man, because of the woman"* | *"Be careful that you don't speak to Jacob either good or bad"* |
| **what he was about to do** | take Sarah | take back his daughters, his flocks, and his gods |
| **compliance** | immediate — *"Abimelech rose early in the morning"* (20:8) | immediate — he says so himself (31:29) |
| **the complaint afterwards** | *"What have you done to us? … you have done deeds to me that ought not to be done"* (20:9) | *"What have you done, that you have deceived me?"* (31:26) |
| **the patriarch's defence** | *"because I thought, surely the fear of God is not in this place"* (20:11) | *"because I was afraid… lest you should take your daughters from me by force"* (31:31) |
| **how it ends** | a gift of livestock, slaves and silver, and a covenant (20:14–16; 21:27) | a treaty, a boundary, a sacrifice and a meal (31:44–54) |

**The Hebrew of the two openings is almost word for word** — ***wayyābōʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾel-ʾăbîmelek
baḥălôm hallaylâ*** against ***wayyābōʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾel-lābān hāʾărammî baḥălōm hallaylâ*** — **and the
formula occurs nowhere else in the Bible.**

**Both men are given the same three-part shape**: a warning they did not ask for, obedience without
argument, and then a grievance delivered to the patriarch's face **which the narrator lets stand
unanswered.** Abimelech asks *"what have you done to us?"* and Abraham's reply is a partial truth
about a half-sister; Laban asks the same question and Jacob answers only the first half of it, since
he does not know about the *teraphim*. **In both scenes the outsider has the better argument and the
patriarch has the protection.**

**One asymmetry is worth stating.** Abimelech is **innocent** — *"in the integrity of my heart and the
innocence of my hands I have done this"* (20:5), and God agrees: *"Yes, I know that in the integrity
of your heart you have done this, and I also withheld you from sinning against me"* (20:6). **Laban is
not innocent and God does not say he is.** He is told to keep his mouth shut, not told he has been
kept from sin, **and he admits in his next breath that he had the power and the intention to do
harm** (31:29). **The same instrument is used on a man who was about to do wrong unknowingly and on a
man who was about to do wrong knowingly.**

## Significance

**Genesis has a graded set of ways God protects this family from outsiders, and the dream is the
middle one.**

- **[[Abram in Egypt]] (12:17)** — no warning at all. Yahweh *"plagued Pharaoh and his house with
  great plagues"*, and Pharaoh works out the cause by unstated means. **Force, no speech.**
- **Genesis 20 and 31** — **a dream, in words, before anything happens.** The outsider is told, and
  complies.
- **[[Isaac in Gerar]] (26:8–11)** — **nothing supernatural whatever.** Abimelech looks out of a
  window, sees Isaac with Rebekah, draws the obvious conclusion, and issues a death penalty against
  anyone who touches them. **The protection comes from a foreign king's own decency.**

**Read across the four, the book is doing something consistent**: the danger to the promise keeps
coming from inside the family's own conduct, and **the correction keeps coming through people outside
the covenant.** See [[The Nations]], [[The Fear of God]], and [[Sinning Unwittingly - Integrity, Ignorance, and Guilt]].

**And both dreams end the same way — in a treaty.** Abimelech and Abraham dig a well and swear at
[[Beersheba]] (21:22–34); Laban and Jacob pile stones and swear at [[Galeed]]. **In Genesis, when God
warns a foreigner off this family, the next thing that happens is a border being agreed**, and both
borders are still standing when the book ends.

**Job 33 turns the two scenes into a doctrine.** Elihu, an outsider to the covenant line himself,
describes exactly what happened to Abimelech and Laban as God's ordinary practice with every man: *"In a
dream, in a vision of the night, when deep sleep falls on men … he opens the ears of men, and seals
their instruction, that he may withdraw man from his purpose"* (Job 33:15–17). The Genesis dreams stop a
man before he harms someone else; Elihu's stop him before he harms himself, and he offers the rule to
Job as the explanation of Job's own nightmares (7:14). **What Genesis narrates twice as an intervention,
Elihu states as a law** — and applies to a man the reader knows was not being warned off anything. See
[[Elihu Speaks in Anger]].
