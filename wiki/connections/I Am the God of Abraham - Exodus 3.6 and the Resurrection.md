# I Am the God of Abraham - Exodus 3.6 and the Resurrection

## Passages involved

- **Exodus 3:6** — *"I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of
  Jacob."* — [[The Burning Bush]]
- **Matthew 22:31–32** — *"But concerning the resurrection of the dead, haven't you read that which was
  spoken to you by God, saying, 'I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob?'
  God is not the God of the dead, but of the living."*
- **Mark 12:26** — *"But about the dead, that they are raised; haven't you read in the book of Moses,
  about the Bush, how God spoke to him, saying, 'I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God
  of Jacob'?"*
- **Luke 20:37–38** — *"But that the dead are raised, even Moses showed at the bush, when he called the
  Lord 'The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.' Now he is not the God of the dead,
  but of the living, for all are alive to him."*

## Analysis

**All three Synoptic Gospels have Jesus answer the Sadducees' question about resurrection from this one
verse** — and from the Torah, the part of Scripture whose authority the Sadducees are commonly said to
have stressed, rather than from Daniel 12:2 or Isaiah 26:19, which state resurrection outright.

**The argument turns on the present tense.** God speaks to Moses centuries after the patriarchs have
died and been buried — Abraham and Isaac at Machpelah, Jacob carried there from Egypt (Genesis 25:9;
49:31; 50:13) — and says *I am* their God, not *I was*. **If God is still their God, they are still in
relation to him; and a God who *"is not the God of the dead"* does not stay in relation to corpses.**
Luke adds the clause that makes the inference explicit: *"for all are alive to him"*.

**The Hebrew has no verb in the sentence.** *ʾĀnōkî ʾĕlōhê ʾābîkā* is a verbless clause, *I — the God
of your father* (checked against the Westminster Leningrad Codex), and the present is supplied by the
grammar of such clauses, not by a word. Matthew's Greek supplies *egō eimi* (*Egō eimi ho theos
Abraam*); Mark's, like the Hebrew, has no verb (*Egō ho theos Abraam*) — both as printed in the SBL
Greek New Testament (2010), whose apparatus was not consulted, so a manuscript variant adding *eimi*
in Mark is not ruled out. Luke puts it in indirect speech. **The argument therefore does not rest on a
tense form but on what the self-introduction implies**: that a covenant God names himself by the people he has bound himself
to, and that the binding outlasts their death.

**The formula has a history in Genesis that sharpens the point.** God introduced himself to Isaac as
*"the God of Abraham your father"* (Genesis 26:24), to Jacob as *"the God of Abraham your father, and
the God of Isaac"* (28:13), and again as *"the God of your father"* (46:3). **At the bush, for the
first time, he names all three — and all three are dead.** The formula that grew one name at a time as
each patriarch received the promise is completed only when none of them is alive to hear it.

**Mark's *"about the Bush"*** — *epi tou batou* — **is how a passage was cited before chapter and verse
existed**: by a catchword for its scene. The pericope already had a title.

## Significance

- **It makes Exodus 3 a resurrection text**, read through Jesus' argument, without adding anything to
  its words.
- **It shows the New Testament reading the patriarchal formula as covenant, not as history** — *the
  God of Abraham* names a relationship that is still in force.
- **It completes the formula's history** traced on [[The Names of God]]: Genesis 26:24, 28:13, and
  46:3 lead here.
