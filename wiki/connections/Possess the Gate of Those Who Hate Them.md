# Possess the Gate of Those Who Hate Them

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 22:15–18** — Yahweh's oath to Abraham on the mountain — [[The Binding of Isaac]]
- **Genesis 24:59–61** — Rebekah's family bless her as she leaves — [[A Wife for Isaac]]
- Compare **Genesis 12:2–3; 13:16; 15:5; 17:6; 26:4; 28:14** — the offspring promise elsewhere
- Compare **Genesis 22:17a** — *"like the stars of the heavens, and like the sand which is on the
  seashore"* — [[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]]
- Downstream: **Genesis 24:60b** in **Ruth 4:11–12** and **Genesis 28:14** in the Jacob cycle

## Analysis

**Two chapters apart, the same clause is spoken twice, by parties who have no connection to each
other.**

> **22:17** … I will multiply your offspring greatly like the stars of the heavens, and like the sand
> which is on the seashore. **Your offspring will possess the gate of his enemies.**

> **24:60** They blessed Rebekah, and said to her, “Our sister, may you be the mother of thousands of
> ten thousands, and **let your offspring possess the gate of those who hate them.**”

In Hebrew:

| | Genesis 22:17 | Genesis 24:60 |
|---|---|---|
| | *wəyîraš zarʿăkā* | *wəyîraš zarʿēk* |
| | *ʾēt šaʿar ʾōyəbāyw* | *ʾēt šaʿar śōnəʾāyw* |
| verb | *yāraš*, possess or dispossess | the same verb, same stem, same form |
| noun | *zeraʿ*, offspring — **your** (masculine) | *zeraʿ* — **your** (feminine) |
| object | *šaʿar*, gate — of **his enemies** | *šaʿar*, gate — of **those who hate them** |

**One verb, one noun, one construct chain, and a synonym.** *ʾŌyēb* and *śōnēʾ* are the standard
poetic pair for enemy and hater and are used interchangeably across the Psalter. The only structural
difference is the pronoun's gender: at Moriah the offspring is Abraham's, here it is Rebekah's.

**And this is the only place in the Hebrew Bible outside Genesis 22:17 where the clause occurs.**
*Possessing the gate* — taking the fortified entrance, which is to say taking the city — is not a
stock blessing formula; it appears twice, in these two verses, and nowhere else.

### Who is saying it, and what they know

**The people at Moriah were Abraham and God, and there were no witnesses.** Isaac is not recorded as
hearing the oath; 22:19 has Abraham returning to *"his young men"*, who were left at the bottom of
the hill (22:5). The oath is sworn in private, on a mountain, to one man.

**The people in Genesis 24:60 are an Aramean household four hundred miles away who have never heard
of it.** Laban and his mother have spent the evening being told about Abraham's flocks, silver, gold,
servants, camels, donkeys, and a son who inherits all of it (24:35–36) — a speech about property, not
about covenant. **The servant does not tell them the promise.** He says Yahweh prospered his journey;
he never mentions offspring like the stars, or a land, or an oath sworn on a mountain.

And then, sending their daughter off, they say the oath.

**Nothing in the text explains this**, and the wiki does not manufacture an explanation. Three
readings are live and they are not equally strong:

- **A stock formula.** Wedding blessings in the ancient Near East wish fertility and victory on a
  bride, and Ruth 4:11–12 shows the Israelite form of exactly that at a town gate. The objection is
  that this particular clause occurs nowhere else in Scripture, which is a poor showing for a stock
  phrase.
- **Deliberate narrative irony.** Genesis puts the covenant oath in the mouths of outsiders who do not
  know they are quoting it — the same move as the Hittites calling Abraham *"a prince of God"* (23:6)
  while selling him a grave, and Pharaoh and Abimelech behaving better than the patriarch. **This is
  the reading the rest of the Abraham cycle supports.** See
  [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] and [[Abraham and Abimelech]].
- **A compositional link.** That a writer or editor shaped 24:60 to echo 22:17 and tie the marriage
  to the oath. This is compatible with the second reading rather than opposed to it.

### What it does structurally

**It is the covenant's transfer receipt.** The promise is sworn to Abraham at 22:17 and restated to
Isaac at 26:4 and to Jacob at 28:14 — but between those, at 24:60, it is spoken over **Rebekah**, and
it is the only time in Genesis the offspring promise is addressed to a woman as her own.

That matters because of what Genesis has done with the matriarchs so far. Sarah is not named in the
promise until 17:16, seven chapters and five promise-scenes after it starts. Hagar receives a
promise about her son and not about herself. **Rebekah is blessed in the covenant's own words before
she has met her husband, by people outside the covenant, and the narrator does not comment.** See
[[Rebekah]] and [[Blessing]].

**And it is the promise's first appearance after Moriah.** The oath at 22:16–18 is the last thing God
says to Abraham in the Bible. The next time those words are heard, a man is holding a camel's rein
outside a house in Mesopotamia. **The covenant's continuation is voiced by people who are not in it,
in a chapter where God does not speak at all** — which is the whole method of [[A Wife for Isaac]].

## Significance

The echo is checkable, short, and hard to explain away, and it does three things at once.

**It confirms that Genesis 24 is a covenant chapter and not a domestic interlude**, despite containing
no divine speech, no altar, no theophany, and no mention of the promise by anybody in it.

**It puts the promise in the mouths of the wrong people**, which is what Genesis keeps doing.
Melchizedek blesses Abram; Pharaoh rebukes him; Abimelech is called righteous by implication and
Abraham a prophet in the same breath; the children of Heth call him a prince of God. **The book is
persistently more generous to outsiders than its readers expect, and 24:60 is the sharpest instance:
they say the covenant back to it.**

**And it makes Rebekah's departure the answer to Moriah.** God swore descendants to a man who had just
been asked to give up the only one he had. Two chapters later the woman those descendants will come
through is blessed with that exact sentence, says *"I will go"*, and gets on a camel.
