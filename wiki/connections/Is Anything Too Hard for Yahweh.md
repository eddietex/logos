# Is Anything Too Hard for Yahweh

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 18:14** — *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh? At the set time I will return to you, when
  the season comes round, and Sarah will have a son."* See
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]].
- **Jeremiah 32:17** — *"Ah Lord Yahweh! Behold, you have made the heavens and the earth by your
  great power and by your outstretched arm. There is nothing too hard for you."*
- **Jeremiah 32:27** — *"Behold, I am Yahweh, the God of all flesh. Is there anything too hard for
  me?"*
- **Zechariah 8:6** — *"If it is marvelous in the eyes of the remnant of this people in those days,
  should it also be marvelous in my eyes?"*
- **Job 42:2** — *"I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be
  restrained."*
- **Luke 1:37** — *"For nothing spoken by God is impossible."*
- **Matthew 19:26; Mark 10:27; Luke 18:27** — *"With men this is impossible, but with God all
  things are possible."*

## Analysis

The Hebrew is ***hă-yippālēʾ mē-YHWH dābār***, and the verb is the point. The root **p-l-ʾ** does
not mean *to be difficult*; it means **to be beyond reach** — extraordinary, surpassing, out of the
ordinary run of things. It is the root behind the *wonders* of the exodus (Exodus 3:20; 15:11), the
*wonderful* of Judges 13:18 where the angel refuses his name, and the *too wonderful for me* of Job
42:3 and Psalm 139:6. So the question is nearer *is anything too wondrous for Yahweh* than *too
difficult*, and *dābār* is both **thing** and **word** — a pun the context earns, since what is
under discussion is a word God has spoken about a ninety-year-old woman.

**It is asked, not asserted.** Genesis puts it as a question with a presumed answer, and everywhere
it recurs it keeps that shape or answers a question exactly like it. It is not a doctrine of
omnipotence in the abstract; every occurrence is attached to a specific, physically implausible
promise that somebody has just balked at.

### The three occasions

| | The implausibility | Who balks | The form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Genesis 18:11–14** | a post-menopausal woman bearing a son | Sarah, laughing inside herself | God asks Abraham |
| **Jeremiah 32:6–27** | buying a field in a country under Babylonian occupation, with the city about to fall | Jeremiah, who obeys and then prays his doubt | Jeremiah says it; God asks it back |
| **Luke 1:34–37** | a virgin conceiving | Mary, asking how | Gabriel says it |

**Jeremiah 32 is the fullest working of it.** God tells the prophet to buy his cousin's field at
Anathoth while the Babylonians are besieging Jerusalem and Jeremiah himself is under guard. He does
it — weighs out seventeen shekels, signs the deed, has it witnessed and sealed in a jar *"that they
may continue many days"* (32:14) — and then prays a prayer that begins by conceding the point
(*"There is nothing too hard for you"*, 32:17) and spends the next nine verses explaining why the
city is about to fall anyway. God's answer opens by handing the sentence back to him as a question:
*"Is there anything too hard for me?"* (32:27), and then promises that *"houses and fields and
vineyards will yet again be bought in this land"* (32:15, 43). **The pattern is Genesis 18's exactly
— a promise about the future secured by a purchase or a birth that looks absurd at the moment it is
made.**

### Luke 1:37 is a quotation, and of this verse

The Septuagint renders Genesis 18:14 *mē adynatei para tō theō rhēma;* — *is any word impossible
with God?* Gabriel's answer to Mary at Luke 1:37 is *ouk adynatēsei para tou theou pan rhēma* —
*no word from God will be impossible*. Same verb, same noun, same construction, in the answer to the
same kind of question about the same kind of impossibility.

The parallel is not only verbal. Luke's annunciation reproduces the Genesis scene in its structure:
a visitor announces a birth, the woman raises the physical objection (*"How can this be, since I am
a virgin?"*), the objection is answered with this sentence, and a second, corroborating pregnancy is
offered as evidence — Elizabeth, *"who was called barren"*, in her sixth month (Luke 1:36), who is a
Sarah figure down to the detail of her husband's disbelief being the one that gets rebuked (1:20).
**Luke reads Genesis 18 as the pattern of what God does, and Mary's *"let it be to me according to
your word"* is what Sarah could not manage in the tent.** The wiki notes without endorsing the long
Christian contrast of the two women; Genesis is markedly less interested in condemning Sarah than
its readers have been, and it lets her name the laugh herself at 21:6.

### The other family of sayings

*"With God all things are possible"* (Matthew 19:26 and parallels) is a different sentence with the
same force, and its context is worth keeping: it answers *"Who then can be saved?"* after the rich
man leaves. **The impossibility being denied there is moral, not physical** — a rich man entering the
kingdom, not an old woman conceiving. The two families of sayings converge on the same claim from
opposite directions, and the Bible never uses either one to promise that any particular thing a
person wants will happen. Both are answers to *this cannot work*, given about something God has
already said he will do.

## Significance

- **It is the sentence Genesis uses to define what kind of God is being dealt with**, and it arrives
  attached to a womb rather than to creation. Jeremiah 32:17 supplies the cosmological version
  (*"you have made the heavens and the earth"*) as the ground for the domestic one, in that order.
- **It is a question in every occurrence but one.** Scripture prefers to put God's capacity as
  something the hearer is asked to concede rather than something asserted at them — which is also
  how Job's book ends, with the man conceding it himself at 42:2 after thirty-eight chapters of
  being asked.
- **The New Testament's most famous annunciation is Genesis 18 quoted.** Luke 1:37 is the
  Septuagint's Genesis 18:14 with the interrogative removed, and Luke builds the whole scene around
  the older one — including the barren relative in her sixth month, who is there to make the point
  that this has happened before.
- **And it is not a general assurance.** Every occurrence is tied to a stated promise with a date
  or a deed attached — *at the set time*, a sealed deed in a jar, a child named before conception.
  Genesis 18:14 is a defence of a specific word, and the second half of the verse is the appointment
  it is defending.
