# The Blood Is the Life

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:3–4 — meat permitted, blood forbidden: *"But flesh with its life, that is, its blood,
  you shall not eat"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Genesis 1:29–30 — the food grant that 9:3 amends ([[The Creation of the World]])
- Genesis 2:7 — *nepeš ḥayyâ*, the living being, the word 9:4 equates with blood
  ([[The Garden of Eden]])
- Leviticus 3:17; 7:26–27; 19:26 — the prohibition repeated as *"a perpetual statute throughout your
  generations"*, with excision as the penalty
- Leviticus 17:10–14 — the reason given: *"the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood
  that makes atonement by reason of the life"*, and the rule that hunted blood must be poured out
  and covered with dust
- Deuteronomy 12:15–16, 20–25; 15:23 — the rule carried into ordinary secular slaughter once meat is
  eaten away from the sanctuary: *"only you shall not eat the blood; you shall pour it out on the
  earth as water… for the blood is the life"*
- 1 Samuel 14:31–35 — Saul's troops eat animals *"with the blood"* after a battle, and it is
  reported as a sin
- Ezekiel 33:25 — *"You eat with the blood… and shall you possess the land?"*
- Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25 — the apostolic decree asks Gentile believers to abstain from *"what is
  strangled, and from blood"*
- John 6:53–56 — *"unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you don't have
  life in yourselves"*
- Hebrews 9:22 — *"apart from shedding of blood there is no remission"*
- *b. Sanhedrin* 56a–b — the seven Noahide commandments, of which *ʾēver min haḥay*, a limb torn
  from a living animal, is derived from Genesis 9:4

## Analysis

**One rule, given twice, for two different reasons.** Genesis 9:4 states the prohibition without
explaining the mechanism: blood may not be eaten because blood is the life. Leviticus 17:11 adds the
second half — the life in the blood is what God has assigned to the altar for atonement — and the
two together produce the whole biblical logic of sacrifice. Blood is off the human table because it
is spoken for. It is the one part of a slaughtered animal that is not yours.

The order matters. The rule comes first at Genesis 9, addressed to all humanity through Noah, with
no priesthood, no tabernacle, no Israel, and no sin offering in existence. Leviticus then supplies
the theology of a rule that was already binding. That is why the prohibition of blood behaves
differently from the rest of the Torah's food law: the clean/unclean distinction of Leviticus 11 is
addressed to Israel, and the blood rule is addressed to humanity — Leviticus 17:10 applies it to
*"any stranger who lives as a foreigner among them"* as well as to the house of Israel.

**Two ways to read the Hebrew of 9:4, and both survive.** *Bāśār bənapšô dāmô lōʾ tōʾkēlû* is
compressed to the point of awkwardness: *flesh with-its-life its-blood you-shall-not-eat*. The plain
reading, and the one Leviticus and Deuteronomy assume, is a prohibition of consuming blood as such.
The rabbinic tradition also derives from it a second and narrower rule — *ʾēver min haḥay*, flesh
cut from an animal while it is still alive — and makes that one of the seven commandments binding on
all the sons of Noah (*b. Sanhedrin* 56a–b). The two readings are not rivals; they are two ways of
refusing to treat a creature's life as though it were only meat. What both preserve is the sense
that the permission of 9:3 is a concession with a condition attached.

**A rule the earliest church kept when it dropped almost everything else.** The decree of Acts 15 is
one of the New Testament's clearest decisions and one of its least discussed. Having ruled that
Gentile believers need not be circumcised or keep the law of Moses, the council nonetheless asks
them to abstain from four things, two of which — *"what is strangled"* and *"blood"* — are Genesis
9:4 and Leviticus 17. The most persuasive account of the list is that it is not a fragment of Mosaic
law retained for convenience but the pre-Mosaic, universal law of Genesis 9 — the requirements the
Torah itself places on the resident foreigner (Leviticus 17–18), which is exactly what a Gentile in
a mixed congregation is. Whether it was intended as permanent or as a concession to table
fellowship is disputed and probably not settleable from the text; that the church kept it for
centuries in the East is a matter of record.

**And then Jesus says the thing that cannot be said.** *"He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood
has eternal life"* (John 6:54). To an audience formed on Genesis 9:4 and Leviticus 17, this is not
a difficult metaphor — it is a prohibition named and inverted, and John reports the reaction
accurately: *"This is a hard saying! Who can listen to it?"*, and *"many of his disciples went back,
and walked no more with him"* (6:60, 66). The offense is deliberate, and it works precisely because
the audience knows the rule.

What makes the saying coherent rather than merely shocking is the premise it shares with the rule it
breaks. Blood is forbidden *because it is life* and *because it atones*. Jesus is claiming both
predicates for himself in the same sentence — *"my blood is true drink… he who eats my flesh and
drinks my blood remains in me, and I in him"* — and the church's meal ever after is built on it.
Blood withheld from every table in the Bible is offered at one.

## Significance

The line from Genesis 9:4 to the eucharist is unbroken and it is a line of one idea: a life is not a
commodity. The rule that gives it its first legal form is given to a man standing on a drowned
planet who has just been told he may eat anything that moves. The permission is enormous and it is
fenced, and the fence is the point — killing for food is allowed and is never to become
thoughtless.

Everything the Bible later says about atonement runs through the fence. Because blood is reserved,
there is something to put on an altar; because blood is life, putting it there means something;
because *"apart from shedding of blood there is no remission"* (Hebrews 9:22), the New Testament's
account of the cross has a grammar available to it. The seed of all of it is a single clause in a
speech to eight survivors, and Genesis gives it without argument.
