# The Divine Council

## Passages involved

- **Job 1:6; 2:1** — *"Now on the day when God's sons came to present themselves before Yahweh,
  Satan also came among them."* — [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] and
  [[The Second Test]]; the book's two sessions are staged in almost identical words, and the second
  adds only that the accuser now comes *"to present himself before Yahweh"* in his own right
- **Job 38:7** — *"when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy"*,
  at the founding of the earth
- **Genesis 1:26; 3:22; 11:7** — *"Let us make man in our image"*; *"the man has become like one of
  us"*; *"let's go down"* — [[Let Us Go Down - The Divine Plural in Genesis]]
- **Genesis 6:1–4** — the sons of God and the daughters of men —
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]
- **1 Kings 22:19–23** — Micaiah: *"I saw Yahweh sitting on his throne, and all the army of heaven
  standing by him on his right hand and on his left… Who will entice Ahab?"*
- **Psalm 82:1** — *"God presides in the great assembly. He judges among the gods."*
- **Psalm 89:5–7** — *"the assembly of the holy ones… who among the sons of the mighty is like
  Yahweh?"*
- **Psalm 29:1** — *"Ascribe to Yahweh, you sons of the mighty"*
- **Deuteronomy 32:8** — the nations divided *"according to the number of the sons of God"* (Dead Sea
  Scrolls and Septuagint; the Masoretic text reads *sons of Israel*)
- **Isaiah 6:1–8** — *"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"*
- **Zechariah 3:1–5** — Joshua the high priest before the Angel of Yahweh, with *haśśāṭān* at his
  right hand
- **Daniel 7:9–10** — thrones set, the Ancient of Days seated, *"the judgment was set, and the books
  were opened"*
- **Revelation 4–5** — the throne, the elders, the living creatures, and the sealed scroll

## Analysis

The Hebrew Bible repeatedly shows Yahweh **surrounded**. Not assisted, not constrained, and never
rivalled — but attended, in a court with members who stand, report, are questioned, volunteer,
object, and are sent. The scene is stable enough across five centuries of text to count as a
convention, and Job 1 is the most developed instance of it.

**The fixed furniture of the scene.** A throne or a seat of judgment; a standing assembly (*ʿădat
ʾēl*, *sôd qədōšîm*, *ṣəbāʾ haššāmayim*, *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*); a matter put to the assembly as a
question; a member who answers; and a commission with terms. **1 Kings 22:19–23 and Job 1:6–12 share
all five**, and the resemblance runs down to the grammar: Yahweh asks an open question (*"Who will
entice Ahab…?"* / *"Have you considered my servant Job?"*), a member proposes a method, and Yahweh
authorises it with a limit or a guarantee (*"You shall entice him, and shall also prevail"* / *"Only
on himself don't stretch out your hand"*). In both, **the resulting harm is carried out by the member
and attributed by the narrative to Yahweh.**

**What the council is for, in each place it appears, is different.** In Isaiah 6 it is a commission.
In 1 Kings 22 it is a decision about a battle. In Psalm 82 it is a trial of the members themselves,
who are sentenced to die like men for judging unjustly. In Zechariah 3 it is a trial of a human
being with an accuser present and an advocate intervening. In Job it is a trial of a proposition
about human beings in general, with the human being in question absent and unrepresented. **Job's
version is the only one where the subject of the proceeding is never informed that it happened.**

**Why the convention is theologically awkward, and why it survived.** The vocabulary is
recognisably that of the wider Near East, where a high god presides over an assembly of lesser gods
who deliberate — the Ugaritic texts supply the closest parallels, and Psalm 82's *"He judges among
the gods"* uses the same word for them that they use. Israel's texts keep the furniture and
demolish the theology: the members are never worshipped, never petitioned, never named as gods with
independent standing, and in Psalm 82 they are executed. **The assembly is retained as a picture of
how God governs, and emptied of anyone who might be an alternative.** Deuteronomy 32:8's variant
reading is the most contested single verse in the discussion, because it appears to assign the
nations to members of the council; the Masoretic *sons of Israel* removes that, and the Qumran and
Greek witnesses do not.

**Genesis, and how much weight the plurals will bear.** *"Let us make man in our image"* has been
read as the council, as a plural of majesty, as self-deliberation, and as the Trinity. The wiki sets
the four out at [[Let Us Go Down - The Divine Plural in Genesis]] and does not settle them. What can
be said here is narrower and firmer: **Genesis 6:2's *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* is the same phrase Job 1:6
uses**, and every other occurrence in the Hebrew Bible is of heavenly beings. Genesis is not obliged
to follow Job's usage, but the burden of proof sits with the reading that it does not. See
[[The Sons of God]].

**What Job does with the convention that nobody else does.** Three things.

**(1) It puts a dissenting voice in the room and lets it win the argument on procedure.** The accuser
does not defy God; he asks a question God cannot answer by assertion, and gets what he asks for.

**(2) It splits the reader from every character.** Isaiah, Micaiah and Zechariah all *see* the
council — the scene is given as a vision to a human being, who then reports it. **Job 1 is given to
nobody.** There is no visionary, no prophet, no dream. The narrator simply tells the reader, and the
information stays with the reader for forty-one chapters while five men argue in ignorance of it.
This is the single formal innovation that makes the book work.

**(3) It leaves the council out of the resolution.** When God finally speaks from the whirlwind, he
does not mention the court, the accuser, or the wager. He mentions, at 38:7, that the sons of God
sang at the creation — **the council reappears exactly once, as spectators of something Job had no
part in**, and that is all the answer Job gets about heaven.

## Significance

The council is the Old Testament's standing answer to a question it never asks directly: **what is
between God and the world?** Not nothing, and not a rival. A retinue that carries out decisions,
supplies alternatives, and occasionally has to be rebuked.

For the book of Job specifically, the convention supplies the one thing the argument requires. **A
theodicy in which the reader is as ignorant as the sufferer cannot be written** — it would just be
the sufferer's complaint, with no vantage from which to see that the friends are wrong. The council
scene gives the reader a place to stand that is not inside the argument, and the book then spends
forty chapters demonstrating what people do when they are reasoning about a case whose crucial fact
is unavailable to them. **The reader's advantage is not an explanation. It is the removal of the
friends' excuse**, and that turns out to be the book's whole verdict at 42:7.

The New Testament inherits the scene rather than abandoning it — Daniel 7's thrones and opened books
become Revelation's throne room, and the accuser becomes a figure who is *"thrown down"* (Revelation
12:10) after *"accusing them before our God day and night"*, which is a description of the job he
holds in Job 1 and a statement that the job is being abolished. See [[Satan]] and
[[Ha-Satan - The Accuser in Job, Zechariah, and Chronicles]].
