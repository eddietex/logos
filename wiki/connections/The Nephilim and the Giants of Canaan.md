# The Nephilim and the Giants of Canaan

## Passages involved

- Genesis 6:4 — [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]
- Genesis 10:8 — [[The Table of Nations]]: *gibbōr* used of Nimrod, the word's first reappearance
  after the flood
- Numbers 13:31–33 *(not yet ingested)* — the spies' report, and the word's only other occurrence
- Deuteronomy 1:28; 2:10–11, 20–21; 3:11; 9:1–2 *(not yet ingested)* — the Anakim, Emim, Zamzummim,
  and Rephaim
- Joshua 11:21–22; 14:12–15; 15:13–14 *(not yet ingested)* — the Anakim driven out, and the remnant
  left in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod
- 1 Samuel 17:4–7 *(not yet ingested)* — Goliath of Gath
- 2 Samuel 21:15–22 *(not yet ingested)* — the last four *"born to the giant in Gath"*

## Analysis

**Two verses, and a chain the Bible does not quite forge.** *Nephilim* occurs exactly twice in the
Hebrew Bible. Genesis 6:4 places them before the flood, with the parenthetical *"and also after
that"*. Numbers 13:33 places them in Canaan, in the mouths of ten frightened men: *"There we saw the
Nephilim, the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim. We were in our own sight as grasshoppers,
and so we were in their sight."* Everything else usually said about them is inference from those two
verses plus the separate, and much better attested, biblical tradition of oversized peoples in and
around Canaan.

**The other word from 6:4 does come back, and it comes back four verses into the new world.** Genesis
6:4 calls the offspring *haggibbōrîm*, *"the mighty men who were of old, men of renown"*, and *gibbōr*
does not occur again until Genesis 10:8: *"Cush became the father of Nimrod. He began to be a mighty
one in the earth."* Genesis draws no line between them and makes no claim of descent — *gibbōr* is an
ordinary Hebrew word for a warrior or a champion, and it will be used of David's men without any such
freight. But it is the vocabulary of the generation the flood destroyed, reused on the first person
the post-flood roster stops to describe, and the man it describes founds Babel and Nineveh. See
[[Nimrod]] and [[Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria]].

**That separate tradition is substantial and is worth keeping distinct.** Deuteronomy 2 supplies a
small ethnography of them: the **Emim** in Moab, *"great and many, and tall as the Anakim"*; the
**Zamzummim** in Ammon, likewise; both reckoned as **Rephaim**, whom the Moabites and Ammonites
displaced. The **Anakim** hold Hebron and the hill country, and Deuteronomy 9:2 preserves the proverb
Israel apparently repeated to itself: *"Who can stand before the sons of Anak?"* Og king of Bashan is
*"the remnant of the Rephaim"*, and Deuteronomy 3:11 vouches for it with a museum piece — an iron
bedstead nine cubits long, still on display in Rabbah when the verse was written. Joshua records the
Anakim cut off from the hill country with a remnant left in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod — which is where,
generations later, Goliath comes from, and where 2 Samuel 21 finds four more *"born to the giant in
Gath"*, one of them with six fingers on each hand.

Notice what the chain looks like when laid out. From Joshua onward it is coherent, geographically
specific, and unremarkable: a tall people, remembered as formidable, progressively displaced, with a
remnant in the Philistine cities. The link to Genesis 6:4 rests entirely on the single clause in
Numbers 13:33 — and that clause is spoken by men whose report the very next chapter condemns as
*"an evil report of the land"* (Numbers 14:36–37) and whose other claims in the same breath are
plainly hyperbolic (*"a land that eats up its inhabitants"*). Caleb and Joshua, who saw the same
people, say nothing about Nephilim. This is not a reason to dismiss the identification, but it is a
reason to notice that the Bible's one bridge between Genesis 6 and the conquest giants is built by
unreliable narrators.

**The flood problem.** If the Nephilim were pre-flood and the flood destroyed all flesh outside the
ark, there should be none in Canaan. The available responses:

- **Genesis 6:4 anticipates it.** *"And also after that"* may be exactly the narrator's provision
  for the fact — the same phenomenon, whatever it was, recurring after the flood. This is the
  reading with actual textual warrant, and it is why the clause is there.
- **Numbers 13:33 is comparative, not genealogical.** The spies reach for the most frightening word
  available and apply it to people who were merely very large, as one might call an opponent a
  giant. The verse's own framing supports this.
- **A survival through the ark**, usually via one of the wives of Noah's sons. This is pure
  speculation; the text gives it nothing.
- **A local flood**, which resolves it at the cost of a much larger argument elsewhere.

**What "giant" is doing here.** The Septuagint rendered *nəpilîm* as *gigantes* and *rəpāʾîm* as
*gigantes* too, collapsing a distinction the Hebrew keeps; the Vulgate followed, and the King James
gave English *"giants"* in Genesis 6:4. A good deal of the popular picture of the Nephilim descends
from a translator's choice rather than from the Hebrew, which never describes them and never gives a
measurement. The only actual dimensions in this whole tradition belong to Og's bed and Goliath's
armour.

## Significance

**The theme is not size; it is fear.** Numbers 13:33 is not making a zoological claim, it is
explaining why an entire generation refused to enter the land. The verse's real weight falls on its
second half — *"we were in our own sight as grasshoppers"* — and the chapter's judgment falls on the
seeing, not on the seen. What the spies got wrong was not the height of the Anakim but the size of
God, and Numbers 14 costs Israel forty years for it. The Nephilim function in Scripture as the
standing image of an obstacle that is genuinely formidable and genuinely not decisive, which is why
the tradition ends the way it does: with the giant of Gath killed by a boy who declined to wear
armour.

**And it links the two great judgments.** Genesis 6:4's Nephilim stand at the threshold of the
flood; Numbers 13:33's stand at the threshold of the land. Both times a world is about to be
remade, and both times the text pauses on the people who look too big for it to happen. In the
first case the obstacle is drowned; in the second, the generation that feared it dies in the
wilderness instead.
