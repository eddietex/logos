# Do Not Interpretations Belong to God - Joseph and Daniel

## Passages involved

- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:5–8, 12, 18, 22: *"Don't interpretations belong to God?
  Please tell it to me"*
- Genesis 41:8, 15–16, 25, 32, 38–39 — the magicians fail, and *"It is not in me: God will give Pharaoh
  an answer of peace"*
- Daniel 2:1–11 — Nebuchadnezzar's dream, and a demand no professional can meet
- Daniel 2:27–30, 45, 47 — *"there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets"*, and *"as for me, this
  secret is not revealed to me for any wisdom that I have more than any living"*
- Daniel 4:4–9, 18–27 — a second dream, the magicians failing again, and an interpretation with a moral
  attached
- Daniel 5:7–12, 16–17 — the writing on the wall; *"let your gifts be to yourself"*
- Daniel 1:3–7, 17–20 — the training of foreign youths for court service, and *"understanding in all
  visions and dreams"*
- Genesis 42:23 — *mēlîṣ*, an interpreter who is a translator: **the other word, and the contrast**

## Analysis

**Two Hebrew words carry the whole of Joseph's vocation and they occur in two chapters of the Bible and
nowhere else.** ***Pātar***, to interpret, and ***pitrôn***, an interpretation, appear fourteen times
across Genesis 40 and 41 — 40:5, 8 (twice), 12, 16, 18, 22; 41:8, 11, 12 (twice), 13, 15 (twice) — and
**the rest of the Hebrew Bible does not use either.** Prophets who receive visions do not *pātar* them;
the wisdom books never mention the root. **Genesis reserves a verb for this and then retires it.**

**The word that replaces it is Aramaic.** ***Pəšar*** is the exact cognate, and it is the standard term
in the Aramaic chapters of Daniel, where it appears about thirty times. **Daniel does in a Babylonian
court what Joseph did in an Egyptian one, and the language of the book supplies the same root for it.**
(The noun also names a whole genre at Qumran, where a *pesher* is a line-by-line reading of a prophet
as though it were a dream about the present.)

**The two careers run the same course, beat for beat.**

| | **Joseph** | **Daniel** |
|---|---|---|
| how he arrives | carried to a foreign capital against his will, a slave | deported to a foreign capital, conscripted into palace service |
| the professionals | *"there is no one who can interpret it"* (40:8); the magicians and wise men fail (41:8) | *"the Chaldeans answered… there is no other who can show it before the king"* (2:10–11); the enchanters fail (4:7) |
| the disclaimer | *"Don't interpretations belong to God?"* (40:8); *"It is not in me"* (41:16) | *"this secret is not revealed to me for any wisdom that I have more than any living"* (2:30) |
| the pattern of two | two officers' dreams, then Pharaoh's doubled pair (41:32) | a dream in chapter 2, another in chapter 4, a wall in chapter 5 |
| the reward | signet ring, linen, gold chain, second in the kingdom (41:42–43) | purple, a gold chain, third ruler in the kingdom (5:29) |
| the king's confession | *"a man in whom is the Spirit of God"* (41:38) | *"your God is the God of gods… a revealer of secrets"* (2:47) |

**The disclaimer is the load-bearing item, and it is not modesty.** Both men deny the skill is theirs in
the sentence immediately before they exercise it, and in both books the denial is the theological claim
the narrative is making. **Egypt and Babylon both have a professional apparatus for this work** — Egypt's
survives in the dream manuals, of which Chester Beatty III is the best known, essentially a two-column
lookup table of images and verdicts; Babylon's in the omen series. **What the Bible's two interpreters
say is that the answer is not in the table.** *"Don't interpretations belong to God?"* is a question
about ownership: the meaning is a property of the sender, and the reader is only the one to whom it is
disclosed.

**Genesis 40:5 makes the same point structurally.** *"Each man according to the interpretation of his
dream"* — **the dreams already had interpretations before anyone read them.** The meaning is not
constructed; it is there. Which is precisely why professionals can fail: **the failure is not
incompetence, it is lack of access.**

**Joseph's version is a question; Daniel's is a doctrine.** *"Don't interpretations belong to God?"* is
eight words to two frightened prisoners. Daniel 2:27–28 is a formal court statement, with a survey of
the professions that cannot do it — *"wise men, enchanters, magicians, or soothsayers"* — followed by
*"but there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets."* **The distance between them is the distance
between a Hebrew slave who has told nobody who he is and a deportee whose God is already the subject of
the conversation.**

**And Genesis marks the boundary of the word itself.** When Joseph needs a *translator* at 42:23, the
text uses ***mēlîṣ***, not *pātar*. **The book knows the difference between converting a language and
receiving a disclosure**, and it never blurs the two.

## Significance

**This is the origin of a biblical type: the exile who is better at the empire's own sacred science than
the empire is.** The type recurs deliberately — Joseph, Daniel and his three companions, Mordecai in a
Persian court, and the magi of Matthew 2, who are the empire's professionals coming the other way.
**Each time, a foreign king learns something true about God from a displaced person with no standing to
teach him.**

Its theological point is narrow and it is made twice in the same words. **Revelation is not a
technique.** It can be delivered to anybody — Pharaoh's baker gets one, as
[[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] did at 20:3 — but **the reading of it is given, not obtained**, and
the man who reads it says so before he reads.
The wiki records the parallel as a shared type and **argues no dependence in either direction.** Daniel
is plainly written with Joseph's career in view — the correspondences are too many and too ordered to be
accidental — but **which way a literary relationship runs, and when, is not something this wiki is in a
position to decide**, and nothing on this page requires it.
