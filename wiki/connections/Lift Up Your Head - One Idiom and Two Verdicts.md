# Lift Up Your Head - One Idiom and Two Verdicts

## Passages involved

- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:13, 19, 20: the same three words used for reinstatement,
  for execution, and for a head-count, in eight verses
- Exodus 30:12; Numbers 1:2, 49; 4:2, 22; 26:2 — *nāśāʾ rōʾš* as the census idiom, *take the sum*
- 2 Kings 25:27–30 — Evil Merodach *"lifted up the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah out of prison"*: the
  last four verses of the history of Israel and Judah
- Jeremiah 52:31–34 — the same event, in the same words, closing the book of Jeremiah
- Psalm 3:3 — *"you are… my glory, and the one who lifts up my head"*
- Psalm 27:6 — *"now my head will be lifted up above my enemies around me"*
- Psalm 110:7 — *"therefore he will lift up his head"*
- Judges 8:28 — Midian *"lifted up their heads no more"*
- Zechariah 1:21; 2:4 — heads lifted in threat rather than favour

## Analysis

***Nāśāʾ rōʾš***, *lift up the head*, is one of the Bible's genuinely ambiguous idioms, and **Genesis 40
is the only place in Scripture that uses the ambiguity on purpose.**

The phrase has three live senses.

1. **Restore to favour** — bring a man out of disgrace and back to his place. This is the sense at
   Genesis 40:13 and at 2 Kings 25:27.
2. **Count, muster, take the sum of** — the technical idiom of the census lists, where *"lift up the
   head of all the congregation"* means take a roll (Exodus 30:12; Numbers 1:2). This is the sense at
   Genesis 40:20.
3. **Behead** — and this one is not an independent idiom at all. **It exists only because Genesis 40:19
   makes it**, by adding ***mēʿāleykā***, *from off you*, to the phrase of 40:13.

**The chapter runs all three past the reader in eight verses.**

> **13** Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head, and restore you to your office. …
> **19** Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you, and will hang you on a
> tree; …
> **20** On the third day, which was Pharaoh’s birthday, he made a feast for all his servants, and he
> lifted up the head of the chief cup bearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants.

**Verse 19 is verse 13 with two words added, and the two words are the difference between a career and a
corpse.** [[Joseph]] gives the baker the identical opening formula, the identical interval, and the
identical royal subject, **and lets the sentence turn on a preposition.** It is the cruellest wordplay in
Scripture and it is not cruelty: it is the shape the answer came in.

**Then verse 20 uses the phrase a third time, and it is the census sense.** *"Among his servants"* —
***bətôk ʿăbādāyw*** — is the construction of a muster; **Pharaoh reviews his staff at his birthday
feast and both men are counted back in.** The verse holds the ambiguity for one more clause than it needs
to, and only verses 21–22 split them: *"he restored the chief cup bearer… but he hanged the chief
baker."* **The reader is made to wait through the neutral sense to find out which of the other two was
meant for whom.**

**The idiom's other great appearance closes the history of the monarchy.** 2 Kings ends with four verses
about a prisoner:

> **27** In the thirty-seventh year of the captivity of Jehoiachin king of Judah… Evil Merodach king of
> Babylon, in the year that he began to reign, lifted up the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah out of
> prison. (2 Kings 25:27)

**A Judean king in a foreign prison is lifted up, given a change of clothes, and seated at a foreign
king's table for the rest of his life** (25:28–30) — **which is, clause for clause, what happens to the
cupbearer of Genesis 40 and then to Joseph at 41:14, 42.** Jeremiah 52 repeats the passage verbatim to
close its own book. **The historians of Israel end the story of the kings on the same idiom Genesis used
in an Egyptian jail**, and they choose the favourable sense.

**The Psalms take the restoration sense and make it God's act.** *"You, Yahweh, are a shield around me,
my glory, and the one who lifts up my head"* (Psalm 3:3) — a psalm headed for the flight from Absalom,
**a man who has lost his place asking to be counted back in.** Psalm 27:6 and Psalm 110:7 run the same
way.

## Significance

**The thread is worth keeping because it is a case where the wordplay is the theology and not an
ornament.** Genesis 40 is asking whether the same act of a king can be salvation or execution depending
on nothing the recipient controls, **and it makes the Hebrew carry the question**: two men, one night,
one room, one dream apiece, one idiom, two verdicts. **Nothing in the chapter explains why one is
restored and the other hanged.** The offence is unstated, the trial is unreported, and the interpreter
has no stake in either outcome.

That is also why the idiom is the right one for **the last verses of 2 Kings**. In a history that has
taken heads literally — Ahab's seventy sons' delivered in baskets at 2 Kings 10:7–8 — **the book closes
on a captive whose head is lifted up by a foreign king who owed him nothing**: a small, unexplained
reversal of fortune in a prison, which is exactly the genre of Genesis 40. **The wiki notes the shape
and claims no literary dependence.** What it does claim is narrower and checkable: **the first Israelite
carried into a foreign country and the last king carried out of the land are both described with these
three words**, and in both places the phrase means a prisoner brought back to a table.
