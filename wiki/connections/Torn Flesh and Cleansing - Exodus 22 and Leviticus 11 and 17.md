# Torn Flesh and Cleansing - Exodus 22 and Leviticus 11 and 17

## Passages involved

- [[Laws of Holiness and Compassion]] — Exodus 22:31.
- [[Clean and Unclean Animals]] — Leviticus 11:39–40.
- [[The Place of Sacrifice and the Life in the Blood]] — Leviticus 17:15–16.
- [[A Holy People and Its Food]] — Deuteronomy 14:21.

## Analysis

**Apparent tension:** Exodus 22:31 forbids torn meat as part of Israel’s holiness
and assigns it to dogs. Leviticus 17:15 addresses someone who eats dead or torn flesh
and requires washing clothes, bathing, and uncleanness until evening. It includes
both natives and foreigners. Verse 16 assigns iniquity to failure to wash or bathe.

Leviticus 11:39–40 already treats touching, eating, or carrying the carcass of an
otherwise edible animal as producing impurity. Its eating clause names clothes
washing and evening, whereas 17:15 also specifies bathing. The later prescription
must not be silently read into the wording of the earlier one.

A purification rule can address the aftermath of an act without granting permission
to do it. That is a possible reconciliation with Exodus, not an explicit explanation
supplied by these verses. The wiki preserves both the prohibition and the remedy;
it does not declare either repealed.

### A third disposal — Deuteronomy 14:21

Deuteronomy forbids Israel to eat *"anything that dies of itself"* and adds: *"You may give it to the
foreigner living among you who is within your gates, that he may eat it; or you may sell it to a
foreigner; for you are a holy people to Yahweh your God."* The first foreigner is the *gēr*, the
second the *nokrî* (WLC 14:21, morphhb).

**This sits in tension with Leviticus 17:15**, which names the *gēr* alongside the native-born as
one who, having eaten what dies of itself or is torn, must wash, bathe, and be unclean until
evening (WLC, morphhb). Leviticus regulates the *gēr*'s eating; Deuteronomy provides the meat for
it. Exodus 22:31 sends torn meat to the dogs. The three texts are kept side by side.

A reconciliation often offered is that Deuteronomy's ban rests on Israel's holiness, which is not
laid on the *gēr*, while Leviticus deals with the impurity that eating still causes. That reading
is possible, but neither text states it. Deuteronomy also does not say the meat is torn, only that
it died of itself (*nəbēlâ*; Leviticus 17:15 has both *nəbēlâ* and *ṭərēpâ*, WLC, morphhb).
Source: stored WEB Deuteronomy 14:21; Leviticus 17:15.
