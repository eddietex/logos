# Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 24:3–4** — Abraham's oath: *"you shall not take a wife for my son of the daughters of the
  Canaanites … But you shall go to my country, and to my relatives"* — [[A Wife for Isaac]]
- **Genesis 26:34–35** — Esau, at forty, marries [[Judith]] and [[Basemath (daughter of Elon)]], both
  Hittites, and *"They grieved Isaac's and Rebekah's spirits"* — [[Isaac in Gerar]]
- **Genesis 27:46** — Rebekah uses that grief as her argument for sending Jacob away —
  [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]]
- **Genesis 28:1–2** — Isaac repeats his father's charge to Jacob, almost word for word —
  [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]]
- **Genesis 28:6–9** — Esau works out the rule from watching it applied to his brother, and marries
  a daughter of [[Ishmael]] — [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]]
- **Genesis 34** — Dinah, Shechem, and a proposal of general intermarriage answered with a massacre
  *(not yet ingested)*
- **Genesis 38:2; 41:45; 46:10** — Judah marries a Canaanite, Joseph an Egyptian priest's daughter,
  and Simeon has a son by a Canaanite woman: the rule broken three times inside the same book
- **Exodus 34:15–16; Deuteronomy 7:1–4** — the prohibition made law, with a reason attached
- **Judges 3:5–6; 1 Kings 11:1–8** — the reason demonstrated
- **Ezra 9–10; Nehemiah 13:23–27** — the prohibition enforced after the exile, at its harshest
- **Ruth 1:16–17; 4:13–22** — a Moabite woman married into the line of David, and the book's answer
- **Matthew 1:3, 5** — Tamar, Rahab, and Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus
- **1 Corinthians 7:12–16; 2 Corinthians 6:14** — the New Testament's two positions

## Analysis

**The first statement of the rule is an oath, not a law, and it is about one marriage.** Abraham does
not legislate; he binds a servant with a sanction, for a single errand, in his own household, and he
gives no reason at all. *"You shall not take a wife for my son of the daughters of the Canaanites,
among whom I live"* — and then the positive half, which is the operative one: *"go to my country, and
to my relatives."*

**Two things about the original form are worth holding onto, because everything downstream loses
them.**

**It is not stated as a religious rule.** Genesis 24 says nothing about idolatry, nothing about false
gods, and nothing about what a Canaanite wife would do to Isaac's faith. The reason Deuteronomy will
supply — *"for he will turn away your son from following me, that they may serve other gods"*
(7:4) — is not here. What Abraham says is *"among whom I live"*: they are the people of the country
he is a resident alien in, and the covenant line is not to dissolve into them. **The concern in
Genesis 24 is separateness, and the ground given for it is geography.**

**And the alternative he specifies is his own family**, which by later Israelite law would be a
problem in the other direction: Rebekah is Abraham's great-niece, Jacob marries two first cousins who
are also sisters, and Leviticus 18:18 forbids marrying two sisters. **The Torah's marriage law
prohibits both what Genesis 24 forbids and what it prescribes**, and Genesis never notices.

### The rule hardening

**Isaac restates it as a command** (28:1–2), and **the restatement drops the machinery and keeps the
rule**: no oath, no sanction, no release clause, no servant, no camels — and the one term Abraham
would not release, that his son must never make the journey, is reversed by that son for his own.
See [[Two Errands to Paddan Aram - Genesis 24 and Genesis 28]]. The trigger is a real grievance: Esau's two Hittite
wives, of whom Genesis says *"They grieved Isaac's and Rebekah's spirits"* — *mōrat rûaḥ*, a
**bitterness of spirit** (26:35) — and Rebekah's argument for sending Jacob away is that if he does
the same, *"what good will my life do me?"* (27:46). She is quoting Abraham's principle at Isaac to
solve a problem that is actually about Esau's knife. See [[Rebekah]] and [[Isaac in Gerar]].

**And 26:34–35 is where the rule first visibly fails inside the family.** Esau is forty — the age his
father was when a servant fetched him a wife from four hundred miles away under this very oath
(24:3; 25:20) — and he marries two women of the neighbourhood with no journey, no negotiation, and no
consent scene. **Genesis reports the marriages in one sentence and their effect in another, and
passes no verdict on him**, exactly as it will later pass none on Judah and Joseph. His own attempt
to correct it, at 28:9, is to marry a daughter of [[Ishmael]] — joining the two lines the covenant
had passed over.

**Then the book breaks it.** Judah marries *"the daughter of a certain Canaanite"* (38:2) and the
line of David runs through his son by Tamar; Joseph marries Asenath, daughter of the priest of On
(41:45), and the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh descend from her. **Genesis reports both without
censure**, in the same book that opened the theme with an oath.

**Exodus and Deuteronomy turn it into law, and supply the reason Genesis omitted.** *"Neither shall
you make marriages with them … for he will turn away your son from following me"* (Deuteronomy
7:3–4). The prohibition is now national, named to seven peoples, and explicitly theological.
**Judges 3:5–6 reports the law being ignored in a single verse**, and 1 Kings 11:1–8 shows the reason
cashed out on the largest possible scale: Solomon's wives *"turned away his heart after other gods."*

**Ezra and Nehemiah enforce it hardest, and cite this history as their warrant.** Ezra 9–10 ends with
foreign wives and their children sent away; Nehemiah 13:26 makes the argument explicitly from
Solomon — *"Did not Solomon king of Israel sin by these things?"* **These are the passages in
Scripture that most directly cite the principle and are hardest to read**, and it is worth being
exact about what they are: an emergency measure taken by a small returned community that had been
nearly extinguished, argued from a stated theological danger, and reported by the text with no
divine speech endorsing it.

**And Scripture argues back.** The book of Ruth marries a Moabite — a nation excluded by name to the
tenth generation (Deuteronomy 23:3) — into the ancestry of David, and gives her the most complete
statement of covenant loyalty in the Hebrew Bible: *"your people will be my people, and your God my
God"* (1:16). Rahab of Jericho is the other case. **Matthew's genealogy names both of them, plus
Tamar, in four verses** (1:3–6), which is a deliberate choice by a writer who could have left every
woman out.

### The New Testament

Two positions, and they are about different things. **1 Corinthians 7:12–16** tells believers already
married to unbelievers to stay: *"the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the wife."* **2 Corinthians
6:14** — *"Don't be unequally yoked with unbelievers"* — is a general instruction about partnership
whose application to marriage is traditional rather than stated. The New Testament's operative
category is **faith rather than nation**, which is the change that makes Galatians 3:28 possible and
which the Ruth material had already anticipated.

## Significance

**The thread shows a principle changing its reason.** Abraham gives none; Deuteronomy gives idolatry;
Ezra gives survival; Paul gives shared allegiance. The rule looks constant and its grounds are not,
and **reading the later reason back into Genesis 24 is the commonest thing done with this passage and
is not supported by the passage.**

**It also shows Scripture arguing with itself in public.** Genesis states the rule and then narrates
Judah and Joseph breaking it with no comment. Deuteronomy excludes Moab and Ruth is a Moabite.
Nehemiah cites Solomon and Matthew cites Ruth and Rahab. **The wiki flags this rather than harmonising
it**, per the schema: the texts are not doing the same thing, and the ones that are hardest to read
are not cancelled by the ones that are easiest.

What Genesis 24 itself establishes is narrower and stranger than what it is usually quoted for: **a
man who has been promised a country tells his servant that his son must not marry into it, and must
never under any circumstances leave it.** The line is to live among the Canaanites, own nothing but a
grave, and marry four hundred miles away. See [[Land and Sojourning]] and [[The Nations]].
