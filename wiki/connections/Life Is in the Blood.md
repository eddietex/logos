# Life Is in the Blood

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:3–4 — *"Every moving thing that lives will be food for you… But flesh with its life,
  that is, its blood, you shall not eat"* ([[The Covenant with Noah]])
- Leviticus 3:17; 7:26–27 — the prohibition as a *"perpetual statute throughout your generations"*,
  with excision as the penalty
- Leviticus 17:10–14 — the reason stated: *"the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood
  that makes atonement by reason of the life"*
- Leviticus 19:26 — *"You shall not eat any meat with the blood still in it"*
- Deuteronomy 12:15–16, 20–25 — the rule repeated three times as slaughter for food is permitted
  away from the sanctuary: *"Only be sure that you don't eat the blood; for the blood is the life"*
- 1 Samuel 14:31–35 — Saul's troops eat *"with the blood"*, and he calls it sin
- Ezekiel 33:25 — *"You eat with the blood… and shall you possess the land?"*
- Acts 15:20, 29 — the Jerusalem council's four requirements for Gentile believers
- Acts 21:25 — the four repeated to Paul, years later, as still standing
- Hebrews 9:22 — *"apart from shedding of blood there is no remission"*

## Analysis

**One prohibition, given three times to three different constituencies.** The rule against
consuming blood is unusual in Scripture for being addressed first to all humanity, then to Israel,
and then — alone among the food laws — to Gentile Christians. Following it across those three
settings is the clearest way to see what the Bible thinks blood is.

**Genesis 9:4 — to everyone, before there is an Israel.** The prohibition arrives attached to a
permission. Humanity may now eat meat, which it could not before (1:29–30); the one reservation is
the blood. And the Hebrew makes the reservation a definition rather than a rule: *bāśār bənapšô
dāmô*, flesh with its ***nepeš***, its blood. *Nepeš* is the word for a living creature's life — the
same word Genesis 2:7 uses of the first man. The blood is not a container for the life; the verse
apposes the two nouns directly. So the sentence is not arbitrary dietary hygiene. It says: the flesh
is yours, the life is not, and blood is where the life is. See [[Blood]].

Nothing in Genesis 9 is addressed to Israel, because there is no Israel. Everything in it is
addressed to the eight people from whom the whole earth is populated. That is what will make this
particular rule portable.

**Leviticus 17:11 — to Israel, with the reason spelled out.** The verse that explains Genesis 9:4
also explains the entire sacrificial system, and it does both in one breath:

> For the life of the flesh is in the blood. I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement
> for your souls; for it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life.

Read it as two halves of a single argument. Because blood is life, blood is not food. Because blood
is life, blood on an altar can stand in for a life that is owed. The prohibition and the sacrificial
mechanism are the same proposition — that a creature's life belongs to God — running in opposite
directions. Which means Israel's blood law is not a food rule with a ritual appendix. It is a
statement about ownership, and the kitchen and the altar are two places it shows.

Deuteronomy 12 then applies it to the practical problem of a people spread across a land with one
sanctuary: you may slaughter and eat wherever you live, *"only be sure that you don't eat the blood."*
Three times in eleven verses. The frequency is a good index of how load-bearing the rule was and
probably of how hard it was to keep.

**Acts 15 — to Gentiles, when nearly everything else is set aside.** The Jerusalem council's
decision is that Gentile believers need not be circumcised or keep the law of Moses, with four
exceptions: *"that they abstain from the pollution of idols, from sexual immorality, from things
strangled, and from blood."* Two of those four are Genesis 9:4 — a strangled animal is one killed
without draining. The council that removed the entire food code kept the blood rule.

Why those four? Two explanations, and they lead different places:

| | The Noahic reading | The Leviticus 17–18 reading |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | the council kept what predates Sinai and so binds everyone | the four match the laws Leviticus 17–18 imposes on the *gēr*, the resident alien |
| Strength | explains why *these* four, and why they are stated as moral rather than ceremonial | tighter textual fit: those chapters cover idol sacrifice, blood, carrion, and sexual union, in that order |
| Weakness | Genesis 9 does not mention idols or sexual immorality; the seven Noahide laws are a later rabbinic construction | makes the decree about table fellowship in mixed congregations, which does not obviously explain why it was still binding in Acts 21:25 |

The two are not fully exclusive — Leviticus 17:10–12 itself extends the blood prohibition explicitly
to *"any stranger who lives as a foreigner among you"*, which is arguably the law codifying what
Genesis 9 already assumed. Either way, the reason the New Testament's one dietary requirement for
Gentiles is a rule about blood traces back through Leviticus to a verse spoken to Noah.

**And the reason it could not simply be dropped.** By the time Acts 15 is written the Christian
movement has made blood the centre of its account of salvation: *"apart from shedding of blood there
is no remission"* (Hebrews 9:22), *"this is my blood of the new covenant"* (Mark 14:24). A community
holding that blood atones because blood is life is not well placed to treat blood as an ordinary
foodstuff. The prohibition and the doctrine come from the same premise, and the premise is Leviticus
17:11 glossing Genesis 9:4.

**Afterlives.** The rule was taken as binding by the early church well past the apostolic period —
Tertullian argues from it that Christians accused of drinking blood cannot even eat animal blood
(*Apology* 9), and the Eastern Orthodox canons retain it. Western Christianity largely let it lapse,
and Augustine's view that the decree was a temporary measure for the sake of Jewish-Gentile unity
became the standard Latin reading. Among modern groups the Jehovah's Witnesses extend the
prohibition to blood transfusion, on the reasoning that Genesis 9:4 forbids taking blood into the
body by any route. That extension is a reading of the rule's purpose rather than of its wording:
Genesis, Leviticus, and Acts all address blood as food, and Leviticus 17's stated reason is that
blood belongs on the altar, which does not obviously reach a transfusion. It is recorded here
because it is the one place the argument over this verse still has consequences.

## Significance

The Bible's oldest food law is not really a food law. It is the earliest statement of the principle
that a creature's life is God's property, given as a limit on a permission — humanity may kill for
food, and may not treat the life it takes as its own. Everything the Old Testament does at an altar
depends on that principle, and Leviticus 17:11 says so directly.

It is also the clearest case in Scripture of a command given before Israel and outlasting it. When
the Jerusalem council had to decide what applies to people who are not under Moses, it reached past
Sinai for something older, and what it found there was Genesis 9.
