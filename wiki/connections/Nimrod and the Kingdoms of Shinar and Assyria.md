# Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 6:4** — *"the mighty men who were of old, men of renown"*: the only *gibbōrîm* before the
  flood. See [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- **Genesis 10:8–12** — Nimrod: *gibbōr*, hunter, and founder of Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh in
  Shinar, and — on the WEB's reading of 10:11 — of Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah, and Resen in Assyria.
  See [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** — the city and tower in Shinar, in which Nimrod is not mentioned; the passage
  names nobody at all. See [[The Tower of Babel]].
- **1 Chronicles 1:10** — the Chronicler keeps the Nimrod sentence and drops nothing else in the
  chapter but the geography.
- **Micah 5:6** — *"They will rule the land of Assyria with the sword, and the land of Nimrod in its
  gates."*
- **2 Kings 17:5–23; 18–19; Daniel 1:1–2** — what those two kingdoms eventually do to Israel and to
  Judah.

## Analysis

**The first kingdom in the Bible is founded by a man described with a pre-flood word.** *Gibbōr*
appears at Genesis 6:4, of the offspring produced by the sons of God — the sentence immediately before
God resolves to blot out humanity — and then not again until Genesis 10:8, four verses into the roster
of the post-flood world: *"He began to be a mighty one in the earth."* Genesis draws no line between
the two, and it does not need to. The flood was supposed to have ended the world of the *gibbōrîm*.
The first man singled out for description on the far side of it is one. See [[Nimrod]] and
[[The Nephilim and the Giants of Canaan]].

**And the cities he founds are the ones that will end both kingdoms of Israel.** Babel is Babylon.
Nineveh and Calah are the two great capitals of Assyria. If 10:11's subject is Nimrod — which is how
the WEB, the Septuagint, the Vulgate, and the Targums take it — then Genesis has assigned the
foundation of both empires of the exile to one man, in two verses, before Abram has been called.
Assyria deports the northern kingdom in 722 (2 Kings 17); Babylon takes Judah and the temple vessels
*"into the land of Shinar"* (Daniel 1:2). See [[Assyria]], [[Nineveh]], [[Babel]], [[Shinar]].

The alternative reading — *"out of that land went forth Asshur"*, with Shem's son of 10:22 as the
subject — is grammatically available and is what the KJV prints. It splits the two empires between two
ancestries and makes 10:11 a migration note. Nothing in the Hebrew settles it, and the wiki keeps
both.

**Micah 5:6 is the one place in Scripture that appears to settle it.** In an oracle about deliverance
from the Assyrian, Micah names the enemy twice in a single line — *"the land of Assyria"* and *"the
land of Nimrod"* — as parallel designations for one territory. That is the only occurrence of the
name outside the Table and its Chronicles copy, and it presupposes exactly the reading in which
Nimrod's realm reached north. Whether Micah is interpreting Genesis or drawing on the same tradition
cannot be determined.

**The reception history goes much further than the text, and it goes there early.** Genesis says
Nimrod began to be mighty, was a proverbial hunter *"before Yahweh"*, and founded cities. It does not
call him wicked, does not connect him to the tower — Genesis 11 names nobody — and does not say what
he hunted. But *lipnê YHWH*, **before Yahweh**, can be read as *in defiance of*, and on that hinge the
whole later portrait turns:

- **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 1.113–114) has Nimrod turn the people from God toward himself and build
  the tower so that a second flood could not reach them.
- ***Targum Pseudo-Jonathan*** and ***Genesis Rabbah*** make him the archetypal tyrant, a hunter of
  men, and Abraham's persecutor — the furnace of Ur is his in the midrash.
- **Jerome and Augustine** pass the rebel reading into Christian exegesis; Augustine has him a giant
  and the tower's builder (*City of God* 16.3–4).
- **Dante** puts him at the bottom of hell, speaking nonsense, punished with the confusion he caused
  (*Inferno* 31).

The usual etymological support — *nimrōd* from *mārad*, **to rebel**, giving *let us rebel* — does not
work as Hebrew morphology, and the identification of Nimrod with a historical king (Sargon of Akkad,
Naram-Sin, Tukulti-Ninurta I) or with the god Ninurta each explains part of the profile and none of
it.

## Significance

Two things this thread is good for, and one it is not.

**It shows Genesis placing empire before it narrates it.** The Table of Nations is not the kind of text
that argues; it lists. But by putting the first *kingdom* in Scripture at Babel, giving it a founder
who carries the vocabulary of the generation the flood destroyed, and running his conquests up to
Nineveh, Genesis 10 has said what it thinks about imperial power without stating a verdict. When the
prophets spend chapters on Babylon and Nineveh, they are not introducing a theme. They are finishing
one.

**It shows how much of a familiar biblical figure can be extrabiblical.** Almost everything most
readers know about Nimrod — rebel, tyrant, tower-builder, giant, hunter of men — comes from Josephus
and the midrash rather than from the five verses. The wiki keeps the two apart on principle: the text
gives a builder of cities whom a proverb remembered, and the rest is interpretation with a traceable
history.

**What it is not:** evidence that Genesis 10 and Genesis 11 are one narrative with a named villain.
Genesis 11:1–9 has no protagonist at all — *"they said to one another"* — and the anonymity is
probably deliberate. Reading Nimrod into it is the oldest interpretive move made on this passage, and
it is still a move.
