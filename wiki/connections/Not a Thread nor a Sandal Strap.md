# Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap

## Passages involved

- Genesis 14:21–24 — Abram refuses the king of Sodom's goods
- Genesis 12:14–20 — the wealth he took from Pharaoh, and what it cost
- Genesis 13:2, 5–7 — that wealth splitting the household
- Genesis 15:1 — *"I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward"*
- Joshua 6:18–19; 7:1, 20–26 — Achan and the devoted spoil
- 1 Samuel 12:3–5 — Samuel's audit of his own tenure
- 1 Samuel 30:21–25 — David's rule about the plunder
- 2 Kings 5:15–27 — Naaman's gift refused by Elisha and taken by Gehazi
- Daniel 5:17 — *"Let your gifts be to yourself"*
- Acts 8:18–24; 20:33–35; 1 Corinthians 9:12–18; 2 Corinthians 11:7–12 — refusing payment in the
  New Testament

## Analysis

**The refusal is the second half of a pair, and the first half is two chapters back.** In Egypt
Abram tells a half-truth about his wife and is paid for it: *"He dealt well with Abram for her sake.
He had sheep, cattle, male donkeys, male servants, female servants, female donkeys, and camels"*
(12:16). That wealth becomes *"very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold"* (13:2), which is
what the land *"was not able to bear"* (13:6), which is what splits the household and sends Lot east
to Sodom — which is why Abram is chasing four kings across Syria in the first place. Genesis never
draws the line. It simply arranges the verses so that the reader can.

Then a second foreign king offers him a second fortune, and this time:

> **22** *"I have lifted up my hand to Yahweh, God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth,*
> **23** *that I will not take a thread nor a sandal strap nor anything that is yours, lest you
> should say, 'I have made Abram rich.'"*

**The idiom names the two cheapest things a person carries.** A thread and a sandal strap are the
merism for *anything whatever*; the point is not that he will decline the big items but that he has
set the line at zero. And the reason he gives is not about wealth being wrong. It is about **credit**:
*lest you should say, "I have made Abram rich."* He has been promised *"I will bless you and make
your name great"* (12:2), and he will not have a king of Sodom in a position to claim he did it.

**Three refinements keep it from being simple piety.**

- **He had decided beforehand.** *I have lifted up my hand* is a perfect: the oath was sworn before
  the offer was made. See [[Oaths and Vows]].
- **He does not impose it on anyone else.** *"I will accept nothing from you except that which the
  young men have eaten, and the portion of the men who went with me: Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre. Let
  them take their portion"* (14:24). His allies did not swear his oath and are not bound by it.
- **He takes from the other king in the same scene.** Melchizedek's bread and wine are accepted
  without hesitation, and Melchizedek's blessing with them; it is the king of Sodom's money he will
  not touch. The chapter distinguishes between what may be received and from whom.

**Scripture keeps returning to the same test, with the results going both ways.**

| | Offered | Answer | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abram, Genesis 14 | all the goods of Sodom | *"not a thread nor a sandal strap"* | *"I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward"* (15:1) |
| Achan, Joshua 7 | devoted spoil at Jericho | took a robe, silver, and gold | Israel routed at Ai; Achan destroyed |
| Samuel, 1 Samuel 12 | invites an audit | *"Whose ox have I taken?"* | *"You have not defrauded us"* |
| Elisha, 2 Kings 5 | Naaman's talents and garments | *"As Yahweh lives … I will receive none"* | Gehazi takes them and takes the leprosy |
| Daniel, Daniel 5 | purple, a gold chain, third rule | *"Let your gifts be to yourself"* | reads the writing anyway |
| Simon, Acts 8 | offers money for the Spirit | *"May your silver perish with you"* | — |
| Paul, 1 Corinthians 9 | a right he holds and won't use | works with his hands | *"that I may not abuse my authority in the Good News"* |

**The reward that answers it comes one verse later.** *"After these things Yahweh's word came to
Abram in a vision, saying, 'Don't be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great
reward'"* (15:1). *Śākār*, **wages** or **payment** — the exact category Abram has just declined
from a man. Genesis does not say the vision is a reply. It puts it in the next verse.

**And the refused word comes back as a promise.** The goods Abram will not take are *rəkūš*, the
noun Genesis 14 uses four times for the plunder (14:11, 12, 16, 21). Thirteen verses later his
offspring are promised that they *"will come out with great wealth"* — *rəkūš gādôl* (15:14) — from
another foreign king's country, and Exodus 12:35–36 pays it out in silver, gold, and clothing. He
declines *rəkūš* so that no man can say *"I have made Abram rich"*; the same commodity arrives from
the one source about which nobody could say it. See
[[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].

## Significance

Genesis 14 is the first chapter in which Abram behaves better than his circumstances required, and
it is the mirror image of Genesis 12: same setup, foreign king with money, and the opposite answer.
Nothing in the text says he learned it from Egypt. The two chapters are three verses of narrative
apart and shaped so alike that the comparison is unavoidable, and Genesis, as usual, arranges the
material and declines to draw the conclusion.

See [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]], [[Abram in Egypt]], [[Abraham]], [[Bera]], and
[[Oaths and Vows]].

## The grandson's version — Genesis 30:31

**Jacob uses the same formula on Laban and means something different by it.**

> **30:31** *"What shall I give you?" Jacob said, "**You shall not give me anything.** If you will do
> this thing for me, I will again feed your flock and keep it."*

***Lōʾ-titten-lî məʾûmâ*** against Abram's ***ʾim-miḥûṭ wəʿad śərôk-naʿal wəʾim-ʾeqqaḥ***. **Both are
refusals of a patron's payment by a man who is about to become rich anyway**, and **both are about
attribution** — Abram will not let a king say *"I have made Abram rich"*, and Jacob will not take a
wage his employer can later revalue. **The difference is what follows the refusal.** Abram walks away
with nothing but his allies' share; **Jacob names an alternative in the same breath and then
engineers it** (30:32–43). See [[Jacob's Flocks]].

**Genesis draws no line between them.** The formula travels two generations, keeps its logic, and
loses its self-denial, and the book does not remark on either half.
