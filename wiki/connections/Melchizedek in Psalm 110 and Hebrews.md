# Melchizedek in Psalm 110 and Hebrews

## Passages involved

- Genesis 14:18–20 — the priest-king of Salem, the bread and wine, the blessing, the tithe
- Psalm 110:1–4 — *"You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek"*
- Hebrews 5:5–10; 6:19–20; 7:1–28 — the extended argument
- Joshua 10:1 — *Adoni-Zedek king of Jerusalem*, the matching royal name
- Psalm 76:2 — Salem and Zion in parallel
- 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), Qumran — Melchizedek as a heavenly deliverer
- Zechariah 6:11–13 — a priest on a throne, *"the counsel of peace shall be between them both"*

## Analysis

**Three verses, then a thousand years of silence, then one line of a psalm.** Genesis 14:18–20 is
the only narrative about Melchizedek in the Bible. Nothing in the Torah, the Former Prophets, or the
Latter Prophets returns to him. Then Psalm 110:4 — *"Yahweh has sworn, and will not change his mind:
'You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek'"* — addressed to the king in Jerusalem, and
sworn.

What the psalm does is convert an individual into a **category**. Genesis has one man who happened
to hold two offices in one city. Psalm 110 has an *order*, *ʿal-dibrātî*, a manner or arrangement,
and hands it to a Davidic king — which solves a real problem for the monarchy. Israel keeps
kingship and priesthood apart, and punishes the kings who cross the line (Saul at Gilgal, 1 Samuel
13:8–14; Uzziah in the temple, 2 Chronicles 26:16–21). A king in Jerusalem who wants a priestly
role cannot have Aaron's. He can, however, claim the priesthood that was already on that site before
Aaron existed — if Salem is Jerusalem, which Psalm 76:2 says it is. See [[Salem]].

**Hebrews then presses on exactly what Genesis omits.** The argument in chapter 7 has four moves,
and each is an argument from the shape of Genesis 14 rather than from a statement in it:

| Hebrews | The move | What in Genesis 14 it uses |
|---|---|---|
| 7:1–3 | *"without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life"* — therefore an image of a priest who does not die | The absence of a genealogy in a book made of genealogies |
| 7:2 | *King of righteousness*, then *king of peace* | The etymologies of *Malkî-ṣedeq* and *Shālēm* |
| 7:4–7 | *"the lesser is blessed by the greater"* — so Melchizedek outranks Abraham | 14:19, the blessing's direction |
| 7:9–10 | Levi *"has paid tithes"* through Abraham, being still in his body | 14:20, the tithe |

The first is explicitly an argument from silence, and Hebrews knows it: *"made like the Son of God"*
(7:3) is a comparison drawn from the record, not a claim that Melchizedek was eternal. The
conclusion is that a priesthood which existed before Levi and is sworn to be permanent (Psalm
110:4's oath, quoted at 7:20–22) is not superseded by Levi's and does not need succession —
*"because he lives forever, has his priesthood unchangeable"* (7:24).

**The chain of custody matters more than any single link.** Genesis reports an encounter and grades
nothing. Psalm 110 makes it a royal-priestly category. Hebrews makes the category christological.
Each step is legitimate on its own terms, and each adds something the previous one did not say. This
wiki treats Hebrews as canonical interpretation rather than as a description of what Genesis 14 was
doing when it was written — the two are different claims, and collapsing them makes the Old
Testament text say less than it does, not more.

**And the New Testament is not the only place the silence was filled.** 11QMelchizedek, a
first-century BC Qumran text independent of Hebrews, casts him as a heavenly figure who proclaims
the release of the final jubilee, executes God's judgment on Belial's lot, and has Psalm 82:1's
*ʾĕlōhîm* applied to him. 2 Enoch supplies a miraculous birth. Rabbinic tradition (*Nedarim* 32b)
goes the other way and identifies him with [[Shem]], who by Genesis 11's numbers outlives Abram —
reading 14:19–20 as the moment priesthood passed from Shem's line to Abram's, because he blessed
Abram before he blessed God. Four traditions, one blank space. See [[Melchizedek]].

## Significance

The chapter's own logic does not need any of this to work: Genesis 14 is a story about a man who
recovers his nephew and refuses to be paid, and the priest is one of two kings who meet him. But the
canon spends more words on Melchizedek than Genesis does, and it spends them on what Genesis leaves
out. He is the clearest case in Scripture of an argument built on an absence — no father, no
genealogy, no death — and of a foreigner outside the covenant line whose office the covenant line
later borrows to describe its own king, and then its Messiah.
