# Put Your Hand Under My Thigh

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 24:2–3, 9** — Abraham and his servant, on the marriage of Isaac — [[A Wife for Isaac]]
- **Genesis 47:29–31** — Jacob and Joseph, on the burial of Jacob *(not yet ingested)*
- Compare **Genesis 21:22–34** — the Bible's first oath between two men, sworn with no gesture but
  with seven ewe lambs — [[The Treaty at Beersheba]]
- Compare **Genesis 14:22** — *"I have lifted up my hand to Yahweh"*: the ordinary gesture
- Compare **Genesis 15:9–18** — the enacted oath, walking between the pieces —
  [[Passing Between the Pieces]]
- Background: **Genesis 17:9–14, 23–27** — the covenant sign, cut into the flesh in question
- Background: **Exodus 1:5** — *"all the souls who came out of Jacob's thigh"*, the same word

## Analysis

**The gesture occurs twice in the Bible and both times in Genesis**, and both times an old man near
death asks a subordinate to guarantee something he will not live to see.

> **24:2** “Please put your hand under my thigh.
> **24:3** I will make you swear by Yahweh, the God of heaven and the God of the earth …”

> **47:29** “If now I have found favor in your sight, please put your hand under my thigh, and deal
> kindly and truly with me. Please don’t bury me in Egypt”

***Yārēk*** is the upper thigh, and in this construction it is a euphemism for the genitals — the
same usage as *"the souls who came out of Jacob's thigh"* (Exodus 1:5) and *"seventy sons … came out
of his thigh"* (Judges 8:30). Nobody has seriously proposed otherwise; the rabbinic and patristic
commentators say so plainly, and the modern translations preserve the Hebrew euphemism rather than
inventing a new one.

**Why there is not stated, and three explanations are standing.**

- **The covenant sign.** Circumcision is cut into this flesh (17:9–14), and the household in question
  is the first one circumcised. Swearing on it is swearing on the mark of the covenant — which is
  the strongest reading for Genesis 24, where the whole object of the oath is to keep the covenant
  line unmixed. See [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].
- **The offspring.** *Yārēk* is Genesis's own metonym for descent. An oath sworn there is sworn on
  the descendants who will be affected by it and who will avenge its breach — which suits both
  cases, since both are about what happens to a family after the swearer is dead.
- **A sanction on the swearer's own line.** On this reading the gesture puts the oath-breaker's
  posterity at risk, which is what an oath needs and what neither text supplies in words.

**None of the three is stated by the text, and they are not exclusive.** The wiki records all three
and adopts none.

### What the two oaths have in common

**Both are administered by a patriarch at the end of his life, to a man of the next generation, about
a country.**

| | Genesis 24 | Genesis 47 |
|---|---|---|
| who swears | the unnamed servant, a slave | Joseph, a son and a vizier |
| what is sworn | not a Canaanite wife; and **Isaac must never go back to Mesopotamia** | not an Egyptian grave; **Jacob must be carried back to Canaan** |
| the danger | absorption by the country lived in | absorption by the country lived in |
| the vocabulary | *ḥesed we-ʾemet* — *"deal kindly and truly"* (24:49) | *ḥesed we-ʾemet* — *"deal kindly and truly with me"* (47:29) |
| the outcome | Rebekah brought to Canaan | Jacob carried to Machpelah (50:12–13) |

**The shared phrase is not a coincidence.** *ḥesed we-ʾemet*, loving kindness and truth, appears in
the servant's mouth at 24:27 and 24:49 and in Jacob's at 47:29 — three of its earliest occurrences in
Scripture, all inside these two scenes, and it is the pair God will use of himself at Exodus 34:6.
**The oath with this gesture is the oath that asks for covenant loyalty by name.**

**And the two oaths are mirror images across the same border.** One forbids the son to go back east;
the other requires the father's body to go back west. **Both are about a family in a country that is
not theirs, refusing to be ended by it** — the first by marriage, the second by burial. See
[[Land and Sojourning]] and [[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].

### The other oath gestures in Genesis

Genesis is unusually interested in how an oath is made physical, and this is one of four methods it
reports:

- **The raised hand** — *"I have lifted up my hand to Yahweh"* (14:22), the ordinary and much the
  commonest gesture in Scripture (Deuteronomy 32:40; Ezekiel 20:5; Revelation 10:5), used by God of
  himself.
- **The animals cut in half** — 15:9–18, the oath enacted rather than spoken, with the sanction shown
  and not said. See [[Passing Between the Pieces]].
- **The seven ewe lambs** — 21:28–31, an object appointed to outlive the memory of the agreement, and
  a place named for it. See [[The Treaty at Beersheba]].
- **The hand under the thigh** — here.

**All four appear in the Abraham cycle**, and the last has no attested parallel outside these two
Genesis passages, in Scripture or in the surrounding legal literature.

## Significance

**It is a small and very concrete piece of evidence about how the patriarchal narratives think about
continuity.** The promise is offspring and land; the two oaths sworn on this gesture are about a
marriage and a grave; and the flesh sworn on is the flesh the covenant sign is cut into and the flesh
the offspring come out of. **The gesture ties the three together without a word of explanation**,
which is the method the whole cycle uses.

It also marks the two moments Genesis chooses to end a patriarch on. **Abraham's last recorded act is
administering this oath** — he does not appear again in the book after 24:9 except to die — and
Jacob's, nearly, is the same. Neither man lives to see either oath kept; both are kept. See
[[Oaths and Vows]].
