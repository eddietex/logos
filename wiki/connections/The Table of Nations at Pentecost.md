# The Table of Nations at Pentecost

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 10:1–32** — seventy peoples, divided *"everyone after his language, after their families,
  in their nations"* (10:5, 20, 31). See [[The Table of Nations]].
- **Genesis 11:1–9** *(not yet ingested)* — one language, one plain, one tower; the languages confused
  and the builders scattered.
- **Acts 2:5–11** — *"devout men, from every nation under the sky"*, each hearing the disciples
  *"speaking in our languages the mighty works of God"*, with a list of fifteen peoples and regions.
- **Revelation 5:9; 7:9; 14:6** — *"every tribe, language, people, and nation"*.

## Analysis

**Luke writes a table of nations of his own.** Acts 2:9–11 gives fifteen entries: Parthians, Medes,
Elamites, and dwellers in Mesopotamia, Judea, Cappadocia, Pontus, Asia, Phrygia, Pamphylia, Egypt, the
parts of Libya around Cyrene, visitors from Rome (both Jews and proselytes), Cretans, and Arabians. The
list has puzzled commentators because it is not a sensible itinerary, not an administrative catalogue
of Roman provinces, and not in geographical order — Judea sits in the middle of it, which is odd for a
list of places people came *from* to Jerusalem. What it does read like is a table: an attempt at
*everywhere*, organized as a rough sweep from east to west and back.

**The overlaps with Genesis 10 are direct.** *Elamites* is Elam, Shem's firstborn (10:22). *Medes* is
Madai, Japheth's third son (10:2). *Mesopotamia* is the region of Shinar and Asshur (10:10–11, 22).
*Egypt* is Mizraim (10:6). *Libya* is Put (10:6). *Cretans* is Caphtorim (10:14). *Arabians* is the
Joktanite south of 10:26–30. And *Asia*, *Phrygia*, *Pamphylia*, *Pontus*, and *Cappadocia* are the
Anatolian territory the Table assigns to Gomer, Tubal, Meshech, and Togarmah. Luke's fifteen are not a
copy of Genesis's seventy, but they land inside its map at point after point, and a reader who knows
Genesis 10 hears the genre before hearing the geography.

**What is reversed, and what is not.** The common formulation — *Pentecost is the reversal of Babel* —
is right in one respect and misleading in two.

- **Right:** Babel's confusion was a judgment that made people unintelligible to each other and
  scattered them; at Pentecost people from everywhere understand at once. The barrier that was raised
  comes down.
- **Misleading, first:** the languages are not abolished. Nobody at Pentecost reverts to one speech.
  Each hears *"in our own language in which we were born"* — the plurality is kept and made
  transparent. If Babel were simply undone, the crowd would speak Hebrew. The gift runs the other way,
  toward the hearers, in their own tongues.
- **Misleading, second:** the scattering is not reversed either. Acts 8:1 scatters the church again,
  this time deliberately, and the rest of the book follows the scattering outward to Rome. Babel's
  dispersal is not cancelled; it is repurposed.

So the better description is that Acts 2 answers **Genesis 10** at least as much as Genesis 11.
Genesis 10 is the roster of peoples, distinguished by language, held together by a common ancestor and
nothing else. Acts 2 gathers a sample of that roster, leaves every distinction in place, and adds the
thing Genesis 10 conspicuously lacks: God speaking. The Table records the nations in thirty-two verses
without a single word from God. At Pentecost each nation hears *"the mighty works of God"* in its own
language.

**One more link Luke makes.** The Table's fourfold refrain — families, languages, lands, nations
(10:5, 20, 31) — becomes the fourfold formula of Revelation: *"every tribe, language, people, and
nation"*, sung five times over (5:9; 7:9; 10:11; 13:7; 14:6). The categories that Genesis 10 uses to
sort humanity are the categories the last book uses to describe worship, unchanged.

## Significance

The Bible does not treat human diversity as a problem to be solved. Genesis 10 lists it without
comment; Genesis 11 shows what happens when people try to prevent it; Acts 2 has God speak into it
rather than through it; and Revelation has it standing before the throne. The one point at which
Scripture narrates a *unified* humanity — one language, one place, one project — is the point at which
God intervenes to break it up.

Against that, the promise of Genesis 12:3 does its work: *"All the families of the earth will be
blessed through you"*, using Genesis 10's own word for families. The nations are not gathered by being
made one. They are blessed as many, which is what the crowd in Jerusalem discovers when the words
arrive in their own languages.
