# Righteous Lot - 2 Peter's Verdict and Genesis's Silence

## Passages involved

- Genesis 13:10–13; 14:12; 19:1–29; 19:30–38 — everything Genesis records about Lot
- Genesis 18:23–33 — *ṣaddîq* seven times in eleven verses, and never once of him
- Genesis 19:29 — the reason the rescue is given: *"God remembered Abraham"*
- Wisdom 10:6 — the deuterocanonical *"righteous man"* rescued from the fire
- 2 Peter 2:6–9 — *"righteous Lot"*, three times in two verses
- Luke 17:28–32 — *"the days of Lot"*, with no verdict on him at all

## Analysis

**Genesis never calls Lot righteous. 2 Peter calls him righteous three times in two sentences and
narrates nothing.** The wiki flags this rather than dissolving it, and the flag is worth stating
precisely, because both halves are stronger than the usual summary allows.

### What Genesis records

It is a list of actions with no adjectives attached.

| | |
|---|---|
| 13:10–11 | Offered the whole country, he takes the well-watered plain, *"traveled east"*, and leaves Canaan |
| 13:12 | *"moved his tent as far as Sodom"* — and 13:13 immediately: the men of Sodom were *"exceedingly wicked and sinners against Yahweh"* |
| 14:12 | *"Lot, Abram's brother's son, who lived in Sodom"* — carried off, recovered at the cost of a campaign, and he says nothing and goes back |
| 19:1 | *"Lot sat in the gate of Sodom"* |
| 19:2–3 | He bows to the ground and presses two strangers into his house rather than let them sleep in the square |
| 19:6–8 | He shuts the door behind him against the mob, and offers them his two daughters |
| 19:16 | *"But he lingered"* — and has to be taken out by the wrist |
| 19:18–20 | He refuses the terms of his rescue and bargains for a nearer town |
| 19:30 | He leaves that town anyway, *"for he was afraid"* |
| 19:31–38 | Made drunk by his daughters on two consecutive nights; fathers Moab and Ammon |

**Genesis grades none of it.** That is its method with Lot from the beginning, and it is the same
method it uses on Sarah's laugh and on Abraham in Egypt. The one evaluative clause in the whole
sequence is 19:16's *"Yahweh being merciful to him"*, which is a statement about God.

**And the omission at 18:23–33 is conspicuous.** *Ṣaddîq*, righteous, occurs seven times in eleven
verses in the chapter immediately preceding, in a negotiation about how many righteous the city
holds, conducted by Lot's uncle, about Lot's city. The word is never applied to Lot, and 19:29 gives
the rescue a different cause altogether: *"God remembered **Abraham**, and sent Lot out of the middle
of the overthrow."*

### What 2 Peter says

> …delivered righteous Lot, who was very distressed by the lustful life of the wicked (for that
> righteous man dwelling among them, was tormented in his righteous soul from day to day with seeing
> and hearing lawless deeds) — 2 Peter 2:7–8

Three occurrences of *dikaios* and its cognates in two verses (*dikaion Lōt*, *ho dikaios*, *psychēn
dikaian*), which is emphatic even by the standards of a letter that likes emphasis. And **the
argument the letter is making requires it.** 2 Peter 2:4–9 is a chain of three examples — the angels
who sinned, the flood with Noah, the cities with Lot — assembled to prove a single proposition
stated at 2:9: *"the Lord knows how to deliver the godly out of temptation and to keep the
unrighteous under punishment."* Lot is there as the rescued godly man, opposite Noah. **The verdict
is load-bearing for the argument; without it the third example does not work.**

### Where the verdict came from

Not from Genesis, and probably not invented by 2 Peter either. Wisdom of Solomon 10:6 already has
it: *"Wisdom rescued a righteous man when the ungodly were perishing"*, in a passage that then
describes the smoking wasteland and the pillar of salt. Philo's *De Abrahamo* runs the same line.
**By the first century the righteous-Lot reading was standing Hellenistic-Jewish tradition**, and 2
Peter is drawing on it rather than exegeting Genesis.

Luke 17:28–32, notably, does not. It uses *"the days of Lot"* as a time-marker for a catastrophe and
says nothing whatever about his character — and the person from that household it does single out is
his wife, against her. See [[Remember Lot's Wife]].

## Significance

**The honest description is that Genesis narrates without grading and 2 Peter grades without
narrating.** Both are canonical; they are not obviously the same portrait; and the wiki's practice
is to record the difference rather than to resolve it in either direction.

Three ways of holding them together are on offer, and none is compelled by the texts:

1. **The relative reading.** *Righteous* in 2 Peter is comparative — against the men of Sodom, a man
   who shelters guests and is distressed by what he sees is righteous, whatever else he is. This
   reads *ṣaddîq* the way Genesis 18 does, as a category rather than a character grade, and it fits
   6:9's *"righteous … among his contemporaries"* of Noah.
2. **The imputed reading.** Lot is delivered for Abraham's sake (19:29) and is called righteous
   because he was delivered, not the reverse. This has the merit of taking 19:29 at its word.
3. **The tradition reading.** 2 Peter reports what Second Temple Judaism said about Lot, and Genesis
   is simply not making the same kind of statement. This is the historically likeliest account of
   where the language came from and says nothing about whether it is right.

**What none of them can do is put an adjective into Genesis.** The book's silence about Lot is
consistent across eight chapters and is plainly deliberate: it is willing to call Noah *righteous*
(6:9; 7:1) and Abraham *blameless* (17:1) in so many words, and it declines to say anything of the
kind about the man it follows from Ur to a cave above Zoar.
