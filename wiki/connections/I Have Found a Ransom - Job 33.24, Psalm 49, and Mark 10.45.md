## Analysis

**The word.** *Kōper* is a payment that buys a life out of forfeit. In the law it is the price an owner
pays when his ox has killed and *"a ransom is laid on him"* (Exodus 21:30), and the half-shekel each man
gives *"for his soul"* at the census (Exodus 30:12). Numbers 35 refuses it for a murderer and for an early return from refuge
(35:31–32); those are distinct prohibitions. Its other sense is the *bribe* — the payment that buys a verdict — and Elihu himself
uses it that way three chapters later (Job 36:18). The word always means a life or a judgment that money
could change.

**Psalm 49 says no man can pay it.** *"None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give God a
ransom for him. For the redemption of their life is costly, no payment is ever enough"* (49:7–8). The
psalm is about the rich, who trust in wealth (49:6), and its point is that the one ransom that matters —
from death — is beyond every fortune. Then the turn: *"But God will redeem my soul from the power of
Sheol, for he will receive me"* (49:15). The ransom a man cannot give, God gives.

**Job 33 is that turn told as a story.** A man on his sickbed draws *"near to the pit"* (33:22). An angel,
*mēlîṣ*, *an interpreter, one among a thousand*, stands *"beside him"* and shows him what is right; and
God *"is gracious to him, and says, 'Deliver him from going down to the pit, I have found a
ransom'"* (33:24). **No one pays, and no price is named.** God *finds* the ransom — *māṣāʾtî kōper* — the
way one finds a thing already there, and the man then sings of it as God's act: *"He has redeemed my
soul from going into the pit"* (33:28, *pādâ*, the verb of Psalm 49:15 and Hosea 13:14). In Elihu's
sequence the ransom comes *after* the mediator speaks and *before* the confession of 33:27 — grace first,
then *"I have sinned"*. The speech never says what the ransom is: the man's repentance, the angel's
intercession, or something God provides from his own side. That silence is where the readings divide.

**Mark 10:45 fills the silence with a person.** *"The Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve,
and to give his life as a ransom for many."* The Greek *lytron* is the word the Septuagint commonly uses
for *kōper* and its cognates, and *for many* (*anti pollōn*) has the substitution of the census and the
ox: one life given in place of others. 1 Timothy 2:5–6 then joins the two halves of Job 33 that Elihu
left apart: **the mediator and the ransom are the same figure** — *"one mediator between God and men, the
man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all."* Elihu's angel stands *beside* the man; Paul's
mediator is *"the man"*, which is what Job asked for at 9:32 — *"he is not a man, as I am"*.

### The bull owner and the limits on ransom

In Exodus 21:29–30 the owner knew the bull's dangerous habit and failed to restrain it. The ransom
is not a standard price attached to every homicide: it is an amount imposed if that remedy is
allowed. Job 33:23–28, by contrast, reports Elihu's account of divine rescue and names no price.
The thematic resemblance does not make the two situations one legal case.

### Proverbs 21 in context

Proverbs 21:18 reads *"The wicked is a ransom for the righteous; the treacherous for the upright"* —
a reversal of fortunes like 11:8, naming no payer, payee, or procedure. The Hebrew word behind
*ransom* here has not been checked in this pass, so the page does not assert that it matches the
*kōper* discussed above.

## Significance

- **The connection is a vocabulary, not a prediction.** Elihu offers a hypothetical — *"If there is
  …"* — and the book never confirms that his angel exists. What the New Testament takes from the Hebrew
  Bible here is a word and a problem: a life forfeit to death, a price no human fortune meets (Psalm 49),
  and a God who says he has found it (Job 33). The wiki records the line without claiming Job 33 was
  written toward Mark 10.
- **It is also a contrast with the other Job.** Job's witness, umpire, and Redeemer (9:33; 16:19; 19:25)
  were to *vindicate* an innocent man. Elihu's mediator *rescues a guilty one*, and the ransom is the
  mechanism. The reader knows from the prologue that Job's case is the first kind, and the epilogue
  bears it out (42:7–8); so Elihu's account, true or not as a general rule, is offered to the wrong man.
  See [[The Witness in Heaven]] and [[Mediation and Advocacy]].
- **The bribe sense is the shadow of the word.** *Kōper* that buys a guilty verdict off is a bribe; *kōper*
  that buys a forfeit life back is a ransom. Job 36:18 uses the first, as the WEB reads it; Job 33:24 the
  second. The difference is who pays, and to whom — and in 33:24 God is the one who finds it. **36:18 is
  disputed**, and in a hard passage (36:16–21): another common reading keeps the sense of 33:24 and takes
  the *great ransom* to be Job's suffering itself, the price that must not turn him aside. On that reading
  Elihu uses the word in one sense only, and the suffering of chapter 36 is the cost of the rescue he
  described in chapter 33. The wiki records both.
- **And it is the inverse of [[The Ram Instead of the Son - Substitution at Moriah]].** There, a life is
  given in another's place and the wiki notes that *nothing is paid to anyone*. Here the word *ransom* is
  used, and still no payer is named. Between them the Old Testament holds both halves of what Mark 10:45
  puts together: a substitute provided by God, and a price.

**A ransom refused.** Proverbs 6:35 is the case where *kōp̄er* is offered by a man and turned down:
the wronged husband *"won't regard any ransom, neither will he rest content, though you give many
gifts"*. It puts adultery, in the husband's eyes, with the murder for which Numbers 35:31 forbids a
ransom. See [[The Lamp of the Commandment]] and
[[No Ransom for the Adulterer - Proverbs 6.29-35, Leviticus 20.10, and Deuteronomy 22.22]].

**A ransom paid in riches.** Proverbs 13:8 — *"The ransom of a man’s life is his riches, but the
poor hear no threats"* — states plainly the practice Psalm 49 denies can reach death: wealth buys a
man out of danger, and the same wealth makes him worth threatening. No claim is made here that 13:8
uses the same Hebrew word as Job 33:24. See [[Hope Deferred]]. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 13:8.
