## Analysis

**Exodus tells the story without a verdict. Every later text supplies one.** At Rephidim God gives water and
rebukes no one; the only judgment in Exodus 17:1–7 is the narrator's, and it is in the name: *Massah*,
*testing* (17:7). **Deuteronomy turns the name into a commandment**: *lōʾ tənassû ʾet-YHWH ʾĕlōhêkem
kaʾăšer nissîtem bammassâ* — **the verb of Exodus 17:2 and the place name built on it, in one sentence**
(Deuteronomy 6:16; WLC). **What Exodus records as an event Deuteronomy forbids as a type.** Deuteronomy 9:22
adds the anger that Exodus left out — *"you provoked Yahweh to wrath"* — and puts Massah in a list with two
Numbers stories (Taberah, Numbers 11:1–3; Kibroth Hattaavah, 11:34; by reference).

**Psalm 95 turns it into liturgy, addressed to *"Today"*.** The psalm calls the congregation to worship and
then warns it in God's voice: *"Don't harden your heart, as at Meribah, as in the day of Massah"* (95:8).
**It reads the event as a hardened heart**, the phrase of Pharaoh (see [[Hardening of the Heart]]) — the
verb in 95:8 is ***qāšâ*** in the causative, *make hard* (*ʾal-taqšû ləbabkem*; WLC), one of the verbs Exodus
uses of Pharaoh's heart (7:3; 13:15). **Israel at Massah is warned against becoming what Pharaoh was.** The
WLC tags *mərîbâ* and *massâ* in 95:8 as common nouns (*kimrîbâ kəyôm massâ*), so the line can be read *as at
the quarrel, as in the day of testing*; the WEB gives the place names.

**Hebrews preaches on the psalm's *"Today"*** (Hebrews 3:7–4:11). It quotes the psalm in Greek, where the two
names have become nouns: ***parapikrasmos***, *rebellion* or *provocation*, and ***peirasmos***, *trial*
(Hebrews 3:8; SBLGNT). **The wiki has not checked the Greek Psalm (Psalm 94 in the Septuagint's numbering)
that Hebrews follows**, and records the correspondence of the Greek nouns to Meribah and Massah as a matter of
sense, not of verified translation history. **Hebrews' point is the psalm's**: the generation that tested God
did not enter his *"rest"* (95:11; Hebrews 3:11, 18–19), and the readers are still inside the *"Today"*.

**Jesus answers the tempter with Deuteronomy 6:16.** In Matthew the devil sets him on the pinnacle of the
temple and quotes Psalm 91 (by reference) — *"On their hands they will bear you up"* — and Jesus answers, *"You shall not test the Lord,
your God"* (Matthew 4:5–7; the same answer comes third in Luke 4:9–12, by reference). **The verb is
*ekpeirazō*** (SBLGNT). **He quotes only the first half of the verse**; the second, *"as you tempted him in
Massah"*, is left unsaid. **The setting supplies it: the wilderness, forty days, hunger.** Where Israel at
Massah demanded proof that God was among them, Jesus refuses to demand it.

**Paul applies it to the church.** *"Let us not test Christ, as some of them tested"* (1 Corinthians 10:9;
*ekpeirazō*, SBLGNT; the SBLGNT marks a textual variant at *Christ*, where some manuscripts read *the Lord*) —
in the same paragraph that calls the rock of the water *"Christ"* (10:4). See
[[The Rock That Followed Them - Exodus 17, Numbers 20, and 1 Corinthians 10]].

**Two texts turn the verb round.** Psalm 81:7 has God say *"I tested you at the waters of Meribah"*, and
Deuteronomy 33:8 has him *"prove"* Levi's *"godly one"* at Massah and *"contend"* with him at Meribah. **In both,
God is the tester — the direction of Marah and the manna (Exodus 15:25; 16:4), not of Massah.** Psalm 81:7 may
refer to the Kadesh Meribah of Numbers 20. **The wiki records the reversal and does not resolve it**: Exodus
17 names the place for Israel's testing of God, these two remember a testing by God, and Scripture keeps both.
See [[Testing]].

## Significance

**Massah is how Scripture names the wrong kind of question.** *"Is Yahweh among us, or not?"* (Exodus 17:7)
is not forbidden for being a question — Moses asks God to go *"among us"* after the golden calf (34:9) — but
for being a condition: show us, or we will stone the man you sent. **Deuteronomy makes it law, the Psalms make
it a warning sung in worship, Hebrews makes it a sermon, and Jesus makes it his answer when the tempter asks
him to put God's promise to the proof.** The connection is the reason a seven-verse story with no punishment in
it became one of the most-cited episodes of the wilderness.
