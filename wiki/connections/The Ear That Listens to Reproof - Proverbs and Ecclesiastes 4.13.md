# The Ear That Listens to Reproof - Proverbs and Ecclesiastes 4.13

## Passages involved

- **Proverbs 12:15** — *"The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but he who is wise listens to
  counsel."* — [[Whoever Loves Correction]]
- **Proverbs 13:1** — *"A wise son listens to his father’s instruction, but a scoffer doesn’t listen to
  rebuke."* — [[Hope Deferred]]
- **Proverbs 15:5** — *"A fool despises his father’s correction, but he who heeds reproof shows
  prudence."* — [[A Gentle Answer]]
- **Proverbs 15:31–32** — *"The ear that listens to reproof lives, and will be at home among the wise.
  He who refuses correction despises his own soul, but he who listens to reproof gets understanding."*
  — [[A Gentle Answer]]
- **Proverbs 29:1** — *"He who is often rebuked and stiffens his neck will be destroyed suddenly, with
  no remedy."* — [[Where There Is No Vision]]
- **Ecclesiastes 4:13** — *"Better is a poor and wise youth than an old and foolish king who doesn’t
  know how to receive admonition any more."* — [[The Youth and the Old King]]

## Analysis

**One test of wisdom.** Proverbs repeatedly defines the wise by whether they listen and the fool
by whether they refuse. The wise *listens to counsel* (12:15) and to a father's instruction
(13:1). The prudent *heeds reproof* (15:5). The ear that listens to reproof *lives* and is at home
among the wise (15:31). Ecclesiastes 4:13 applies the same test to a king, and he fails it. His
folly is not defined by any act, only by his no longer knowing how to receive admonition.

**What the king loses.** Proverbs 15:32 says that refusing correction *despises his own soul*, and
29:1 that the one who stiffens his neck after many rebukes is destroyed suddenly. Ecclesiastes
4:13–16 narrates nothing as dramatic. The old king is simply ranked below a poor youth and
replaced. The story fits Proverbs' warning without saying how the king fell.

**"Any more."** The Preacher's *ʿôḏ*, *any more* (Hebrew read in Mechon-Mamre's Masoretic text of
Ecclesiastes 4, 2026-10-27), adds something Proverbs does not stress: the capacity can be lost.
Proverbs 29:1's *often rebuked* comes closest, implying a pattern of refusal over time. Together
the texts present teachability as a habit kept or abandoned, not as a fixed trait. Proverbs 19:20
gives the positive version: *"Listen to counsel and receive instruction, that you may be wise in
your latter end."* The old king is a picture of a latter end without it.

**Rank and age do not decide.** Proverbs addresses mostly sons and students, while Ecclesiastes
4:13 addresses a king. The books agree that office does not exempt anyone from correction; compare
[[A King Under Instruction - Proverbs 31.1-5 and Deuteronomy 17.17-20]], where a king is taught by
his mother and by the law. The youth's poverty and the king's age show that the ranking depends on
wisdom alone.

## Significance

The connection shows Ecclesiastes using one of Proverbs' basic distinctions between [[Wisdom]] and
[[Folly]] and placing it in a story about [[Kingship and Empire]]. It also links the saying to
[[Wise in His Own Eyes - Proverbs 3.7, 26.12, Isaiah 5.21, and Romans 12.16]], since Proverbs 12:15's
fool is right in his own eyes.
