# Let That Day Be Darkness - Job 3 as Counter-Creation

## Passages involved

- Genesis 1:1–2:3 — [[The Creation of the World]]
- Job 3:1–10 — the first movement of Job's first speech — [[Let the Day Perish]]
- Job 38:4–21 — the whirlwind's opening, which takes the same images back —
  [[The Whirlwind Answers Chapter 3]]
- Genesis 6:9–8:22 — the wiki's other un-creation, and the nearest relative of this one —
  [[The Flood as Un-Creation]]
- Jeremiah 4:23–26 — the prophet seeing the earth returned to the formless void of Genesis 1:2, the
  heavens without light, as a picture of judgment on Judah

## Analysis

**Job's first eight verses of poetry are Genesis 1 spoken backwards, and the grammar gives it
away.** Genesis 1 is built on the jussive: *yəhî ʾôr*, **let there be light**, and nine more like
it. **Job 3:3–10 is built on the same form, eleven times, and every one of them asks for the
removal of something day one put in place.**

> **Genesis 1:3** God said, "Let there be light," and there was light.
> **Genesis 1:4** God saw the light, and saw that it was good. God divided the light from the
> darkness.

> **Job 3:4** Let that day be darkness. Don't let God from above seek for it, neither let the light
> shine on it.
> **Job 3:5** Let darkness and the shadow of death claim it for their own.

***Yəhî ḥōšek* against *yəhî ʾôr*.** The sentence is four Hebrew letters away from the first thing
God says in the Bible, and it asks for the opposite. **Then verse 5 undoes 1:4** — where God
*divided* the light from the darkness, Job asks that darkness **claim the day back**, the verb
being one of redemption or repossession, as of a kinsman reclaiming what was sold. **Job is asking
that the separation of day one be treated as a transaction and reversed.**

The correspondences run on, and they run in order:

| Genesis 1 | Job 3 |
|---|---|
| *"Let there be light"* (1:3) | *"Let that day be darkness… neither let the light shine on it"* (3:4) |
| *"God divided the light from the darkness"* (1:4) | *"Let darkness and the shadow of death claim it for their own"* (3:5) |
| *"God saw the light, and saw that it was good"* (1:4) | *"Don't let God from above seek for it"* (3:4) — the inspection refused |
| *"God called the light 'day', and the darkness he called 'night'"* (1:5) | day and night addressed separately and cursed in turn (3:4–5 / 3:6–9) |
| *"let them be for signs to mark seasons, days, and years"* (1:14) | *"Let it not rejoice among the days of the year. Let it not come into the number of the months"* (3:6) |
| *"There was evening and there was morning"* (1:5) | *"Let it look for light, but have none, neither let it see the eyelids of the morning"* (3:9) |
| *"Be fruitful, and multiply"* (1:22, 1:28) | *"Behold, let that night be barren"* (3:7) |
| The waters and the deep, bounded (1:6–10) | *"who are ready to rouse up leviathan"* (3:8) — the boundary attacked |

**Three of these are worth stating on their own.**

**The calendar.** Genesis 1:14 gives the lights their job: *"let them be for signs to mark seasons,
days, and years"*. **Job asks for the reverse administrative act** — that his day be struck from the
year and his night from the count of the months. Not darkened, *deleted*; the request is not that it
be a bad day but that it not be a day. **This is the most precise un-creation in the passage**,
because it attacks the function Genesis 1 assigned rather than the substance.

**The barren night.** *"Let that night be barren"* — the word is *galmûd*, hard, stony, fruitless.
**Genesis 1's first blessing is fertility** (1:22, and again on humanity at 1:28), and Job asks that
one night be sterilised retroactively. It is a coherent request only because the night has already
been personified as an announcer — *"the night which said, 'There is a boy conceived'"* (3:3) —
so it can be held responsible for a conception and punished with infertility.

**Leviathan.** *"Let them curse it who curse the day, who are ready to rouse up leviathan"* (3:8).
Genesis 1 contains no combat; the deep is simply there and is divided and bounded. **But the rest of
the Hebrew Bible knows a version of creation in which the sea-monster is an adversary God
defeated** (Psalm 74:13–14, Psalm 89:10, Isaiah 27:1, Isaiah 51:9), and **Job 3:8 asks for it to be
woken up.** If the bounding of chaos is what made a habitable world, then rousing Leviathan is
un-creation by the other route — and it puts the two halves of Job's request together: **unmake the
light, and unleash what the light was imposed on.** See the Notes on [[Let the Day Perish]] for the
reading that takes the day-cursers as literal professionals, and the reading that takes them as
rhetoric.

**What distinguishes this from the flood, and it is the whole point.**
[[The Flood as Un-Creation]] is an un-creation God performs. **Job 3 is an un-creation a man
requests, in God's own grammar, and does not get.** Nothing in the chapter happens. The day stays in
the calendar, the light keeps being given (3:20, 3:23 complain that it is), and the speech is
answered by Eliphaz rather than by weather. **Job 3 is the Bible's only attempt at creation in
reverse by someone with no power to perform it**, which is why it reads as grief and not as
judgment.

**The scale of the request is also worth being honest about.** Job asks for one day. He does not ask
for the world to end, does not curse creation generally, and does not say the world should not
exist. **The cosmic apparatus is deployed against a single date on the calendar**, and the
mismatch — Genesis 1's whole vocabulary, aimed at one birthday — is what gives the chapter its
particular sound of a man using everything he has.

## Significance

**It establishes at the start of the poetry that Job's quarrel is with the created order and not
with his circumstances**, which is why the book can end with a speech about ostriches and rain on
uninhabited land and have that count as an answer. **The friends never understand this.** They spend
twenty-two chapters treating Job's problem as a moral accounting question, and his opening statement
was a cosmological one.

**It also sets up the whirlwind exactly.** When God finally speaks he does not begin with Job's
innocence, his suffering, or the friends' theology. **He begins with the founding of the earth, the
sea shut behind doors, the commanding of the morning, and the place where darkness lives** — the
inventory of chapter 3, item by item, in the order Job attacked it. See
[[The Whirlwind Answers Chapter 3]]. **A chapter that asked for creation to be revoked is answered
by a tour of creation**, and the wiki reads that as the book's structural claim: the answer to Job
is not an argument but the thing itself.

Compare [[In the Beginning - Genesis 1 and John 1]] for the canon's other major rewriting of
Genesis 1:1–5, which goes in the opposite direction: John 1:5 has the light shining in the darkness
and the darkness failing to overcome it, where Job asks for the darkness to win one night back.
