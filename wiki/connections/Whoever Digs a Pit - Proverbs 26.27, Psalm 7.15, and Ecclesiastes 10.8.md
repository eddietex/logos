## Analysis

**Retribution without a judge.** The pit is a trap: a hole dug and hidden to catch an animal or a
man. In each of these texts the digger falls into his own trap. Proverbs 26:27 states it flatly, and
adds a second image of its own: the stone rolled toward someone else *returns* (*tāšûḇ*) on the man
who rolled it. Neither half names God or a court. The consequence takes the shape of the act. That is
the self-acting form of [[Retribution]] the first lecture of Proverbs had already used. The gang that
says *"Let's lay in wait for blood"* (1:11) is answered with its own verbs: *"these lay wait for their
own blood"* (1:18). (Hebrew of Proverbs 26:27 and 28:10 from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the
Masorah*, read 2026-10-26.)

**Proverbs 28:10** applies the principle to a moral trap: the man who leads the upright astray falls
into *bišḥûṯô*, *his own pit* (WEB *"his own trap"*). Here too the trap is of the man's own making.

**The Psalms pray it.** Psalm 7:15–16 makes the same observation, but inside a prayer for vindication.
The pit falls back on its maker, and so do *"the trouble he causes"* and *"his violence"*, *"on the
crown of his own head"*. Psalm 57:6 tells it from the side of the intended victim: *"They dig a pit
before me. They fall into the middle of it themselves."* In the Psalms the principle is something a
person under threat can appeal to. In Proverbs it is something the reader is warned about before he
picks up the shovel.

**Ecclesiastes qualifies it.** Ecclesiastes 10:8 has nearly the same first line, but with *may*: *"He
who digs a pit may fall into it; and whoever breaks through a wall may be bitten by a snake."* In the
WEB it reads as a statement about risk — work has hazards — rather than about justice. The Hebrew
does not itself decide between *shall* and *may*: both verses use the same imperfect, *yippôl*
(see the section on Ecclesiastes 10 below). The difference between them is the WEB's reading of
context.

**Not a rule for every case.** Like the other sentences on retribution in Proverbs, 26:27 states the
usual course of things. It does not promise that every trap-setter is caught, and the Psalms' prayers
suppose he sometimes is not yet caught. See the discussion of reading Proverbs after Job on the
[[Proverbs]] book page and [[Retribution]].

### Proverbs 28 in context

The trap now belongs to someone who leads the upright astray, and the second clause promises good to
the blameless. Read within chapter 28, the reversal stands alongside the actual oppression of
helpless people by wicked rulers (15–16). The moral warning does not establish that every victim has
already been rescued.

### Ecclesiastes 10:8 in context

Ecclesiastes 10:8 has been ingested in [[Folly in High Places and Everyday Work]]. In the Hebrew
read in Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (2026-10-27), Proverbs 26:27 has *kōreh-šaḥaṯ
bāh yippôl* and Ecclesiastes 10:8 *ḥōpēr gûmmāṣ bô yippôl*: different verbs for *digging* and
different nouns for the *pit*, but the same verb form for the fall. An earlier version of this page
left that unchecked. The WEB's *may* in Ecclesiastes is therefore the translator's reading, not a
modal word in the text. It suits the context: 10:9–11 go on to quarrying, woodcutting, the blunt
ax, and the charmer, all hazards and limits of ordinary work rather than the trap of the wicked.
Read within its chapter, Ecclesiastes 10:8 belongs with workmen's risks. Read beside Proverbs, it
also recalls the self-acting retribution of 26:27; the passage does not decide between the two.

Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 10:8–11; Proverbs 26:27. Hebrew as stated.

## Significance

The pit is one of Scripture's most common images for the way evil recoils on the one who does it. It
appears in wisdom, prayer, and reflection, each with its own use. See also
[[Sow Wickedness, Reap Trouble - Proverbs 22.8, Job 4.8, and Hosea 8.7]] for the harvest form of
the same idea, and [[As He Has Done to Me - Proverbs 24.29 and the Law of Retaliation]] for the
retaliation the son is forbidden to take himself.
