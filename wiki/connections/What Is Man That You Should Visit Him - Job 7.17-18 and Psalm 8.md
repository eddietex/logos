# What Is Man That You Should Visit Him - Job 7.17-18 and Psalm 8

## Passages involved

- **Job 7:17–18** — *"What is man, that you should magnify him, that you should set your mind on
  him, that you should visit him every morning, and test him every moment?"* —
  [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]
- **Psalm 8:3–8** — *"What is man, that you think of him? What is the son of man, that you care for
  him? For you have made him a little lower than the angels, and crowned him with glory and honor. You make
  him ruler over the works of your hands"*
- **Psalm 144:3–4** — *"Yahweh, what is man, that you care for him? Or the son of man, that you
  think of him? Man is like a breath. His days are like a shadow that passes away."*
- **Job 7:16** — *"Leave me alone, for my days are but a breath"* — *hebel*, the word of Psalm 144:4
- **Job 1:8; 2:3** — *"Have you considered my servant Job?"*, literally *set your heart on*
- **Genesis 1:26–28** — the dominion Psalm 8 sings about — [[The Creation of the World]]
- **Hebrews 2:5–9** — Psalm 8 read of Jesus, *"crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering
  of death"*
- **Job 15:14; 25:4–6** — the friends' *what is man* —
  [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]
- **Job 25:5–6** — moon and stars, *man*, *son of man*, in Psalm 8's order, ending in a worm —
  [[Bildad's Third Speech]]

## Analysis

**Three times the Hebrew Bible asks God *what is man*, and Job's version is the one that wishes he
would stop asking.**

| | The question | What God's attention does | Where it ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Psalm 8:4** | *mâ-ʾĕnôš kî-tizkərennû … kî tipqədennû* — that you remember him, that you visit him | crowns him, makes him ruler | *"how majestic is your name in all the earth!"* |
| **Psalm 144:3** | *mâ-ʾādām wattēdāʿēhû … wattəḥaššəbēhû* — that you know him, that you think of him | notices something as brief as a breath | a plea for rescue |
| **Job 7:17–18** | *mâ-ʾĕnôš kî-təgaddəlennû … wattipqədennû* — that you make him great, that you visit him | inspects him every morning, tests him every moment | *"How long will you not look away from me?"* |

The verbal overlap with Psalm 8 is the opening formula, *mâ-ʾĕnôš kî*, and the verb *pāqad* — to
visit, to attend to, to muster, to inspect. In the psalm *pāqad* is care. In Job it is inspection.
**Job knows both senses.** Three chapters later he uses the noun for care — *"Your visitation
(pəquddātəkā) has preserved my spirit"* (10:12) — and then, in the next breath, suspects the care
was cover for the inspection: *"Yet you hid these things in your heart… if I sin, then you mark
me"* (10:13–14). See [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]].
**Job takes the most exalted thing the Psalter says about the human being and reads it as a
description of his condition**: yes, God makes much of man; yes, God attends to him; that is
exactly what is wrong. The psalm's *"crowned him with glory"* becomes Job's *magnify him* —
*gādal*, to make great — and the greatness is the size of the target.

Whether Job is quoting Psalm 8 as a text or both draw on a common liturgical formula cannot be
settled, since neither can be dated against the other. But the reversal is too precise to be
accidental, and it is widely read as deliberate parody. **It is the Bible quarrelling with itself in
its own words**, the way Job's chapter 3 used the grammar of Genesis 1 to un-create a day (see
[[Let That Day Be Darkness - Job 3 as Counter-Creation]]).

**Psalm 144 is the closer cousin in mood.** It puts the *what is man* question next to *"man is like
a breath (hebel)"*, and Job does the same in the reverse order: *"my days are but a breath
(hebel)"* (7:16), then *"what is man?"* (7:17). Both use the human being's brevity to frame God's
attention. The psalmist finds the attention astonishing and asks for more of it — *"Part your
heavens, Yahweh, and come down"* (144:5). Job finds it intolerable and asks to be left alone.

**What the reader of Job knows that Job does not.** *"That you should set your mind on him"* —
*wəkî-tāšît ʾēlāyw libbekā* — is very nearly the question God asked in the council: *"Have you
considered my servant Job?"* — *hăśamtā libbəkā ʿal-ʿabdî ʾiyyôb*, literally *have you set your heart
on* (1:8; 2:3). The verbs differ (*šît*, *śîm*) and the idiom is the same. **Job complains that God
has set his heart on him. The reader has watched God invite the accuser to do exactly that.** And
*"test him every moment"* — *bāḥan* — is the first time anyone in the book uses the word for what is
happening to Job. He guesses right, and draws the wrong conclusion: that the tester is hostile.

**The friends' *what is man* runs the other way.** Eliphaz's *"What is man, that he should be
clean?"* (15:14) and Bildad's *"man, who is a worm"* (25:6) use the question to diminish the human
being before God; see [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]. Job's version takes the human being's
smallness for granted and asks why God bothers. **The friends say man is too low to complain. Job
says man is too small to be worth all this surveillance.** Psalm 8 says man is small and crowned.
Nobody in the dialogue says that.

**Bildad's last speech runs the psalm's sequence to the bottom.** Psalm 8:3–4 goes from *"the moon and
the stars, which you have ordained"* to *ʾĕnôš* (*man*) to *ben-ʾādām* (*the son of man*). Job 25:5–6
goes from *"even the moon has no brightness, and the stars are not pure in his sight"* to *ʾĕnôš* to
*ben-ʾādām* — the same four terms in the same order — and its predicate is *worm*, twice. **So Psalm 8
is parodied twice in Job, from opposite sides**: Job reads its attention as surveillance (7:17–18),
Bildad reads its heavens as a scale on which man comes last (25:5–6). Neither keeps the crown. See
[[Bildad's Third Speech]] and [[A Worm and No Man - Job 25.6, Psalm 22.6, and Isaiah 41.14]].

**Hebrews 2:5–9 puts suffering inside Psalm 8.** Hebrews quotes the psalm's *what is man* and its
crowning, observes that *"we don't see all things subjected to him, yet"*, and then: *"But we see
him who has been made a little lower than the angels, Jesus, because of the suffering of death
crowned with glory and honor."* Hebrews does not cite Job and is not answering him. But it does the
thing neither the psalm nor Job does: it holds the glory and the suffering together in a single
human being, and makes the suffering the road to the crown. **Psalm 8 had the crown without the
suffering; Job 7 had the suffering without the crown.**

## Significance

- **It is the clearest instance of Job turning a text of praise into a text of protest**, and so the
  clearest evidence that the book's poet works with Israel's worship tradition as raw material. The
  same technique recurs: 9:10 takes Eliphaz's doxology (5:9) and makes it despair, and 12:13–25
  is a hymn to God's power in which every act is destructive.
- **It shows Job's complaint is not that God is absent.** The usual grammar of lament is *"how long
  will you hide your face?"* (Psalm 13:1). Job's is *"how long will you not look away from me?"*
  (7:19). Psalm 139:7–12 — *"Where could I go from your Spirit?"* — is the same omnipresence, felt
  as comfort; Job feels it as a siege. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].
- **It reframes Job's request to be left alone.** *"Leave me alone"* (7:16, 7:19; again at 10:20,
  14:6) is not unbelief. It is the opposite: a man who cannot stop experiencing God's attention, and
  who has no category yet for an attention that is neither punishment nor glory. The whirlwind
  speeches supply one, by spending four chapters on God's attention to animals no human being has
  ever seen.
- **It gives the *what is man* question its full canonical range**: wonder (Psalm 8), brevity
  (Psalm 144), contempt (the friends), protest (Job), and Christological fulfilment (Hebrews 2).
