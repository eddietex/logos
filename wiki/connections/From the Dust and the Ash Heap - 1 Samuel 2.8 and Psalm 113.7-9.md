# From the Dust and the Ash Heap - 1 Samuel 2.8 and Psalm 113.7-9

## Passages involved

- [[The Song of Hannah]] — 1 Samuel 2:5, 8 *(stored WEB)*
- Psalm 113:7–9 *(WEB checked at bible-api.com; not yet ingested)*

## Analysis

One line is shared, nearly word for word, between Hannah's song and Psalm 113:

| | 1 Samuel 2:8 | Psalm 113:7–8 |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | *He raises up the poor out of the dust. He lifts up the needy from the dunghill, To make them sit with princes* | *He raises up the poor out of the dust. Lifts up the needy from the ash heap; that he may set him with princes* |
| WLC | *mēqîm mēʿāp̄ār dāl mēʾašpōṯ yārîm ʾeḇyôn ləhôšîḇ ʿim-nəḏîḇîm* | *məqîmî mēʿāp̄ār dāl mēʾašpōṯ yārîm ʾeḇyôn ləhôšîḇî ʿim-nəḏîḇîm* |

The Hebrew differs only in the psalm's archaic *-î* ending on two participles. The WEB's *dunghill* and
*ash heap* translate the same word, *ʾašpōṯ*, which occurs in the WLC files searched (Genesis through Ruth,
1 Samuel, Job, Psalms) only in these two verses.

**The barren woman follows in both.** The two texts then diverge, and the divergence is what makes the
parallel more than a borrowed line. Hannah has already said *the barren has borne seven* (2:5) and goes on
to *the throne of glory* and *the pillars of the earth* (8). The psalm goes straight to the barren woman:
*He settles the barren woman in her home, as a joyful mother of children* (113:9). Both, then, put the
poor lifted from the dust beside the barren woman given children, the same two reversals in the opposite
order. *ʿĂqārâ*, *barren*, is the word in both (WLC 1 Samuel 2:5; Psalm 113:9).

**Who borrows from whom** cannot be decided from the texts. The wiki reports the shared line and does not
argue a direction.

**Why it matters for Hannah.** Psalm 113 makes the barren woman one instance of a general pattern: the God
*high above all nations* who *stoops down to see* (113:4, 6, WEB checked) raises the low. Hannah's song
does the same with her own story, folding *the barren has borne seven* into a list of soldiers, the hungry,
and the dead. Read beside the psalm, her song's lack of personal detail looks deliberate: she sings her
child as one case of what God does.

## Significance

- The pairing of poverty and barrenness, two kinds of emptiness raised up, links
  [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] and [[Barrenness]] in both texts.
- Psalm 113 begins the group traditionally sung at Passover (Psalms 113–118), a liturgical use recalled
  and not verified here.
- See also [[The Barren Matriarchs]] and
  [[The Song of Hannah and the Song of Mary - 1 Samuel 2.1-10 and Luke 1.46-55]].
