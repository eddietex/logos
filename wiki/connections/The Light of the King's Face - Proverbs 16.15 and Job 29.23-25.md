# The Light of the King's Face - Proverbs 16.15 and Job 29.23-25

## Passages involved

- **Proverbs 16:14–15** — *"The king's wrath is a messenger of death, but a wise man will pacify it. In
  the light of the king's face is life. His favor is like a cloud of the spring rain."* —
  [[The Plans of the Heart]]
- **Job 29:21–25** — *"They waited for me as for the rain. Their mouths drank as with the spring rain. I
  smiled on them when they had no confidence. They didn't reject the light of my face. I chose out their
  way, and sat as chief. I lived as a king in the army, as one who comforts the mourners."* —
  [[The Months of Old]]
- **Proverbs 19:12** — *"The king's wrath is like the roaring of a lion, but his favor is like dew on
  the grass."* *(stored WEB, not yet ingested)*
- **Proverbs 20:2** — *"The terror of a king is like the roaring of a lion. He who provokes him to anger
  forfeits his own life."* *(stored WEB, not yet ingested)*
- **Proverbs 14:35** — *"The king's favor is toward a servant who deals wisely, but his wrath is toward
  one who causes shame."* — [[A Way That Seems Right]]

## Analysis

**The same two images, in the same order.** Proverbs 16:15 has *ʾôr-pənê-melek*, *the light of the
king's face*, and his favour *kəʿāb malqôš*, *like a cloud of the spring rain*. Job 29:23–24 has the
people opening their mouths *ləmalqôš*, *for the spring rain*, and not rejecting *ʾôr pānay*, *the light
of my face* (Hebrew of both from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*). The *malqôš* is the late
rain of spring that brings the crop to harvest; in a dry country it is the rain everything waits for.
Both texts make a ruler's goodwill that rain, and both make his face a light.

**Proverbs describes a king; Job describes himself as one.** The proverb is about a king in general,
seen from below: his favour is life, his wrath is death, and a wise man knows how to turn the one into
the other (16:14). Job's speech is about one man's former standing, seen from the inside: *"I lived as
a king in the army"* (29:25). Job was never a king, and his *"as"* says so. But in looking back on the
months of old he uses the royal sayings' vocabulary for what he was to his town — the one whose smile
gave the hopeless confidence, whose word ended the discussion (29:21–22), whose favour came like the
spring rain.

**Job adds what the proverb leaves out.** The king of Proverbs 16 is to be pacified; his wrath is the
danger. The king Job remembers being is *"one who comforts the mourners"* (29:25), and the chapter
around it lists the widow, the fatherless, the blind, and the lame he helped (29:12–16). The royal
favour Job claims was spent on the people least able to court it, which is the king of Proverbs 16:12–13
— established by righteousness, delighted by truthful lips — rather than the one of 16:14.

**And Job has lost it.** Chapter 30 follows: *"But now those who are younger than I have me in
derision"* (Job 30:1). The man whose face was a light is now mocked by those whose fathers he would not
have put *"with my sheep dogs"*. Proverbs states the king's favour as a fixed good; Job is the case of a man who had the
favour of a whole town, as a king does, and saw it gone. The wiki keeps the two side by side as it has
kept the sentence collection's other promises beside Job.

**The lion's roar.** Proverbs 19:12 and 20:2 will restate 16:14–15 with a harder image for the wrath —
*"like the roaring of a lion"* — and 19:12 a gentler one for the favour, *"dew on the grass"*.

Sources: Proverbs 14:35; 16:12–15; 19:12; 20:2; Job 29:12–16, 21–25; 30:1, checked in stored WEB. Hebrew
of Proverbs 16:15 and Job 29:23–24 from Sefaria, 2026-10-25. Which text, if either, knew the other is
not claimed.

## Significance

The connection joins [[Kingship and Empire]] to [[Light and Darkness]] and to the rain of
[[Famine and Plenty]], and it uses Job's memory of the months of old to show what a righteous king's
favour was for: [[Compassion and Mercy]] toward the people who could not court it. It also adds a
case to the wiki's reading of [[Retribution]]: favour, in Job, is something a righteous man can lose.
