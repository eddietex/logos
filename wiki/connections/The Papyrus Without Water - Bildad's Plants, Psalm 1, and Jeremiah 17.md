# The Papyrus Without Water - Bildad's Plants, Psalm 1, and Jeremiah 17

## Passages involved

- **Job 8:11–19** — *"Can the papyrus grow up without mire? Can the rushes grow without water? …
  So are the paths of all who forget God. The hope of the godless man shall perish, whose confidence
  shall break apart, whose trust is a spider's web."* — [[Bildad's First Speech]]
- **Psalm 1:3–4** — the righteous man *"like a tree planted by the streams of water, that produces
  its fruit in its season, whose leaf also does not wither"*; the wicked *"like the chaff which the
  wind drives away."*
- **Jeremiah 17:5–8** — *"Cursed is the man who trusts in man… he shall be like a bush in the
  desert"*; *"Blessed is the man who trusts in Yahweh, and whose confidence is in Yahweh. For he will
  be as a tree planted by the waters, who spreads out its roots by the river."*
- **Job 14:7–9** — *"For there is hope for a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout again…
  Though its root grows old in the earth… yet through the scent of water it will bud."* Job's reply.
- **Job 15:32–33** — Eliphaz: *"His branch shall not be green. He shall shake off his unripe grape
  as the vine."* — [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]]
- **Job 18:16** — Bildad again: *"His roots shall be dried up beneath. Above shall his branch be
  cut off."* — [[Bildad's Second Speech]]
- **Job 19:10** — *"My hope he has plucked up like a tree."* — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Job 29:19** — Job remembering his prosperity: *"My root is spread out to the waters. The dew
  lies all night on my branch."*
- **Isaiah 59:5–6** — the wicked *"weave the spider's web… Their webs won't become garments."*

## Analysis

**The wisdom tradition has one image for the two ways, and Bildad is quoting it.** A plant lives or
dies by its access to water, and the water is God. Psalm 1 sets the righteous man's tree by streams
of water and the wicked man beside it as chaff. Jeremiah 17 makes the image explicitly about *trust*:
the man who trusts in human strength is a desert shrub that *"shall not see when good comes"*, and
the man whose trust is Yahweh is a tree whose roots reach the river, unafraid of drought. Bildad's
papyrus is the same lesson told about the marsh rather than the orchard: lush, fast, and the first
thing in the marsh to die when the water goes, *"before any other reed"* (Job 8:12).

**The vocabulary of trust is shared, not just the picture.** Bildad's godless man has a
*mibṭāḥ* — *"whose trust is a spider's web"* (8:14). Jeremiah's blessed man is the one *ʾăšer
hāyâ YHWH mibṭaḥô*, *whose trust Yahweh is* (17:7). The same noun names the thing a man leans on,
and the two texts disagree only about what it is made of. Bildad's line is then doubled in the next
verse — *"He shall lean on his house, but it shall not stand"* — because the Hebrew for a spider's
web is *bêt ʿakkābîš*, a spider's *house*. Isaiah 59 has the same web for the same people: what the
wicked weave *"won't become garments."*

**What makes Job 8 different is the direction in which the image is read.** In Psalm 1 and Jeremiah
17 the image moves from cause to effect: trust God, and you will flourish. Bildad runs it backwards,
from effect to cause: this plant has withered, therefore it had no water. That is the friends'
method throughout, and the image hides it, because in a marsh the inference is sound — a dead reed
really did lose its water. Applied to a man, it assumes that the only thing that can wither a
righteous man is the absence of God. The reader of Job 1–2 knows otherwise.

**Job answers the image three times, and each time changes a different part of it.**

- **14:7–9 — the felled tree.** Job grants Bildad his plant and gives it something Bildad did not:
  a second life. *"There is hope for a tree"* — a stump revives *"through the scent of water."*
  Bildad's papyrus needs water and dies without it; Job's tree needs only the *smell* of it. And then
  the turn: a man is not like that. *"But man dies, and is laid low… man lies down and doesn't rise"*
  (14:10–12). Job's complaint is not that the image is false, but that for human beings it is not
  true enough.
- **19:10 — the gardener.** *"My hope he has plucked up like a tree."* Job grants the uprooting of
  8:18 and 18:16 and supplies the agent. Bildad's plants wither by losing water or are torn up by an
  unnamed hand; Job names the hand.
- **29:19 — the root by the water.** Remembering the days before the prologue's catastrophes, Job
  describes himself in exactly the terms of Psalm 1 and Jeremiah 17: *"My root is spread out to the
  waters. The dew lies all night on my branch."* That is his testimony that he was the watered tree,
  not the papyrus — and the book's prologue agrees with him.

**The epilogue settles the image without using it.** The plant Bildad said had lost its water is
restored (42:10–17), and it is restored not because it found water it had lost but because God had
never withdrawn it — the withering was a test, not a drought.

## Significance

- Job 8:11–19 shows the friends' theology at its most attractive. It is not cruel abstract doctrine;
  it is the image of Psalm 1, the book that opens Israel's prayer, and Jeremiah's, one of the
  prophets. Bildad is quoting the tradition faithfully. The book's quarrel is with his reading of a
  case, not with the image.
- It is the first of a chain of plant images that runs through the dialogue — Bildad's papyrus (8),
  Job's stump (14), Eliphaz's barren vine (15:32–33), Bildad's dried roots (18:16), Job's uprooted
  tree (19:10), Job's watered root (29:19). The friends always use the image of the wicked; Job uses
  it of himself.
