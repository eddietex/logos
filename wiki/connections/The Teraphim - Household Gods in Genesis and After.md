# The Teraphim - Household Gods in Genesis and After

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 31:19, 30–35** — Rachel steals her father's *teraphim*, hides them in a camel's saddle,
  and sits on them through a search — [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]]
- **Genesis 35:2–4** — *"put away the foreign gods that are among you"*, buried under the oak at
  Shechem *(not yet ingested)*
- **Judges 17:5; 18:14–20** — Micah's household shrine, with an ephod and *teraphim*, and the Danites
  who carry the lot of it off
- **1 Samuel 15:23** — *"stubbornness is as idolatry and teraphim"*
- **1 Samuel 19:11–17** — Michal puts *teraphim* in David's bed and tells her father's men he is sick
- **2 Kings 23:24** — Josiah purges *teraphim* along with mediums and household idols
- **Ezekiel 21:21** — the king of Babylon at the crossroads, shaking arrows and consulting *teraphim*
- **Hosea 3:4; Zechariah 10:2** — Israel left *"without ephod or teraphim"*, and *teraphim* that
  *"have spoken vanity"*

## Analysis

***Tərāpîm*** is **always plural in form and does not have to be plural in sense** — 1 Samuel 19:13
puts *the teraphim* in a bed as a single body-sized object, while Genesis 31:34 has them fit inside a
camel's saddle. **The word's etymology is unsettled** (a link to *repāʾîm*, the shades of the dead, is
one standing proposal; the wiki reports it and adopts nothing), and **no biblical text ever describes
what one looks like.**

**What the Bible does show is what they were used for**, and it is two things:

1. **Household religion.** Micah has them in a private shrine with an ephod and a hired Levite
   (Judges 17:5) — **domestic, hereditary, and not obviously understood by its owner as apostasy**;
   he says *"now I know that Yahweh will do me good"* in the same breath (17:13).
2. **Divination.** Ezekiel 21:21 has the king of Babylon consulting them for a military decision;
   1 Samuel 15:23 lists them with *qesem*, divination; Zechariah 10:2 says they *speak vanity*.
   **Hosea 3:4 puts them beside the ephod as an instrument of enquiry Israel will be deprived of.**

**Genesis 31 is where they enter Scripture, and it explains none of this.** The chapter supplies:
Laban owned them; Rachel took them; Laban called them ***my gods*** and searched four tents on his
knees for them; **Rachel hid them under a saddle, sat on it, and pleaded her period**; they were not
found; **and nobody in Genesis ever mentions them again.**

**Three explanations for Rachel are standing, and the text supports none of them explicitly.**

1. **They were valuable.** Objects of metal, taken by a woman who has just said her dowry was eaten
   (31:15). **Simple, and it fits the sisters' speech** — but Genesis says nothing about their
   material.
2. **Possession carried a claim.** The reading built on the Nuzi tablets from Mesopotamia, in which
   household gods figure in inheritance and adoption arrangements; on it, Rachel is securing her
   husband's or her sons' standing in Laban's estate. **It is the most-repeated explanation and the
   most contested**, because the Nuzi documents do not say what the popular version of them says, and
   because Genesis's own account of the estate is that the daughters had already written it off
   (31:14).
3. **They were the household's protection**, and she wanted it, or wanted her father not to have it.
   **This fits the one fact the chapter does supply** — that this household divines (30:27) — and
   explains Laban's reaction, which is far angrier about the gods than about the flocks.

**The wiki adopts none of the three and records that Genesis gives Rachel no motive at all.** It
gives her a motive for nothing else in the chapter either.

**What the scene does do is make the objects look small.** They are hidden in a saddlebag, sat on,
and searched for by a man groping around a tent floor. **A god that can be sat on by a woman who then
apologises for not standing up is being shown something about**, and Genesis never says what. **The
open mockery of idols — mouths that do not speak, a carpenter burning half his log — is a later
development** (Psalm 115:5; Isaiah 44:9–20), **and this chapter neither performs it nor forbids it.**

## Significance

**The closest thing in Scripture to Genesis 31 is 1 Samuel 19**, and the resemblance is exact enough
to be worth stating: **a daughter of the man in power uses her father's household gods to help her
husband escape her father, and stops the pursuit with a lie about a body** — Michal says David is
sick, Rachel says she cannot rise. **Neither woman is condemned; neither is praised; the object is
never mentioned again in either story.** The wiki records the parallel and does not claim a literary
dependence in either direction.

**The thread's ending is the one Genesis half-supplies.** Four chapters after Rachel takes them,
Jacob tells his household *"put away the foreign gods that are among you"* and buries them under the
oak at Shechem (35:2–4). **Genesis does not say the *teraphim* were among them**, does not name
Rachel, and does not say the burial was commanded — **but it is the only disposal the book records,
and it happens in the household the objects were carried into.**

**By the end of the Old Testament they have become a synonym for what a purge removes** (2 Kings
23:24) **and for what a people deprived of everything will not even have left** (Hosea 3:4). **They
enter Scripture as a woman's private theft and leave it as a category.**
