# Sin Against God - Joseph at 39.9 and David at Psalm 51

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 20:6, 9** — *"I withheld you from sinning **against me**"*, said by God to a Philistine king;
  and *"you have brought on me and on my kingdom **a great sin**"* — [[Abraham and Abimelech]]
- **Genesis 39:9** — *"How then can I do this great wickedness, and **sin against God**?"* —
  [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]]
- **Genesis 42:22; 44:16** — Reuben's *"don't sin against the child"*, and Judah's *"God has found out
  the iniquity of your servants"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **2 Samuel 12:13** — *"I have sinned **against Yahweh**"*, David's four words to Nathan
- **Psalm 51:4** — *"**Against you, and you only, have I sinned**, and done that which is evil in your
  sight"*
- **Exodus 20:14** — the commandment neither Joseph nor Abimelech had
- **Luke 15:18, 21** — *"Father, I have sinned **against heaven** and in your sight"*

## Analysis

**Two men in the Bible articulate the same theology about the same act, and they are on opposite sides
of it.**

> **39:9** No one is greater in this house than I am, and he has not kept back anything from me but you,
> because you are his wife. **How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?**

> **Psalm 51:4** Against you, and you only, have I sinned, and done that which is evil in your sight.

**Joseph says it while refusing. David says it after.** **Joseph is a slave with no legal standing, no
law to cite, and everything to lose. David is a king with the law in hand, who has already lost
nothing.** The sentence is the same; the position could not be more different.

### What is strange about both statements

**In each case there is an obvious human victim, and the speaker names God instead.**

**For Joseph**, the injured parties are visible and he has just listed them: **his master**, who has
handed him the entire estate and withheld one thing; **the wife**, whose marriage it is; **the
household**, which he runs. **He argues from exactly that for three clauses** — *"my master doesn't
know what is with me in the house … he has put all that he has into my hand … he has not kept back
anything from me but you, because you are his wife"* — **and then the conclusion changes court.**
*"How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?"*

**For David** the discrepancy is far sharper, and it is the standing difficulty with Psalm 51:4. **He
has committed adultery with Bathsheba and had Uriah killed**, and the psalm says *"against you, **and
you only**"*. **Uriah is dead.** The line has been read three ways and the wiki does not rank them:

1. **A statement about jurisdiction, not injury.** A king is above human prosecution; **there is no
   court in Israel that can try David**, and the psalm concedes that the only bench with standing is
   God's. Nathan's parable works this way too — it manoeuvres the only available judge into sentencing
   himself.
2. **A statement about what makes the act *sin* rather than merely harm.** The wrong to Uriah is real
   and named elsewhere (2 Samuel 12:9); **what makes it *ḥēṭʾ* is that it is against God**, and the
   psalm is defining the category rather than counting the victims.
3. **Liturgical intensification.** *"Only"* in a penitential poem is emphasis, not arithmetic, in the
   same way *"I am a worm, and no man"* is.

**Joseph's version has none of that difficulty**, because the act has not happened. **He is not
minimising a human victim; he is identifying which wrong would be the one he could not undo.**

### Sin against God before there is a commandment

**Neither statement's speaker is in the same legal situation, and that is the thread's real content.**

**Joseph has no law.** There is no Sinai, no seventh commandment, no Leviticus 18. **Genesis has already
staged the problem twice**, and both times in a foreigner's mouth: **Pharaoh** at 12:18 rebukes Abram for
the wife-sister arrangement, and **[[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]]** at 20:9 calls it *"a great sin"* —
**and God has already told Abimelech, in the same chapter, *"I withheld you from sinning against me"***
(20:6). **The formula Joseph uses is put into Scripture first by God, speaking to a Philistine king, in
defence of a woman a patriarch had handed over.** See
[[Because Abraham Obeyed My Voice - Law Before Sinai]].

**So the category exists in Genesis with no statute behind it**, and the book is consistent about it:
the people who state it most clearly are **the ones outside the covenant line**, and the one insider who
states it is **a seventeen-year-old slave in Egypt**.

**David has the law and quotes none of it.** 2 Samuel 12:13 is four words — ***ḥāṭāʾtî la-YHWH***, *I
have sinned against Yahweh* — **and it is the shortest confession by a king in the Hebrew Bible.**
Nathan's reply is immediate. **The comparison worth making is with Saul**, who says *"I have sinned"*
twice (1 Samuel 15:24, 30) and both times attaches a request: *"honour me now before the elders."*
**David attaches nothing.**

### The verbal detail

**Joseph says *ʾĕlōhîm*, not *Yahweh*** — ***wəḥāṭāʾtî lēʾlōhîm***. **He is speaking to an Egyptian
woman, and he uses the word she could hear.** The chapter around him says *Yahweh* eight times, all of
it the narrator's. **The covenant name is in the room and the character does not use it.**

**David says *Yahweh*** at 2 Samuel 12:13 and **addresses God in the second person** in the psalm —
*against **you***. **The psalm is prayer and the Genesis line is argument**, which is most of the
difference in register between them.

**Both use the same verb.** ***Ḥāṭāʾ*** — *to miss*, in its non-theological sense a word for missing a
target (Judges 20:16). **It enters the Bible at Genesis 4:7**, where sin is *crouching at the door*, and
its first use in a human mouth about a specific act is **Pharaoh's** at 12:18 — or rather is not, because
Pharaoh does not use the word; **the first is Abimelech's, at 20:9.**

### Where the thread goes

**Luke 15:18 gives the formula its most-quoted form.** *"Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your
sight"* — **and the prodigal names both parties**, which is the arrangement Genesis 39 and Psalm 51 each
hold to only one half of. **The son has a human victim standing in front of him and names him second.**

**And the New Testament reads Joseph's version as the paradigm without citing it.** *"Flee sexual
immorality"* (1 Corinthians 6:18) is the verb of Genesis 39:12; *"your body is a temple of the Holy
Spirit"* (6:19) is the argument of 39:9 relocated. **Neither passage mentions Joseph.** Stephen's summary
in Acts 7:9–10 skips the episode entirely: *"God was with him, and delivered him out of all his
afflictions"* — **which is the narrator's sentence from 39:2, not Joseph's from 39:9.**

## Significance

**The thread's content is that the Bible's sharpest statements of what sin *is* are made by people with
no legal apparatus available to them** — a slave who has no standing to be wronged and no court to
appeal to, and a king who is above any court that exists. **In both cases the human machinery for
adjudicating the act is missing, and in both cases the speaker reaches past it.**

**It also marks where Genesis 39 sits in the book's moral development.** [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]]
passes no verdict on attempted fratricide, a sale, and a sustained lie. **Genesis 39 contains the first
sentence in the Joseph cycle that names an act as wrong and says who it is wrong against**, and it is
spoken about something that does not happen. **The narration still passes no verdict** — on the
proposition, on the false charge, or on the imprisonment. **The only moral vocabulary in the chapter is
in the mouth of the person refusing**, and Genesis puts no comment of its own beside it.
