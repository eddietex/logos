# Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 15:13–16** — *"Know for sure that your offspring will live as foreigners in a land that
  is not theirs, and will serve them. They will afflict them four hundred years… In the fourth
  generation they will come here again."* See [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].
- **Exodus 12:40–41** — *"Now the time that the children of Israel lived in Egypt was four hundred
  thirty years."*
- **Exodus 6:16–20** — Levi, Kohath, Amram, Moses: four names between the descent and the exodus.
- **Exodus 12:35–36** — the *"great wealth"* of Genesis 15:14, itemized as silver, gold, and
  clothing.
- **Numbers 32:13; Judges 11:26; 1 Kings 6:1** — the other chronological anchors, and they do not all
  agree.
- **Acts 7:6** — Stephen quotes the round four hundred.
- **Galatians 3:16–17** — Paul counts four hundred thirty years *from the promise to the law*, which
  is a different span from Exodus 12:40's.

## Analysis

Genesis 15:13 is the Bible's first prediction, and it is the one place in Scripture where the exodus
is announced to the man whose descendants it happens to. It is also the origin of the Old Testament's
most tangled chronological problem, and the tangle is worth laying out plainly rather than resolving.

### The numbers, as they stand

| Text | What it counts | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 15:13 | affliction in a land not theirs | 400 years |
| Genesis 15:16 | until the return to Canaan | 4 generations |
| Exodus 12:40 (Hebrew) | Israel's stay **in Egypt** | 430 years |
| Exodus 12:40 (Septuagint, Samaritan) | in **Egypt and Canaan** | 430 years |
| Exodus 6:16–20 | Levi → Kohath → Amram → Moses | 4 names |
| Acts 7:6 | affliction | 400 years |
| Galatians 3:17 | promise → law | 430 years |

Two pairs pull against each other.

**Four hundred against four generations.** If a *dôr* is a begetting cycle of twenty-five to forty
years, four of them is a century or a century and a half, not four. Three answers are standard and
all are ancient: *dôr* here means a lifespan rather than a generation, and four lifespans of roughly
a hundred years is exactly four hundred (Genesis's patriarchs do live that long); or the four hundred
is a round symbolic figure and the four generations are literal; or the sojourn really was about two
centuries and Exodus 12:40's four hundred thirty covers Canaan as well.

**The Hebrew Exodus 12:40 against the Greek and Samaritan.** The Masoretic text says four hundred
thirty years in Egypt. The Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch both read *"in the land of Egypt
and in the land of Canaan"*, which splits the total and gives roughly two hundred fifteen years in
each — and two hundred fifteen years in Egypt fits the Levi–Kohath–Amram–Moses genealogy without
strain, since Exodus 6 gives Kohath a hundred thirty-three years and Amram a hundred thirty-seven.

**Paul follows the Greek.** Galatians 3:17 counts four hundred thirty years *"from the promise to the
law"* — Abraham to Sinai, not Jacob to the exodus. That is only possible on the Septuagint's reading
of Exodus 12:40, and Paul is using it because his argument needs the promise to be earlier than the
law by a stated interval, not because he is doing chronology for its own sake.

**And the round four hundred survives everything.** Stephen quotes it at Acts 7:6, in a speech that
elsewhere follows the Septuagint closely. Both figures were current, and no New Testament writer
shows any anxiety about the difference.

### Why the wiki does not pick one

Every reconstruction has to adjust something: the meaning of *dôr*, the text of Exodus 12:40, the
genealogies of Exodus 6, or the date of the exodus itself (1 Kings 6:1's four hundred eighty years
before Solomon's temple points to a fifteenth-century exodus; Exodus 1:11's Rameses points to a
thirteenth-century one, and Judges 11:26's three hundred years points somewhere in between). The
biblical numbers are internally recoverable in more than one way and none of the ways is forced. The
honest report is the spread.

### What the prophecy is doing in the chapter

The chronological puzzle can obscure what Genesis 15:13–16 is for, so it is worth stating.

**It is an answer to a question about the land.** Abram asks *"how will I know that I will inherit
it?"* (15:8), and the answer he gets is: four hundred years of slavery first, and you personally will
be dead and buried before any of it begins. Genesis treats this as reassurance. What makes it
reassuring is that a promise with a delay measured in centuries is a promise nobody could have
invented to be comfortable, and that God attaches to it a return date, a judged nation, and a
departure with plunder.

**It makes the exodus a covenant obligation before it is an event.** When Exodus 2:24 says *"God
heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob"*, the
covenant it means is this one. The rescue from Egypt is not a new decision; it is Genesis 15 falling
due. See [[Remembrance]].

**And it repeats a descent Abram has already made.** He went down to Egypt in a famine, was afflicted
by nothing but his own fear, and came out with livestock and servants after God struck Pharaoh with
plagues (12:10–20). Chapter 15 tells him his offspring will do the same thing at national scale. See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

### The wealth

*"Afterward they will come out with great wealth"* (15:14) is *rəkūš gādôl*, and *rəkūš* is Genesis
14's word for the plunder of Sodom — used four times in that chapter (14:11, 12, 16, 21), including in
the offer Abram refuses under oath. He turns down *rəkūš* from a king so that no one can say *"I have
made Abram rich"* (14:23), and one chapter later he is told his offspring will leave Egypt loaded
with it. The one who gives it will not be a king of Sodom. Exodus 12:35–36 pays out: *"articles of
silver, and articles of gold, and clothing"*, asked for and given, *"and they plundered the
Egyptians."* See [[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].

## Significance

- **The exodus is promised before the family that undergoes it exists.** Genesis 15:13 is spoken to a
  childless man, and its subject is a nation in bondage. Everything Exodus does, Genesis has already
  put on a schedule.
- **The delay is theological, not incidental.** 15:16 gives the reason — *"the iniquity of the
  Amorite is not yet full"* — which makes four centuries of Israelite slavery the time in which
  another people's guilt accumulates. See [[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]].
- **And the chronology is genuinely open.** The Bible transmits two figures, three ancient texts of
  the key verse, and two New Testament citations that use different ones. A reader is entitled to a
  reconstruction and not entitled to certainty.
