# At Night with a Man Who Had Drunk - Genesis 19.30-38 and Ruth 3

## Passages involved

- [[Lot and His Daughters]] — Genesis 19:30–38; *They made their father drink wine that night: and the
  firstborn went in, and lay with her father. He didn't know when she lay down, nor when she arose* (19:33).
  The firstborn's son is *Moab … the father of the Moabites to this day* (19:37).
- [[Ruth at the Threshing Floor]] — Ruth 3:1–18; *When Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was merry,
  he went to lie down … She came softly, uncovered his feet, and laid down* (3:7).
- Compare [[Judah and Tamar]] — Genesis 38:12–26; a widow who goes to a man in disguise to obtain the child
  owed to her dead husband, named by Ruth 4:12. See
  [[Levirate Marriage - Genesis 38, Deuteronomy 25, and Ruth]].
- Compare [[The Drunkenness of Noah and the Drunkenness of Lot]] — wine, a father, and what his children
  do while he does not know.

## Analysis

**The shared verbs.** Genesis 19:33 tells the elder daughter's night in four verbs: she *came*
(*wattāḇōʾ*), *lay down* (*wattiškaḇ*), and her father *did not know* (*lōʾ yāḏaʿ*) when she lay down or
when she *arose* (*ûḇəqûmāh*); 19:35 repeats them for the younger (WLC). Ruth 3 uses the same four: Ruth
*came* softly and *lay down* (*wattāḇōʾ … wattiškāḇ*, 3:7), *rose up* (*wattāqom*, 3:14), and Boaz says
*Let it not be known* (*ʾal-yiwwāḏaʿ*, 3:14) (WLC). Both nights begin with a man who has drunk — Lot made
drunk on purpose, Boaz who *had eaten and drunk, and his heart was merry*. Both are the act of women
without husbands, planned in a conversation between two women (Genesis 19:31–32, 34; Ruth 3:1–5), for the
sake of a family line: *that we may preserve our father's family line* (19:32), and, in Ruth, the redeemer's duty
that will *raise up the name of the dead* (4:5, 10; not yet ingested).

**The differences.** Lot does not know (19:33, 35); Boaz wakes at midnight and asks *Who are you?* (3:8–9).
The daughters say *there is not a man in the earth to come in to us* (19:31); Boaz praises Ruth for not
following *young men, whether poor or rich* (3:10) — there were men, and she came to this one for the
family's sake. The daughters act in secret and the act is incest; Ruth names herself and asks for marriage
and redemption in words (3:9), and Boaz answers with a blessing, a promise, an obstacle under the law, and
an oath (3:10–13). Genesis reports conception (19:36); Ruth 3 reports nothing but sleep, and the
conception comes after the marriage at the gate (4:13). The narrator of Ruth does not say what happened on
the threshing floor beyond that she *lay at his feet until the morning* (3:14).

**Moab on both sides.** The first story ends with the birth of Moab (19:37). The heroine of the second is
called *the Moabitess* five times in the book (1:22; 2:2, 21; 4:5, 10), though not in chapter 3. A reader
who knows where Moab came from meets a Moabite woman lying down at night beside a man who has drunk. Ruth
never mentions Lot, and the comparison is drawn from the shared verbs and the shared circumstances, not
from any quotation; the wiki records it as a reading. See
[[Moab and Ammon - The Two Nations Born in the Cave]].

**The third night.** Between these two stands Judges' night at Gibeah, which began with *let your heart be
merry* (Judges 19:6) and *As they were making their hearts merry* (19:22) — *yāṭaḇ* with *heart*, the
phrase of Ruth 3:7 (WLC) — and ended with a woman dead at a door. See
[[The Levite's Concubine at Gibeah]]. Ruth stands after Judges in the English canon (see [[Ruth]]).

**Limits.** The four verbs are among the commonest in Hebrew, and any night scene might use them; the
case rests on their clustering with wine, a woman's initiative, a family line, and Moab. Whether the author
of Ruth meant the echo is not recoverable. The verbs are compared from the WLC; the Genesis and Ruth texts
are quoted from the stored WEB.

## Significance

If the echo is heard, Ruth 3 is the Moabite story told the other way: the same night, the same verbs, and
a woman who asks openly, a man who is awake and keeps the law, and a child born after a marriage. It sits
beside the book's own comparison with Tamar (Ruth 4:12), which also takes a story of a woman's initiative
at the edge of sexual sin and sets it inside the line of [[Perez]]. See [[Ruth (person)]], [[Boaz]],
[[Lot]], [[Sexual Sin]], and [[Nakedness and Covering]].
