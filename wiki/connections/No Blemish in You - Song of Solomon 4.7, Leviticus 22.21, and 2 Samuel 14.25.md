# No Blemish in You - Song of Solomon 4.7, Leviticus 22.21, and 2 Samuel 14.25

## Passages involved

- **Song of Solomon 4:7** — [[You Are All Beautiful]]: *"You are all beautiful, my love. There is no
  spot in you."* (*ûmûm ʾên bāḵ*)
- **Leviticus 21:17–23** — [[The Holiness of the Priests]] *(stored WEB; ingested)* — *"None of your offspring
  throughout their generations who has a defect may approach to offer the bread of his God"*
  (*ʾăšer yihyeh ḇô mûm*)
- **Leviticus 22:20–21** *(stored WEB; not yet ingested)* — *"But whatever has a
  defect, that you shall not offer"*; a peace offering *"shall be perfect to be accepted. It shall have
  no defect"* (*tāmîm … kol-mûm lōʾ yihyeh-bô*)

## Analysis

**One word in two settings.** *Mûm* is a *defect* or *blemish*. In Leviticus it is a ritual word. A
descendant of Aaron with a *mûm* may not bring the bread of his God to the altar (21:17–18), and an
animal with a *mûm* may not be offered, because *"it shall not be acceptable for you"* (22:20). An
offering must be *tāmîm*, *whole*, and have no *mûm* (22:21).

In 2 Samuel and Daniel the same word is used about people's appearance, without any ritual setting.
Absalom is praised for his beauty, and *"there was no defect in him"* from head to foot (2 Samuel
14:25). The youths chosen to serve in the king's palace have no *mûm* and are *ṭôḇê marʾeh*,
*good-looking* (Daniel 1:4). The WEB uses *defect* in all of these verses; at Song 4:7 it uses *spot*.

**The Song's use.** Song 4:7 belongs with 2 Samuel 14:25 and Daniel 1:4. It is praise of a body:
*kullāḵ yāpâ*, *all of you is beautiful*, and no *mûm*. Absalom's verse has the same shape, the
whole body (*from the sole of his foot … to the crown of his head*) followed by *no mûm*. The Song gives
the whole body in the verses before (4:1–5) and then the summary.

Many Christian readers take Song 4:7 with Ephesians 5:27, where the assembly is presented *"without
defect"*, and read the Song's bride as the church. That reading is an interpretation of the whole Song,
which the wiki records and does not adopt. The Song itself is love poetry, and 4:7 does not mention
an altar, a priest, or an offering. Because the Greek of Ephesians 5:27 has not been checked, this page
makes no claim about which Greek word stands behind *"without defect"* there or whether it echoes *mûm*.

**A contrast worth noting.** Absalom's flawless beauty comes just before his rebellion against
his father David (2 Samuel 15, not yet ingested). In 2 Samuel the praise of his body does not mean
praise of his character. The Song makes no such judgment about the woman. The two verses share a word
and a form, not a moral.

**Further praise in the English text.** [[My Dove Is Unique]] calls the woman the lover's perfect
one (6:9), following his praise of her whole beauty in 4:7. This is an English-text
comparison only: it does not assert that the Hebrew term is the same as the word studied
above. The contrast with many royal women and their ensuing praise places 6:9 within
love poetry. Neither verse by itself declares her sinless or suitable for sacrifice.
Source for this addition: stored WEB Song 4:7; 6:8–10.

**The full priestly rule now ingested.** Leviticus 21:22 explicitly allows the descendant
excluded from altar service to eat holy and most holy food. Verse 23 limits approach to
the veil and altar. The exclusion concerns eligibility for these actions, not a stated
personal offense or loss of every priestly entitlement. This qualification also prevents
reading bodily wholeness as a universal measure of moral worth. Source: stored WEB
Leviticus 21:17–23; this addition makes no new source-language claim.

## Significance

The word shows that Hebrew could use the same language for a fit offering and for a beautiful person.
The Song uses it for the second. The wiki keeps both uses visible so that the ritual sense is neither
read into the Song nor forgotten. See [[Sacrifice and Offering]], [[Seeing and Being Seen]], and
[[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]].
