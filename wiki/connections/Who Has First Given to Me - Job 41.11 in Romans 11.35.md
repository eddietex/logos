# Who Has First Given to Me - Job 41.11 in Romans 11.35

## Passages involved

- **Job 41:11** — Yahweh, in the second whirlwind speech: *"Who has first given to me, that I should repay
  him? Everything under the heavens is mine."* — [[Behemoth and Leviathan]]
- **Romans 11:33–36** — *"Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God! How
  unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out! 'For who has known the mind of the Lord?
  Or who has been his counselor?' 'Or who has first given to him, and it will be repaid to him again?' For
  of him, and through him, and to him, are all things. To him be the glory for ever! Amen."*
- **Isaiah 40:13–14** — *"Who has directed Yahweh's Spirit, or has taught him as his counselor? Who did he
  take counsel with …?"*: the source of Romans 11:34, the line before the quotation of Job.
- **Job 22:2–3** — Eliphaz: *"Is it any pleasure to the Almighty, that you are righteous? Or does it
  benefit him, that you make your ways perfect?"* — [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]
- **Job 35:7** — Elihu: *"If you are righteous, what do you give him? Or what does he receive from your
  hand?"* — [[Elihu on God's Justice]]
- **Job 1:9** — the accuser: *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* — [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]]
- **1 Corinthians 3:19** — the other marked New Testament quotation of Job (5:13) —
  [[He Takes the Wise in Their Craftiness - Job 5.13 in 1 Corinthians 3.19]]

## Analysis

**Job 41:11 is the sentence in the whirlwind speeches that comes nearest to a thesis, and it is the one
Paul chose.** In Job it sits at the hinge of the Leviathan portrait: no one is fierce enough to rouse
Leviathan; *"Who then is he who can stand before me? Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?
Everything under the heavens is mine"* (41:10–11). The argument runs from a creature no one can face, to
its maker no one can face, to the reason: **God is in no one's debt, because there is nothing anyone could
give him that is not already his.**

**In Job, the line answers the whole book's economics.** The accuser's question was whether Job feared God
*"for nothing"* (1:9) — whether piety was a purchase. The friends answered yes in the other direction:
righteousness earns, and suffering is the bill. Eliphaz (22:2–3) and Elihu (35:7) both reached the premise
that a man's righteousness gives God nothing, and both used it against Job — as a reason God would not be
moved by his innocence. Job's lawsuit accepted the economics from his side: his integrity was a debt God had
failed to pay. **41:11 states the premise in God's own mouth and draws neither conclusion.** God owes
nothing; that is not a reason he will not act, and it is not a verdict on Job. It removes the ledger, and
with it both the friends' theology and the grounds of Job's suit. See
[[Can a Man Be Profitable to God - Job 22.2-3, Job 35.6-8, and Luke 17.10]].

**In Romans, it closes the argument of chapters 9–11.** Paul has spent three chapters on why Israel has
largely refused the gospel and whether God's promises have failed, and he ends — *"God has shut up all to
disobedience, that he might have mercy on all"* (11:32) — not with a further explanation but with a doxology
built from two quotations: Isaiah 40:13 (*who has known the mind of the Lord?*) and Job 41:11 (*who has first
given to him?*). **Paul uses the two Old Testament texts in which God's freedom from human counsel and
human claim is stated most bluntly**, and uses Job's in exactly Job's sense: mercy cannot be owed, so it can
be given to all.

**The form of the quotation.** Romans 11:35 is in the third person (*to him*), Job 41:11 in the first (*to
me*), as the setting requires. The WEB of Job follows the Hebrew (*hiqdîmanî wa'ăšallēm*, literally *who has
come before me, that I should repay?*); the Septuagint of this verse reads quite differently. Paul's Greek
agrees with the Hebrew sense, which is the same pattern as his quotation of Job 5:13 at 1 Corinthians 3:19.
**One uncertainty is recorded and not resolved:** some manuscripts of the Septuagint of Isaiah 40 carry an
extra line after 40:13–14 almost identical to Romans 11:35, which would make Paul's two quotations a single
continuous citation of Isaiah. Most scholars judge that line a later addition made under the influence of
Romans, and take Romans 11:35 as Job 41:11; the wiki follows that view. The Septuagint wording is not quoted
here because it has not been checked against a published text in this pass.

## Significance

- **It is one of only two marked New Testament quotations of Job,** and the only one from God's speeches —
  the other is from Eliphaz (1 Corinthians 3:19), and the unmarked echo at Philippians 1:19 is from Job
  himself. See [[This Will Turn Out for My Deliverance - Job 13.16 in Philippians 1.19]].
- **It shows how the New Testament read the whirlwind.** Paul does not use the speeches as a rebuke to
  questioning; he uses them as praise at the end of the hardest question he asks. The line that closes
  Job's lawsuit opens a doxology.
- **It explains the epilogue in advance.** When Job's fortunes are restored (42:10), the book has already
  ruled out reading the restoration as wages. Whatever Job receives is not repayment. See [[Retribution]].
