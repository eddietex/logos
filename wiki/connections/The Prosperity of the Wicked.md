# The Prosperity of the Wicked

## Passages involved

- **Job 15:20–35** — Eliphaz: *"The wicked man writhes in pain all his days"* —
  [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]]
- **Job 18:5–21** — Bildad: the lamp put out, the six snares, the erased name —
  [[Bildad's Second Speech]]
- **Job 20:4–29** — Zophar: *"the triumphing of the wicked is short"* — [[Zophar's Second Speech]]
- **Job 21:7–34** — Job's answer: *"Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow mighty in
  power?"* *(not yet ingested)*
- **Job 24:1–25** — Job again, on crimes that go unpunished in broad daylight *(not yet ingested)*
- **Job 27:13–23** — the same doctrine as the friends', in Job's mouth, in the disturbed third cycle
  *(not yet ingested)*
- **Psalm 37:1–2, 9–11, 35–36** — *"Don't fret because of evildoers … for they shall soon be cut
  down like the grass"*
- **Psalm 73:1–28** — *"my feet were almost gone … For I was envious of the arrogant, when I saw the
  prosperity of the wicked"*, and the turn at verse 17
- **Psalm 49:5–20** — the rich who *"call their lands after their own names"*, and death as the
  leveller
- **Jeremiah 12:1–2** — *"Why does the way of the wicked prosper? Why are all they at ease who deal
  very treacherously?"*
- **Habakkuk 1:2–4, 13** — *"Why do you tolerate those who deal treacherously, and keep silent when
  the wicked swallows up the man who is more righteous than he?"*
- **Malachi 3:13–15** — *"It is vain to serve God … now we call the proud happy."*
- **Ecclesiastes 7:15; 8:14; 9:2** — the flat empirical denial
- **Luke 16:19–31** — the rich man and Lazarus, the accounting relocated past death
- **2 Peter 2:9; 3:8–9** — judgment reserved, and the delay explained as patience

## Analysis

**The friends say the same thing three times in six chapters, and the repetition is the evidence.**
The second cycle of the dialogue contains three long set-piece portraits of the wicked man's
downfall — Eliphaz's at 15:20–35, Bildad's at 18:5–21, Zophar's at 20:4–29 — and none of them
answers anything Job has said. He has claimed clean hands, asked for a witness in heaven, demanded a
guarantor, begged for pity, and named a living Redeemer, and the friends have replied, three times,
with a description of what happens to bad men. The portraits differ only in imagery: Eliphaz's
wicked man is a soldier charging a fortified God, Bildad's walks into a snare, Zophar's eats
something that turns to venom. The argument does not develop, because there is no argument left —
there is a doctrine and a man who does not fit it, and the friends have decided to restate the
doctrine louder.

**Each portrait is also a portrait of Job**, and the reader can check it item by item: fire from the
sky consuming the flocks (Job 1:16, against Zophar's *"unfanned fire … in his tent"*, 20:26), a
house collapsed on the children (1:19, against Bildad's *"neither son nor grandson"*, 18:19), a body
consumed (2:7, against 18:13), a name become a byword (17:6, against 18:17). The friends never
accuse Job directly in these chapters. They describe him and let the description do the work.

**Zophar's version is the one that gives the game away**, because it makes a concession the others
avoid. *"The triumphing of the wicked is short, the joy of the godless but for a moment"* (20:5)
grants that the wicked triumph. The claim has moved from *the wicked suffer* to *the wicked suffer
eventually*, and the second claim cannot support the inference the friends need: if the timing is
variable, a man's present ruin proves nothing about his past. See [[Retribution]] for the general
form of this failure.

**Job's answer in chapter 21 is the frontal one, and its method is observation.** He does not
dispute that God judges. He counts. *"Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow mighty in
power? Their child is established with them in their sight … Their houses are safe from fear,
neither is the rod of God upon them"* (21:7–9). He takes Bildad's own image and turns it into a
frequency question — *"How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out…"* (21:17); see
[[The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out]] — and he finishes with the funeral: the wicked man is carried to
the grave, a watch is kept over his tomb, *"the clods of the valley shall be sweet to him"*, and
*"All men shall draw after him, as there were innumerable before him"* (21:32–33). The good death
is the detail that hurts. The friends can accommodate a wicked man who dies rich; they cannot
accommodate one who dies rich, old, and mourned.

**Psalm 73 is the same problem worked by someone who does not have to win an argument**, and it is
the closest thing in Scripture to a resolution. The psalmist admits the doctrine nearly failed for
him — *"my feet were almost gone; my steps had nearly slipped"* — and he admits why: he looked, and
the wicked were fine. What changes at verse 17 is not the evidence. *"Until I went into God's
sanctuary, and considered their latter end"* relocates the accounting rather than denying the
observation, and the psalm then ends somewhere Job's friends never go: *"Whom do I have in heaven?
There is no one on earth whom I desire besides you"* — the relationship valued independently of what
it pays, which is the exact thing the accuser said at Job 1:9 that nobody does.

Psalm 37 takes the other route, counselling patience on a shorter horizon (*"they shall soon be cut
down like the grass"*), and is vulnerable to the objection Job makes: *soon* is doing a great deal
of work. Jeremiah 12:1, Habakkuk 1:13, and Malachi 3:14–15 all put the question without softening
it, and Habakkuk gets an answer that concerns the timetable rather than the principle.

## Significance

This is the wisdom tradition's central unresolved argument with itself, and the book of Job is
where it is conducted at the greatest length and with the highest stakes. Three points are worth
holding.

**The friends' evidence base is the tradition; Job's is the world.** Bildad appeals to *"past
generations"* (8:8), Zophar to what has been known *"from old time, since man was placed on earth"*
(20:4), Eliphaz to a night vision and to the *"gray-headed and the very aged"* (15:10). Job appeals
to what can be seen — *"Haven't you asked wayfaring men? Don't you know their evidences…"* (21:29).
God's verdict at 42:7 is delivered against the men with the better pedigree.

**Nobody in the Hebrew Bible solves it by denying the observation.** Not Job, not the psalmists, not
Jeremiah, not Habakkuk, not Ecclesiastes. What varies is where the accounting is thought to close:
soon (Psalm 37), in the sanctuary's longer view (Psalm 73), at death (Psalm 49), past death (Luke
16, and see [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]]), or nowhere that can be seen from
here (Ecclesiastes 8:14). The New Testament's characteristic move is to relocate it to a final
judgment, which resolves the theology and leaves the experience exactly where Job left it.

**And the book's own answer is not an answer to this question at all.** When God finally speaks, he
says nothing about the wicked, nothing about their prosperity, and nothing about timing. He asks
about the sea's doors, the morning stars, the wild donkey, the ostrich that forgets her eggs, and
two monsters. Whatever the whirlwind does for Job, it does not do it by settling the account the
friends were trying to balance — and Job, who had demanded exactly that settlement for
twenty-nine chapters, stops asking.
