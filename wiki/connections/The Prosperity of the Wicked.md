# The Prosperity of the Wicked

## Analysis

**The friends say the same thing three times in six chapters, and the repetition is the evidence.**
The second cycle of the dialogue contains three long set-piece portraits of the wicked man's
downfall — Eliphaz's at 15:20–35, Bildad's at 18:5–21, Zophar's at 20:4–29 — and none of them
answers anything Job has said. He has claimed clean hands, asked for a witness in heaven, demanded a
guarantor, begged for pity, and named a living Redeemer, and the friends have replied, three times,
with a description of what happens to bad men. The portraits differ only in imagery: Eliphaz's
wicked man is a soldier charging a fortified God, Bildad's walks into a snare, Zophar's eats
something that turns to venom. The argument does not develop, because there is no argument left —
there is a doctrine and a man who does not fit it, and the friends have decided to restate the
doctrine louder.

**Each portrait is also a portrait of Job**, and the reader can check it item by item: fire from the
sky consuming the flocks (Job 1:16, against Zophar's *"unfanned fire … in his tent"*, 20:26), a
house collapsed on the children (1:19, against Bildad's *"neither son nor grandson"*, 18:19), a body
consumed (2:7, against 18:13), a name become a byword (17:6, against 18:17). The friends never
accuse Job directly in these chapters. They describe him and let the description do the work.

**Zophar's version is the one that gives the game away**, because it makes a concession the others
avoid. *"The triumphing of the wicked is short, the joy of the godless but for a moment"* (20:5)
grants that the wicked triumph. The claim has moved from *the wicked suffer* to *the wicked suffer
eventually*, and the second claim cannot support the inference the friends need: if the timing is
variable, a man's present ruin proves nothing about his past. See [[Retribution]] for the general
form of this failure.

**Job's answer in chapter 21 is the frontal one, and its method is observation.** He does not
dispute that God judges. He counts. *"Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow mighty in
power? Their child is established with them in their sight … Their houses are safe from fear,
neither is the rod of God upon them"* (21:7–9). He takes Bildad's own image and turns it into a
frequency question — *"How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out…"* (21:17); see
[[The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out]] — and he finishes with the funeral: the wicked man is carried to
the grave, a watch is kept over his tomb, *"the clods of the valley shall be sweet to him"*, and
*"All men shall draw after him, as there were innumerable before him"* (21:32–33). Two words make the rebuttal exact. Zophar granted the wicked their joy *"for a moment"*, *ʿădê-rāgaʿ*
(20:5); Job moves the moment to their death — *"In an instant (bəregaʿ) they go down to Sheol"*
(21:13), a quick descent at the end of a long life. And the tomb over which the mourners keep watch
is a *gādîš* (21:32), the *shock of grain* Eliphaz promised the righteous at 5:26; see
[[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]]. When the doctrine retreats to
the wicked man's children, Job refuses that too (21:19–21); see
[[Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers - Job 21.19, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 18]]. The good death
is the detail that hurts. The friends can accommodate a wicked man who dies rich; they cannot
accommodate one who dies rich, old, and mourned.

**Psalm 73 is the same problem worked by someone who does not have to win an argument**, and it is
the closest thing in Scripture to a resolution. The psalmist admits the doctrine nearly failed for
him — *"my feet were almost gone; my steps had nearly slipped"* — and he admits why: he looked, and
the wicked were fine. What changes at verse 17 is not the evidence. *"Until I went into God's
sanctuary, and considered their latter end"* relocates the accounting rather than denying the
observation, and the psalm then ends somewhere Job's friends never go: *"Whom do I have in heaven?
There is no one on earth whom I desire besides you"* — the relationship valued independently of what
it pays, which is the exact thing the accuser said at Job 1:9 that nobody does.

Psalm 37 takes the other route, counselling patience on a shorter horizon (*"they shall soon be cut
down like the grass"*), and is vulnerable to the objection Job makes: *soon* is doing a great deal
of work. Jeremiah 12:1, Habakkuk 1:13, and Malachi 3:14–15 all put the question without softening
it, and Habakkuk gets an answer that concerns the timetable rather than the principle.

**The friends concede the point exactly once, and do not notice.** Eliphaz's reply to chapter 21
grants it in a subordinate clause: the men who told God *"Depart from us"* are the men whose houses
God *"filled … with good things"* (22:18). Then he moves on, to the Flood generation swept away
(22:16) and the righteous laughing at the ruin of the wicked (22:19–20) — the portrait reasserted
beside the concession that contradicts it. Job's answer in chapter 24 is to describe the wicked at
work in the open, taking pledges from widows and clothes from the poor, and to say *"God doesn't
regard the folly"* (24:12).

**Chapter 24 adds the one thing chapter 21 left out: the victims.** Job's first answer counted the
wicked's blessings; his second counts the cost of them to other people — the orphan's donkey, the
widow's ox, the child taken from the breast for a debt, harvesters who carry sheaves hungry and tread
winepresses thirsty (24:3–11). And it puts God closer to the prosperity than any other text in the
thread: *"God preserves the mighty by his power … God gives them security, and they rest in it. His
eyes are on their ways"* (24:22–23). The section that follows, 24:18–20, sounds like the friends'
portrait of the wicked man's ruin; the wiki reads it as either a citation of their view or Job's
imprecation, answered by 24:24 — the wicked are cut off *"as all others"*, at the same harvest as
everyone. See [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] for the crux.
**Chapter 27 then puts the friends' portrait in Job's own mouth, and the wiki flags the result as a
contradiction rather than resolving it.** *"This is the portion of a wicked man with God"* (27:13)
is Zophar's closing line (20:29), and what follows reverses chapter 21 point by point: the children
multiplied *"for the sword"* (27:14), the man who *"lies down rich"* and wakes to nothing (27:19), and
*"A storm steals him away in the night"* (27:20) — the very phrase, *gənābattû sûpâ*, of 21:18's
sceptical *"how often?"* Either the section is Zophar's missing third speech, or Job is wishing the
friends' doctrine on his accuser (*"Let my enemy be as the wicked"*, 27:7), or conceding its end-point
while denying its inference, or reciting it back to them. See [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]] and
[[The Portion of a Wicked Man - Job 20.29 and Job 27.13]]. **On any reading, this is the last time
the portrait is painted in the dialogue.**

### The sentence collection — Proverbs 10:3, 22, 24–30

Proverbs 10 now supplies a sustained positive statement of the rule Job challenges. Verse 24 assigns
realized fear to the wicked; Job 3:25 reports realized fear for the upright man of Job 1:1. The
whirlwind removes the wicked in 10:25, but Job 1:18–22 narrates wind destroying an upright man’s
children. The shortened wicked lifespan of 10:27 conflicts with Job 21:7–13’s old and prosperous
wicked. Job 24:5–11 adds workers hungry despite their labor, preventing 10:4 from explaining every
poverty. These are explicit tensions in outcomes, not evidence that Job is secretly wicked or that
Proverbs denies the value of work.

See [[A Wise Son Makes a Glad Father]]. Source: Proverbs 10 and the cited Job comparisons checked
in stored WEB, 2026-10-25.

### Righteous offspring and violent riches — Proverbs 11

[[A False Balance]] heightens the tension. Proverbs 11:8 promises righteous deliverance and reversal
onto the wicked; 11:21 promises that righteous offspring will be delivered. Job 1:1, 18–22 instead
records an upright man’s children dying, while Job 21:7–13 observes wicked people’s children safe
and joyful. Job 2:3 expressly denies that Job’s ruin had a cause in wrongdoing. Those outcomes
contradict an exceptionless reading of the promises and cannot be silently harmonized by assigning
Job hidden guilt.

Proverbs 11:16 itself grants that violent men obtain riches, but 11:28 denies that trust in riches
lasts. The distinction between acquiring wealth and being ultimately secure allows a moral warning
without denying the visible success of violence. Verse 31 still locates repayment in the earth; the
text supplies no timetable that resolves Job’s observations. Source: stored WEB Proverbs 11; Job
1:1, 18–22; 2:3; 21:7–13.

### The standing house and the suffering righteous — Proverbs 12

Proverbs 12:3 and 7 promise an unmoved righteous root and a standing righteous house, and 12:21 says
that no mischief happens to the righteous. Job 1:1, 18–22 and 2:3, 7 directly contradict an
exceptionless reading: an upright man loses his children in a fallen house and suffers bodily harm
without cause. Job 21:7–13 meanwhile describes wicked households secure and flourishing. These
claims remain together; no undisclosed guilt is supplied to harmonize them.

Proverbs 12:24’s movement from diligence to rule and laziness to slave labor also has a limit. Job
24:5–11 depicts hungry laborers, and Exodus 1:11–14 depicts forced labor imposed by rulers. The
proverbial warning cannot be reversed into a verdict on the poor or enslaved. Reading it as general
instruction is the wiki’s interpretation, not an explicit qualification written into the sentence.

See [[Whoever Loves Correction]]. Sources: the cited Proverbs, Job, and Exodus verses checked in
stored WEB.

### Prosperity as reward, and the swept field — Proverbs 13

Proverbs 13:21 says *"prosperity rewards the righteous"*, and 13:22 that *"the wealth of the sinner
is stored for the righteous"*. Job 1:13–19 narrates an upright man's goods and children lost in a
day, and Job 21:7–8 the wicked growing old with their children established; an exceptionless reading
of 13:21–22 cannot stand beside them, and the wiki keeps both. Proverbs 13:23 qualifies its own
neighbours: a poor man's productive field is swept away by *injustice*, so that 13:18's poverty for
the undisciplined cannot be read backwards as a verdict on every poor man. See [[Hope Deferred]] and
[[Stored for the Righteous - Proverbs 13.22 and Job 27.16-17]]. Sources: the cited Proverbs and Job
verses checked in stored WEB.

## Significance

This is the wisdom tradition's central unresolved argument with itself, and the book of Job is
where it is conducted at the greatest length and with the highest stakes. Three points are worth
holding.

**The friends' evidence base is the tradition; Job's is the world.** Bildad appeals to *"past
generations"* (8:8), Zophar to what has been known *"from old time, since man was placed on earth"*
(20:4), Eliphaz to a night vision and to the *"gray-headed and the very aged"* (15:10). Job appeals
to what can be seen — *"Haven't you asked wayfaring men? Don't you know their evidences…"* (21:29).
God's verdict at 42:7 is delivered against the men with the better pedigree.

**Nobody in the Hebrew Bible solves it by denying the observation.** Not Job, not the psalmists, not
Jeremiah, not Habakkuk, not Ecclesiastes. What varies is where the accounting is thought to close:
soon (Psalm 37), in the sanctuary's longer view (Psalm 73), at death (Psalm 49), past death (Luke
16, and see [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]]), or nowhere that can be seen from
here (Ecclesiastes 8:14). The New Testament's characteristic move is to relocate it to a final
judgment, which resolves the theology and leaves the experience exactly where Job left it.

**And the book's own answer is not an answer to this question at all.** When God finally speaks, he
says nothing about the wicked, nothing about their prosperity, and nothing about timing. He asks
about the sea's doors, the morning stars, the wild donkey, the ostrich that forgets her eggs, and
two monsters. Whatever the whirlwind does for Job, it does not do it by settling the account the
friends were trying to balance — and Job, who had demanded exactly that settlement for
twenty-nine chapters, stops asking.
