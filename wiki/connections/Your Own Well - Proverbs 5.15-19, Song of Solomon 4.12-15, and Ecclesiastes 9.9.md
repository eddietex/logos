# Your Own Well - Proverbs 5.15-19, Song of Solomon 4.12-15, and Ecclesiastes 9.9

## Passages involved

- [[One Event Comes to All]] — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12.

- **Proverbs 5:15–19** — [[The Strange Woman and the Wife of Your Youth]]: *"Drink water out of
  your own cistern, running water out of your own well … Let your spring be blessed. Rejoice in the
  wife of your youth … Be captivated always with her love."*
- **Song of Solomon 4:12–15** *(not yet ingested)* — *"A locked up garden is my sister, my bride; a
  locked up spring, a sealed fountain … a fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, flowing
  streams from Lebanon."*
- **Song of Solomon 2:7** — [[Delight and the Unawakened Love]]: *"I adjure you, daughters of
  Jerusalem, by the roes, or by the hinds of the field"*
- **Ecclesiastes 9:9** — [[One Event Comes to All]] — *"Live joyfully with the wife whom you love all the
  days of your life of vanity … for that is your portion in life."*
- Compare [[The Betrothal at the Well]] — Genesis 24, Genesis 29, and Exodus 2, where marriages
  begin at a literal well.

## Analysis

**The wife as water.** Proverbs 5:15–18 speaks of the wife entirely in water words: cistern
(*bôr*), well (*bəʾēr*), springs (*maʿyānôṯ*), streams (*palḡê-mayim*), and spring or fountain
(*māqôr*; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*). The Song of Solomon uses
the same field of images from the lover's side: the bride is *"a locked up spring, a sealed
fountain"* and *"a well of living waters"*. In both, the water is private — *"for yourself alone,
not for strangers with you"* (Proverbs 5:17); *locked up* and *sealed* (Song 4:12). The image says
two things at once: the wife is what refreshes, and she is not common property. Whether the Song
borrows from Proverbs, or both draw on a common love-poetry idiom, the texts do not say, and the
wiki claims only the shared image.

**Joy commanded, not tolerated.** Proverbs 5:18–19 gives two imperatives — *rejoice* and *be
captivated* — and Ecclesiastes 9:9 gives a third, *"Live joyfully with the wife whom you love"*. In
the wisdom books delight in one's own wife is not a concession to weakness but a positive command,
set against the strange woman in Proverbs and against the vanity of life *"under the sun"* in
Ecclesiastes, which calls it *"your portion"*. The New Testament's closest statements are
directive in the same way: *"let each man have his own wife, and let each woman have her own
husband"*, and *"Don't deprive one another"* (1 Corinthians 7:2, 5); *"Let marriage be held in
honor among all, and let the bed be undefiled"* (Hebrews 13:4). Those texts are cited for the
shared direction, not as quotations of Proverbs.

**Wells in narrative and in metaphor.** In Genesis and Exodus a well is where a wife is found; in
Proverbs a wife is the well. The type-scene and the metaphor are distinct things, and nothing in
Proverbs alludes to the patriarchal stories, but both treat water drawn from a well as the
setting of marriage. See [[The Betrothal at the Well]] and
[[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]] for wells as contested property.

**The same words in the strange woman's mouth.** Proverbs 7:17–18 gives her the vocabulary this page
traces: a bed perfumed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon — spices of the Song's garden (Song of Solomon
4:14) — and *"let's take our fill of loving"*, *nirweh ḏōḏîm*, with 5:19's verb for the wife who
satisfies (*yərawwuḵā*) and the Song's word for love (4:10, *ḏōḏayiḵ*). What chapter 5 gave the son
for his own wife, chapter 7 shows offered by another. See [[The Young Man Void of Understanding]] and
[[Myrrh, Aloes, and Cinnamon - Proverbs 7.17, Song of Solomon 4.14, and Psalm 45.8]]. Song of
Solomon 4:10, 14 verified WEB at bible-api.com, not stored; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra
according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-25.

### One event comes to all — Ecclesiastes 9:1–12

Ecclesiastes 9:9 is now ingested within 9:1–12, where marital joy belongs with bread,
wine, clothing, and energetic work before death. Proverbs 5:18–19 commends delight
in one's wife against the temptation of adultery; Ecclesiastes commends it within days
of vanity. Both give a positive place to marital love, but Ecclesiastes 9:9 supplies
no water image. Its connection to this thread is the command to enjoy one's wife,
not shared imagery or demonstrated borrowing. The wife is a generic addressee's wife,
not an identified spouse of the Preacher. Sources for this extension: stored WEB
Proverbs 5:15–19 and Ecclesiastes 9:1–12. Earlier external verification records remain.

See [[One Event Comes to All]].

**The doe and the hinds.** Proverbs 5:19 calls the wife *"a loving doe and a graceful deer"*.
Song 2:7 swears the daughters of Jerusalem *"by the roes, or by the hinds of the field"*. The
Hebrew noun for the doe of Proverbs (*ʾayyelet*) and the hinds of the Song (*ʾaylôt*) is the
same word, singular and plural (Sefaria, *Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-28). Proverbs
uses the animal as a picture of the wife; the Song uses it in an oath about love. The shared
word is all the parallel claims; no dependence is asserted.

## Significance

Proverbs answers the strange woman not with a warning alone but with a picture of something
better, and that picture is shared by the Bible's love poetry and by Ecclesiastes. The thread
keeps the wiki's [[Sexual Sin]] material from reading as purely negative: in these books marriage
is the positive good the prohibitions protect. See [[Marriage]] and [[Love]].
