# A Garden Locked - Song of Solomon 4.12-5.1, Eden, and the Royal Parks

## Passages involved

- [[Altogether Lovely]] — Song of Solomon 6:2–3; his garden, the beds of spices, and lilies.

- [[A Garden Locked and Opened]] — Song of Solomon 4:12–5:1: *"A locked up garden is my sister, my
  bride; a locked up spring, a sealed fountain"* (4:12); *"Let my beloved come into his garden, and
  taste his precious fruits"* (4:16); *"I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride"* (5:1).
- [[The Garden of Eden]] — Genesis 2:8–10: *"Yahweh God planted a garden eastward, in Eden … every tree
  to grow that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food … A river went out of Eden to water the
  garden."*
- [[The Trial of Pleasure]] — Ecclesiastes 2:5–6: *"I made myself gardens and parks, and I planted
  trees in them of all kinds of fruit. I made myself pools of water."*
- Song of Solomon 8:13 *(stored WEB, not yet ingested)* —
  *"You who dwell in the gardens"* (8:13).
- [[Come into the Fields, My Beloved]] — Song of Solomon 7:10 — *"I am my beloved's. His desire is toward
  me"*, beside Genesis 3:16; see [[Desire and Rule - Genesis 3.16 and 4.7]].

- [[Return, Return, Shulammite]] — Song of Solomon 6:11–7:9.

- [[Our Sister and My Own Vineyard]] — Song of Solomon 8:8–12.

## Analysis

**What the three gardens share.** Each is planted, watered, and full of fruit. In Genesis, God plants
the garden, fills it with trees *"pleasant to the sight, and good for food"*, and sends a river *"to
water the garden"* (2:8–10). The Preacher plants gardens and parks with *"all kinds of fruit"* and
makes pools *"to water"* them (Ecclesiastes 2:5–6). The Song's garden has *"precious fruits"*, a
*"fountain of gardens"*, and *"flowing streams from Lebanon"* (4:13, 15). The Hebrew word for garden,
*gan*, is the same in all three (Sefaria, *Miqra according to the Masorah*).

**Pardēs.** The Song calls the woman's growth *pardēs rimmônîm*, *an orchard of pomegranates* (4:13),
and the Preacher made *gannôṯ ûp̄ardēsîm*, *gardens and parks* (Ecclesiastes 2:5; Sefaria for both).
*Pardēs* is generally taken as a Persian loanword for an enclosed park; this is a standard
observation, not verified here in a lexicon. Its Greek form *paradeisos* is the Septuagint's word for
the garden of Eden, as recorded on [[Eden]], and is the source of English *paradise*. Genesis 2 does
not itself use *pardēs*. So the word links the Song with the royal parks of Ecclesiastes, and only
through later translation with Eden.

**Locked and given.** Eden was closed after the fall, with cherubim at its east (Genesis 3:24). The
Song's garden is locked too, but for the opposite reason: it is kept for one lover, and at 4:16 it is
opened to him by the one who kept it. The Preacher's parks are made *for himself* (*ʿāśîṯî lî*,
Ecclesiastes 2:5) and end in *"vanity and a chasing after wind"* (2:11). The Song's garden is given
away, and its last word is *"Eat, friends! Drink"* (5:1).

**Desire reversed?** At Genesis 3:16 the woman is told, *"Your desire will be for your husband, and he
will rule over you."* At Song 7:10 the woman says, *"I am my beloved's. His desire is toward me."* The
noun is *tǝšûqâ* in both (Sefaria; Song 7:11 in the Hebrew numbering), and it occurs in only one other
verse, Genesis 4:7, according to [[Desire and Rule - Genesis 3.16 and 4.7]]. Some modern readers take
the Song as a deliberate reversal of Genesis 3: desire running from the man to the woman, with no rule,
in a garden. The Song names neither Eden nor Adam, and the wiki records this as a reading, not a
demonstrated allusion. Song 7:10 is now ingested in [[Come into the Fields, My Beloved]].

**The allegorical garden.** Jewish interpretation has read the locked garden as Israel kept for God,
and Christian interpretation as the church or the soul kept for Christ. In both, the garden becomes an
image of a people's or a soul's purity, and the Eden connection is natural to them. The wiki records
these as readings. The Song's own garden is a woman, her bridegroom is let in, and the eating and
drinking are theirs.

**The garden after absence.** In [[Altogether Lovely]] the woman says he has gone down to
his garden, to feed in the gardens and gather lilies (6:2), then declares mutual belonging
(6:3). The garden image returns after the failed search of 5:6. Because 4:12 explicitly
compared the woman to a garden, the return can suggest renewed intimacy. It does not
establish a literal location, another woman, or an account of how he was found. The
singular and plural gardens are both retained rather than harmonized into a map.
Source for this addition: stored WEB Song 4:12, 16; 5:1, 6; 6:1–3.

The nut tree grove in [[Return, Return, Shulammite]] brings the garden motif into
another descent and inspection of vines and pomegranates (6:11). The plant growth
recalls the spring invitation of 2:13 and the orchard of 4:13. Unlike 4:12, 6:11 does
not explicitly equate the grove with the woman. Neither the speaker's identity nor
the grove's geography is established; no new literal location or second beloved is inferred.

Source for this addition: stored WEB Song 6:11–7:9 and the Song comparisons cited above.
No new source-language claim is made.

In [[Our Sister and My Own Vineyard]], the personal-vineyard reading of 8:12 gains support
from the explicit garden comparison of 4:12 and the invitation of 4:16. The woman’s
agency in giving access can coexist with calling the vineyard her own. Neither
passage establishes a literal estate map or a transfer of ownership of the woman.
Source for this addition: stored WEB Song 1:6; 4:12–16; 8:7–12.

## Significance

The Song uses the Bible's first image of a good place for the love of a man and a woman. Whether or not
the poet had Genesis in mind, the comparison is old and recurring, and it lets the wiki connect
[[Marriage]], [[Love]], and [[Joy and Enjoyment]] in the Song with [[Creation]] in Genesis without
claiming that the Song is a commentary on Genesis. Ecclesiastes supplies the contrast: a king's gardens
built for himself, which did not satisfy.
