# Not Willing to Make Her a Public Example - Deuteronomy 22.23-24 and Matthew 1.18-19

## Passages involved

- **Deuteronomy 22:23–24** — [[Marriage Accusations and Sexual Offenses]]: *"If there is a young lady who is
  a virgin pledged to be married to a husband, and a man finds her in the city, and lies with her; then you
  shall bring them both out to the gate of that city, and you shall stone them to death with stones … the
  man, because he has humbled his neighbor's wife."* (stored WEB)
- **Deuteronomy 24:1** *(stored, not yet ingested)* — *"he shall write her a bill of divorce, and put it in
  her hand, and send her out of his house."*
- **Deuteronomy 20:7** — [[Laws for Going to War]]: *"What man is there who has pledged to be married a
  wife, and has not taken her?"* (stored WEB)
- **Matthew 1:18–20, 24** *(not yet ingested; WEB checked at bible-api.com)* — *"After his mother, Mary, was
  engaged to Joseph, before they came together, she was found pregnant by the Holy Spirit. Joseph, her
  husband, being a righteous man, and not willing to make her a public example, intended to put her away
  secretly."* The angel tells him not to be afraid *"to take to yourself Mary, your wife"* (1:20), and he
  *"took his wife to himself"* (1:24).

## Analysis

**Betrothal as marriage.** Deuteronomy calls the pledged woman *"his neighbor's wife"* (22:24), *ʾēšet
rēʿēhû* (WLC), before the marriage is completed; 20:7 calls the woman a man has pledged *"a wife"* he
*"has not taken"*. Matthew uses the same pattern: Mary is *"engaged"* to Joseph and *"before they came
together"*, yet Joseph is *"her husband"* and Mary is *"your wife"* (WEB). In both, the pledge already
makes the relationship a marriage in law, and ending it is described as putting away. The Greek of Matthew
was not checked, so the wiki does not claim a shared vocabulary between the two texts beyond the WEB's
English.

**The two options.** On the face of Deuteronomy, a pledged woman found to have been with another man
faces a public procedure *"at the gate of that city"* (22:24), and the law's distinctions — city or field,
cried or did not — are made there. Deuteronomy 24:1 gives a husband another course, the bill of divorce,
for a wife in whom *"he has found some unseemly thing"*. Matthew presents Joseph as choosing between public
exposure and a quiet putting away, and choosing the second *"being a righteous man"*. Matthew does not
cite Deuteronomy, and does not say which law Joseph had in mind or whether he believed Mary guilty of
anything.

**What the comparison does not claim.** Matthew's account is not a case under 22:23–27. The text says
Mary was *"found pregnant by the Holy Spirit"*; there is no other man, and the narrative is told from
Joseph's uncertainty, not from a charge. Whether stoning for a pledged woman was carried out in Joseph's
day is a historical question the texts do not answer, and the wiki does not answer it. The connection is
only that Matthew's narrative presupposes the legal shape Deuteronomy describes — a pledged woman as a
wife, a husband who can make the matter public or end it privately — and that Matthew names Joseph's choice
of the private course as righteousness.

**Righteousness and the law.** The verse puts *"righteous"* and *"not willing to make her a public
example"* side by side without saying whether the second follows from the first or qualifies it. Readers
have taken it both ways: Joseph is righteous and therefore will not shame her, or righteous and therefore
cannot complete the marriage, yet merciful in how he ends it. The wiki records both readings.

## Significance

Deuteronomy 22:23–24 is the background against which Matthew's first scene of Joseph is legible: the
betrothed woman counts as a wife, and her pregnancy before the couple *"came together"* is a matter the law
would take to the gate. Matthew's Joseph takes neither the public course nor, in the end, the private one;
he *"did as the angel of the Lord commanded him, and took his wife to himself"* (1:24). See [[Marriage]],
[[Righteousness]], and [[Compassion and Mercy]].
