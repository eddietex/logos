# The Life Is in the Blood

## Passages involved

- Genesis 9:4 — *"But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat"* —
  [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- Genesis 4:10–11 — the brother's blood crying from the ground — [[Cain and Abel]]
- Leviticus 3:17; 7:26–27 — a perpetual statute: *"you shall eat neither fat nor blood"*
- Leviticus 17:10–14 — the reason given: *"the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood
  that makes atonement by reason of the life"*
- Deuteronomy 12:15–16, 20–25 — meat permitted anywhere, blood poured out like water, *"for the
  blood is the life"*
- 1 Samuel 14:31–35 — Saul's army eating meat with the blood, and a king building an altar to stop it
- Ezekiel 33:25 — *"You eat with the blood"*, listed among the reasons the land is forfeit
- Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25 — the Jerusalem council's four abstentions for Gentile believers
- Hebrews 9:22 — *"apart from shedding of blood there is no remission"*
- John 6:53–56 — *"unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you don't have
  life in yourselves"*

## Analysis

**One prohibition attaches to the whole human race, and this is it.** Genesis 9:3 gives *"every
moving thing that lives"* as food, without species distinction, without ritual qualification, and
without any of the machinery Leviticus 11 will later erect. Then 9:4 takes one thing back: *bāśār
bənapšô dāmô* — *flesh with its life, its blood*.

The premise is stated rather than argued, and it is not a rule about hygiene, cruelty, or squeamishness.
**Nepeš** is the word Genesis 2:7 uses of the man at the moment the breath enters him — *nepeš
ḥayyāh*, a living being — and here it is predicated of an animal and then identified with its blood.
The claim is ontological: the blood *is* the life, the life belongs to God, and a creature's death
may feed a person but its life may not be consumed along with it. Meat is a grant; blood is withheld
from the grant.

Everything the Torah later does with blood is an unpacking of that sentence.

**Leviticus supplies the reason and joins it to the altar.** Leviticus 17:11 is the single most
quoted verse on the subject and it makes the connection Genesis leaves implicit: *"For the life of
the flesh is in the blood. I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your souls; for
it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life."* Blood is off the table because it is
reserved for the altar — the prohibition and the sacrificial system are the same doctrine seen from
two sides. This is why 17:10 attaches the harshest formula in the priestly code to eating it (*"I
will set my face against that soul… and will cut him off from among his people"*), and why 17:13
requires that the blood of hunted game, which never goes near an altar, still be poured out and
covered with earth.

That last provision is where this thread crosses another. Job demands *"Earth, don't cover my blood"*
(16:18), which only makes sense against Leviticus 17:13 — the covering that ends an animal's claim
is exactly what Job refuses for his own. See [[Do Not Cover My Blood]].

**Deuteronomy generalizes the rule and keeps the reason.** When Deuteronomy 12 permits slaughter for
food anywhere in the land rather than only at the sanctuary — a substantial relaxation — the one
thing it will not relax is this: *"Only be sure that you don't eat the blood; for the blood is the
life. You shall not eat the life with the flesh. You shall not eat it. You shall pour it out on the
earth as water"* (12:23–24). The reason clause is Genesis 9:4's, verbatim in substance. Every later
practice of kosher slaughter, with its draining and salting, descends from these verses.

**And a narrative shows it being enforced.** After the battle at Michmash, Saul's starving army
*"flew on the plunder, and took sheep, cattle, and calves, and killed them on the ground; and the
people ate them with the blood"* (1 Samuel 14:32). Saul's response is to roll a great stone over for
slaughtering on, and the narrator says he *"built an altar to Yahweh"* — the first he built. Ezekiel
later lists eating with the blood among the practices that forfeit the land (33:25). The rule is not
a dead letter in the Hebrew Bible; it is the one food law with a story attached.

**The apostolic decree keeps it, and this is where the thread becomes genuinely strange.** The
Jerusalem council in Acts 15 exists to decide what, if anything, from the law of Moses binds Gentile
believers. Its answer sets aside circumcision, sabbath, and the whole apparatus of clean and
unclean — and then keeps four things: *"that you abstain from things sacrificed to idols, from
blood, from things strangled, and from sexual immorality"* (15:29). Two of the four are Genesis 9:4:
*blood* is the prohibition itself, and *things strangled* are animals killed in a way that leaves the
blood in the carcass. A council that has just declined to impose the Torah on the nations reaches
past the Torah to the covenant made with Noah, which was addressed to the nations in the first place.

That is the most economical explanation of an otherwise puzzling list, and it has been offered since
antiquity: the four abstentions are the commandments that were never Israel's alone. The rabbinic
tradition independently derives a set of seven universal commandments from Genesis 2 and 9
(*b. Sanhedrin* 56a), and the overlap with Acts 15 — idolatry, sexual immorality, blood, flesh from a
living animal — is close enough that the two lists are usually discussed together. See the notes on
[[The Covenant with Noah]].

## Significance

**It makes eating a theological act before there is any religion to speak of.** The first rule given
to humanity after the flood is a rule about dinner, and its ground is a claim about what a life is.
Genesis has already established that human blood cries out from the ground (4:10) and is required by
God (9:5); 9:4 says something smaller and stranger about animal blood — that it too is a life, that
the life is not the eater's, and that the difference between a permitted meal and a violation is
whether the blood was returned to the ground first.

**It supplies the logic Hebrews needs.** *"Apart from shedding of blood there is no remission"*
(Hebrews 9:22) is not an arbitrary ritual premise; it follows from Leviticus 17:11, which follows
from Genesis 9:4. Blood atones **because** it is the life, and the whole New Testament argument about
the death of Christ runs through that identification — *"the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from
all sin"* (1 John 1:7), *"you were redeemed… with precious blood"* (1 Peter 1:18–19).

**And it makes John 6 as offensive as it is meant to be.** *"He who eats my flesh and drinks my
blood has eternal life"* (John 6:54) is said to people for whom drinking blood is the one food rule
that has bound every human being since Noah. The scandal in that chapter is not metaphor-shock; it is
that Jesus takes the single universal prohibition — the one thing withheld because *the life is in
it* — and offers it as the thing that gives life. The demand is unintelligible except against Genesis
9:4, and it is designed to be.
