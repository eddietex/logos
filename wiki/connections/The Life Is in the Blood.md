# The Life Is in the Blood

## Passages involved

- **Genesis 1:29–30** — the grant of *"every herb yielding seed"* that Genesis 9:3 amends —
  [[The Creation of the World]]
- **Genesis 2:7** — *nepeš ḥayyāh*, a living being: the word 9:4 predicates of an animal and then
  identifies with its blood — [[The Garden of Eden]]
- **Genesis 9:3–4** — *"Every moving thing that lives will be food for you… But flesh with its life,
  that is, its blood, you shall not eat"* — [[The Covenant with Noah]]
- **Leviticus 3:17; 7:26–27; 19:26** — the prohibition legislated for Israel as *"a perpetual
  statute throughout your generations"*, with excision as the penalty
- **Leviticus 17:10–14** — the reason given: *"For the life of the flesh is in the blood. I have
  given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your souls"*, plus the requirement that the
  blood of hunted game be poured out and covered with dust
- **Deuteronomy 12:15–16, 20–25; 15:23** — the rule carried into ordinary slaughter once meat may be
  eaten away from the sanctuary: *"Only be sure that you don't eat the blood; for the blood is the
  life"*
- **1 Samuel 14:31–35** — Saul's starving army eats *"with the blood"*, and is reported as sinning
  against Yahweh
- **Ezekiel 33:25** — *"You eat with the blood, and lift up your eyes to your idols, and shed blood.
  So should you possess the land?"*
- **Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25** — the Jerusalem decree's *"what is strangled"* and *"blood"*
- **John 6:53–56** — *"unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you don't
  have life in yourselves"*
- **Hebrews 9:22** — *"apart from shedding of blood there is no remission"*

## Analysis

**The Bible's oldest food law arrives attached to a permission, as its one reservation.** Genesis
1:29–30 gave humanity seed-bearing plants and fruit, and gave the green herb to the animals; nothing
in the first chapter authorizes eating meat. Genesis 9:3 lifts that restriction in the widest terms
available — *"Every moving thing that lives will be food for you. As I gave you the green herb, I
have given everything to you"* — with no species distinction, no ritual qualification, and none of
the machinery Leviticus 11 will later erect. Then the next verse takes one thing back, and only one:
*"But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat."*

**The Hebrew states it as a definition rather than a rule.** *Bāśār bənapšô dāmô lōʾ tōʾkēlû* is
compressed to the edge of awkwardness — *flesh with-its-life its-blood you-shall-not-eat*. **Nepeš**
is the word Genesis 2:7 uses of the man at the moment the breath enters him, *nepeš ḥayyāh*, a
living being; here it is predicated of an animal and then set in apposition to its blood. The verse
does not say blood carries or contains the life. It puts the two nouns side by side. So the
prohibition is not hygiene, not squeamishness, and not kindness to animals: the flesh is yours, the
life is not, and blood is where the life is.

The canon afterwards uses both prepositions without appearing to notice a difference. Leviticus
17:11 has the life *in* the blood; Leviticus 17:14 and Deuteronomy 12:23 have the blood *be* the
life — *"for the blood is the life"*. Nothing is ever built on the distinction, which is itself
evidence that the identification was the point.

**Leviticus supplies the reason, and the reason is the altar.** The verse that explains Genesis 9:4
also explains the entire sacrificial system, and does both in one breath:

> For the life of the flesh is in the blood. I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement
> for your souls; for it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life. *(Leviticus
> 17:11)*

Read it as two halves of one argument. Because blood is life, blood is not food. Because blood is
life, blood on an altar can stand in for a life that is owed. The prohibition and the sacrificial
mechanism are the same proposition — that a creature's life belongs to God — running in opposite
directions, which is why Israel's blood law is not a food rule with a ritual appendix attached. It
is a claim about ownership, and the kitchen and the altar are the two places it shows.

That is also why 17:10 attaches the harshest formula in the priestly code to eating blood — *"I will
set my face against that soul who eats blood, and will cut him off from among his people"* — and why
17:13 requires the blood of hunted game, which never goes near an altar, to be poured out and
covered with dust anyway. The blood is not owed to the sanctuary. It is owed to God, and the
sanctuary is merely where that is usually settled.

That last provision is where this thread crosses another: Job's demand that the earth *not* cover
his blood (16:18) is intelligible only against Leviticus 17:13, since the covering that closes an
animal's claim is exactly what he refuses for his own. See [[Do Not Cover My Blood]].

**Deuteronomy generalizes the rule and keeps the reason.** Deuteronomy 12 faces the practical
problem of a people spread across a land with one sanctuary, and relaxes a great deal: you may
slaughter and eat *"within all your gates"*, clean and unclean alike, as freely as gazelle and deer.
The one thing it will not relax is stated three times in eleven verses — *"Only you shall not eat
the blood. You shall pour it out on the earth like water"* (12:16), *"Only be sure that you don't
eat the blood; for the blood is the life. You shall not eat the life with the meat"* (12:23), and
again at 15:23. The reason clause is Genesis 9:4's, in substance verbatim. The frequency is a fair
index of how load-bearing the rule was, and probably of how hard it was to keep.

**It is the one food law in the Hebrew Bible with a story attached.** After the rout at Michmash,
Saul's famished troops *"pounced on the plunder, and took sheep, cattle, and calves, and killed them
on the ground; and the people ate them with the blood"* (1 Samuel 14:32). The report reaching Saul
is not that the men have broken a dietary regulation but that *"the people are sinning against
Yahweh"*; his response is to have a great stone rolled up for slaughtering on, and the narrator adds
that he *"built an altar to Yahweh. This was the first altar that he built"* — the fix for eating
blood is to put the killing where blood can be handled properly. Centuries later Ezekiel lists
eating with the blood first among the practices that forfeit the land (33:25), in the same breath as
idolatry and murder. The prohibition is not a dead letter between Sinai and Acts.

**And it is the one dietary rule the church kept when it set the rest aside.** The Jerusalem council
released Gentile believers from circumcision and the law of Moses, then asked four things of them,
two of which are Genesis 9:4: *"blood"* is the prohibition itself, and *"what is strangled"* is meat
killed in a way that leaves the blood in the carcass (Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25). Where those four came
from is genuinely disputed — whether from the pre-Mosaic law of Genesis 9 or from the rules
Leviticus 17–18 imposes on the resident foreigner — as is the rabbinic derivation of *ever min
ha-chai* from the same verse. All of that, with the argument on both sides and the rule's afterlife
in the early church, is set out on [[The Noahic Commandments and the Council of Jerusalem]]; what
belongs here is only the observation that the one food rule to survive the council's pruning is the
one whose stated ground is that blood is a life.

**Then Jesus names the prohibition and inverts it.** *"Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man
and drink his blood, you don't have life in yourselves… For my flesh is food indeed, and my blood is
drink indeed"* (John 6:53, 55). To an audience formed on Genesis 9:4 and Leviticus 17 this is not a
difficult metaphor; it is the single prohibition binding on every human being since Noah, stated and
reversed. John reports the reaction accurately — *"This is a hard saying! Who can listen to it?"*
(6:60), and *"many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him"* (6:66). The offense is
deliberate and it works precisely because the hearers know the rule.

What makes the saying coherent rather than merely shocking is that it keeps the premise of the rule
it breaks. Blood is withheld because it is life and because it atones; both predicates are claimed
in the same sentence, and the church's meal is built on the claim. *"Apart from shedding of blood
there is no remission"* (Hebrews 9:22) is not an arbitrary ritual axiom either — it follows from
Leviticus 17:11, which follows from Genesis 9:4. Blood atones **because** it is the life.

**The one place the argument still has practical consequences.** The Jehovah's Witnesses extend the
prohibition to blood transfusion, reasoning that Genesis 9:4 forbids taking blood into the body by
any route, and that a transfusion is a route. The extension is a reading of the rule's purpose
rather than of its wording, and both halves of that should be said plainly: Genesis, Leviticus,
Deuteronomy, and Acts all address blood as **food**, and Leviticus 17:11's stated ground is that
blood belongs on the altar, which does not obviously reach a procedure that puts a life back into a
body rather than taking one out. The wiki notes the position because it is the one live descendant
of this thread, and notes the objection because the texts themselves supply it.

## Significance

**The Bible's first food law is not really a food law.** It is the earliest statement of the
principle that a creature's life is God's property, and it is given as a limit on a permission:
humanity may kill to eat, and may not treat the life it takes as its own. The permission is
enormous, the fence around it is one clause long, and the fence is the point — killing for food is
allowed and is never to become thoughtless.

**Everything the Old Testament does at an altar runs through that fence.** Because blood is
reserved, there is something to put on an altar; because blood is a life, putting it there means
something; and because those two things are true, the New Testament's account of the cross has a
grammar available to it before it says a word. Leviticus 17:11 makes the connection explicit, and
Hebrews 9:22 states the conclusion.

**It is also the clearest case in Scripture of a command given before Israel and outlasting it.**
Genesis 9 addresses eight people who are, at that moment, the whole human race; nothing in it is
addressed to a nation, because there is no nation. That is what made the rule portable. When the
Jerusalem council had to decide what binds people who are not under Moses, it reached past Sinai for
something older, and what it found there was a sentence about blood.
