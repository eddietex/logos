# Mehetabel

## Overview

**The wife of Edom's last king, and the only person in the Hebrew Bible whose pedigree runs through
two mothers.**

> **39** … The name of his city was Pau. His wife’s name was Mehetabel, the daughter of Matred, the
> daughter of Mezahab.

**Four names in half a verse, three of them women's, and none of them explained.** She is the only
woman in Edom's king list, the only queen Genesis names anywhere, and the last person named in the
register before it turns to the chiefs. See [[The Descendants of Esau]].

***Məhêṭabʾēl*** is **God does good** or **God benefits** — *yāṭab*, to be good, with *ʾēl*. It is the
last of a long series of El-names in a chapter about a nation outside the covenant, and Genesis
remarks on none of them.

## The genealogy

***"The daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab."*** **A woman traced back two generations, and
both links are through women.** Biblical genealogies name women regularly, but almost always as
somebody's daughter attached to a father's line; a mother and a grandmother given as the whole of a
pedigree occurs here and nowhere else.

**Why is not recoverable, and the tradition's attempts are revealing about the tradition rather than
about her.** *Mê-zāhāb* reads as **waters of gold**, which is an unusual thing for a person to be
called and a very ordinary thing for a place to be called — so the plainest explanation is that the
last two names are **toponyms**, and the verse is saying where the queen came from rather than who her
mother was. **That would make the phrase a small instance of the thing the chapter's final register
does at scale**: at 36:40–43 chiefs are listed *"after their places"*, and several of the eleven names
there — *Mibzar*, **fortress**; *Timna*, a copper valley in the Arabah; *Magdiel* — are territories
wearing the grammar of a family tree.

The rabbinic reading went the other way and made *Mezahab* a character: a man so rich that gold was
nothing to him, or — in the harsher version — the phrase is a comment on the queen's ancestry rather
than a compliment. **Both are midrash on an obscurity, and the wiki records them as such.**
