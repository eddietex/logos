# God

## Overview

The one who acts in the Bible's first sentence and never leaves the story. Genesis 1:1–2:3
introduces him with no origin, no genealogy, and no rivals — he simply *is*, prior to the
heavens and the earth, and everything else in the account exists because he speaks it into being.

The name used throughout this first pericope is **Elohim**, a grammatically plural form taking
singular verbs, the generic Hebrew word for God used here for God as creator of everything and
everyone. From Genesis 2:4 the text switches to **YHWH Elohim** ("the LORD God"), the personal
covenant name, as the focus narrows from the cosmos to a garden and a relationship. Tracking
which name a passage uses is one of the standard tools of Pentateuchal source criticism, and is
worth noting on each passage page as ingestion proceeds.

What Genesis 1 establishes about him, before any other page can add to it:

- **He creates by speech.** *"God said… and it was so."* No struggle, no material handed to him,
  no assistance.
- **He evaluates.** Seven times he sees what he has made and calls it good, the last time *very*
  good. Creation carries his verdict of worth intrinsically.
- **He separates and names.** Light from darkness, waters from waters, day from night — ordering
  is as much his creative act as making is.
- **He blesses** (1:22, 1:28, 2:3) — the first thing he does toward living creatures, and toward
  the seventh day.
- **He rests, and makes rest holy** (2:2–3) — not from exhaustion, but as a completion; see
  [[Sabbath]].
- **He speaks of himself in the plural** at the one moment he makes humanity (1:26) — see the
  note on the passage page.

### Yahweh God, from Genesis 2:4

[[The Garden of Eden]] introduces a second portrait, and the shift in name marks it. The God who
spoke the cosmos into order is now shown with his hands in the material — and Genesis makes no
attempt to reconcile the two pictures, because the point of setting them adjacent is that they
are one being.

- **He works like a craftsman.** He *forms* the man from dust as a potter forms clay (*yāṣar*),
  *plants* a garden, *builds* (*bānâ*) a woman. Where chapter 1 has him commanding from outside,
  here he is inside the scene.
- **He gives life from himself.** He breathes into the man's nostrils — personally, at close
  range — and dust becomes a living being. Life is not a property granted but a breath lent; see
  [[Life and Death]].
- **He provides before he commands.** Garden, food, beauty, and work all precede the single
  prohibition of 2:17.
- **He entrusts.** The garden is given to be cultivated and kept, and the naming of the animals is
  left entirely to the man: *"whatever the man called every living creature became its name"*. He
  makes room for a will other than his own — which is what makes Genesis 3 possible.
- **He judges something "not good."** *"It is not good for the man to be alone"* (2:18), the first
  negative verdict in the Bible, spoken over his own creation and immediately acted on.
- **He is anthropomorphic without embarrassment.** He forms, breathes, plants, takes, closes up
  flesh, brings the woman to the man, and in 3:8 walks in the garden in the cool of the day.

### As judge, from Genesis 3

[[The Fall]] shows him responding to disobedience for the first time, and the sequence becomes the
template for nearly every judgment scene in Scripture — see [[Judgment]].

- **He comes, and he asks.** *"Where are you?"* (3:9) is not a request for information. The
  questioning descends the chain of the story and requires each party to speak for themselves
  before any sentence is given. The serpent alone is asked nothing.
- **He sentences without cursing the humans.** The formula *"cursed are you"* falls on the serpent
  (3:14) and on the ground (3:17); the man's and the woman's sentences are pronounced without it.
  See [[Curse]].
- **He concedes what is true.** *"Behold, the man has become like one of us, knowing good and
  evil"* (3:22) — the serpent's central claim, granted in God's own words.
- **He provides for the people he has just sentenced.** He makes coats of skins and clothes them
  (3:21). Judgment and provision arrive in the same scene, and they will keep doing so: a mark for
  Cain, an ark for Noah, a call to Abram after Babel.
- **He limits the damage.** The stated reason for the expulsion is not retribution but prevention
  — *"lest he reach out his hand, and also take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever"*
  (3:22). Endless life in this condition is treated as the thing to be stopped.
- **He speaks of himself in the plural again** — *"like one of us"*, matching *"let us make man"*
  of 1:26, at the moment the resemblance is most ambiguous.

### Outside the garden, from Genesis 4

[[Cain and Abel]] runs the same sequence a second time, on a worse offence, and adds two things
the garden scene did not have.

- **He warns before the act.** No one in Genesis 3 is told in advance what is at the door. Cain is:
  *"If you don't do well, sin crouches at the door. Its desire is for you, but you are to rule
  over it"* (4:7). God addresses the anger before it becomes anything, states the danger, and says
  it can be mastered — which is the first time in Scripture a person is told they can win.
- **He accepts and refuses without giving a reason.** *"Yahweh respected Abel and his offering,
  but he didn't respect Cain and his offering"* (4:4–5). The text records the verdict and withholds
  the criterion; both times, the person is named before the gift. See
  [[Sacrifice and Offering]].
- **He hears what has no voice.** *"The voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the
  ground"* (4:10). The victim who is given no words alive is heard dead.
- **He curses a person for the first time** (4:11) — the *ʾārûr* formula that fell on the serpent
  and the ground in Genesis 3 now falls on a human being. See [[Curse]].
- **He protects the man he has just condemned.** The sevenfold vengeance and the sign of 4:15 are
  given in answer to the murderer's fear for his own life, and they work: Cain lives. Judgment and
  provision arrive together again, and the beneficiary this time has killed someone.
- **He asks a question he is not answered.** *"Where is Abel, your brother?"* gets a lie and a
  counter-question — *"Am I my brother's keeper?"* — and God simply moves past it to *"What have
  you done?"*, the same words he spoke to the woman in 3:13.

### His first silence, Genesis 4:17–24

[[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] is the first passage in the Bible in which God does not
appear at all. He does not speak, act, come, question, sentence, or provide. A city is built under
a sentence he pronounced, a man takes two wives sixteen verses after *"they will become one
flesh"*, and a descendant of Cain sings about killing a man and appropriates a promise God made in
his own mouth (4:15) as a threat of his own — and there is no divine response to any of it. After
three chapters in which he has been in every scene, the absence is conspicuous, and it is the first
instance of something Genesis will do repeatedly: let a stretch of human history run without
comment.

He returns in the last two verses, and only obliquely. Eve credits him with Seth (*"God has given
me another child"*, 4:25 — *ʾĕlōhîm*, where at 4:1 she said *Yahweh*), and then *"At that time men
began to call on Yahweh's name"* (4:26). In the chapter's final line God is not a speaker but the
one spoken to. See [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].

## Timeline / key events

- Creates the heavens and the earth and rests on the seventh day —
  [[The Creation of the World]]
- Forms the man, plants the garden, commands, and builds the woman — [[The Garden of Eden]]
- Walks in the garden, questions, sentences, clothes, and exiles — [[The Fall]]
- Receives one offering and not the other, warns Cain, questions him, curses him, and guards his
  life with a sign — [[Cain and Abel]]
- Absent from the whole of Cain's genealogy; returns only as the one Eve credits with Seth's birth
  and the one whose name people begin to call on —
  [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]]
