# Melchizedek

## Overview

Three verses in Genesis, one line in the Psalms, and an entire chapter of Hebrews built on what
Genesis leaves out.

*"Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine: and he was priest of God Most High"*
(Genesis 14:18). He enters after Abram's return from the rescue of Lot, blesses him, receives a
tenth of the spoil, and is not mentioned again in the Old Testament except at Psalm 110:4. Genesis
gives him no father, no mother, no genealogy, no birth, no death, no successor, and no further
scene — in a book whose organizing device is the recurring *toledot* formula, *this is the history
of the generations of*, that is conspicuous. See [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].

**His name is a sentence, and so is his city's.** *Malkî-ṣedeq* is *my king is righteousness*, or,
read as a construct, *king of righteousness* — which is how Hebrews 7:2 takes it. The formation is
not unique to him: Joshua 10:1 has *Adoni-Zedek*, *my lord is righteousness*, king of Jerusalem
some four hundred years later, which suggests *ṣedeq* was an element in that city's royal
titulature rather than a personal coincidence. *Shālēm* shares its consonants with *shālôm*, and
Hebrews 7:2 accordingly makes him *king of peace* as well. See [[Salem]].

**He is the first priest in the Bible.** *Kōhēn* does not occur before Genesis 14:18. There is no
tabernacle, no commanded sacrifice, no altar mentioned in the scene, no Israel, and no Levi — whose
great-grandfather is standing in front of him. Sacrifice has been going on since Genesis 4 and Job 1
without anyone being called a priest; the office arrives here, attached to a Canaanite king who
worships under a name Abram has not used. See [[Priesthood]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

**And he is given the first tithe in Scripture.** *"Abram gave him a tenth of all"* (14:20) —
voluntary, out of war spoil, with no law requiring it and no covenant obliging it. The Hebrew leaves
the subject unstated, and Hebrews 7:2, 4 is the earliest surviving reading that names Abram as the
giver; it is the reading of every major tradition and the one this wiki follows. See [[Tithing]] and
[[The Tithe Before the Law]].

## What he says

The whole of his recorded speech is two lines:

> **19** *"Blessed be Abram of God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth:*
> **20** *and blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand."*

It blesses in both directions in one breath — a man, and God — and it credits Abram's victory to
someone who was not on the battlefield. It is also the first blessing in Genesis spoken by one human
being over another who is not his son or grandson, and the first visible cashing of Genesis 12:3,
*"I will bless those who bless you."* The divine title he uses, *ʾĒl ʿElyôn*, **God Most High**,
with the epithet *qōnēh šāmayim wāʾāreṣ*, *possessor of heaven and earth*, appears here for the
first time in Scripture; *qānâ* covers both *acquire* and *create*, and cognate titles are attested
in Ugaritic and Phoenician. Abram then repeats the whole title back with *Yahweh* in front of it
(14:22), which is the closest thing in Genesis to a statement that the God worshipped elsewhere
under another name is the God who called him. See [[The Names of God]].

## Who he was

Genesis says a king of Salem and a priest of God Most High, and nothing else. Everything past that
is inference, and the traditions run in four directions:

- **A Canaanite priest-king of Jerusalem** — the plain historical reading. City rulers in the Late
  Bronze Levant routinely held both offices, the Jerusalem king in Joshua 10:1 carries a matching
  *ṣedeq* name, and Psalm 76:2 equates Salem with Zion. On this reading Genesis 14 records Abram
  meeting a foreign worshipper of the high god and recognizing him, which is remarkable enough
  without any further identification.
- **Shem** — a rabbinic tradition (*Nedarim* 32b; Targum Neofiti and Pseudo-Jonathan at Genesis
  14:18) that takes Genesis 11's numbers literally, notes that Shem outlives Abram, and identifies
  the two. It then reads the blessing of 14:19–20 as the occasion on which priesthood passed from
  Shem's line to Abram's, because he blessed Abram before he blessed God. It is a harmonization, and
  it has the merit of explaining how a non-Israelite could be a legitimate priest of the true God.
- **A heavenly deliverer** — 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), a first-century BC text from Qumran, makes him
  a celestial figure who proclaims the liberty of the final jubilee, executes God's judgment, and
  presides over the heavenly council, with Psalm 82:1's *ʾĕlōhîm* applied to him. 2 Enoch adds a
  miraculous-birth legend. Neither is dependent on Hebrews, and both show what the silence of
  Genesis 14 was already generating before the New Testament.
- **A pre-incarnate appearance of Christ** — a minority Christian reading, from some of the fathers
  onward. Hebrews 7:3 tells against it rather than for it: *"made like the Son of God"* is a
  comparison, and a comparison distinguishes.

This wiki records the range and endorses none. What can be said flatly is that Genesis withholds his
genealogy, and that everything built on him is built on the withholding.

## In Psalm 110 and Hebrews

Psalm 110:4 — *"You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek"* — is the single Old Testament
return to Genesis 14, and it does something Genesis did not: it makes his priesthood a *category*,
and attaches it to a Davidic king. That is the hinge Hebrews 5–7 turns on. Hebrews argues that a
priesthood older than Levi's, held by a man who blessed Abraham and was tithed by him, is the
superior one — because *"the lesser is blessed by the greater"* (7:7), and because Levi, still in
Abraham's body, *"has paid tithes"* through him (7:9–10). It reads the absent genealogy as
theologically loaded: *"without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning
of days nor end of life"* (7:3), and therefore a standing image of a priest who does not die and is
not replaced. Hebrews knows this is an argument from silence and uses the silence deliberately. See
[[Melchizedek in Psalm 110 and Hebrews]].
