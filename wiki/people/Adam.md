# Adam

## Overview

The first human being. His name is not really a name at first: Hebrew *ʾādām* is the ordinary
word for "human", and through Genesis 2–3 the text almost always writes *hā'ādām*, "**the** man",
with the definite article that a proper name cannot take. It becomes a personal name only as the
narrative moves on (clearly by Genesis 4:25 and 5:1–5). Read either way, the point stands: the
first man is *humanity*, and what happens to him is told as what happens to everyone.

He is made in two motions — dust of the ground, then breath in the nostrils — and named for the
first of them (*ʾādām* from *ʾădāmâ*, "ground"). He does not receive a soul so much as become a
living being when the breath arrives. His relationship to the soil is the spine of his story: he
is taken from it, put in a garden to work it, sent back out to till it after the fall, and
returns to it in death.

In [[The Garden of Eden]] he is given, in order: a place, a job (*to cultivate and keep it*), a
command with a consequence attached, and — after a search through all the animals fails to
produce one — a counterpart. He names every creature, which is the dominion of Genesis 1:28
being exercised; he does not name the woman, but recognises her in the first human speech in
Scripture, a two-line poem.

Later Scripture makes him a corporate figure. Paul reads him as the head of a humanity under
death, set against Christ as the head of a humanity under life (Romans 5:12–21, 1 Corinthians
15:21–22, 45–49) — the "first Adam" and the "last Adam". Luke's genealogy runs Jesus's descent all
the way back to *"Adam, the son of God"* (Luke 3:38).
