# Laban

## Overview

Rebekah's brother, Jacob's uncle and father-in-law twice over, and the Bible's most durable portrait
of a man who is never actually unkind and never once disinterested.

**Genesis introduces him with a causal clause, and the cause is jewellery.**

> **29** Rebekah had a brother, and his name was Laban. Laban ran out to the man, to the spring.
> **30** When he saw the ring, and the bracelets on his sister’s hands, and when he heard the words
> of Rebekah his sister … he came to the man.

The narrator gives the running first, then goes back and explains it: *when he saw the ring … and
when he heard*. **Sight of the gold precedes hearing of the report**, in that order, and only then is
the welcome spoken — *"Come in, you blessed of Yahweh. Why do you stand outside? For I have prepared
the house, and room for the camels."* It is a warm, correct, complete hospitality, and Genesis has
just told you what set it in motion. See [[A Wife for Isaac]] and [[Hospitality]].

**Nothing he does in Genesis 24 is wrong.** He unloads the camels, feeds them, gives water for feet,
sets out food, hears the whole case, concedes it to Yahweh, hands his sister over, and blesses her
with the covenant promise he has never heard (24:60). The chapter's only friction is his request to
keep her *"a few days, at least ten"* (24:55) and his agreement to let her decide. **He is a good
host and a good brother, and the wiki says so** — the reading that makes him a villain here is
imported from chapters 29 to 31 and is not in this text.

What *is* in this text is the order of the verbs.

### The house that is not his

He runs a negotiation his father should be running. *"Then Laban and Bethuel answered"* (24:50) puts
the son's name first; the gifts at 24:53 go *"to her brother and her mother"* with no father in the
sentence; the delay at 24:55 is asked for by *"her brother and her mother"*. [[Bethuel]] is alive and
present and speaks once, jointly. **Genesis does not explain this**, and the standing readings — an
aged or infirm father, a household in which a brother had authority over a sister's marriage, a name
intruded into 24:50 — are all guesses. The wiki flags it and settles nothing.

***"We can't speak to you bad or good."*** *Raʿ ʾô-ṭôb* (24:50) is an idiom for having no standing to
answer either way. **God will use the same idiom on him** a generation later: *"Be careful that you
don't speak to Jacob either good or bad"* (31:24, repeated by Laban himself at 31:29). His first
recorded position and his last are both that there is nothing he can say.

### What is still ahead

This page grows as those pericopes arrive; what follows is the shape of it.

Rebekah sends Jacob to him to escape Esau (27:43), and Genesis 29–31 is the longest sustained
portrait of a household in the book. He meets Jacob at the same kind of well, embraces him, and says
*"Surely you are my bone and my flesh"* (29:14) — and then, a month later: *"Because you are my
brother, should you therefore serve me for nothing? Tell me, what will your wages be?"* (29:15).
**The affection and the accounting are in the same speech**, which is the same construction as 24:30.

Seven years for Rachel, and Leah in the tent in the morning (29:23–25). Seven more. Wages changed
*"ten times"* (31:7, 41). Speckled and spotted flocks, and a son-in-law who out-manoeuvres him
(30:31–43). A seven-day pursuit across the hill country after his stolen household gods, ending in a
heap of stones, a shared meal, a boundary oath, and — *"The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the
God of their father, judge between us"* (31:53) — the closest thing in Scripture to a glimpse of what
the branch that stayed in Mesopotamia worshipped. Jacob pointedly swears by *"the fear of his father
Isaac"* instead. See [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]].

He kisses his daughters and grandchildren goodbye at 31:55 and leaves the book.

**Genesis calls him *"the Syrian"* — *hāʾărammî*, the Aramean — four times** (25:20; 28:5; 31:20, 24),
which is how the book marks the branch that never crossed into Canaan as having become another
people. Deuteronomy 26:5 keeps the memory in Israel's own confession: *"A Syrian ready to perish was
my father."* See [[The Nations]].

**The name.** *Lābān* is the ordinary Hebrew adjective **white**. Like *Milcah*, *Sarai*, and
*Terah*, it sits in a family whose names have been connected to the moon-cult of Haran; *Lebanon* is
built on the same root, for its snow. Genesis makes nothing of it.
