# The Children of Heth

## Overview

The assembly at the gate of Hebron who sell Abraham a burial plot — and who behave, for eighteen
verses, better than almost anyone else in Genesis.

*Bənê-ḥēt*, **the children of Heth**, and **Heth is a son of Canaan** in the Table of Nations
(10:15) — second-named after Sidon, ahead of the Jebusite, the Amorite, and the rest. By Genesis's
own genealogy, therefore, these are Canaanites. See [[The Table of Nations]] and
[[Canaan (son of Ham)]].

### What they do in Genesis 23

They are addressed as a body, they answer as a body, and they are the reason the transaction is
binding. Abraham asks them collectively for *"a possession of a burying-place"* (23:4); they reply
collectively, and the reply is a courtesy and a refusal at the same time:

> **6** “Hear us, my lord. You are a prince of God among us. Bury your dead in the best of our tombs.
> None of us will withhold from you his tomb. Bury your dead.”

***Nəśîʾ ʾĕlōhîm*** — *a prince of God*, or read as a superlative, *a mighty prince*. **They rate him
higher than he rates himself**: he has just called himself *a stranger and a foreigner*. And what
they offer him is the use of their graves, which is hospitality, and not the ownership of one, which
is what he asked for.

Their second function is legal. The sale is transacted *"in the hearing of the children of Heth, even
of all who went in at the gate of his city"* (23:10), and the deed is recorded *"in the presence of
the children of Heth, before all who went in at the gate of his city"* (23:18), and the closing
verse names them as the conveying party: *"deeded to Abraham **by the children of Heth**"* (23:20).
**Nothing is signed. They are the archive** — the public whose hearing makes a fact durable in a
society without registries. The nearest parallel in Scripture is the ten elders convened at the gate
of Bethlehem in Ruth 4:1–11. See [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]].

### Who were they?

Unresolved, and probably unresolvable from the text.

- **The Anatolian Hittites** — the Late Bronze empire centred on Hattusa, whose law and diplomacy
  are well documented. Their writ did not run in the Judean hill country at any date proposed for
  Abraham, and Genesis's Hittites are a local clan with a field, not an imperial presence.
- **Neo-Hittite states** — the successor kingdoms of northern Syria after 1200 BC, which the Assyrian
  records call *Hatti*, and which are a much better fit for the Hittites named in later Israelite
  history (Uriah the Hittite, 2 Samuel 11; Solomon's Hittite wives and the *"kings of the Hittites"*,
  1 Kings 10:29; 11:1).
- **A Canaanite group Genesis simply calls by that name** — which is what Genesis 10:15 says, and
  what the narrative treats them as.

Genesis is consistent about placing them in the south: Esau marries *"Judith, the daughter of Beeri
the Hittite, and Basemath, the daughter of Elon the Hittite"* (26:34), and Rebekah's complaint about
*"the daughters of Heth"* (27:46) is what sends Jacob to Haran. Ezekiel later throws the ancestry at
Jerusalem: *"your father was an Amorite, and your mother a Hittite"* (16:3). And they are a fixture
of the conquest lists — *"the Hittites, the Girgashites, the Amorites…"* (Deuteronomy 7:1; Joshua
3:10) — as one of the peoples Israel is to dispossess.

**Which is the sharpest thing about Genesis 23.** The people from whom Abraham buys the only ground
he ever owns are named, in the Torah's own lists, among the peoples his descendants are to drive
out. **Genesis does not remark on it**, and the chapter is the friendliest encounter between a
patriarch and the inhabitants of the land in the whole book.

### A proposal about the negotiation

M. R. Lehmann argued in 1953 that the exchange reflects **Hittite Laws §46–47**, under which the
holder of an entire field assumes the feudal service attached to it while the holder of part does
not — so Ephron would want to sell the whole and Abraham to buy the corner, which is exactly the
shape of 23:9–11. The argument is widely cited and widely doubted: the provision's reading is
contested, Hittite imperial law is not plausibly in force at Hebron, and the whole-versus-part
bargaining shape appears in conveyance narratives across the ancient Near East. **Named here as an
observation about the negotiation's form, not as an identification of these people.**

## Timeline / key events

- Named as descendants of Heth, son of Canaan — Genesis 10:15, [[The Table of Nations]]
- Address Abraham as *a prince of God* and offer him the best of their tombs — Genesis 23:5–6,
  [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]]
- Witness the sale of Machpelah at the gate, and are named as the conveying party — Genesis 23:10,
  16–20
- Named as the conveying party again when Abraham himself is buried in the field — *"which Abraham
  purchased of the children of Heth"* — Genesis 25:10,
  [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]]
- Sell to Abraham the field Jacob's sons are later told to bury him in — Genesis 49:29–32
  *(not yet ingested)*
- Esau marries two Hittite women, to Isaac and Rebekah's grief — Genesis 26:34–35, [[Isaac in Gerar]]
- *"The daughters of Heth"*, twice in one verse, are the argument Rebekah gives Isaac for sending
  Jacob out of the country — Genesis 27:46, [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]]
