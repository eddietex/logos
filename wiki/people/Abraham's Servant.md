# Abraham's Servant

## Overview

**The most fully drawn character in Genesis who is never given a name.**

He holds the whole of Genesis 24 — the longest chapter in the book — and the narrator calls him
*hāʿebed*, **the servant**, and *hāʾîš*, **the man**, and nothing else. He speaks more words than
Abraham does in the chapter, more than Rebekah, more than Laban. He prays the first petition for
guidance in Scripture, and the narrator twice stops to report what is going on inside him — he
*looked steadfastly … remaining silent, to know whether Yahweh had made his journey prosperous or
not* (24:21), and he prayed *in my heart* (24:45). And Genesis withholds his name across sixty-seven
verses in which it had every opportunity to supply one.

His office is stated once, in the sentence that introduces him: *"his servant, the elder of his
house, who ruled over all that he had"* (24:2). *Zəqan bêtô*, **the elder of his house** — the senior
steward of a household that by this point runs to flocks, herds, silver, gold, male and female
servants, camels and donkeys (24:35), and to at least three hundred eighteen men born in it
(14:14). **He is property, and he administers an estate.** See [[Slavery and Servitude]].

### What he does

- **He swears.** Hand under the thigh, *by Yahweh, the God of heaven and the God of the earth*
  (24:2–3, 9) — and before he swears he raises the practical objection nobody else in the chapter
  raises: *"What if the woman isn't willing to follow me to this land?"* (24:5). He is the only
  character in Genesis to negotiate the terms of an oath before taking it.
- **He prays.** *"Yahweh, the God of my master Abraham, please give me success today, and show
  kindness to my master Abraham"* (24:12). **No one in the Bible has done this before** — Abraham's
  conversations with God are all begun by God's appearing, and Abraham's intercession for Abimelech
  (20:17) is at God's instruction. This is the first unbidden human petition in Scripture, the first
  request for guidance, and the first prayer answered before it is finished (24:15). See [[Prayer]].
- **He designs the sign, and it is not a coin-toss.** *"Drink, and I will also give your camels a
  drink"* (24:14): ten thirsty camels, one pitcher, and a stranger. The test selects for a specific
  and costly generosity rather than for chance.
- **He watches without speaking.** *"The man looked steadfastly at her, remaining silent, to know
  whether Yahweh had made his journey prosperous or not"* (24:21) — *mištāʾēh*, gazing in suspense.
  He does not conclude anything until the last camel has finished.
- **He bows and blesses, twice.** *"Blessed be Yahweh, the God of my master Abraham, who has not
  forsaken his loving kindness and his truth"* (24:27), and again at 24:52.
- **He refuses to eat until he has stated his business** (24:33), which reverses the order of every
  hospitality scene in Genesis so far.
- **He argues the case, and he edits it.** The retelling at 24:34–49 narrows Abraham's mandate onto
  Bethuel's house specifically, moves the release clause from the bride's willingness to the family's
  consent, moves his prayer inside his heart, reverses the order of gift and question, volunteers the
  size of the inheritance — and **omits, twice over, Abraham's insistence that Isaac must never come
  to Mesopotamia**. None of it is false and all of it is advocacy. See [[A Wife for Isaac]].
- **He refuses the delay** — *"Don't hinder me, since Yahweh has prospered my way"* (24:56) — and is
  overruled only by a question put to Rebekah, which he wins.
- **And he hands her over in one clause.** *"The servant told Isaac all the things that he had done"*
  (24:66), and the chapter is finished with him.

### The one thing he is called by somebody else

*"It is my master"* (24:65) — his answer to Rebekah when she asks who is walking in the field.
**The only words he speaks after leaving her family's house**, and they transfer the title. *My
master* occurs eighteen times in the chapter, always of Abraham; from this verse the master is
Isaac.

### Is he Eliezer?

Jewish tradition says yes, as a matter of course, and the identification is reasonable:
[[Eliezer of Damascus]] is Abram's steward and prospective heir at 15:2, the office described at 24:2
is the same office, and the interval is workable. **It is also unsupported by anything in the text.** Genesis
names very minor figures freely — Reumah, Phicol, Ephron, Aner, Eshcol — and declines to name this
one through the longest chapter in the book. That reads as a choice rather than an omission, and the
likeliest reason is the one the chapter keeps making: **the errand is his master's, the credit is
Yahweh's, and the man in the middle is not the point.** The wiki records the tradition and does not
adopt it, on both pages.

## Timeline / key events

- Introduced as *the elder of his house, who ruled over all that he had* — Genesis 24:2,
  [[A Wife for Isaac]]
- Objects, obtains a release clause, and swears with his hand under Abraham's thigh — Genesis 24:5–9
- Travels to [[Aram Naharaim]] with ten camels and *a variety of good things* — Genesis 24:10
- Prays for success and proposes the sign at the well outside the city of Nahor — Genesis 24:12–14
- Answered before he has finished speaking; watches [[Rebekah]] water ten camels in silence —
  Genesis 24:15–21
- Gives the gold ring and bracelets, then asks whose daughter she is — Genesis 24:22–23
- Bows and blesses Yahweh — Genesis 24:26–27, and again at 24:52
- Refuses food until he has told his message — Genesis 24:33
- Retells the whole errand to Laban and Bethuel, with six changes — Genesis 24:34–49
- Refuses a ten-day delay and takes Rebekah the same morning — Genesis 24:54–61
- Identifies Isaac to Rebekah in four words — *"It is my master"* — and reports to him —
  Genesis 24:65–66

## Related people

- [[Abraham]] — his master; *my master* occurs eighteen times in the chapter and always means him,
  and the errand outlives their last scene together
- [[Isaac]] — his master's son, whom he never names in his own mouth except as *my master's son*, and
  who becomes *my master* in the chapter's last exchange
- [[Rebekah]] — the answer to his prayer, and the only person in the chapter who asks him anything
- [[Laban]] — his host and his negotiating counterpart
- [[Bethuel]] — nominally the father he came to deal with, and effectively a second signature
- [[Eliezer of Damascus]] — the steward of Genesis 15:2, whom tradition identifies with him; the wiki
  records the identification without adopting it
- [[God]] — whom he addresses first, thanks twice, and credits for everything, and who does not speak
  to him or to anyone else in the chapter
