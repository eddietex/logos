# Lot's Daughters

## Overview

**Two unnamed women who are offered to a mob, dragged out of a burning city, and then, in the next
scene, act.** Genesis gives them no names and a great deal of narrative.

They are introduced as a bargaining chip: *"See now, I have two virgin daughters. Please let me
bring them out to you, and you may do to them what seems good to you. Only don't do anything to
these men, because they have come under the shadow of my roof"* (19:8). **The verse is the ugliest
in the Abraham cycle and Genesis passes no comment on it** — neither on the father who makes the
offer nor on the guest obligation that is being honoured at their expense. What the chapter supplies
instead is the outcome: the mob refuses (*"Stand back!"*, 19:9), and the daughters are never handed
over.

They are then in every list of the household. *"Sons-in-law, your sons, your daughters, and whoever
you have in the city"* (19:12); *"Take your wife, and your two daughters who are here"* (19:15);
*"the men grabbed his hand, his wife's hand, and his two daughters' hands"* (19:16). Four hands
belong to them in that verse — they are pulled out of Sodom exactly as their father is, over the
same hesitation, and they are the only members of the household who reach Zoar alive with him.

***"who are here"*** (19:15) is a small clause with weight. Together with 19:14's sons-in-law *"who
were pledged to marry his daughters"*, it is the reason the wiki reads the betrothal sense of
*lōqḥê bənōtāyw* rather than the married one: two daughters, in the house, unmarried, and men
outside who were going to marry them and do not believe him.

### In the cave — Genesis 19:30–38

**The next pericope is theirs, and it is the only passage in Scripture where either of them speaks.**
Everything before it was done to them; here they plan, argue a reason, act, compare notes, and act
again.

> **31** The firstborn said to the younger, “Our father is old, and there is not a man in the earth
> to come in to us in the way of all the earth.

**The elder does all the talking.** She states the problem, proposes the solution, goes first, and
reports back the next morning so that her sister will go second (19:31–34). The younger has no
recorded word. It is a small distribution of initiative that later readers built a great deal on;
see the note on the two names below.

***"that we may preserve our father's family line"*** (19:32, repeated verbatim at 19:34) is their
stated motive, and it is not desire. The Hebrew is *ûnəḥayyeh mē-ʾābînû zāraʿ*, *and we will keep
seed alive from our father* — **the ark's own idiom.** Genesis 7:3 loads the birds *"to keep seed
alive on the surface of all the earth"*, and the pairing of *ḥāyâ* (piel, *keep alive*) with *zeraʿ*
(*seed*) occurs nowhere else in the book. Whether they believe the world has ended, or only their
world, the words Genesis gives them are the words it used for the survival of life on earth. See
[[Lot and His Daughters]].

**The instrument is wine, and it is administered twice.** *"They made their father drink wine that
night"*, and again the next; and twice the narrator says *"He didn't know when she lay down, nor
when she arose."* The verb is *yādaʿ*, which is the verb the men of Sodom used of the visitors
(19:5) and the verb that described these two women as virgins (19:8). **The chapter's three uses of
it run: a town demands to know two strangers, two daughters have known no one, and their father does
not know his own bed.**

They bear **[[Moab (person)]]** and **[[Ben Ammi]]** — the ancestors of Moab and Ammon, Israel's two
nearest and most difficult neighbours, and, through Ruth the Moabitess, of David. Genesis reports
all of it without a word of evaluation, exactly as at 19:8, and hangs two national etymologies on
it.

**The wiki notes the shape without smoothing it: the daughters are offered to men in 19:8, in a
sentence that says *you may do to them what seems good to you*, and take a man in 19:32.** Genesis
places the two scenes eleven verses apart and never connects them out loud.

### What their names are not, and what their sons' names are

**Neither woman is named anywhere in Scripture**, which is worth stating beside the fact that both
are the mother of a nation and one is an ancestor of David. The only names in the pericope are their
sons'. *Ben ʿAmmî*, **son of my kinsman**, is transparent Hebrew and needs no gloss; *Môʾāb* is
given no etymology at all in the Masoretic text, though the traditional reading is *mē-ʾāb*, **from
father**, and the Septuagint supplies the clause outright.

That asymmetry carries the main line of Jewish reception: the elder as the brazen one who named her
son after the act, the younger as discreet, with the difference read back into Deuteronomy 2:9 and
2:19 (*Bava Kamma* 38b). Behind it stands the older and more sympathetic reading — that the sisters
acted to save the human race from what they took for a second flood (*Genesis Rabbah* 51), which
*Nazir* 23a–b treats as a transgression performed for a good end and traces Ruth from. **The wiki
records the tradition and adopts no verdict**, because Genesis supplies their motive and withholds
its judgment, and because it does the same for their father.
