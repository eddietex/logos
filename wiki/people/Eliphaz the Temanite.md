# Eliphaz the Temanite

## Overview

The first of Job's three friends to arrive, the first to speak, the one who speaks longest and most
often, and the only one God addresses by name. He is named first at 2:11 and last at 42:9, and the
book treats him throughout as the senior figure of the three — Bildad and Zophar largely restate
him with less patience and less material.

He is from [[Teman]], an Edomite centre so associated with wisdom that Jeremiah can ask *"Is wisdom
no more in Teman?"* (49:7) and Obadiah can speak of destroying *"the wise men out of Edom"* (8).
The book gives its most confident traditionalist a hometown famous for the very thing he claims,
and then has him ground his argument in inherited wisdom (15:17–19) after inherited wisdom is
exactly what fails.

His three speeches (4–5, 15, 22) trace a hardening that is easy to miss if they are read
separately. **In chapter 4–5** he apologizes for speaking, credits Job's past kindness to the
troubled, bases his doctrine on a night vision, and closes with thirteen verses of promised
restoration — *"Behold, happy is the man whom God corrects"*. **In chapter 15** the courtesy and the
restoration are gone; he calls Job's words the east wind, makes Job's own mouth the evidence against
him, replaces the vision with the authority of the aged, and spends nineteen verses on the doom of
the wicked in terms that describe Job's actual losses without naming him. **In chapter 22** he
invents crimes: taking pledges from a brother for nothing, stripping the naked of their clothing,
withholding water from the weary and bread from the hungry, sending widows away empty. Nothing in
the book supports a single charge. The trajectory runs courtesy → insinuation → fabrication, and
it is driven by the doctrine, not by malice: if suffering is payment, and this suffering is
enormous, then the sins must be there, and if they cannot be found they must be supplied.

## What he is right about, and what he is condemned for

This distinction is the reason his pages are worth reading carefully, and the wiki keeps it
explicit.

**Much of what Eliphaz says is true, and some of it is quoted approvingly elsewhere in Scripture.**
Paul quotes 5:13 — *"He takes the wise in their own craftiness"* — at 1 Corinthians 3:19, the only
direct New Testament quotation of the book of Job, and quotes it as authority. His statement that
no one born of a woman is clean (15:14) is broadly what Genesis 6:5, Psalm 143:2, and Romans
3:10–23 say. His portrait of God's greatness in chapter 5 is unobjectionable.

**What he is condemned for is what he does with it.** *"My wrath is kindled against you, and
against your two friends; for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job
has"* (42:7). The fault is located *"of me"* — in the picture of God his argument requires. That God
is one whose dealings can be read backwards off a man's circumstances, which makes him predictable,
manageable, and unable to be wronged. Defending that God required Eliphaz to be wrong about a real
man in front of him and to keep being wrong more aggressively each time he was contradicted, and
the book's judgment falls there.

The epilogue's remedy is exact: Eliphaz must take seven bulls and seven rams to Job and have Job
pray for him, *"for I will accept him"* (42:8). The man he diagnosed becomes his priest.

## Timeline / key events

- **Job 2:11–13** — [[Seven Days of Silence]]. Hears of the disaster in [[Teman]], sends word to two
  other countries, arranges a meeting, travels, fails to recognise Job from a distance, weeps, tears
  his robe, throws dust over his head, and sits on the ground with him seven days and seven nights
  without speaking. **Named first here, as he will be at 42:9 and as he will speak first at 4:1.**
- **Job 4:1–5:27** — the first speech: the night vision (*"a spirit passed before my face. The hair
  of my flesh stood up"*), the first *"what is man"* passage at 4:17–19, and the offer of
  restoration.
- **Job 15:1–35** — [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]], the consolations withdrawn.
- **Job 16:1–17:16** — answered in [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]], and told at
  17:10 *"I shall not find a wise man among you"*.
- **Job 18–20** — silent through the rest of the second cycle, and included in the plurals of
  [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] (19:2–5, 28–29). [[Zophar's Second Speech]] names the offence he
  will make personal: *"he has oppressed and forsaken the poor. He has violently taken away a
  house"* (20:19).
- **Job 22:1–30** — the third speech, and the fabricated charges — 22:6–9 is 20:19 with Job's name
  attached and details supplied that the book gives no evidence for.
- **Job 42:7–9** — named by God, rebuked, and required to bring sacrifices and be prayed for by Job.

## Appears in

- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:11–13, his first appearance and **the only thing he does in the
  book that God does not rebuke.** Read the invented charges of 22:6–9 — stripping the naked,
  withholding bread from the hungry — against a man who crossed a desert to sit in another man's
  ashes for a week, and the book's real subject comes into view: **not a cruel man, but a decent one
  whose doctrine required him to become cruel.** The trajectory begins the moment he opens his mouth
  at 4:1, and not before.
- [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] — his own chapter, and the wiki's fullest treatment of his method.
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — as the speaker being answered, and as one of
  the three *"miserable comforters"*.
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — inside Bildad's *"we"* at 18:2–3.
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — inside Job's plurals, and among those warned of the sword at
  19:29.
- [[Zophar's Second Speech]] — where the charge he will personalise at 22:6–9 is first drafted.

- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26; **silent here, and what he says next is the measure of him.**
  Chapter 3 contains no claim about God's justice, Job's innocence, or the cause of anything.
  **Eliphaz opens at 4:1 with a complete doctrine of retribution** (4:7, *"who ever perished, being
  innocent?"*), and his first words to Job — *"isn't your piety your confidence? Isn't the integrity
  of your ways your hope?"* (4:6) — answer 3:25's *"the thing which I fear comes on me"* as though it
  were a theological error rather than a confession. **He is the first person in the book to hear a
  lament and reply to an argument**, and the other two follow him

## Related people

- [[Job (person)]] — the man he came to comfort and stayed to prosecute.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the other two friends, who follow his lead
  in each cycle.
- [[God]] — whom he defends throughout and is rebuked by at the end.
- **Elihu** — the fourth speaker, who at 32:9 (*"It is not the great who are wise, nor the aged who
  understand justice"*) contradicts the credential Eliphaz claims at 15:10, and who is nonetheless
  not included in God's rebuke at 42:7.
- [[Eliphaz (son of Esau)]] — Genesis 36:4, 10–11 names a son of Esau called Eliphaz whose own son is
  **Teman**. The overlap of both names is striking, and it is the main evidence that the book places
  its cast in Edomite tradition; whether the two Eliphazes are meant as the same man is not
  determinable, and Job never claims a genealogy. **Genesis 36 is now ingested** at
  [[The Descendants of Esau]], and it turns out to hold four of this book's names — Uz at 36:28,
  Eliphaz at 36:4, Teman at 36:11, and a [[Jobab]] at 36:33 whom the Septuagint's appendix identifies
  with Job. **The wiki reads the coincidence as evidence of a setting and not of an
  identification**; the case is at [[Jobab of Bozrah and the Septuagint's Job]].
- [[Timna]] — the Genesis Eliphaz's concubine, and the mother of [[Amalek]]. Nothing in the book of
  Job corresponds to her; she is listed here only to mark how far the two Eliphazes' families
  diverge once the genealogy is actually read.

## Themes associated

- [[Retribution]] — its principal spokesman in the book.
- [[Wisdom]] — claimed by vision in chapter 4, by ancestry in chapter 15, and denied him by Job at
  17:10 and by God at 42:7.
- [[Sin]] — 4:17–19 and 15:14–16, the friends' anthropology at its two most quotable statements.
- [[Judgment]] — his catalogue of the wicked man's fate is a judgment scene stretched over a
  lifetime.
- [[Suffering]] — which in his hands is never anything but evidence.

## Notes

- **The name.** *ʾĔlîpaz*, usually parsed as *my God is fine gold* (from *paz*) or *God is
  strength*. It is a good name, and the book gives its most misguided character no marker of
  villainy — one of several signs that the friends are meant as sincere, orthodox, well-intentioned
  men rather than as antagonists.
- **The night vision (4:12–17).** Eliphaz is the only character in the dialogue who claims a
  revelation, and the description is genuinely eerie: a word coming secretly, a whisper, dread,
  trembling in all his bones, a form he could not discern standing before his eyes, silence, and
  then a voice. What the voice says — *"Shall mortal man be more just than God?"* — is a
  commonplace, and the mismatch between the apparatus and the content is one of the book's quieter
  jokes. It is also the reason his shift to the authority of the aged in chapter 15 is worth
  noticing: he does not repeat the vision after it fails to persuade.
- **1 Corinthians 3:19.** Paul's citation of Job 5:13 raises a question the wiki flags rather than
  settles: how a New Testament author can quote as authoritative a man God says did not speak
  rightly. The usual answers are that a true proposition remains true in a wrong mouth, and that
  inspiration attaches to the book's report of the speech rather than to the speaker. Both are
  reasonable; neither is stated in either text. What the case does establish is that God's verdict
  at 42:7 cannot be read as *everything the friends said was false*, which is how it is often
  paraphrased.
