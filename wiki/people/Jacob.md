# Jacob

## Overview

The third patriarch, the father of the twelve tribes, and **the only one of the three whose name the
nation ends up carrying** — under his second name, Israel, given at a river in the dark after a
fight he did not start and did not win outright.

**He enters Scripture holding onto somebody.** *"After that, his brother came out, and his hand had
hold on Esau's heel"* (25:26). Genesis names him for the grip — ***Yaʿăqōb*** from ***ʿāqēb***,
**heel** — and then, twenty-seven verses of narrative later, lets his brother re-derive the same name
from ***ʿāqab***, **to supplant, to overreach**: *"Isn't he rightly named Jacob? For he has supplanted
me these two times"* (27:36). **One name, two etymologies, and the book supplies the innocent one at
birth and the accusation from the mouth of the man he took it from.**

As a name-type it is neither. *Yaʿaqōb-ʾēl* — *may God protect* — is attested across the second
millennium BC in Amorite and Egyptian sources, so the puns belong to Genesis's theology rather than
to the name's history, exactly as with *Yiṣḥāq*. See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].

**He is the patriarch Genesis declines to editorialise about.** Abraham's lies were flagged by
foreign kings; Isaac's whole life is narrated as things done to him. Jacob buys, deceives, bargains,
flees, and prospers, and **the narrator's only evaluative sentence in his first chapter falls on his
brother** (25:34). The verdicts on Jacob all come from characters inside the story — Esau, Laban,
and eventually the man at the ford — and the reader is left to do the arithmetic.

### In this pericope — Genesis 25:19–34

**Born second, by moments, and immediately shown reaching for the position he was born out of.**

He is characterised in nine words: ***ʾîš tām, yōšēb ʾōhālîm*** — *a quiet man, living in tents*
(WEB). **The adjective is a problem and this wiki flags it rather than smoothing it.** *Tām* is the
word for moral completeness — [[Noah]] is *tāmîm*, *"blameless among the people of his time"* (6:9),
and [[Job (person)]] is *tām wəyāšār*, *"blameless and upright"* (Job 1:1). The versions render it
here as *quiet*, *mild*, *plain*, or *civilised*, because the moral sense sits four verses before
25:31–33. **The moral reading and the way-of-life reading are both recorded on
[[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]] and neither is adopted.**

**His mother loves him and the narrator gives no reason.** *"Rebekah loved Jacob"* (25:28) — one
clause, no subordinate explanation, against the faintly ridiculous motive supplied for his father's
preference. **A reader four verses from the oracle can supply one; Genesis will not.**

**And the transaction is his, entirely.** He is at the pot; his brother comes in famished; and Jacob
does not offer food, he names a price — *"First, sell me your birthright"* — and then, when the price
is agreed, **refuses to close on a word and requires an oath**: *"Swear to me first."* Then he hands
over bread and lentil stew. **He is the only person in Genesis who converts a moment of another
person's weakness into a sworn instrument**, and the narrator does not say a thing about it. See
[[Birthright and the Firstborn]] and [[Oaths and Vows]].

**What he acquires here is not what he steals in chapter 27.** The *bəkōrâ* (birthright) and the
*bərākâ* (blessing) are separate; he buys the first and takes the second by disguise. **Esau at 27:36
is the one who puts them together** — *"He took away my birthright. See, now he has taken away my
blessing"* — and it is the closest Genesis comes to an indictment.

### What is still ahead

This page grows as those pericopes arrive; what follows is the shape of it, not a substitute for it.

He takes the blessing in goatskins while his father is blind (27), flees to Paddan Aram, and is met
at Bethel by a stairway and by the Abrahamic promise given to him directly for the first time
(28:13–15). He works fourteen years for two sisters and is himself substituted younger-for-elder on
his wedding night — **the deception he ran, run back on him, by his mother's brother** (29:23–25). He
fathers eleven sons and a daughter in Paddan Aram, out-breeds Laban's flocks, and leaves under
pursuit (30–31). He wrestles a man at the Jabbok until daybreak, is renamed **Israel**, and limps
away (32:22–32). He meets Esau expecting an army and is embraced (33). He loses Rachel at Bethlehem
and Joseph, as he believes, to a wild animal, and tells Pharaoh *"Few and evil have been the days of
the years of my life"* (47:9). He dies in Egypt after blessing twelve sons and two grandsons —
**crossing his hands over Ephraim and Manasseh to put the younger first on purpose** (48:13–20) — and
is carried back to Machpelah.

## Related people

- [[Esau]] — his twin, elder by moments; the brother whose heel he holds at birth, whose birthright
  he buys, whose blessing he takes, and who runs to embrace him twenty years later
- [[Isaac]] — his father, who prayed for the pregnancy that produced him and then loved the other son
- [[Rebekah]] — his mother, who receives the oracle about him, loves him without a stated reason, and
  will engineer the blessing and then send him away to save his life
- [[Abraham]] — his grandfather, alive until Jacob was fifteen on the book's own arithmetic, and never
  shown in a scene with him
- [[Laban]] — his mother's brother, named in 25:20 for no reason this pericope needs; his employer,
  father-in-law, and the man who does the younger-for-elder substitution back to him
- [[God]] — who names the outcome before the twins are born, speaks it to his mother rather than to
  him, and does not address him until Bethel
- [[Bethuel]] — his maternal grandfather
