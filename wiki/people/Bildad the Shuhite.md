# Bildad the Shuhite

## Overview

The second of Job's three friends, and the blunt one. Where [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] opens his
first speech with an apology for speaking, Bildad opens his with *"How long will you speak these
things? Shall the words of your mouth be a mighty wind?"* (8:2), and within two verses he has said
the thing no one else says out loud: *"If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered
them into the hand of their disobedience"* (8:4). Job's ten children died in a single afternoon,
and Bildad's account of it is that they got what they deserved. It is the cruellest sentence in the
book, and it is offered as consolation.

His authority is tradition, stated more baldly than Eliphaz states it: *"Please inquire of past
generations. Find out about the learning of their fathers … shall they not teach you, tell you,
and utter words out of their heart?"* (8:8, 10). His three speeches shrink as the book goes on —
twenty-two verses in chapter 8, twenty-one in chapter 18, and six in chapter 25 — and the last of
them is the clearest sign that the friends have run out of material. Whether the brevity of chapter
25 is design or textual damage is disputed; see the Notes.

He is a **Shuhite**, most often connected with Shuah, a son of Abraham by Keturah sent *"eastward,
to the east country"* (Genesis 25:2, 6) — which would place him, like the rest of the cast, among
the peoples east and south-east of Canaan rather than in Israel.

## In the ingested passages

Bildad does not speak in Job 15–17. He is present, and he is included twice.

In [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] he is inside Eliphaz's *"we"*: *"What do you know, that we don't
know? … With us are both the gray-headed and the very aged men"* (15:9–10). The friends argue as a
bloc, and Eliphaz claims their combined authority.

In [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] Job answers the bloc in the plural — *"You
are **all** miserable comforters!"* (16:2) and *"as for **you all**, come on now again; I shall not
find a wise man among you"* (17:10). Bildad's own second speech follows immediately in chapter 18,
and it takes the invitation literally.

## Timeline / key events

- **Job 2:11–13** — arrives with the others and sits seven days in silence.
- **Job 8:1–22** — the first speech: God does not pervert justice; your children got what they
  earned; inquire of past generations; the hypocrite's hope perishes.
- **Job 15–17** — silent, but included in Eliphaz's *"we"* and in Job's *"you all"*.
- **Job 18:1–21** — the second speech: twenty-one verses on the fate of the wicked, with the light
  in his tent put out and terror pursuing him.
- **Job 25:1–6** — the third speech, six verses: *"How then can man be just with God? Or how can he
  who is born of a woman be clean? … how much less man, who is a worm, the son of man, who is a
  worm!"* The last thing any of the friends says.
- **Job 42:7–9** — included in *"your two friends"*, rebuked with Eliphaz, and required to bring
  sacrifices and be prayed for by Job.

## Notes

- **The name.** *Bildad* is uncertain; proposals include a form of *Bel has loved* (with the
  Babylonian divine name) and a Semitic construction meaning *son of contention* or *the darling of
  Hadad*. None is secure, and unlike *Eliphaz* it does not obviously mean anything in Hebrew.
- **Chapter 25's six verses.** In the third cycle the pattern of the dialogue visibly breaks: Bildad
  gets six verses, Zophar gets none at all, and parts of Job's replies in chapters 26–27 sound like
  the friends' doctrine rather than his. Two readings. **Textual disorder** — the third cycle
  suffered damage or dislocation in transmission, and material now in Job's mouth (26:5–14,
  27:13–23) originally belonged to Bildad and to a missing third speech of Zophar; this is a common
  reconstruction and various editions rearrange accordingly. **Deliberate collapse** — the
  breakdown *is* the point, dramatizing the exhaustion of the friends' position, with Bildad
  managing a short reprise and Zophar unable to speak at all. The wiki takes no position and will
  record the arguments on the passage pages when chapters 25–27 are ingested; the observation worth
  keeping now is that the second cycle, where Job 15–17 sits, is the last one in which the pattern
  is intact.
