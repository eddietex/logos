# Job (person)

The page is titled `Job (person)` rather than `Job` because `[[Job]]` is the book page,
`wiki/books/Job.md`. Two files with the same basename make an unqualified wikilink resolve
unpredictably in Obsidian, and — worse — the link checker cannot see the ambiguity, because it
matches on basename too. See the naming rule for book/person collisions in `AGENTS.md`.

## Overview

A man of the land of Uz, *"blameless and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from
evil"* (1:1) — a verdict the narrator gives in the first sentence and God then repeats twice, at
1:8 and 2:3, before any character in the book has spoken. That triple certification is the single
most important fact about him, because every human being in the following forty chapters, Job
included, argues without it.

He is not an Israelite. Uz lies east or south-east of Canaan, his friends come from Edomite and
Arabian territory, he is called *"the greatest of all the children of the east"* (1:3), he offers
his own sacrifices with no priesthood, and there is no covenant, no law, no Sinai and no temple
anywhere in the book. Whatever Job's story is, it is not told as part of Israel's story, and the
God he argues with is named *ʾĒl*, *ʾĔlôah*, and *Šadday* almost throughout the poetry — *Yahweh*
belongs to the prose frame. This gives the book its reach: the problem is posed as a human problem
before it is a covenantal one.

The wiki has him from two directions so far: the prologue — the five verses of [[Job of Uz]] that
introduce him before anything happens, and
[[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]], where everything is taken and he blesses the name
of the one who took it — and then, forty chapters of argument in, the second cycle of the dialogue
— the man on the other end of three friends' speeches and the speaker of the two replies that run
from Job 16:1 to 17:16 and through the whole of chapter 19. Those replies are where the two poles
of his character are furthest apart. He accuses God of tearing him like a beast, dashing him to
pieces, and setting him up as an archery target — and eleven verses later declares *"my witness is
in heaven"* and asks that figure to argue his case against God. Three chapters on he says God has
walled up his road, stripped his crown, plucked up his hope like a tree, and driven off every human
being who ever loved him — and then, out of that, *"I know that my Redeemer lives."* The book's
final verdict on him (42:7, *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job
has"*) is delivered to the friends, and it commends the man who said all of it.

## What he holds to, and what he gives up

Job's position is narrower than it is usually described, and stating it precisely is the whole
game.

**He does not deny that God is sovereign.** The violence of 16:9–14 is a complaint that God is
doing this, not a suggestion that someone else is or that no one is.

**He does not claim sinlessness.** He claims innocence *of anything proportionate to this*, and he
states it in specific terms: *"there is no violence in my hands, and my prayer is pure"* (16:17).
The friends will eventually accuse him of crimes — Eliphaz invents a list at 22:6–9 — and none of
them is supported by anything in the book.

**What he refuses is the trade.** The friends offer a path back: confess, and be restored. Taking
it would require him to affirm something he believes false about himself and about God's dealings.
He will not, and his refusal costs him his last comfort, since a confession would at least have
made the world legible again. In the ingested speech this is why 16:8 stings — *"You have shriveled
me up. This is a witness against me"* — his ruined body is being read as the confession he declines
to make. At 27:5–6 the refusal becomes an oath: *"Far be it from me that I should justify you. Until
I die I will not put away my integrity from me."* See [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]].

**What he wants is a hearing, not relief.** Every legal figure he reaches for — the umpire of 9:33,
the witness and voucher of 16:19, the surety of 17:3, the Redeemer of 19:25 — is an officer of a
trial, not a physician. He asks to be answered far more often than he asks to be healed. The one
exception is the exception that proves it: *"Have pity on me, have pity on me, you my friends"*
(19:21), the single time in the book he asks the three men for anything, and it is not granted.

**And the last of the four officers is not a legal one.** *Gōʾēl*, at 19:25, comes from family law:
the nearest able kinsman, on whom the duty to act falls by blood rather than by contract. Job
asserts one immediately after eight verses establishing that his brothers, relatives, friends,
servants, wife, and children are gone or estranged. The claim is made precisely in the hole the
speech has just dug. See [[The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood]].

## Timeline / key events

Prose prologue and epilogue (Job 1–2, 42:7–17), given here as background for the ingested
speeches; each becomes a linked passage page as it is ingested.

- **Job 1:1–5** — [[Job of Uz]]. Introduced in [[Uz]]: blameless and upright, seven sons and three
  daughters, immense flocks, and a standing practice of offering burnt offerings on his children's
  behalf *"It may be that my sons have sinned, and renounced God in their hearts."*
- **Job 1:6–22** — [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]]. The heavenly council; God, not
  the accuser, raises Job's name; the accuser's question *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"*; four
  messengers in one afternoon; all his livestock, servants, and ten children destroyed. He tears his
  robe, shaves his head, worships, and says *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away."*
- **Job 2:1–10** — [[The Second Test]]. The second council, God's announcement that Job *"still
  maintains his integrity"* and that he was *incited to ruin him without cause*; the accuser's
  *"skin for skin"*; the sores from sole to head; the potsherd and the ashes; his wife's *"Do you
  still maintain your integrity? Renounce God, and die"*; his reply, and the narrator's second
  verdict that *"Job didn't sin with his lips."*
- **Job 2:11–13** — [[Seven Days of Silence]]. Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar arrive from three
  countries, fail to recognise him, mourn him with the rites for the dead, and sit with him seven
  days in silence, *"for they saw that his grief was very great."* This is the last thing the friends
  do that the book approves.
- **Job 3:1–26** — [[Let the Day Perish]]. He breaks the silence by cursing the day of his birth —
  **his day, not God** — in a speech addressed to no one, and asks not to die but never to have been
  born. The poetry begins here, and so does the argument.
- **Job 4:1–5:27** — [[Eliphaz's First Speech]]. Silent, and described for the first time by someone
  who knew him: a teacher who *"strengthened the weak hands"* and *"made firm the feeble knees"*
  (4:3–4). Told that his fear of God should be his confidence, that the innocent never perish, and
  that he is being corrected and should not despise it.
- **Job 6:1–7:21** — [[The Arrows of the Almighty]]. His first reply to anyone: his anguish weighed,
  God named as the archer (6:4), the friends a dry wadi (6:15–20), *"Teach me… wherein I have
  erred"* (6:24), the weaver's shuttle (7:6), and then **his first words to God** — *"Oh remember that
  my life is a breath"* (7:7) — ending in Psalm 8 reversed (7:17–18) and *"why do you not pardon my
  disobedience?"* (7:21).
- **Job 8:1–22** — [[Bildad's First Speech]]. Told that God does not pervert justice, that his
  children were *"delivered … into the hand of their disobedience"* (8:4), that if he seeks God
  early and is *"pure and upright"* his latter end will greatly increase (8:5–7), and that God will
  not cast away a blameless man (8:20) — his own words *tām* and *yāšār*, set as conditions.
- **Job 9:1–10:22** — [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]]. Bildad conceded in a line, and then the case: *"how can man
  be just with God?"* (9:2), the creation hymn turned to threat, *"he multiplies my wounds without
  cause (ḥinnām)"* (9:17) — the prologue's word, which he never heard — *"I am blameless"* (9:21),
  *"there is no umpire between us"* (9:33), and to God, *"Show me why you contend with me"* (10:2),
  *"you know that I am not wicked"* (10:7), the hands that made him (10:8–12), and *"why, then, have
  you brought me out of the womb?"* (10:18).
- **Job 11:1–20** — [[Zophar's First Speech]]. Told that he talks too much and mocks, misquoted as
  claiming *"My doctrine is pure"* (11:4), and told that God *"exacts of you less than your iniquity
  deserves"* (11:6); then promised, on condition of repentance, that he will lift up his face, forget
  his misery, and *"lie down, and no one shall make you afraid"* (11:15–19) — almost all of which the
  epilogue gives him without the repentance.
- **Job 12:1–14:22** — [[Though He Slay Me]]. The longest speech of the first cycle and its close.
  *"Wisdom shall die with you"* (12:2); himself as *"the just, the blameless man"* made a joke (12:4);
  the animals who know *"Yahweh's hand has done this"* (12:9); a hymn in which God overthrows every
  human authority (12:13–25). Then the friends dismissed as *"forgers of lies"* who show partiality to
  God (13:4–10); *"Behold, he will kill me. I have no hope. Nevertheless, I will maintain my ways
  before him"* (13:15); *"I know that I am righteous"* (13:18); and to God, man born of woman, the tree
  that has hope, and *"If a man dies, shall he live again?"* (14:14).
- **Job 4–14** — the first cycle. Eliphaz's night vision, Bildad's appeal to the fathers, Zophar's
  demand that he repent; Job's replies grow from grief toward litigation, reaching *"there is no
  umpire between us"* at 9:33, and *"I know that I am righteous"* at 13:18.
- **Job 15** — [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]]. The consolations of the first cycle are withdrawn.
- **Job 16:1–17:16** — [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]]. God as assailant, the
  cry that must not be covered, the witness in heaven, and hope descending to the gates of Sheol.
- **Job 18:1–21** — [[Bildad's Second Speech]]. Twenty-one verses on the fate of the wicked,
  answering nothing Job has said, and using his dead children a second time.
- **Job 19:1–29** — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]. The siege, the total social abandonment, the
  one plea for pity, the wish for words cut into rock, and the living Redeemer.
- **Job 20:1–29** — [[Zophar's Second Speech]]. The wicked man's brief triumph, and the first
  specific offence anyone in the book names.
- **Job 21:1–34** — [[Why Do the Wicked Live]]. The frontal attack on retribution, answering
  chapters 15, 18 and 20 together and speaking to the friends throughout, never to God. The wicked
  grow old with their children around them and their herds calving; they tell God *"Depart from
  us"* and are left alone; their lamp goes out less often than Bildad says; their children should not
  pay for them (21:19); and they die *"in an instant"*, whole, and are buried with a vigil and a
  crowd. The man who *"dies in bitterness of soul"* (21:25) is himself.
- **Job 22:1–30** — [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]. Accused for the first time of specific crimes —
  pledges taken from a brother *"for nothing"*, the naked stripped, bread and water withheld, widows
  sent away empty, the fatherless broken — none of which the book supports; quoted falsely (*"What
  does God know?"*, 22:13) and exactly (*"The counsel of the wicked is far from me"*, 22:18); and
  promised that if he returns he will pray and be heard, and deliver *"him who is not innocent"*
  through the cleanness of his hands (22:30), which he does for Eliphaz at 42:8.
- **Job 23:1–24:25** — [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]]. Answers neither the charges nor
  the offer. Wishes he could find God's *seat* and argue his case there, sure that God *"would listen
  to me"* (23:6); searches east, west, north and south and finds nobody (23:8–9); says *"he knows the
  way that I take. When he has tried me, I shall come out like gold"* (23:10); and is terrified
  (23:15–16). Then describes the poor, the robbed and the night's criminals, all unpunished, and
  dares the friends to prove him a liar (24:25).
- **Job 25:1–6** — [[Bildad's Third Speech]]. Not addressed at all: six verses with no *you* in them,
  his own 9:2 quoted back in the same Hebrew with its lawsuit sense removed, his dread (23:15) made an
  attribute of God (25:2), and his worm (7:5; 17:14) made a definition of man (25:6). The last word
  any friend speaks.
- **Job 26:1–27:23** — [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]]. Speaks twice in succession where Zophar
  should have spoken. Mocks Bildad's help (26:2–4), outbids his hymn with one that runs from Sheol to
  the heavens and ends *"How small a whisper do we hear of him!"* (26:14), and swears: *"As God lives,
  who has taken away my right … Until I die I will not put away my integrity from me"* (27:2, 5). Then,
  as the text stands, recites the wicked man's ruin (27:13–23) in terms that contradict his own
  chapter 21.
- **Job 28:1–28** — [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]]. Still under the heading of 27:1, but with no *I*,
  no *you*, and no complaint: a hymn on the miner who finds everything in the earth and the wisdom no
  one can find or buy, ending *"the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart from evil is
  understanding"* (28:28) — **the narrator's description of him at 1:1, turned into a definition.**
- **Job 29–31** — Job's closing defence and oath of innocence, in which he calls down curses on
  himself if the charges are true.
- **Job 32–37** — Elihu, who is angry at everyone and whom nobody answers.
- **Job 38–41** — God speaks from the whirlwind, and asks questions instead of giving reasons.
- **Job 42:1–6** — *"I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you."*
- **Job 42:7–17** — God rebukes the three friends and has Job pray for them; Job's fortunes are
  doubled, and he is given ten more children and four generations of descendants.

## Appears in

- [[Job of Uz]] — his introduction: the narrator's fourfold verdict, the household, the wealth, and
  the burnt offerings offered for sins his children may not have committed.
- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — silent while his case is argued in a room he
  does not know exists, then given the eighteen Hebrew words of 1:21. **The first thing he is shown
  doing after total loss is a full mourning rite** — robe torn, head shaved, face to the ground —
  **and then one act nobody required**, worship. He names no human agent for losses that were two
  thirds armed robbery, and the narrator certifies that naming Yahweh instead was not a charge of
  wrongdoing.
- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:1–10. **The passage takes away the body chapter 1 left him.** At 1:20
  he could stand, tear his robe and worship; here he can scrape with a potsherd and sit. He is
  struck with the named disease of Deuteronomy 28's curse list, which makes him legible to everyone
  who will look at him and legible wrongly. His answer to his wife — *"Shall we receive good at the
  hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?"* — is **the position he never abandons**, stated in
  the first person plural, and the last thing he says before chapter 3 turns the volume up for
  twenty-eight chapters.
- [[Seven Days of Silence]] — Job 2:11–13, and **the only portrait of him in the book drawn from
  outside.** He says nothing and is not described; what the reader gets is three men who knew him
  failing to recognise him from a distance, performing the death rites over him, and then sitting on
  the ground for a week unable to think of anything to say. **His condition is measured entirely in
  other people's reactions to it**, and the measurement is the most reliable one in the book, because
  it is the last thing the friends do before they begin explaining.
- [[Let the Day Perish]] — Job 3:1–26, **his first words in the poetry and the furthest thing in the
  book from his last words in the prose.** Nineteen verses after *"shall we receive good at the hand
  of God, and shall we not receive evil?"* he is asking that his birthday be struck out of the
  calendar, in the grammar of Genesis 1. **Three things the chapter establishes about him hold for
  the rest of the book.** He curses **his day and not God**, with the mild verb *qālal*, which is the
  accuser's prediction failing at the moment it looks fulfilled. **He does not ask to die** — he asks
  never to have been born, and asks in the third person why the suicidal are kept alive. And he says
  God has *"hedged"* him in (3:23), **using the accuser's own word from 1:10 with its meaning
  reversed**, without any possibility of knowing he is quoting; see
  [[The Hedge Around Him - Job 1.10 and Job 3.23]]. The narrator's formula *"Job didn't sin with his
  lips"* is not repeated after this chapter, and the wiki reads the silence as the formula's job
  having ended with the accuser rather than as a verdict.
- [[Eliphaz's First Speech]] — Job 4:1–5:27; silent, and **the first person in the book to be
  described by a friend.** Eliphaz's portrait of him in 4:3–4 — a man whose words held up the
  falling — agrees with the narrator's and anticipates Job's own in chapter 29. What follows is the
  first of many readings of him from the outside, and the kindest: **his piety and integrity, the
  two things God boasted of in the council, offered back to him as a *confidence* and a *hope* that
  should be paying out** (4:6). His reply in chapter 6 begins on Eliphaz's word *kaʿas*,
  resentment, and asks only that it be weighed.
- [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] — Job 6:1–7:21, **his second speech and the first one addressed to
  anyone, and by its end the first addressed to God.** Three things begin here and continue to
  chapter 31. **He names God as the cause** — *"the arrows of the Almighty are within me"* (6:4) —
  where chapter 3 had said only that God had hedged him in. **He refuses a confession he cannot make
  specific**: he asks to be taught his error (6:24), swears to the friends' faces that he will not
  lie (6:28, in the accuser's own oath formula from 1:11), and says *"my cause is righteous"* (6:29).
  **And he turns to God**: from 7:7 every *you* is God's, and the complaint is not absence but
  surveillance — *"what is man, that you should… visit him every morning, and test him every
  moment?"* (7:17–18). He is the first character in the book to use the word *test*, and he is
  right. His consolation is not hope, which he refuses (6:11; 7:6), but integrity: *"I have not
  denied the words of the Holy One"* (6:10). See
  [[What Is Man That You Should Visit Him - Job 7.17-18 and Psalm 8]] and
  [[Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives]].
- [[Bildad's First Speech]] — Job 8:1–22, silent and addressed throughout, **and offered as
  conditions the two things the narrator and God have already said he is**: *"if you were pure and
  upright"* (8:6), *"God will not cast away a blameless man"* (8:20). His last words to God are taken
  and turned: God seeking him *diligently* (7:21) becomes him seeking God *diligently* (8:5), and his
  *"I loathe my life"* (7:16) becomes the verb of God's not rejecting the blameless. His reply in
  chapter 9 completes the syllogism with both words: *"I am blameless… I despise my life… he destroys
  the blameless and the wicked"* (9:21–22). And Bildad's *"your latter end would greatly increase"*
  (8:7) is what 42:12 says happened to him, nearly word for word.
- [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] — Job 9:1–10:22, his third speech and **the first in which he does not address the
  friends at all** after the opening line. He becomes a litigant: he calls himself *tām* for the first
  time (9:21), names the umpire he lacks (9:33), and asks God for the charge (10:2). Twice he says
  without knowing it what the reader knows to be true — that he is wounded *ḥinnām*, without cause
  (9:17; 2:3), and that God knows he is not wicked (10:7; 1:8). **And he asks the potter's forbidden
  question** (9:12; 10:8–9) — *what are you doing with what you made?* — which the epilogue never
  rebukes.
- [[Zophar's First Speech]] — addressed throughout, and **quoted back to himself**: his *yiṣdaq*
  (9:2), his *mock* (9:23), his God who passes by and cannot be hindered (9:11–12), and his failing
  wadi (6:15) all reappear in Zophar's mouth. His prayer *"you know that I am not wicked"* (10:7) is
  reported as a boast of pure doctrine, and his *"I still shall not lift up my head"* (10:15) is
  answered with a lifted face on condition he confess.
- [[Though He Slay Me]] — Job 12:1–14:22, his fourth speech and the end of the first cycle. **Three
  firsts.** The first time he turns on the friends' method itself — *"forgers of lies… physicians of
  no value"* (13:4), men who *"show partiality"* (*nāśāʾ pānîm*) to God and will be reproved for it
  (13:8–10), which is what 42:7–8 does, in the same idiom. The first time he says ***ʾeṣdāq***, *I am
  righteous* (13:18), answering his own question of 9:2. And the first time he imagines God
  remembering him beyond death — *"Oh that you would hide me in Sheol… and remember me!"* (14:13) —
  as a wish, taken back five verses later. 13:15 is either *"I have no hope"* (the written text, WEB)
  or *"yet will I trust in him"* (the read text, KJV); on both readings he will argue his ways to
  God's face. He calls himself *ṣaddîq tāmîm* (12:4), Noah's epithet. See
  [[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]].
- [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] — silent, addressed throughout, and portrayed without being named in
  the catalogue of the wicked at 15:20–35.
- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — his own reply, and the speech in which the
  book's highest and lowest lines sit forty verses apart.
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — spoken to in four verses and portrayed, unnamed, in seventeen.
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — his own reply, and the chapter in which he loses every human
  being he has and asserts a kinsman anyway.
- [[Zophar's Second Speech]] — described again without being named, and offered, this time, no way
  back.
- [[The Binding of Isaac]] — not in the chapter, and its closest structural relative: Genesis 22 and
  Job 1–2 are the Bible's two narratives in which the reader is told the test and the man is not, and
  *"you fear God"* is said of both men.
- [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]] — Genesis 46:13 *(by exclusion)*; **the WEB spells [[Issachar]]'s third
  son *Iob*, and he is not this man.** The name there is *yôb* (Numbers 26:24 reads *Jashub*); **the
  Job of the book is *ʾiyyôb*, a different word.** **This is the second false lead the wiki has had to
  disarm around the name**, after the Septuagint's identification of [[Jobab]] with Job at 36:33
- [[Why Do the Wicked Live]] — Job 21:1–34; **the one speech in which he argues the friends'
  subject rather than his own, and it is still about him.** Every blessing he lists for the wicked —
  children in sight, a house without fear, herds that do not miscarry, children dancing to harp and
  pipe — is something he has lost, and the second of the two deaths of 21:23–26, *"in bitterness of
  soul"*, is in his own words from 3:20, 7:11 and 10:1. Asks the friends for the one consolation of
  listening (21:2) and for the silence of a hand on the mouth (21:5) that he will make himself at
  40:4. Refuses to let the wicked man's children pay for him (21:19), with ten dead children of his
  own.
- [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] — Job 22:1–30; silent, and **for the first time charged with named
  crimes.** The charges are the canonical description of the righteous man negated clause by clause,
  and they contradict Eliphaz's own description of him at 4:3–4; he answers them in 24:2–12 by
  pinning them on real oppressors, and in 29:12–17 and 31:16–22 by swearing to the opposite. The one
  word of the charge sheet that rings is *ḥinnām*, *for nothing* (22:6) — the accuser's word about him
  (1:9), God's (2:3), and his own (9:17). The speech's last promise describes what he will do in the
  epilogue: deliver a guilty man by his prayer.
- [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]] — Job 23:1–24:25; **a soliloquy, addressed to nobody
  until the dare of the last verse.** The hearing he wants has no umpire in it now — he thinks he
  would win alone (23:6–7) — and the obstacle is not God's power but God's absence. **23:10 is the one
  place he calls his ordeal a test, and he is right**, without having been told; he uses the insight
  to predict, not to explain. His answer to the charge sheet is to find its crimes committed by
  others in the open (24:2–11), and his answer to Eliphaz's *"What does God know?"* (22:13) is that
  God sees them — *"His eyes are on their ways"* (24:23) — and does not put them down (24:12). See
  [[I Shall Come Out Like Gold - Job 23.10 and the Refiner's Fire]].
- [[Bildad's Third Speech]] — Job 25:1–6; **spoken past him.** His dare (24:25) is met with a hymn and
  a definition of man, and none of his cases is mentioned. **Each of Bildad's lines takes something of
  Job's and reverses it**: *"how can man be just with God?"* (9:2) at 25:4, the *paḥad* of 23:15 at
  25:2, God's *troops* encamped around his tent (19:12) as an uncountable host at 25:3, and the worm
  he used of his sores and his grave (7:5; 17:14; 21:26; 24:20) as what a living man is (25:6). See
  [[A Worm and No Man - Job 25.6, Psalm 22.6, and Isaiah 41.14]].
- [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]] — Job 26:1–27:23; **the oath on which the whole case rests.**
  He swears by the God he accuses (*ḥay-ʾēl … hēsîr mišpāṭî*, 27:2), and answers God's *removing* his
  right with his own refusal to *remove* his integrity — the same verb, *sûr*, at 27:2 and 27:5. The
  prologue's *tummâ* and *ḥāzaq* (2:3, 9) are in his mouth for the first time: **his wife's question
  answered in her own words, and God's verdict confirmed by a man who never heard it.**
  **Contradiction flagged:** 27:13–23, as received, says the storm steals the wicked man away by night
  (*gənābattû sûpâ*, 27:20) — the exact phrase of 21:18, where Job asked *"how often?"* and implied
  *rarely*. The wiki keeps both claims in his mouth and records the four readings (Zophar's lost
  speech; imprecation on his accusers, 27:7; concession of the end but not the inference; citation)
  on the passage page and at [[The Portion of a Wicked Man - Job 20.29 and Job 27.13]].
- [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] — Job 28:1–28; **the hymn that names him without naming him.**
  Wisdom cannot be mined, bought, or found in the deep, the sea, the sky or the underworld; God alone
  knows its place; and to man God says *"the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart from evil is
  understanding"* (28:28) — ***yirʾat ʾădōnāy … wəsûr mērāʿ***, the words of 1:1, *yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm
  wəsār mērāʿ*. **By the hymn's definition, the man the friends call a fool was the wisest man in the
  book before the book began.** The poem is his only by the absence of a new heading; its calm sits
  oddly between the oath of chapter 27 and the renewed complaint of 29–31, and the readings of who
  speaks it (Job; the narrator; an independent poem) are recorded on the passage page. See
  [[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]].

## Related people

- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — the friend who speaks first, longest, and most, and the one God
  addresses by name at 42:7.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the other two, answered in the plural
  throughout Job 16–17.
- [[God]] — Job's real interlocutor from chapter 3 onward. He speaks *about* the friends and *to*
  God, and the friends do the reverse.
- [[Satan]] — *haśśāṭān*, who asks at 1:9 whether Job fears God *for nothing*, and whom Job never
  hears of. **The single most important thing about the relationship is that it is one-directional**:
  the accuser names Job four times and Job never names him once, in forty-two chapters.
- [[Job's Children]] — seven sons and three daughters, unnamed until the epilogue; the reason for
  the first sacrifice in the book and the worst of his losses.
- [[Job's Wife]] — two lines and no name: 2:9, where she tells him to renounce God and die, and
  19:17, where *"My breath is offensive to my wife."* She is the only person in the book who tells
  him to stop, and Job's answer to her (*"Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not
  receive evil?"*) is the book's first statement of the position he never abandons. **He does not
  call her foolish** — the Hebrew convicts her sentence and not her — **and he answers in the
  plural**, which is the only acknowledgement anywhere in the book that she buried the same ten
  children.
- **Elihu** — a fourth speaker, introduced at 32:2 without warning and unmentioned in the epilogue.
- **Noah and Daniel** — named with Job at Ezekiel 14:14, 20 as three men whose righteousness would
  save only themselves; see [[Noah]] and
  [[Blameless Before Anyone Is Watching]]. The pairing is the earliest evidence outside this book
  that Job was a known figure of proverbial righteousness.
- [[Jobab]] — the second king of Edom at Genesis 36:33, whom **the Septuagint's appendix to this book
  says is Job**, supplying the genealogy, nationality, and throne the Hebrew text withholds. **The
  wiki records the identification and does not adopt it**: Job is never called a king, his father is
  never named, Edom is never mentioned, and chapter 1's exhaustive inventory of his losses has no
  kingdom in it. What the overlap does support is the book's Edomite *setting* — see
  [[Jobab of Bozrah and the Septuagint's Job]], [[Uz]], and [[The Descendants of Esau]].

## Themes associated

- [[The Fear of God]] — the term the narrator uses of him at 1:1 and God repeats at 1:8, and the
  one the accuser attacks: not *whether* Job fears God but *why*.
- [[Suffering]] — the Bible's central case, and the one where the cause is disclosed to the reader
  and withheld from the sufferer.
- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine his case is built to break.
- [[Righteousness]] — declared of him by the narrator and by God before he does anything, and then
  disbelieved by everyone in the room.
- [[Integrity]] — *tummâ*, what God says he is still holding at 2:3, what his wife asks him to drop
  at 2:9, and what he swears to keep at 27:5. **It is the one thing the friends' offer would cost
  him**, since a confession would buy restoration at the price of a false account of himself.
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — the four legal officers he reaches for, from 9:33 to 19:25.
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — mourner at 1:20, mourned as though dead at 2:12–13, and the speaker of
  Scripture's most extreme lament at 3:1–26; see [[Let the Day Perish]].
- [[Light and Darkness]] — his native vocabulary. He asks for darkness in five words in three verses
  (3:4–6), uses *light* to mean *being alive* (3:20, 3:23), calls Sheol *"the land of darkness"*
  (10:21), says God *"has set darkness in my paths"* (19:8), and is told at 38:2 that what he has
  darkened is counsel.
- [[Hope]] — 17:15's *"where then is my hope?"*, asked rather than answered, and 19:10's *"My hope
  he has plucked up like a tree"*, followed fifteen verses later by *I know*.
- [[Wisdom]] — he is the counter-example the wisdom tradition had to absorb, and the reason
  Ecclesiastes and Job sit next to Proverbs in the canon.
- [[Judgment]] — his demand is for a trial, and the book's difficulty is that the judge is also the
  defendant.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — he is his own priest at 1:5 and the friends' priest at 42:8; see
  [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

## Notes

- **Was there a historical Job?** Three positions. **Historical figure** — supported by Ezekiel
  14:14, 20, which names Job alongside Noah and Daniel as though all three were known, and by James
  5:11's *"you have heard of the perseverance of Job"*. **Historical core, poetic elaboration** —
  an old Edomite or trans-Jordanian tradition about a righteous sufferer, given a forty-chapter
  poetic dialogue by a later Israelite author; the prose frame and the poetry differ markedly in
  language and in the divine names they use, which is the main evidence. **A parable** — the
  reading of the Babylonian Talmud's minority opinion (*Bava Batra* 15a: *"Job never existed and
  was never created; he is a parable"*), and of many since. The book itself does nothing to settle
  this and loses nothing under any of the three, because its argument is about the case, not about
  the man.
- **The name.** *ʾIyyôb*. Two derivations are usually offered: from *ʾāyab*, *to be hostile*, giving
  something like *the assailed one* or *the enemy*; or from a West Semitic name meaning *where is
  my father?*, which is attested in the Amarna letters and at Mari for the second millennium BC.
  Neither is secure enough to build on, though the first is difficult to read without noticing that
  16:9 calls God *my adversary*.
- **"The patience of Job."** The phrase comes from James 5:11 in the King James Version, and the
  Greek word is *hypomonē*, endurance — staying under a weight, not accepting it quietly. It has
  done real damage as a description of this book, because the Job of chapters 3–31 is loud,
  accusatory, and relentless, and it is precisely that Job whom God commends at 42:7. What James
  praises is that he did not let go, and he did not let go by shouting.
