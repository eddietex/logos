# Iscah

## Overview

One mention, in a subordinate clause, and then nothing — and the fact that Genesis bothered is the
whole of her interest.

*"The name of Nahor's wife was Milcah, the daughter of Haran who was also the father of Iscah"*
(Genesis 11:29). That is her entire appearance in Scripture. She is not in the Chronicler's parallel,
not in any genealogy, not in the New Testament. Genesis is not in the habit of naming people it will
not use; when it does — Naamah at 4:22, Iscah here — the name is left standing without explanation.

**Why the verse names her at all** is the question every ancient reader asked, and the answer they
gave was that she is Sarai — [[Sarah]] — under another name. The identification appears in Josephus
(*Antiquities* 1.6.5), in the Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 69b), and in Rashi's comment on this verse, and
its logic is tidy: Genesis 11:29 supplies [[Milcah]]'s father and pointedly withholds Sarai's, so
naming a second daughter of [[Haran (son of Terah)]] in the same breath is the text's way of
supplying it obliquely. On that reading both brothers married nieces, the verse is symmetrical, and
the otherwise pointless name is doing work.

**Against it stands Genesis 20:12**, where Abraham says of Sarah *"she is indeed my sister, the
daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother"* — which makes her [[Terah]]'s daughter and
Abram's half-sister, not Haran's daughter and his niece. To hold both, *"daughter of my father"* has
to be stretched to *granddaughter*, which Hebrew kinship terms will sometimes bear but which sits
badly in a sentence whose entire purpose is to be exact about degree of relation. The wiki records
the identification as an old and widespread reading and does not adopt it, because 20:12 is the only
text in Scripture that is actually a statement about Sarah's parentage.

**The name.** *Yiskâ* is usually derived from *sākâ*, *to look* or *to gaze* — hence the rabbinic
gloss that she was a seer, or that she was so beautiful people gazed at her, both of which are
homily built on the etymology rather than on the verse. The English name **Jessica** descends from
this one by way of Shakespeare, who used *Jessica* for Shylock's daughter in *The Merchant of
Venice*; it is the only place her name has gone.
