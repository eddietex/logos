# The Sons of God

## Overview

A class of beings the Bible introduces in a subordinate clause, uses twice, and never explains.

*Bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* — literally *sons of the God*, or *sons of the gods*, depending on how the definite
article and the plural are taken — appears at Genesis 6:2 and 6:4 and nowhere else in Genesis. They
*see*, they judge the daughters of men *beautiful*, and they *take* whomever they choose. That is
the entirety of what the passage says they do. They are not named, not numbered, not described, not
addressed, not questioned, and — most strikingly, in a book whose God interrogates a murderer and a
serpent's victims — not sentenced. The verdict that follows in 6:3, 6:5, and 6:7 falls on humanity.

Their identity is the oldest open question in Genesis, and it matters more than a curiosity of
vocabulary, because it determines what kind of disorder the flood is a response to. If they are
divine beings, the primeval history's final transgression is the boundary between heaven and earth
failing, and the flood answers a cosmological breach. If they are the godly line of Seth, it is
apostasy by marriage, and the flood answers the corruption of the last uncorrupted human family. If
they are dynastic rulers, it is tyranny, and the flood answers the political violence that
Genesis 6:11 will name outright.

**The three readings, and where each is strongest.** The full argument is set out in
[[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]; in brief:

| Reading | Strongest ground | Hardest problem |
|---|---|---|
| Divine beings / angels | Every other occurrence of the phrase in the Hebrew Bible means heavenly beings | Nothing in the passage condemns them, and the punishment falls on humanity |
| The line of Seth | Two chapters have just contrasted the two genealogies; explains why humanity is judged | The phrase is never used of humans; does not account for the Nephilim |
| Kings / divine rulers | Fits *"mighty men … men of renown"* exactly; matches known royal titulary | Genesis 6 says nothing about kingship |

**Where the phrase occurs elsewhere.** This is the argument that carries most weight, so it is worth
having the list in one place. *Bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* stands at Job 1:6 and 2:1, where they *"came to
present themselves before Yahweh"* and the Adversary comes among them, and at Job 38:7, where they
*"shouted for joy"* at the founding of the earth. The variant *bənê ʾēlîm* stands at Psalm 29:1 and
89:6, both times of heavenly beings summoned to ascribe glory. Daniel 3:25 has the Aramaic
equivalent of *a son of the gods* for the fourth figure in the furnace. Deuteronomy 32:8 divides the
nations *"according to the number of the sons of God"* in the Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagint
readings — the Masoretic text has *sons of Israel* — which is the clearest surviving trace in the
Hebrew Bible of a divine council under Yahweh. In none of these is a human being in view.

Against that: Genesis is not obliged to use a phrase the way Job and the Psalms use it, Hosea 1:10
does call Israelites *"sons of the living God"*, and the argument from usage cannot by itself
establish what a narrator meant in a paragraph this compressed.

**What the New Testament does with them.** Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 speak of angels who *"didn't keep
their first domain, but deserted their own dwelling place"* and are kept in chains for judgment, and
Jude places the notice between the exodus generation and Sodom — that is, in roughly the position
Genesis 6 occupies. Neither text quotes Genesis 6, and neither names the sons of God. Whether they
are reading this passage, or the *1 Enoch* elaboration of it, or something else, is discussed at
[[The Sons of God and the Angels That Sinned]].
