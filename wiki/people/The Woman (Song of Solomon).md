# The Woman (Song of Solomon)

## Overview

The opening desiring voice in [[Song of Solomon]], tracked by her literary role rather
than by an asserted personal name. In [[Love Better Than Wine]] she seeks kisses, praises
the beloved's love and fragrance, and asks to be taken away (1:2–4). The supplied WEB's
Beloved cue at the end of 1:4 is retained as an edition's speaker assignment.

Her initiative is central: desire is spoken by her, not only described by another.
The opening does not identify her as a named wife of Solomon, state her age, or narrate
a wedding. The verse's king does not settle the historical identity of either lover.
Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 1:1–4.

In [[Dark and Lovely]] she speaks about herself. She tells [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]]
that she is *"dark, but lovely"*, like Kedar's tents and Solomon's curtains (1:5). She asks not
to be stared at and gives the reason: the sun has scorched her. Her mother's sons were angry
with her and made her keeper of the vineyards, so she has not kept her own (1:6). This is the
first family detail the Song gives about her. It shows her under her brothers' authority and
working outdoors, which fits poorly with any simple picture of her as a royal-court figure.
She then asks *"you whom my soul loves"* where he rests his flock at noon, not wanting to be
*"as one who is veiled"* among his companions' flocks (1:7). She is called *"most beautiful among
women"* and told to graze her young goats by the shepherds' tents (1:8). So she keeps goats
as well as vineyards. Whether her *"own vineyard"* is also a figure for herself is possible
but unconfirmed in this unit. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 1:5–8.

In [[Delight and the Unawakened Love]] she is praised for the first time by a male voice. She
is compared to a steed in Pharaoh's chariots and told *"you are beautiful"*, her eyes doves
(1:9, 15). She answers in kind. Her perfume spreads at the king's table, and her beloved is a
sachet of myrrh and henna from En Gedi (1:12–14). She returns his *"you are beautiful"* word for
word (1:16). She calls herself a rose of Sharon and a lily of the valleys, and he calls her a
lily among thorns (2:1–2). Then she speaks at length: he is an apple tree among the trees, she
was brought to the banquet hall, she is faint with love, and she describes his embrace (2:3–6).
She closes the unit by putting the daughters of Jerusalem under oath not to awaken love
*"until it so desires"* (2:7). The adjuration is hers, so the first restraint on love in the
Song is spoken by the woman who has just described its fulfillment. The speaker assignment
follows the WEB's forms of address; the WEB gives no cues here. Source: stored WEB Song of
Solomon 1:9–2:7.

In [[Rise Up, My Love]] she narrates. She hears the beloved's voice, sees him coming over the hills,
and finds him at *"our wall"*, looking in at the windows (2:8–9). She is inside, and the unit does
not say whose house it is. She quotes his invitation twice, *"Rise up, my love, my beautiful one, and
come away"* (2:10, 13), and is addressed as *"my dove in the clefts of the rock"*, hidden, with a
face he wants to see and a voice he wants to hear (2:14). She does not reply to the invitation with
an act. She replies with *"My beloved is mine, and I am his"* (2:16), the first form of the Song's
belonging formula, and with a summons of her own, *"turn, my beloved"* (2:17). Whether *turn* calls
him back or sends him off is left open. In this unit, as in 2:7, the woman's last words set the
terms. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 2:8–17; feminine and masculine forms read from Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*.

In [[Seeking Him by Night]] she is the only voice, and every verb is hers. At night on her bed she
seeks *"him whom my soul loves"* and does not find him (3:1). She gets up and goes about the city,
through its streets and squares, and does not find him (3:2). The watchmen find her, and she asks
them whether they have seen him (3:3). Then she finds him, holds him, and *"would not let him go"*
until she has brought him into her mother's house, *"into the room of her who conceived me"* (3:4).
This is the first mention of her mother as a person rather than as the mother of her brothers (1:6).
She closes, as at 2:7, by adjuring the daughters of Jerusalem in the same words (3:5). In 2:8–17 he
came looking for her; here she goes looking for him, at night and in public. Whether the search is a
dream is not said. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 3:1–5; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra
according to the Masorah*.

In [[Solomon's Wedding Procession]] she does not speak and is not named or described. The unit opens
with *"Who is this who comes up from the wilderness"* (3:6), and *this* is feminine in the Hebrew,
*mî zōʾṯ* (Sefaria). It may refer to the carriage, whose word is also feminine, or to a woman carried
in it. The same question at 8:5 is asked of a woman *"leaning on her beloved"*. If the procession is
a wedding of the lovers, she is the unseen bride; the unit itself names no bride. See
[[Who Is This Coming Up from the Wilderness - Song of Solomon 3.6, 6.10, and 8.5]]. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 3:6–11; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

In [[You Are All Beautiful]] she is described and does not speak. The man praises her eyes, hair,
teeth, lips, mouth, temples, neck, and breasts (4:1–5), twice noting that part of her face is
*"behind your veil"* (4:1, 3), and ends, *"You are all beautiful, my love. There is no spot in you"*
(4:7). The word for *spot*, *mûm*, is the *defect* that disqualifies a priest or an offering in
Leviticus and is said of Absalom's beauty at 2 Samuel 14:25; see
[[No Blemish in You - Song of Solomon 4.7, Leviticus 22.21, and 2 Samuel 14.25]]. Her own line of 2:17
is taken up by him in 4:6. Nothing in the unit names her a bride; that word first comes at 4:8.
Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 4:1–7; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

In [[A Garden Locked and Opened]] she is called *"my bride"* for the first time, and *"my sister, my bride"* (4:8–12).
Every use of *bride* in the Song falls in this unit. She is called down from the northern heights
(4:8), praised for her love, her perfume, her lips, and her garments (4:10–11), and pictured as *"A
locked up garden … a locked up spring, a sealed fountain"* (4:12), full of fruit and spices and
watered by *"a well of living waters"* (4:15). She speaks once, at 4:16, and opens the garden: she
calls on the north and south winds to blow on it and says, *"Let my beloved come into his garden, and
taste his precious fruits."* The garden that is *my garden* in one line is *his garden* in the next.
Whether *my garden* is hers or his is not marked. *Sister* is affection, not kinship; at 8:1 she
wishes he were like her brother. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 4:8–5:1; Hebrew read from
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*.

In [[I Opened, but He Had Gone]] she is asleep, *"but my heart was awake"*, when he knocks and asks
her to open (5:2). She hesitates. *"I have taken off my robe. Indeed, must I put it on? I have washed
my feet. Indeed, must I soil them?"* (5:3). When she does get up and open, her hands dripping with
myrrh, he has gone (5:5–6). She seeks him and does not find him, calls him and gets no answer. The
watchmen find her again, as at 3:3, but this time they beat and wound her, and the keepers of the
walls take her cloak (5:7). She ends by asking the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him, if they find
him, that she is *"faint with love"* (5:8), the words she used at 2:5 in his embrace. This is the
first time the Song shows her own delay costing her the beloved, and the first time she is harmed.
The poem does not blame her for the delay or explain the violence. Whether the scene is a dream is
not said. She is called *"my sister"* but not *"my bride"*, a word the Song does not use again.
Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 5:2–8; Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the
Masorah*.

In [[Altogether Lovely]] she answers the question about her beloved's distinction by describing
his body part by part (5:10–16). The gold, doves, spices, gems, marble, and cedars express her
delight; she closes by calling him her beloved and her friend. When the audience offers to
seek him with her, she says he has gone to his garden and declares that she belongs to him
and he to her (6:1–3). **The shift from 5:6 is left explicit:** she could not find him then,
but speaks confidently of his whereabouts now. The poem supplies no intervening discovery
or reunion. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 5:9–6:3.

In [[My Dove Is Unique]] she is compared to Tirzah, Jerusalem, and an army with banners;
her hair, teeth, and veiled temples are described again (6:4–7). The lover calls her his
unique dove; her mother regards her as her only daughter and favorite, and other women
praise her (6:8–9). Only daughter does not mean only child: the brothers of 1:6 remain
compatible with this description. The morning, moon, and sun comparisons (6:10) do not
narrate a change in the complexion she described at 1:5–6. She has no clearly assigned
speech here. Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 1:5–6; 6:4–10.

In [[Return, Return, Shulammite]], she is addressed as Shulammite and prince's daughter, and praised from feet to head
(6:13–7:5). Her body becomes a palm tree and its fruit (7:7–8). The turn to my beloved
in 7:9 may be her answer within the wine image, but the WEB marks no speaker change.
Neither the title nor the royal address establishes a historical identity or parentage.
Source: stored WEB Song of Solomon 6:11–7:9.

In [[Come into the Fields, My Beloved]] (Song of Solomon 7:10–8:4),
She declares that she belongs to him and that his desire is toward her, then invites
him into the fields and vineyards (7:10–12). Fruit stored for him expresses her initiative.
She wishes for the freedom to kiss him outside without contempt, lead him into her
mother's house, and share wine and an embrace (8:1–3). Her charge to the daughters
at 8:4 preserves restraint beside desire; the poem does not narrate the journey.
Source: stored WEB Song 7:10–8:4; comparisons at 2:7; 3:5; 5:8.

### Love strong as death — Song of Solomon 8:5–7

In [[Love Is Strong as Death]], she comes up leaning on her beloved (8:5). The ensuing intimate address and seal
request are plausibly hers, though the stored WEB supplies no speaker labels. The
poem moves from a particular pair to love that fire, flood, and money cannot master.

Source: stored WEB Song 8:5–7; comparisons use the stored Song and Proverbs 6:30–35.

In [[Our Sister and My Own Vineyard]], She answers the little-sister description with a wall and mature towers, then speaks of
her own vineyard beside Solomon’s (8:10–12). Reading these as her words gives her
self-assessment weight beside the family plans. The speaker assignment is probable,
not explicit; no continuous biography or rejection of a named suitor is established.
Source: stored WEB Song 8:8–12.

## Timeline / key events

- Song of Solomon 1:2–4 — desires kisses, compares love with wine and a name with
  fragrance, and seeks nearness. These are poetic utterances, not a dated biography.
- Song of Solomon 1:5–8 — calls herself dark and lovely before the daughters of Jerusalem,
  tells of her brothers' anger and her vineyard work, and seeks the shepherd-beloved at noon.
- Song of Solomon 1:9–2:7 — praised and praising in turn; faint with love in his embrace; lays
  the first adjuration on the daughters of Jerusalem.
- Song of Solomon 2:8–17 — hears him coming, reports his spring invitation, is called his dove,
  says *"My beloved is mine, and I am his"*, and sends him to the mountains of Bether.
- Song of Solomon 3:1–5 — seeks him at night through the city, asks the watchmen, finds him and
  brings him to her mother's house, and adjures the daughters a second time.
- Song of Solomon 3:6–11 — perhaps the *"this"* coming up from the wilderness, perhaps the unseen
  bride of Solomon's wedding procession; the unit does not say.
- Song of Solomon 4:1–7 — described part by part by the man, and called all beautiful, with no
  blemish; she does not speak.
- Song of Solomon 4:8–5:1 — called his bride and a locked garden; invites him into the garden (4:16).
- Song of Solomon 5:2–8 — delays when he knocks, opens too late, seeks and calls him in vain, is beaten
  by the watchmen, and asks the daughters to tell him she is faint with love.

- Song of Solomon 5:9–6:3 — describes him to the daughters as beloved and friend, names his garden as his
  destination, and renews mutual belonging.
- Song of Solomon 6:4–10 — praised as unique; other women call her blessed.

- Song of Solomon 6:11–7:9 — addressed as Shulammite and praised; possible answer in 7:9.

- Song of Solomon 7:10–8:4 — invites him and imagines public affection and homecoming.

- Song of Solomon 8:8–12 — family and vineyard exchange. [[Our Sister and My Own Vineyard]]
