# Lamech (son of Methushael)

The seventh generation from Adam in the line of Cain. Not to be confused with
Lamech the father of Noah, the seventh generation from Adam in the line of Seth — the two lines
reuse names to a degree that is almost certainly deliberate; see
[[Two Lines from Adam - Cain's Genealogy and Seth's]].

## Overview

Genesis spends five verses on him, and every one of them is a first. He is the first man in
Scripture to take two wives. He is the first man to have his children's occupations recorded. He
speaks the first poem in the Bible by anyone other than Adam. And what he uses it for is to
announce that he has killed a man and that anyone who touches him will suffer eleven times what was
promised to the murderer he is descended from.

The genealogy of [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] moves fast — *"To Enoch was born Irad.
Irad became the father of Mehujael…"* — precisely so that it can slow down here. Four names pass in
a single verse; Lamech gets 4:19–24.

**The two wives.** *"Lamech took two wives: the name of the first one was Adah, and the name of the
second one was Zillah"* (4:19). This is sixteen verses after *"they will become one flesh"* (2:24)
and the first departure from it in the Bible. Genesis reports it flatly, with no sentence and no
narratorial aside, and then does the same thing for every polygamous household it goes on to
narrate — Abraham's, Jacob's — each of which is a source of grief. The evaluation is structural
rather than stated. See [[Marriage]].

**The three sons.** Adah bears Jabal and Jubal; Zillah bears Tubal Cain, and a daughter, Naamah, is
named without further comment. The three sons are not given births so much as domains: herding,
music, and metalwork — see [[Jabal]], [[Jubal]], [[Tubal Cain]]. The founders of human culture are
this man's children, which is the passage's least comfortable fact and the one it declines to
soften.

**The song.** *"Adah and Zillah, hear my voice. You wives of Lamech, listen to my speech, for I
have slain a man for wounding me, a young man for bruising me. If Cain will be avenged seven times,
truly Lamech seventy-seven times"* (4:23–24). It is proper Hebrew verse: paired lines, the second
of each restating the first, his own name set in the middle. The occasion for that craft is a
killing, and a disproportionate one — a life taken for a wound. Where Cain killed and then feared
for his own life, and was answered with a divine guarantee of sevenfold vengeance on anyone who
struck him (4:15), Lamech takes that guarantee, transfers it from God's mouth to his own, and
multiplies it. The chapter's trajectory is exact: the murderer hid, and his descendant sings.

The Hebrew of *"I have slain"* is ambiguous between a completed act and a threatened one (*"I would
kill"*), so the poem is either a confession delivered as a trophy or a warning issued in advance.
Nothing else about it changes either way. It is traditionally called the Song of the Sword, and it
is the last thing said in the line of Cain — the narrative drops that line here and never picks it
up again.

The number outlives him. When Peter asks whether forgiving seven times is enough, Jesus answers
with Lamech's figure — the Septuagint's Greek for 4:24 and for Matthew 18:22 is the same phrase —
and turns the Bible's high-water mark for revenge into its measure for pardon. See
[[Seventy-Sevenfold - Lamech's Boast and Jesus on Forgiveness]].

## Related people

- [[Cain]] — his ancestor, whose sevenfold protection he appropriates and inflates
- [[Enoch (son of Cain)]] — his great-great-grandfather, the first name after Cain in the line
- [[Jabal]], [[Jubal]], [[Tubal Cain]] — his sons
- [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] — his namesake in Seth's line, the only other person in these chapters
  to speak, and the counterweight to everything he says: a sentence of hope over a newborn against a
  song about a killing — and, by the numbers of Genesis 5:31, a lifespan of 777 years against this
  Lamech's claim to be avenged seventy-seven times
- Adah and Zillah — his wives, the first women named in Scripture after Eve, and the addressees of
  its first poem; Genesis gives them no words of their own
- Lamech the father of Noah — his namesake in the Sethite line, who speaks the other recorded
  poem of Genesis 5 and whose subject is rest rather than revenge
