# Enoch (son of Jared)

## Overview

The seventh generation from Adam, and the one exception in a chapter about death.

Genesis 5 gives every man the same three-part entry and ends each one with *"then he died."* Enoch's
entry begins normally — *"Enoch lived sixty-five years, then became the father of Methuselah"* — and
then does something the register has no slot for: *"After Methuselah's birth, Enoch walked with God
for three hundred years."* No one has walked with God before. The phrase (*hithpael* of *hālak* with
*ʾet*, roughly *walked back and forth with*, *walked habitually with*) is not defined and is not
explained; it is simply asserted, twice, about one man, and it is the only thing the chapter says
about anyone's character.

His total is the shortest in the chapter by more than five hundred years — **365**, the number of
days in a solar year, which no one has ever managed to think was accidental — and the closing
formula is replaced: *"Enoch walked with God, and he was not found, for God took him"* (5:24). The
verb is *lāqaḥ*, the same word used when Elijah is taken up in 2 Kings 2 and when the psalmist hopes
God will take him from Sheol (Psalm 49:15, 73:24). Against eight deaths, one man is subtracted from
the list.

He is not the Enoch of Genesis 4:17. That one is [[Enoch (son of Cain)]], the first-born of the
first murderer, silent, with the Bible's first city named after him. The two are set in opposite
positions in mirror-image genealogies, and what the text does with each name is the exact reverse of
what it does with the other: one Enoch has a place named for him, the other leaves the place
entirely. See [[Two Lines from Adam - Cain's Genealogy and Seth's]].

Almost everything else attached to his name is later. He became the central figure of an enormous
apocalyptic literature — *1 Enoch*, *2 Enoch*, and the *Book of Jubilees* — that made him a scribe,
a seer of the heavenly courts, a calendrical authority, and the accuser of the fallen watchers. Jude
14–15 quotes one of those books directly, calling him *"the seventh from Adam"*, which shows both
that the seventh-generation count was being made in antiquity and that Jude's readers knew the
tradition. Hebrews 11:5 goes back to Genesis and reads the taking as a verdict on his faith:
*"By faith Enoch was taken away, so that he wouldn't see death."* All of it is set out on
[[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]].
