# The Chief Cupbearer

*Titled by his office, as Genesis leaves him. He is one of the two figures in Genesis 40 who have
speech, a dream reported at length, and a verdict, and he is the one who recurs — which is why he has a
page where the keeper of the prison does not. No period qualifier: see the note on naming at the bottom
of this page.*

## Overview

**The man whose forgetting is the hinge of the Joseph narrative.**

> **1** After these things, the butler of the king of Egypt and his baker offended their lord, the king
> of Egypt.
> **2** Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief cup bearer and the chief baker. (40:1–2)

***Śar hammašqîm***, *chief of those who give drink*. **The WEB says *butler* at 40:1 and *chief cup
bearer* from 40:2 on; the Hebrew uses the full title throughout.** The office is real and it is not
domestic staff: **the man who hands the king his cup is the last line of defence against poison**, which
makes the post one of unusual trust and unusual physical access. Egyptian New Kingdom texts have *wbꜣ
nsw*, *royal butler*, held by men who carry out royal commissions well outside the dining hall, **and
foreigners hold it.** Its Assyrian counterpart, *rab šāqê* — literally *chief cupbearer* — is the title
of the officer who negotiates outside the walls of Jerusalem at 2 Kings 18, and **Nehemiah, cupbearer to
Artaxerxes, is the Bible's clearest picture of what the access was worth**: he raises the rebuilding of
a province with the king because he was standing there with the wine (Nehemiah 1:11–2:8).

**Genesis never says what he did wrong.** *"Offended their lord"* is ***ḥāṭəʾû***, the ordinary verb
*sinned*; the text supplies no offence, no trial, and no sentence — only that Pharaoh *"was angry"* and
put two officers in the prison inside [[Potiphar]]'s house.

### His dream is his job

> **9** The chief cup bearer told his dream to Joseph, and said to him, “In my dream, behold, a vine
> was in front of me,
> **10** and in the vine were three branches. It was as though it budded, it blossomed, and its clusters
> produced ripe grapes.
> **11** Pharaoh’s cup was in my hand; and I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh’s cup, and I
> gave the cup into Pharaoh’s hand.” (40:9–11)

**Nothing strange happens in it.** A vine grows through three stages in one clause — budding, blossom,
ripe fruit — and he does the thing he is for. **The compression is the only dream-like element**, and it
is what lets [[Joseph]] read the interval as three days rather than three years.

**Pressing fresh grapes straight into a cup is not how wine was made**, in Egypt or anywhere else; it is
the office performed in a single gesture. **The last image is the cup in the king's hand**, and the
chapter repeats that clause three times in eleven verses — in the dream (40:11), in the interpretation
(40:13), and in the fulfilment (40:21).

### He is restored exactly as told, and forgets

> **21** He restored the chief cup bearer to his position again, and he gave the cup into Pharaoh’s
> hand; … **23** Yet the chief cup bearer didn’t remember Joseph, but forgot him. (40:21, 23)

**Joseph asked him for three things** — *remember me*, *show kindness* (***ḥesed***), *make mention of
me to Pharaoh* — **and he is not recorded as answering.** Genesis gives him no reply to the request at
all: **he speaks once in the chapter, to tell his dream, and never again.**

**And the last verse says his failure twice**, positively and negatively: ***lōʾ-zākar … wayyiškāḥēhû***,
*did not remember … and forgot him*. **In Genesis the verb *zākar* has had God as its subject four times
and an action in the next clause every time** — the flood turns, Lot is pulled out, Rachel conceives.
**Here it is given to a man, in the negative, as a chapter's last word.** See [[Remembrance]].

### Two years later he says the name

> **9** Then the chief cup bearer spoke to Pharaoh, saying, “I remember my faults today. (41:9)

***Ḥăṭāʾay*** — **the same root as the offence that put him in the prison at 40:1**, now used of what he
did to the man who was in it with him. **He is the only person in Genesis who gets Joseph in front of
Pharaoh**, and his testimony is exact: *"a young man there with us, a Hebrew, servant to the captain of
the guard … as he interpreted to us, so it was"* (41:12–13).

**Genesis passes no verdict on him.** It does not call the forgetting ingratitude, it does not say God
used it, and it does not say the two years were for anything. **The only line of interpretation anywhere
in Scripture is Psalm 105:19** — *"until the time that his word happened, and Yahweh's word proved him
true"* — **and it is about Joseph, not about him.**

## Timeline / key events

- Offends the king of Egypt, with the chief baker, and is imprisoned in the captain of the guard's house
  — Genesis 40:1–3, [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]]
- Assigned, with the baker, to Joseph, who waits on them — Genesis 40:4
- Dreams the vine, the three branches, and the cup — Genesis 40:9–11
- Told the three branches are three days and that Pharaoh will *lift up his head* and restore him —
  Genesis 40:12–13
- Asked to remember Joseph, to show him *ḥesed*, and to mention him to Pharaoh — Genesis 40:14–15
- Restored on the third day, at Pharaoh's birthday feast; *"he gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand"* —
  Genesis 40:20–21
- *"Didn't remember Joseph, but forgot him"* — Genesis 40:23
- Two years later, tells Pharaoh *"I remember my faults today"* and names the Hebrew in the prison —
  Genesis 41:9–13 *(not yet ingested)*

## Appears in

- [[The Cupbearer and the Baker]] — Genesis 40:1–23; **the chapter's survivor, and the reason its last
  verse stings.** He offends a king for a reason Genesis withholds, dreams his own office, is told he
  will be restored in three days, is restored in three days, **and does not do the one thing he was
  asked.** He speaks once. **The chapter that proves Joseph's interpretations true is also the chapter
  that shows the proof buying him nothing**

## On the naming of this page

`AGENTS.md` qualifies a figure known only by a title with the patriarch or period he appears with —
[[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]], [[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]] — **because Genesis narrates several
holders of each of those offices and a bare filename would silently collide.** That does not apply here:
**Scripture has exactly one anonymous chief cupbearer**, and the one other biblical officer holding the
title, the Assyrian *rab šāqê* of 2 Kings 18:17, **reaches the English Bible as a proper name,
Rabshakeh**. **If a collision ever arises, the rule applies and both pages take qualifiers in the pass
that creates the second.**
