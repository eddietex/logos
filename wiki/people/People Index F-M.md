| [[God]] | Yahweh leads, humbles, tests, feeds, and disciplines Israel as a father (2–5). He gives the good land and the power to acquire wealth (7–18), and warns that forgetting him brings destruction (19–20). Deuteronomy 9:1–6: Yahweh goes before Israel as a devouring fire, bringing down stronger nations (3). Their wickedness and his oath to the fathers explain the gift; Israel’s righteousness does not (4–6). Deuteronomy 9:7–29: Yahweh threatens destruction and a new nation from Moses (13–14), yet listens to his intercession (19). The prayer appeals to his redemption, servants, and ownership of Israel (26–29).  Deuteronomy 10:1–11: Yahweh writes the former commandments again, sets Levi apart, hears Moses, and refuses to destroy Israel (2–4, 8–11). Restored tablets and the command to depart give concrete expression to continued covenant commitment.  Deuteronomy 10:12–22: Yahweh owns heaven and earth yet loves and chooses the fathers’ descendants (14–15). Deuteronomy 11:1–17: his seen deeds ground obedience; his eyes are on the rain-fed land, and he gives or withholds its rain. Deuteronomy 12:1–28: Yahweh chooses one place for his name and is not to be worshiped as the nations worshiped (2–5). Deuteronomy 26:16–19: Commands obedience, declares Israel his possession, and promises honor and holiness.  Deuteronomy 27: Yahweh gives the land promised to the ancestors (2–3), receives burnt and peace offerings and rejoicing (5–7), and is the God whose people Israel is declared to have become (9).  Deuteronomy 27:11–26: Yahweh is named as the one to whom the crafted image is an abomination (15). The commanded curse liturgy places even secret wrongdoing under covenant accountability. Deuteronomy 28: conditional blessing in the land.  Deuteronomy 28:15–68: Yahweh is explicitly the agent of disease, defeat, exile, and terrifying insecurity (20–28, 63–68). Verse 63 places rejoicing over destruction beside rejoicing to do good; this disturbing claim must remain beside the promises of covenant preservation in Leviticus 26:44–45. Deuteronomy 29: Yahweh establishes Israel as his people by oath (12–13), yet has not given a knowing heart to this day (4). He judges the self-assured apostate (20–21); hidden things belong to him while revealed law belongs to the community (29).  Deuteronomy 31: Yahweh will go before Israel, defeat the nations, and remain with Joshua (3–8). Tension retained: the assurance against forsaking in 31:6, 8 stands beside threatened forsaking after Israel’s apostasy in 31:16–18. Encouragement for entry must not erase the chapter’s covenant warning. Deuteronomy 31:14–23: Yahweh appears in the cloud, foretells covenant betrayal, and appoints the song as testimony (15–21). His threatened forsaking after apostasy (17–18) stands in explicit tension with his assurance against forsaking in 31:6, 8; his presence with Joshua is reaffirmed in 23. Neither side cancels the other.  Deuteronomy 32: Yahweh is the faithful Rock and parent whose care Israel repays with abandonment (4–18). He hides his face and judges, yet restrains annihilation lest enemies claim the victory (20–27), has compassion on powerless servants (36), and alone kills, makes alive, wounds, and heals (39). Judgment and compassion remain together; neither cancels the other.  Deuteronomy 32:48–52: Yahweh commands Moses to ascend, view the land, and die (48–52). His gift of Canaan to Israel remains explicit beside his judgment on the leader who failed to uphold his holiness; the gift does not exempt Moses from accountability.  Deuteronomy 33: Yahweh comes in glory, loves his people, gives instruction, and sustains Israel as dwelling place, help, shield, and sword (2–5, 26–29). The blessing stands beside, rather than cancels, the judgment forecast in chapter 32.  Joshua 2: Rahab confesses Yahweh as God in heaven and on earth after hearing of the exodus and the defeat of Sihon and Og (9–11). His gift of the land grounds both her plea for protection and the spies’ confident report (14, 24); no new divine speech is narrated here.  Joshua 3: Yahweh promises to establish Joshua before Israel (7). The crossing will show that the living God is among them and will drive out the seven named peoples (10); his rule is described as extending over all the earth (11, 13). The sign demonstrates presence before conquest is complete. Joshua 4: Yahweh commands the memorial, magnifies Joshua, and restores the river after the priests leave (1–3, 14–18). All peoples are to know his mighty hand, and Israel is to fear him continually (24).  Joshua 5:13–15: The commander identifies his army as Yahweh's, and the place of encounter is holy (14–15).  Joshua 6: Yahweh declares Jericho given to Joshua before its walls fall (2–5). The concluding notice attributes Joshua’s success to Yahweh’s presence (27); the conquest is not credited to Israel’s independent strength. Joshua 10:28–43: Yahweh delivers Libnah and Lachish (30, 32), and the narrator attributes the campaign’s success to his fighting for Israel (42). Verse 40 presents the destruction as obedience to his command, recalling Deuteronomy 20:16–18; it is not a new direct speech. Joshua 5:10–12: No divine speech or name occurs in these three verses. Read with Exodus 16 and Joshua 5:6, the cessation of manna when land produce becomes available marks a change in provision, not an assertion that God has abandoned Israel. Joshua 13: Yahweh names the land still unpossessed, promises to drive out its inhabitants, and commands Joshua to allocate it (1–7). The promise in verse 6 accompanies an unfinished task; it is not a report that all inhabitants have already been removed.  Joshua 13:8–33: Yahweh is Levi’s inheritance (33); his offerings are the tribe’s portion (14). The absence of a territorial allotment is paired with provision, not abandonment.  Joshua 14:1–5: Yahweh’s command through Moses governs the lot and the people’s division of the land (2, 5). No new divine speech is narrated; the report presents obedience to an earlier command. Joshua 14:6–15: Caleb attributes his survival across forty-five years to Yahweh’s promise (10). He asks for the difficult hill country in dependence on Yahweh’s presence (12); the narrator confirms his wholehearted following (14). Preservation and promised inheritance accompany further action.  Joshua 15:13–19: Yahweh’s command to Joshua grounds Caleb’s portion among Judah (13). No new divine speech is quoted in this scene; the springs are granted by Caleb in response to Achsah.  Joshua 17:14–18: Joseph’s descendants attribute their numerous population to Yahweh’s blessing (14). No divine speech is recorded in this exchange; the assurance of expulsion is spoken by Joshua.  Joshua 18:1–10: Joshua identifies Yahweh as the giver of the land and casts the lots before him (3, 6, 8, 10). No new divine speech or visible manifestation is narrated.  Joshua 19:49–51: Joshua’s city is granted according to Yahweh’s command (50), and the division by lot takes place before Yahweh (51). No new divine speech is quoted or visible manifestation reported.  Joshua 20: Yahweh commands Joshua to implement the refuge provision previously spoken through Moses (1–2).  Joshua 21: Israel gives the cities according to Yahweh’s command through Moses (2–3, 8).  Joshua 21:43–45: The narrator attributes land, rest, and victory to Yahweh and declares every good promise fulfilled (43–45). | [[Moses]] | Moses exhorts Israel to remember its forty years and addresses the imagined claim of self-made wealth (2, 17). His witness against apostasy closes the unit (19–20); no new journey or action by Moses is narrated. Deuteronomy 9:1–6: Moses answers the fear of superior enemies with Yahweh’s presence, then forbids the victorious people to credit their righteousness (1–6). This continues his address, not a narrated crossing. Deuteronomy 9:7–29: Moses recalls receiving and breaking the tablets, destroying the calf, and fasting in intercession (9–21). He prays for Aaron as well as the people, appealing to redemption and the patriarchs rather than Israel’s merit (20, 26–29).  Deuteronomy 10:1–11: Moses makes the wooden ark, cuts replacement tablets, ascends and descends, and deposits the writing (1–5). His forty-day stay and heard intercession lead to a renewed command to lead the people (10–11). Exodus 37:1 credits the ark’s making to Bezalel; Deuteronomy credits Moses. Delegated workmanship is a possible reading, not an explanation this passage supplies.  Deuteronomy 10:12–22: Moses turns from recalled restoration to the question of what Yahweh requires (12–13). Deuteronomy 11:1–17: addresses the eyewitnesses, contrasts Egypt's foot-watered fields with the land of rain, and warns against a deceived heart. Deuteronomy 12:1–28: Moses opens the statutes with the law of the place and counts himself in the practice that must end (8). Deuteronomy 26:16–19: Closes the law code with reciprocal declarations and wholehearted obedience.  Deuteronomy 27: Moses shares the command with Israel's elders (1), then the call to listen with the Levitical priests (9–10).  Deuteronomy 27:11–26: Moses commands the tribal arrangement and responsive curses on the same day (11). He prescribes future words and positions rather than reporting a completed ceremony. Deuteronomy 28: conditional blessing in the land.  Deuteronomy 28:15–68: Moses continues the address with conditional sanctions, not a report of their fulfillment (15, 45). The future king and invader remain unnamed. Deuteronomy 29: Moses summons all Israel to the covenant in Moab (1–2). His speech joins remembered rescue, an inclusive assembly, warnings against secret apostasy, and the duty to do the revealed law.  Deuteronomy 31: At 120, Moses announces that he can no longer go out and come in and recalls Yahweh’s prohibition on crossing the Jordan (2). He publicly encourages Joshua (7–8). His stated inability stands beside 34:7’s report of undiminished sight and strength: the leadership idiom and divine prohibition caution against treating this as a simple diagnosis of bodily decline. Deuteronomy 31:9–13: Moses writes the law and entrusts it to the Levitical priests and all Israel’s elders (9). He commands its recurring public reading (10–13), providing for instruction beyond his own lifetime. The extent of “this law” is not specified in these verses.  Deuteronomy 31:14–23: Moses is told that his death approaches, presents himself with Joshua at the Tent, and writes and teaches the song that same day (14, 22). His impending absence is met with both a successor and testimony preserved in the people's mouths.  Deuteronomy 32: Moses delivers the entire song before Israel (31:30; 32:44–45), with Joshua named beside him. He then commands wholehearted attention and teaching children to obey: these words are their life in the land (46–47). The song is not merely a farewell performance.  Deuteronomy 32:48–52: Moses is commanded to ascend Mount Nebo in Moab, see Canaan, and die there as Aaron died on Mount Hor (48–52). The cause is trespass at Meribah and failure to uphold Yahweh’s holiness (51). This agrees with Numbers 20:12, while Deuteronomy 1:37 and 3:26 emphasize anger for the people’s sakes. Both emphases remain; no ascent or death is yet narrated.  Deuteronomy 33: Called the man of God, Moses blesses Israel before his death (1).
