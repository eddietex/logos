# Satan

The page takes the WEB's spelling because that is the word a reader of this wiki's raw text will
meet at Job 1:6, and because *the Accuser* as a filename would be found by nobody. **The spelling is
the one thing on this page that should not be trusted.** What the Hebrew of Job actually says is
***haśśāṭān*** — *the* śāṭān — and the rest of this page is about the difference.

## Overview

A member of the heavenly court in Job 1–2 whose whole function is to ask whether a righteous man is
righteous for nothing. He appears in the book twice, speaks eight times, is given a specific and
limited authorisation on each occasion, and then vanishes at 2:7 and is never mentioned again by
any character, in heaven or on earth, including in the epilogue that settles every other account.
**No one inside the book of Job ever learns he exists.**

**He is not called Satan.** All fourteen occurrences in Job 1–2 carry the definite article, and
Hebrew proper names do not take the article. *Haśśāṭān* is a title — *the adversary*, *the accuser*,
roughly *the Prosecutor* — and the noun behind it is an ordinary word for an opponent. David is
called a *śāṭān* by the Philistine commanders at 1 Samuel 29:4; Hadad and Rezon are *śāṭān* to
Solomon at 1 Kings 11:14 and 11:23; Solomon says at 1 Kings 5:4 that he has no *śāṭān*; the psalmist
asks for one to be set at his enemy's right hand at Psalm 109:6. **And at Numbers 22:22 and 32 the
Angel of Yahweh himself stands in Balaam's road *ləśāṭān*, as an adversary** — the same word, used
of a figure sent by God to obstruct. See [[The Angel of Yahweh]].

**What he does in Job, precisely.** He arrives among [[The Sons of God]] at a court session; he is
asked where he has been and answers that he has been patrolling the earth (*šûṭ*, to rove, the verb
of Zechariah 1:10–11's reconnaissance riders); **God, not he, raises the subject of Job**; he
responds with a question about motive; he proposes a test and predicts an outcome; he is granted
power over Job's property with his person excluded, and later over his person with his life
excluded; and both times he *"went out from the presence of Yahweh"* and did it. He initiates
nothing, owns nothing, and is refused nothing he asks for. He is also, on the text's own showing,
**wrong** — twice.

**He is not the serpent, and Genesis does not say he is.** [[The Serpent]] of Genesis 3 is *nāḥāš*,
called *"more subtle than any animal of the field which Yahweh God had made"*, and Genesis offers no
identification beyond that. The equation of the two is made first in the intertestamental literature
(*Wisdom of Solomon* 2:24) and then decisively at Revelation 12:9 and 20:2, *"the old serpent, he who
is called the devil and Satan"*. **The wiki records the identification as a later reading and does
not project it back into Genesis**; see
[[The Protoevangelium - Genesis 3.15 and the Seed of the Woman]]. What the two figures do have in
common is a method, and it is worth stating exactly: in
Genesis 3 a question is asked about **God's** motive for a prohibition, and in Job 1:9 a question is
asked about a **man's** motive for obedience. Neither makes an accusation. Both make one available.

**The trajectory from office to person.** Three Old Testament passages put the word in a heavenly
setting, and they do not read the same way.

| Passage | Form | What he is |
|---|---|---|
| Job 1:6–12; 2:1–7 | *haśśāṭān*, with the article | An officer of the court; challenges a man, acts only by permission |
| Zechariah 3:1–2 | *haśśāṭān*, with the article | A prosecutor at a trial, and rebuked by Yahweh for it |
| 1 Chronicles 21:1 | *śāṭān*, no article | Ambiguous: *an adversary*, or a name — and the parallel at 2 Samuel 24:1 has Yahweh's anger in the same slot |

The full comparison is at [[Ha-Satan - The Accuser in Job, Zechariah, and Chronicles]]. The short
version is that **the Old Testament never describes a rival to God**, that the word's movement from
office toward name is visible inside the Hebrew Bible and completed outside it, and that by the
first century the Greek *diabolos* — *slanderer*, the Septuagint's rendering of *śāṭān* — carries a
figure with a will of his own, a kingdom, and an end.

## Timeline / key events

- **Job 1:6** — arrives among the sons of God at a court session *"to present themselves before
  Yahweh"*; one of the assembly, not an intruder —
  [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]]
- **Job 1:7** — reports that he has been *"going back and forth in the earth"*; the answer of a
  patrol, and the verse behind 1 Peter 5:8's *"walks around"*
- **Job 1:9–11** — ***"Does Job fear God for nothing?"*** The book's hinge question, and the only
  thing he is in the book to say. He follows it with the hedge, the blessing on the work of Job's
  hands, and the prediction
- **Job 1:12** — granted everything Job has and forbidden Job's person; goes out and does it
- **Job 2:1–5** — the second session; *"Skin for skin. Yes, all that a man has he will give for his
  life"*, the second and last of his claims
- **Job 2:6–7** — granted Job's body and forbidden his life; strikes him with sores and **leaves the
  book**
- **Job 42:7–17** — absent from the epilogue. The friends are rebuked, Job is vindicated and
  restored, and 42:11 sums the whole thing up as *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him"*

## Appears in

- [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] — Job 1:6–22; his entrance, his question, the
  first permission, and its execution.
- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:1–10; his **last appearance in the book and in the Hebrew Bible's
  earliest extended treatment of him**. He is told at 2:3 that he was wrong and that God calls the
  ruin *ḥinnām*, and **he neither concedes nor repeats his claim — he substitutes a smaller one.**
  *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* was a claim that disinterested piety does not exist; *"all that
  a man has he will give for his life"* is a claim about self-preservation that nobody would dispute
  and that quietly grants the first round. He is granted the body, strikes it **himself** with no
  Sabeans, fire, Chaldeans or wind in between, and exits mid-verse at 2:7.

## Related people

- [[God]] — who raises Job's name before the accuser has mentioned it, grants both permissions, sets
  both limits, and at 2:3 says *"you incited me against him, to ruin him without cause"* — conceding
  the incitement and not the responsibility.
- [[Job (person)]] — the subject of the challenge, and the only person in Scripture God twice calls
  *"blameless and upright"* to someone else's face.
- [[The Sons of God]] — the assembly he comes among, in the only two Old Testament scenes that show
  the court in session with a dissenting voice in it.
- [[The Serpent]] — Genesis 3's questioner, identified with this figure by Revelation 12:9 and not
  by Genesis.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — who is himself called a *śāṭān* at Numbers 22:22, which is the single
  hardest fact for any reading that treats the word as a name.
- [[Job's Wife]] — who says at 2:9 the words he predicted twice at 1:11 and 2:5, in his own verb,
  two verses after he has left and without ever having met him. **If she is his instrument, she is
  one the text never shows him arranging** — see
  [[Renounce God and Die - The Euphemism and the Accuser's Prediction]].

## Themes associated

- [[Testing]] — he is the mechanism of the only test in Scripture whose reasons are argued out loud.
- [[The Fear of God]] — whose motive he is the first and only person in the Bible to question.
- [[Retribution]] — which he believes in as firmly as Job's friends do, and runs in the opposite

- [[Judgment]] — the setting of both his appearances is a court, and in Zechariah 3 he loses.

## Notes

- **Why the wiki does not retitle the page.** *The Accuser* is the better translation and *Satan* is
  the better filename. The WEB is the wiki's raw text and prints *Satan* fourteen times in two
  chapters, so a reader arriving from `raw/bible/Job.md` will search for that; the `also_known_as`
  frontmatter carries the alternatives for anyone searching the other way. The cost is that the
  filename asserts something the Hebrew does not, and this note is the mitigation.

- **What the article actually settles, and what it does not.** It settles that *śāṭān* in Job is
  functioning as a common noun with a definite article — an office. It does **not** settle that the
  office is held by a different being from the one later texts name, and it does not make the figure
  benign. He is wrong about Job, he says so twice, and Zechariah 3:2 has Yahweh rebuke him outright:
  *"Yahweh rebuke you, Satan! Yes, Yahweh who has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you!"* **An officer of the
  court who is rebuked by the judge for doing his job is already halfway to being something else.**

- **"Skin for skin" (2:4).** *ʿÔr bəʿad-ʿôr*, and nobody knows what it means. The leading proposals
  are a trader's formula for an even exchange (hide for hide), a proverb about giving up someone
  else's skin to save your own, and a wrestling or protective image — one layer of hide surrendered
  to shield the next. The gloss that follows it in the verse is unambiguous whichever way the idiom
  runs: *"all that a man has he will give for his life."* The challenge has narrowed from *does he
  love God for nothing* to *does he love anything more than his own body*, which is a smaller claim
  and a nastier one. **And the narrowing is a retreat.** God has just told him the first claim
  failed; he does not defend it, withdraw it, or say a word about it, and the proverb does the work
  of pretending the second claim was the point all along. **He is never refuted in the book because
  he never stands still long enough to be** — and after 2:7 he has no further proposal available,
  since the only thing left to take is the life the hedge protects. See [[The Second Test]].

- **"You incited me against him" (2:3), and 1 Chronicles 21:1.** The verb God uses of the accuser is
  ***hēsît*** — to incite, entice, stir up against — and its usual company in Scripture is seduction
  to evil: the family member who entices toward other gods at Deuteronomy 13:6, the Rabshakeh
  enticing Jerusalem at 2 Kings 18:32, Jeremiah 38:22. **The single closest parallel is the one place
  outside Job where *śāṭān* appears in a divine context**: 1 Chronicles 21:1, where he *"stood up
  against Israel and incited David"* — *wayyāset* — to number the people, against 2 Samuel 24:1,
  where Yahweh's anger *incited* David to do the identical thing with the identical verb. **In Job
  2:3 both subjects are in one sentence**: the accuser incites, and God is the one incited, and God
  states it about himself. See
  [[Ha-Satan - The Accuser in Job, Zechariah, and Chronicles]].

- **The Septuagint and the devil.** The Greek translates *haśśāṭān* as *ho diabolos*, *the slanderer*
  — with the article, faithfully — and it is that word, not a transliteration, that the New Testament
  inherits. Where the New Testament transliterates instead (*Satanas*, e.g. Mark 1:13, Luke 22:31,
  2 Corinthians 12:7), it does so **as a name**, article or no article. The English *Satan* in Job
  is that later usage read backwards into the Hebrew.

- **Luke 22:31–32 is Job 1 with one thing added.** *"Simon, Simon, behold, Satan asked to have all
  of you, that he might sift you as wheat, but I prayed for you, that your faith wouldn't fail."*
  The request, the permission implied by the request, and the sifting are all Joban; what is new is
  that the intercessor is named in the same sentence, and that the man is told in advance. Job gets
  neither. See [[The Witness in Heaven]] for the figure he reaches for instead.
