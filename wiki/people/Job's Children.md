# Job's Children

## Overview

Seven sons and three daughters, unnamed, introduced in a single verse (1:2) and killed in a single
verse (1:19). They are the largest human loss in the book and the one the argument keeps returning
to, and the text tells us almost nothing about them: that they feasted in rotation, that they
included their sisters, and that their father worried about their hearts.

**The book uses them three times, in three different ways.** In the prologue they are the occasion
of Job's piety and then the last and worst item on the messengers' list. In the dialogue they
become ammunition — Bildad tells Job to his face that they died for their own sin (8:4). In the
epilogue they are replaced, and the arithmetic of the replacement is one of the most argued-over
details in Scripture.

## Timeline / key events

- **Job 1:2** — seven sons and three daughters, named as a number rather than as names. Ten, and
  seven-and-three, is the same stylised completeness as the livestock counts in the next verse.
- **Job 1:4** — a rotating cycle of feasts, each brother hosting *"in the house of each one on his
  birthday"*, with the three sisters sent for. The detail of the sisters is the reason the feasts
  are hard to read as debauchery.
- **Job 1:5** — their father sanctifies them and offers a burnt offering for each after every
  round, on the hypothesis *"It may be that my sons have sinned, and renounced God in their
  hearts."* Nothing in the text says they had.
- **Job 1:13, 18–19** — they are eating and drinking in the oldest brother's house when a great
  wind from the wilderness strikes the four corners of it and kills all ten. This is the fourth and
  final message, held to last.
- **Job 8:4** — Bildad: *"If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into the
  hand of their disobedience."* The friends' theology applied to ten dead young people, offered as
  consolation.
- **Job 19:17** — Job counts their absence among his humiliations, along with his wife's
  estrangement.
- **Job 29:5** — his lament for the days *"when the Almighty was yet with me, and my children were
  around me."*
- **Job 42:13–15** — seven sons and three daughters again. Only here the daughters are named —
  Jemimah, Keziah, and Keren Happuch — described as the most beautiful women in the land, and given
  an inheritance among their brothers, which was not the ordinary practice.

## The doubling that isn't

At 42:10 Job's fortunes are restored, and the livestock inventory of 1:3 comes back exactly
doubled: fourteen thousand sheep, six thousand camels, a thousand yoke of oxen, a thousand
donkeys. The children come back at the original count — seven sons and three daughters, not
fourteen and six.

The asymmetry is deliberate and the readings of it are worth keeping side by side.

- **Children are not property.** The most common reading, and the one the structure supports:
  the text doubles what can be doubled and refuses to treat persons as replaceable stock. A
  restored fortune is a restored fortune; ten dead children are not undone by ten more.
- **The doubling is there, counting the dead.** The rabbinic reading (*Bava Batra* 16b) says the
  first ten are not lost, so Job does in fact end with twenty. This requires a hope for the dead
  that the poem elsewhere finds hard to hold — Job's own view of Sheol at 14:12 and 17:13–16 is
  bleak — and it is usually offered as a theological gloss rather than as exegesis.
- **The naming is the compensation.** Whatever the arithmetic, the second set of daughters gets
  what the first set did not: names in the text, and property. Their brothers are the ones left
  anonymous this time. It is a small, pointed reversal in a book that does very little of that.

None of these readings removes the difficulty, and the book does not appear to want it removed.
The restoration in Job 42 is a genuine ending, and it is not an answer to the deaths in Job 1.

## Themes associated

- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — they are the reason for the first offering in the book, and the
  first offering in Scripture made for a merely possible sin.
- [[Suffering]] — the part of Job's suffering that the epilogue does not reverse.
- [[Retribution]] — Bildad's use of their deaths at 8:4 is the doctrine at its most brutal, and the
  clearest demonstration that it is not merely a theory about God but a way of speaking to the
  bereaved.
- [[Blessing]] — counted among Job's goods in 1:2–3 and restored in 42:13, which raises the whole
  question of what it means for a person to be counted as a blessing.
- [[Life and Death]] — ten deaths in one sentence, and no explanation offered to anyone inside the
  book.

## Notes

- **Why they are unnamed the first time.** The prologue names Job, his three friends, and their
  towns; it does not name his wife, his children, or his servants. The narrative economy is
  deliberate — the ten are a count, part of the inventory the accuser is pointing at when he says
  *"all that he has"* (1:11). The naming of the second three daughters at 42:14 lands the harder
  for it.
- **Jemimah, Keziah, Keren Happuch.** *Dove*, *cassia* (a cinnamon-like spice), and *horn of
  eye-paint* — a bird and two cosmetics, and the only personal names in the book that are plainly
  Hebrew rather than archaic or foreign. Giving daughters an inheritance alongside sons (42:15) is
  irregular against Numbers 27:1–11, where daughters inherit only in the absence of sons, and it is
  one of the details that makes the epilogue read as a deliberate excess rather than a simple
  restoration.
