# The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)

## Overview

The speaker to whom Ecclesiastes attributes its words, described at 1:1 as the son of [[David]],
king in [[Jerusalem]]. The title is kept with a book qualifier so that a literary role does
not become an assertion of an otherwise unidentified historical person's name or period.

The opening assigns him the verdict of vanity and introduces the question of human gain.
[[Solomon]] is the traditional association and a natural comparison with Proverbs 1:1, but
Ecclesiastes 1:1 does not name him. This page tracks the textual speaker without deciding the
book's historical authorship. See
[[The Wisdom of Solomon - 1 Kings 3-4 and the Headings of Proverbs]].

In [[The Burden of Wisdom]] he speaks in the first person for the first time: *"I, the Preacher,
was king over Israel in Jerusalem"* (1:12). He claims great wisdom above all who were before him
in Jerusalem (1:16), and uses it to search out everything done under the sky and to know madness
and folly. He reports the search as a burden God has given, and concludes that much wisdom
brings much grief (1:18). The comparison with predecessors in Jerusalem is a difficulty for a
Solomonic identification that this page records without resolving; see the passage notes.

In [[The Trial of Pleasure]] he turns the inquiry on mirth, with his heart *"yet guiding me with
wisdom"* (2:3). He builds, plants, acquires servants, flocks, silver and gold, and singers, and
increases more than all who were before him in Jerusalem (2:9). He withholds his heart from no joy
and calls it his portion (2:10), and then judges all of it vanity with *"no profit under the sun"*
(2:11).

In [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]] he turns back to wisdom, madness, and folly (2:12). He finds that wisdom excels folly as
light excels darkness (2:13), and that one event happens to both (2:14). Applying it to himself —
*"why was I then more wise?"* (2:15) — he reports that he hated life (2:17).

In [[The Labor Left to Another]] he hates the labor he must leave to an unknown successor
(2:18–19), describes despair and a heart without rest (2:20–23), and recognizes enjoyment in
labor as coming from God's hand (2:24). In the WEB of 2:25 he appeals to his own experience of
enjoyment. His account of God's giving in 2:26 ends again with vanity; the speaker is not
identified as the sinner whose accumulation passes to another.

In [[A Time for Every Purpose]] he moves from the catalogue of times to the question of
profit (3:9), observes the burden God gives (10), and names the limit on knowing God's work
(11). He also claims to know that rejoicing and doing good are fitting (12), and that God's
work endures and calls forth fear (14). Limited understanding does not prevent every positive
claim. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

In [[From Dust to Dust]] he observes corrupt justice, affirms God's judgment, and describes God's
testing of humanity through its likeness to animals (3:16–20). He asks about the spirits'
destinations (21) and commends rejoicing in works despite the unseen future (22). The
questioning voice and the positive affirmations remain together. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–22.

In [[The Tears of the Oppressed]] he turns from rejoicing in works (3:22) to victims' tears and the
power of their oppressors (4:1). He praises the dead above the living, and judges the person
who has not yet existed better off than either for not seeing evil (4:2–3). This is his
response to observed oppression, not a report that he himself has died or a command to die.
The tension with his preceding commendation of enjoyment remains explicit.

In [[Two Are Better Than One]] he observes that all labor and achievement is the envy of a
neighbor (4:4), rejects the fool's folded hands (5), and ranks a quiet handful above two handfuls
with labor (6). He describes a man with neither son nor brother who labors without end and asks
for whom (8), then commends two over one for reward, help, warmth, and defense (9–12). After the
preference for the dead in 4:2–3, he names a concrete good among the living.

In [[The Youth and the Old King]] he ranks a poor and wise youth above an old and foolish king
who no longer knows how to receive admonition (4:13). He tells of one who came out of prison to
be king though born poor (14), sees all the living with the successor (15), and judges that
popularity vanity because those who come after will not rejoice in him (16). Though he himself
speaks as a king (1:12), the story is told of others, not as autobiography.

In [[Let Your Words Be Few]] he gives the reader direct commands for the first time. Guard your
steps in God's house, draw near to listen rather than offer the sacrifice of fools (5:1), let
your words be few because God is in heaven (2), pay what you vow (4–5), and do not excuse an
unpaid vow as a mistake (6). He ends with *"you must fear God"* (7). The observer of the earlier
chapters becomes a teacher here.

In [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]] he observes harmful wealth and loss,
asks what profit remains at life's empty-handed end, and nevertheless finds eating,
drinking, and enjoying labor good and proper (5:8–20). The gift of enjoyment belongs beside
his account of oppression and distress, not in place of it. Source: stored WEB 5:8–20.

In [[Riches Without Enjoyment]] he observes an evil heavy on people: wealth God gives
without power to eat of it (6:1–2). He judges a stillborn child better off than a father
with many children and years but no good or burial (3–6), then questions the gain of
labor, wisdom, and practical knowledge before unfilled appetite (7–8). The sight of the
eyes is preferred to desire's wandering, with a closing vanity verdict (9). These are
observations and hypothetical cases, not an assertion that he himself had a hundred
children or lived two thousand years. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9.

In [[Who Knows What Is Good]] he turns from the case of wealth without enjoyment to human limits
(6:10–12): naming long ago, the mightier party, many words without profit, and life like
a shadow. His question about knowing good remains beside his positive judgments in
3:12–13 and 5:18; no answer is supplied here. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:10–12.

In [[The House of Mourning]] he answers the question of good with comparisons (7:1–14): a
good name above perfume, the day of death above birth, the house of mourning above the house
of feasting, sorrow above laughter, the rebuke of the wise above the song of fools. He
concedes that a bribe can corrupt the wise (7), commends patience over pride (8), and ranks
wisdom with an inheritance as a defense that preserves life (11–12). He closes with God's
work: joy in prosperity, consideration in adversity (14). Preferring mourning to feasting
stands in tension with his earlier commendations of enjoyment, which the passage page keeps
explicit. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:1–14.

In [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]] he returns to first-person report (7:15–29).
He has seen a righteous man perish and a wicked man live long (15), counsels against being overly righteous and too wicked
on the ground of the fear of God (16–18), and grants that no one does good without sin (20).
He resolved to be wise and found wisdom far off (23–24); his search for the scheme of things
yields a woman more bitter than death (26), one man among a thousand (28), and the finding that
God made man upright (29). At 7:27 he is named by title — *"says the Preacher"* — for the
first time since 1:12; the Hebrew verb form there is discussed with its uncertainty on the
passage page. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7:15–29.

In [[The King and the Limits of Power]] he commends wisdom's effect on the face, urges keeping the
king's command because of the oath to God, and joins discernment of time and procedure to
ignorance of the future (8:1–7). He then names the limits of human power, especially over
death, and reports harmful dominion (8–9). This is counsel and observation, not a report
that the unnamed king is himself. The safety affirmed in 8:5 remains beside the misery
and hurt of 8:6, 9. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:1–9.

### The wicked buried and the work beyond finding — Ecclesiastes 8:10–17

He observes the wicked buried and forgotten (10), links delayed sentence to greater
evil (11), and affirms good for those who fear God despite long-lived crime (12–13).
He then reports outcomes reversed (14), commends joy (15), and describes searching that
cannot comprehend God's work (16–17). His positive claims are preserved beside his
observations and admitted limit, without assigning them to different speakers.

See [[The Wicked Buried and the Work Beyond Finding]]. Source: stored WEB Ecclesiastes 8:10–17.

## Timeline / key events

- Ecclesiastes 8:1–9 — [[The King and the Limits of Power]]; royal command, wise timing, and limits on human power.

- Ecclesiastes 7:15–29 — [[Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question]]; reversed outcomes, the fear of God, and a search that finds little.

- Ecclesiastes 7:1–14 — [[The House of Mourning]]; better-than sayings on death, mourning, wisdom, and God's work.

- Ecclesiastes 6:10–12 — [[Who Knows What Is Good]]; questions about good and what follows a person.

- Ecclesiastes 6:1–9 — [[Riches Without Enjoyment]]; abundance and long life tested against good unexperienced.

- Ecclesiastes 5:8–20 — [[The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment]]; wealth tested and enjoyment received.

- Ecclesiastes 5:1–7 — [[Let Your Words Be Few]]; first direct instruction: few words, paid vows, and the fear of God.

- Ecclesiastes 4:13–16 — [[The Youth and the Old King]]; the wise youth ranked above the foolish king, and a successor's passing popularity.

- Ecclesiastes 4:4–12 — [[Two Are Better Than One]]; envious labor, the man alone, and the advantage of companions.

- Ecclesiastes 4:1–3 — [[The Tears of the Oppressed]]; observed oppression and the severe comparison of lives.

- Ecclesiastes 3:16–22 — [[From Dust to Dust]]; injustice, judgment, shared mortality, and a present portion.

- Ecclesiastes 3:1–15 — [[A Time for Every Purpose]] ; the poem of times and the reflection on God’s
  work.

- Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 — introduced by royal title; the opening tests labor, recurrence,
  satisfaction, and memory. [[Vanity of Vanities]]. This is a literary sequence, not a dated biography.
- Ecclesiastes 1:12–18 — reports his inquiry by wisdom into all that is done, and its cost.
  [[The Burden of Wisdom]].
- Ecclesiastes 2:1–11 — tests mirth and great works; finds joy but no profit.
  [[The Trial of Pleasure]].
- Ecclesiastes 2:12–17 — weighs wisdom against folly before death; hates life.
  [[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]].
- Ecclesiastes 2:18–26 — despairs over his legacy and recognizes enjoyment from God.
  [[The Labor Left to Another]].
