# Zophar the Naamathite

## Overview

The third and least patient of Job's friends, and the one who gets the fewest words. He speaks
twice — chapters 11 and 20 — and in the third cycle he does not speak at all, which is either the
clearest sign that the friends have been argued into silence or the clearest sign that the text of
Job 25–27 has been disturbed (see [[Bildad the Shuhite]] for both readings).

His first speech is the one that says the quiet part. *"Should your boastings make men hold their
peace? … Oh that God would speak, and open his lips against you, that he would show you the secrets
of wisdom! … Know therefore that God exacts of you **less than your iniquity deserves**"* (11:3, 5–6).
Zophar's position is not merely that Job is guilty but that he is getting off lightly, and he is
the only friend to say so. He then demands repentance in the plainest terms of any of the three
(11:13–20), and his second speech (chapter 20) is twenty-nine verses on the brevity of the wicked
man's triumph — the sweetness of evil turning to the venom of asps in his belly, riches swallowed
and vomited up.

He is a **Naamathite**, from a Naamah otherwise unlocated; the Naamah of Joshua 15:41 is in the
Judean lowlands and does not suit the book's eastern setting, so the place is usually taken to be a
site in northern Arabia or Edom that has left no other trace.

## In the ingested passages

Zophar does not speak in Job 15–17, and is included in the same two ways as Bildad.

He is inside Eliphaz's *"we"* and *"with us"* at 15:9–10 in [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]], and inside
Job's plurals at 16:2 and 17:10 in [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]]. Job's *"You
are all miserable comforters"* is addressed to a group whose position, at this point in the book,
Zophar has stated more severely than either of the others.

There is a particular edge in that. When Job says at 16:17 *"there is no violence in my hands, and
my prayer is pure"*, the standing rebuttal in the room is Zophar's 11:6, that God is exacting less
than his iniquity deserves. The reader knows which of the two is right.

## Timeline / key events

- **Job 2:11–13** — arrives with the others and sits seven days in silence.
- **Job 11:1–20** — the first speech: God knows worthless men, you are getting less than you
  deserve, put away iniquity and you will forget your misery.
- **Job 15–17** — silent, and included in the friends' *"we"* and Job's *"you all"*.
- **Job 20:1–29** — the second speech: *"the triumphing of the wicked is short"*, and twenty-nine
  verses on how short.
- **Job 21:1–34** — answered directly by Job's *"Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow
  mighty in power?"*, which is the book's frontal rebuttal of everything Zophar has just said.
- **Job 42:7–9** — included in *"your two friends"*, rebuked, and required to bring sacrifices and
  be prayed for by Job.

## Related people

- [[Job (person)]] — whom he tells, uniquely among the friends, that his punishment is too light.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] and [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — the other two, whose doctrine he pushes
  furthest.
- [[God]] — whose speech he wishes for at 11:5 and who, when he finally speaks at 38:1, says
  nothing that helps Zophar.

## Themes associated

- [[Retribution]] — carried to its limit: not only does suffering measure guilt, it *under*measures
  it.
- [[Wisdom]] — 11:7–9, *"Can you fathom the mystery of God? … deeper than Sheol"*, a genuinely fine
  passage on divine inscrutability, deployed to close down a question rather than to open one.
- [[Judgment]] — the whole of chapter 20.
- [[Suffering]] — as a receipt, and a discounted one.

## Notes

- **The name.** *Ṣōpar*, of uncertain meaning; often linked to *ṣippôr*, *bird*, or to a root
  meaning *to chirp* or *to leap*. Nothing in the book plays on it.
- **His best passage, and its use.** Job 11:7–9 — *"Can you fathom the mystery of God? Or can you
  probe the limits of the Almighty? They are high as heaven. What can you do? They are deeper than
  Sheol. What can you know?"* — is close in substance to what God himself will say out of the
  whirlwind, and is worth marking as the wiki's cleanest example of the friends' problem. Zophar's
  premise is correct and God will confirm it; his inference from it is that Job should stop
  talking, and God's inference from it is that Job should keep listening. The same doctrine of
  divine inscrutability functions in Zophar's mouth as a gag and in God's mouth as an invitation.
