# Sarah

## Overview

**A note on the name.** She is *Sarai* (*Śāray*) from Genesis 11:29 until God renames her *Sarah*
(*Śārâ*) at 17:15, and *Sarah* thereafter and throughout the rest of the Bible. This wiki files her
under the later name for the same reason it files her husband under [[Abraham]] — it is the name the
canon uses — and writes *Sarai* in the prose wherever the text under discussion does. Both forms
appear to mean **princess**; the change at 17:15 is not a change of meaning, which is unusual among
biblical renamings and has never been satisfactorily explained.

### In this pericope — Genesis 11:29–31

Three appearances, and one of them is the hinge of the book.

*"Abram and Nahor married wives. The name of Abram's wife was Sarai, and the name of Nahor's wife
was Milcah, the daughter of Haran who was also the father of Iscah"* (11:29). Note the asymmetry:
[[Milcah]]'s father is given and Sarai's is not, in the same sentence. That silence is where an old
question starts.

*"Sarai was barren. She had no child"* (11:30). Six words in Hebrew — *wattəhî śāray ʿăqārâ ʾên lāh
wālād* — the fact, and then the fact again, with no cause offered, no lament, no prayer, and no
comment. It is **the first infertility in the Bible**, and it is placed at the end of twenty verses
whose entire content is men fathering sons. The register runs on procreation for nine generations
and then hits a wall, and the wall is named. Everything from the promise of 12:2 to the birth of
Isaac in 21:2 is the working-out of this half-verse; the New Testament reads it the same way —
*"the deadness of Sarah's womb"* (Romans 4:19), and *"by faith, even Sarah herself received power
to conceive"* (Hebrews 11:11). See [[Barrenness]].

*"Terah took … Sarai his daughter-in-law, his son Abram's wife"* (11:31). She leaves Ur as an object
of her father-in-law's verb, like her husband, and settles in [[Haran (city)]].

### In the next pericope — Genesis 12:1–9

She is named once, in the inventory of what Abram took out of Haran: *"Abram took Sarai his wife,
Lot his brother's son, all their possessions … and the people whom they had acquired in Haran"*
(12:5). No word to her, no word from her, and no mention of her in the promise.

**That silence is the pericope's sharpest edge.** The promise Abram is given is *"I will make of you
a great nation"* (12:2), and the last thing said about his wife, four verses earlier, was that she
could not have a child. Genesis places the obstacle and the promise back to back and does not
connect them; nobody in chapter 12 mentions the difficulty, and God does not address it for another
five chapters. Everything from Hagar (16) to the laughter behind the tent flap (18:12) to Isaac
(21:2) is the working-out of a collision the text sets up here without a word. See
[[Barrenness]] and [[The Call of Abram]].

She is also, on the arithmetic of 17:17, sixty-five years old when she leaves Haran — which is worth
holding beside what the next pericope has the Egyptians say about her.

### Whose daughter she was

Genesis 11:29 withholds it and two later texts fill it differently.

- **Genesis 20:12** — Abraham, explaining himself to Abimelech: *"Besides, she is indeed my sister,
  the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother; and she became my wife."* On the
  plain sense she is [[Terah]]'s daughter by a second wife, and Abram's half-sister. This is the only
  statement in Scripture that directly answers the question, and it is the reading this wiki records
  first for that reason.
- **The identification with [[Iscah]]** — found in Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.6.5), in the Talmud (b.
  Sanhedrin 69b), and in Rashi on 11:29. Iscah is named in that verse and nowhere else in the Bible,
  which invites the question of why she is named at all; if she is Sarai, then both brothers married
  daughters of [[Haran (son of Terah)]], and the verse is symmetrical after all. The cost is Genesis
  20:12, which then has to mean *granddaughter* — possible in Hebrew usage but a stretch in a verse
  whose whole point is precision about degrees of kinship.

The wiki records both and adopts neither, while noting that only one of them is a statement about
her parentage rather than an inference from a name.

**She is ten years younger than Abraham** (17:17: he is a hundred, she is ninety), which puts her
birth around Terah's hundred-and-fortieth year on the chronology that has Abram born at Terah 130.
She dies at 127 (23:1) — the only woman in the Bible whose age at death is given.

**The marriage is one the Torah later forbids.** A union with a half-sister is prohibited at
Leviticus 18:9 and 20:17 and cursed at Deuteronomy 27:22. Genesis narrates it without comment, as it
narrates Nahor's marriage to his niece in the same verse and Jacob's to two sisters later. The
patriarchal narratives are set before Sinai and are not written to be exemplary under it.

### Where the rest of her is

Ahead, in pericopes not yet ingested: Egypt and the sister ruse (12:10–20), Hagar (16), the renaming
and the promise (17:15–21), the laughter behind the tent flap (18:9–15), Abimelech (20), the birth of
Isaac and the sending away of Hagar (21), and her death and the purchase of Machpelah (23). She is
the only matriarch God addresses directly, and 1 Peter 3:6 and Hebrews 11:11 both make her an
exemplar. None of that is legible from Genesis 11, where she is a name, a marriage, and an absence.
