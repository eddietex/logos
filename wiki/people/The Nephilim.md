# The Nephilim

## Overview

A word that appears twice in the Hebrew Bible and has generated a literature out of all proportion
to its evidence.

Genesis 6:4: *"The Nephilim were in the earth in those days, and also after that, when God's sons
came in to men's daughters and had children with them. Those were the mighty men who were of old,
men of renown."* Numbers 13:33, in the report of the spies sent into Canaan: *"There we saw the
Nephilim … and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers."* That is all of it. Every other detail
that attaches to the name in popular imagination comes from *1 Enoch*, from the Septuagint's
translation choice, or from readers joining the two verses to the giant traditions elsewhere in the
Old Testament.

**The name.** Modern translations transliterate rather than translate because the derivation is not
secure. The natural Hebrew root is *nāpal*, *to fall* — *the fallen ones*, or possibly *those who
fall upon*, i.e. attackers — which fits the angelic reading of Genesis 6:2 so conveniently that
caution is warranted. An alternative traces it to an Aramaic term for giant. The Septuagint decided
the question for most of Christian history by rendering it *gigantes*, and through the Vulgate's
*gigantes* into English as *giants* in the King James Version; that reading gets independent support
from Numbers 13:33, where the point of the comparison is unmistakably size.

**What Genesis 6:4 actually claims.** Three things, and the third is often lost:

1. **They were in the earth in those days.** They are *contemporaries* of the events of 6:1–2. The
   verse does not say they were created by them.
2. **And also after that.** The parenthesis is doing real work — it acknowledges, inside the flood
   narrative's prologue, that the Nephilim did not stop with the flood. It is the verse's own
   provision for Numbers 13:33, and it is one of the reasons some readers take Genesis 6:4 as an
   editorial gloss on an older tradition rather than as narrative.
3. **Those were the mighty men who were of old, men of renown.** *Gibbōrîm* — warriors, heroes,
   champions — and *ʾanšê haššēm*, *men of the name*. Whether *"those"* refers back to the Nephilim
   or to the children of 6:4's second clause is grammatically open, and translations differ.
   Notably, this is not a condemnation. Genesis 6 never says the Nephilim were wicked. What it says
   is that they were famous — and five chapters later, at Babel, humanity says *"let's make
   ourselves a name"* and God comes down to stop them.

**Where the giants of the conquest come in.** Numbers 13:33 identifies the Nephilim with *"the sons
of Anak"*, and the Anakim recur through Deuteronomy 2 and 9 and Joshua 11 and 14 as the
formidable pre-Israelite inhabitants of the hill country, alongside the Rephaim, the Emim, and the
Zamzummim. Og of Bashan, the last of the Rephaim, is remembered chiefly for the dimensions of his
bed (Deuteronomy 3:11), and Goliath and his kin at 2 Samuel 21:15–22 are the tradition's last
appearance. Whether any of this is genealogically continuous with Genesis 6:4 or whether Numbers
13:33 is the spies' own hyperbole — which is, after all, part of the *"evil report"* the chapter
condemns them for — is the live question. See [[The Nephilim and the Giants of Canaan]].

**What the flood does to them.** On any reading in which the Nephilim are pre-flood beings and the
flood is universal, they should not exist afterwards, and Genesis 6:4 says they do. The explanations
offered are: that *"and also after that"* means the same phenomenon recurred later; that the
Nephilim came through the flood in the line of one of Noah's sons' wives, which is speculation with
no textual support; that the flood was regional; or that Numbers 13:33 uses the word loosely for
outsized enemies without asserting descent. None is demanded by the text, and Genesis does not treat
it as a problem.
