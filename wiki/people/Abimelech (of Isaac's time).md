# Abimelech (of Isaac's time)

## Overview

**King of [[Gerar]] in Isaac's generation — the man who catches the third wife-sister lie by looking
out of a window, protects the liar and his wife by capital edict, evicts him for getting rich, and
then travels inland to ask him for an oath.**

**On the name and the page.** *ʾĂbî-melek* is **my father is king**, or, read as a confession,
*the (divine) king is my father*. It is borne by the king of Gerar in Genesis 20–21, by this king
here, by Gideon's son in Judges 9, and — in the superscription of Psalm 34 — by the Philistine king
1 Samuel 21 calls Achish, which is the clearest evidence that the word served as a royal title and
not only as a personal name. **This wiki therefore qualifies both Gerar pages by the patriarch they
appear with**, exactly as it does for [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]], and **does not decide** between
one long-lived man, a father and a son, a hereditary office, and one tradition attached to two
patriarchs. The same three options apply to his general, who bears the same name in both scenes. See
[[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]], [[Phicol]], and
[[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].

**What separates him from his namesake is that God never speaks to him.** The Abimelech of Genesis 20
receives the first dream in Scripture, argues with God, and is told his integrity is genuine. **This
one gets no dream, no warning, and no vindication.** He works it out by eye, and everything he does
afterwards he does on his own judgment.

### In this pericope — Genesis 26:1–33

**He is in the chapter four times, and each time he is the one who moves.**

**1. He receives them.** *"Isaac went to Abimelech king of the Philistines, to Gerar"* (26:1). The
famine is in Canaan; the food is in his territory; he takes them in, and the text records no
condition attached.

**2. He discovers the lie.**

> **8** When he had been there a long time, Abimelech king of the Philistines looked out at a window,
> and saw, and, behold, Isaac was caressing Rebekah, his wife.

***Wayyašqēp bəʿad haḥallôn*** — **the least supernatural discovery in the three episodes.** Pharaoh
learned by plague; his namesake learned in a dream from God; **he looks out of a window.** Kings and
queens at windows in the Hebrew Bible are usually about to see something they did not want to see
(Judges 5:28; 2 Samuel 6:16; 2 Kings 9:30–32), and the thing he sees is a man behaving toward his
sister in a way no brother behaves.

**3. He prosecutes a risk, not an injury.**

> **10** Abimelech said, “What is this you have done to us? One of the people might easily have lain
> with your wife, and you would have brought guilt on us!”

***"What is this you have done to us?"*** — *mah-zōʾt ʿāśîtā lānû* — is the third occurrence of the
foreign king's rebuke, after 12:18 and 20:9, and **it is the only one in which nothing happened.**
Nobody took Rebekah. **He is complaining about the exposure**, which lasted, the text says, *a long
time*, and about *ʾāšām*, **guilt** — the collective liability the later law provides an offering
for. He is right, and Isaac's answer is one clause about fearing for his own life.

**4. He legislates.**

> **11** Abimelech commanded all the people, saying, “He who touches this man or his wife will surely
> be put to death.”

***Môt yûmāt*** is the death-penalty formula of the law codes, and **this is the first royal decree
in Scripture.** Its content is the protection of a resident alien and his wife, issued about a man
who had just lied to him, and it goes further than his namesake's *"Behold, my land is before you"*
(20:15). **Across the three wife-sister episodes the foreigner's conduct improves steadily and the
patriarch's does not.** See [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].

**Then he evicts them.**

> **16** Abimelech said to Isaac, “Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we.”

***ʿĀṣamtā mimmennû məʾōd.*** **One sentence, no charge, no compensation, and no reference to his own
edict.** It follows two verses in which Isaac reaps a hundredfold, becomes very great, and is envied
by the Philistines, and it is what a small kingdom does about a household inside it that has outgrown
it. **The wording is within a word of Exodus 1:9**, where a king of Egypt says the same thing about
this man's descendants and does something worse about it. See
[[Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt]].

**And then he comes back.**

> **28** They said, “We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you. We said, ‘Let there now be an oath
> between us, even between us and you, and let us make a covenant with you,
> **29** that you will do us no harm, as we have not touched you, and as we have done to you nothing
> but good, and have sent you away in peace.’ You are now the blessed of Yahweh.”

He travels from Gerar to [[Beersheba]] with his counsellor [[Ahuzzath]] and his general [[Phicol]] —
**king, court, and army, a full embassy to a man he expelled** — and asks for the protection he can
no longer command. *"We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you"* is his namesake's *"God is with you in
all that you do"* (21:22) in the same political register: **a man God is with is a man you sign with
rather than a man you evict**, a conclusion he reaches one chapter after doing the evicting.

**His account of his own conduct is generous and not quite false.** *"We have not touched you"* is
true in the narrow sense his own edict made it true; it passes over the wells filled in with earth,
the two disputes at Esek and Sitnah, and the expulsion. **Isaac, who asks the one sharp question in
the chapter — *"Why have you come to me, since you hate me?"* (26:27) — does not contest it**, makes
them a feast, swears in the morning, and sends them away in peace.

### What he is for, in the book

**He is the third foreign ruler in this book to hold the moral vocabulary, and the first to write it
into law.** Pharaoh asked the question and expelled the household; the first Abimelech asked it,
paid, and invited them to stay; **this one asks it and makes harming them a capital offence.** The
chosen family is never the one with the moral vocabulary in these stories, and Genesis makes no
attempt to soften it.

**And he is the only king in Genesis who is both generous and frightened.** The edict and the
eviction are five verses apart and both are his. Genesis presents them without a word of comment,
which is the same thing it does with Isaac's lie and Isaac's harvest.

## Timeline / key events

- Receives Isaac at Gerar in a famine — Genesis 26:1, [[Isaac in Gerar]]
- Sees from a window that Rebekah is Isaac's wife — Genesis 26:8
- Rebukes Isaac, and prosecutes the guilt his kingdom nearly incurred — Genesis 26:9–10
- Decrees the death penalty for anyone who touches Isaac or Rebekah — Genesis 26:11
- Expels the household: *"Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we"* — Genesis 26:16
- Comes to Beersheba with Ahuzzath and Phicol and asks for an oath — Genesis 26:26–29
- Is given a feast, swears in the morning, and departs in peace — Genesis 26:30–31

## Related people

- [[Isaac]] — whom he shelters, catches, protects, throws out, and comes back to swear with
- [[Rebekah]] — whom he never speaks to, and about whom he issues the decree; unlike Sarah in both
  earlier episodes, she is never taken
- [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] — his namesake a generation earlier, in the same town, with the
  same general, over the same well; whether they are one man, two, or one office is not answerable
  from Genesis
- [[Phicol]] — the captain of his army, silent here as in Genesis 21
- [[Ahuzzath]] — *his friend*, which is a court office; named only in this scene
- [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] — the first of the three foreign rulers who catch a patriarch out
- [[God]] — who does not speak to him, does not appear to him, and is nonetheless the reason his
  court gives for coming to Beersheba
