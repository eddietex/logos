# Rachel

## Overview

Laban's younger daughter, Jacob's second wife and his loved one, mother of Joseph and Benjamin —
and **the woman the Bible loses.** She is the only matriarch not buried at Machpelah, the only one
who dies in childbirth, and the one Jeremiah brings back six hundred years later to weep over a road
of deportees.

**She is introduced inside somebody else's sentence.** Jacob asks the shepherds whether Laban is
well; they begin to answer and break off — *"It is well. See, Rachel, his daughter, is coming with the
sheep"* (29:6). **The type-scene delivers the woman mid-clause**, exactly as Genesis 24:15 delivered
Rebekah *"before he had finished speaking."* See [[The Betrothal at the Well]].

**Her name is the ordinary Hebrew word for a ewe** — ***rāḥēl***, the noun of Isaiah 53:7, *"as a
sheep before its shearers is silent."* Genesis introduces her keeping her father's flock (*kî rōʿâ
hîʾ*, 29:9, the participle feminine), so **the book has a woman called *ewe* driving sheep**, and
makes nothing of it. Her sister's name, [[Leah]], is usually connected to Akkadian *littu*, **cow**,
though that is a reconstruction rather than a gloss the text gives.

### In this pericope — Genesis 29:1–30

**She is the object of the first love in the Bible that comes before a marriage rather than after
one.** *"Jacob loved Rachel"* (29:18) — the verb's earliest use of a wife is 24:67, where *"Isaac …
took Rebekah, and she became his wife. **He loved her**"*, in that order. **Here the order reverses**,
and it costs seven years, then a week, then seven years more. See [[Love]].

**And she has no line in the passage.** A stranger rolls a stone off a well, waters her flock, kisses
her, and weeps, and only then tells her who he is (29:10–12); she runs to her father. **Her own father
does not name her** in the sentence that agrees to the marriage — *"It is better that I give **her** to
you"* (29:19) — and a week after the wedding she was promised, she is handed over with a slave,
[[Bilhah]], attached (29:29). **Genesis records nothing she said, felt, or agreed to in thirty
verses**, and then makes her the subject of the very next clause it withholds from her: *"but Rachel
was barren"* (29:31).

*"He loved also Rachel more than Leah"* (29:30). **The comparative is the sentence the rest of her
life runs on**, and Genesis states it in the narrator's own voice rather than through a character. See
[[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]].

### What is still ahead

This page grows as those pericopes arrive; what follows is the shape of it.

She is barren while her sister bears four sons in four verses, and says the most desperate sentence
any matriarch speaks: *"Give me children, or else I will die"* (30:1) — and Jacob answers with anger,
not prayer, which is what his father did do for his mother (25:21). She gives Bilhah to him and names
the sons herself; she trades a night with her husband for mandrakes (30:14–16); and then *"God
remembered Rachel … and opened her womb"* (30:22), and she names her son **Joseph**, *may he add*, and
asks for another. She steals her father's household gods and sits on them (31:19, 34), and Jacob,
not knowing, pronounces a death sentence over whoever has them (31:32). She dies on the road to
Ephrath bearing that second son, names him **Ben Oni**, *son of my sorrow*, with her last breath, and
is overruled: *"his father called him Benjamin"* (35:18). **Jacob buries her where she falls and sets
up a pillar** — the fourth and last of his standing stones — and she is the one matriarch not carried
to Machpelah.

Her name outlives all of it. Ruth 4:11 blesses a bride *"like Rachel and like Leah, which two built
the house of Israel"* — **and names her first**, though the royal line comes through her sister. And
Jeremiah 31:15 sets her weeping at Ramah for children who *"are no more"*, which Matthew 2:18 puts
over the killing of the infants at Bethlehem. See
[[Like Rachel and Like Leah - The Builders of the House of Israel]].

## Appears in

- [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]] — Genesis 29:1–30; announced inside an answer to a different question,
  keeping her father's sheep. She is kissed before she is told who the man is, is named as a
  seven-year price, is not named by her father in the sentence that sells her, and is delivered a week
  late. **She speaks no word in the passage**, and the first thing Genesis says about her after it is
  that she was barren
