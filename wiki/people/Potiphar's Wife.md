# Potiphar's Wife

*Named by whom she appears with, because Genesis does not name her at all — the same convention that
produced [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] and [[Shua's Daughter]]. **The later tradition supplies names and
the wiki does not adopt them**; see* ***The names she was later given*** *below.*

## Overview

**She moves the entire plot of Genesis 39 and says eleven words to the man she is moving it around.**

> **7** After these things, his master’s wife set her eyes on Joseph; and she said, “Lie with me.”
> (39:7)

***Šikbâ ʿimmî*** — **two words in Hebrew, and they are the whole of her recorded speech to Joseph.**
She says them at 39:7 and again, as she takes hold of his garment, at 39:12. **Genesis gives her no
other line addressed to him across an unspecified stretch of *day by day* (39:10)** — and then gives her
two full paragraphs of speech *about* him, to two different audiences.

**She is the only woman in the Bible who propositions a man in the imperative, unveiled, in plain
terms.** The usual comparisons do not hold: **[[Lot]]'s daughters** act on a sleeping man and say nothing
to him (19:32–35); **[[Tamar]]** negotiates a price behind a veil and asks for a pledge (38:16–18); Ruth
uncovers feet at a threshing floor and asks for a redeemer. **This woman states the request to a man she
owns.** The power in the scene runs the same direction as the property — **which is the one fact almost
every retelling of the chapter inverts.**

### The accusation, and what is true in it

> **14** she called to the men of her house, and spoke to them, saying, “Behold, he has brought a Hebrew
> in to us to mock us. He came in to me to lie with me, and I cried with a loud voice.
> **15** When he heard that I lifted up my voice and cried, he left his garment by me, and ran outside.”
> **16** She laid up his garment by her, until his master came home.
> **17** She spoke to him according to these words, saying, “The Hebrew servant, whom you have brought to
> us, came in to me to mock me,
> **18** and as I lifted up my voice and cried, he left his garment by me, and ran outside.”

**Almost all of it is true.** He was in the house; they were alone; the garment came off in her hand; he
ran. **She does not fabricate an event — she reverses one.** It is precisely the family's own technique,
practised on the family: **[[Jacob]] never volunteered *I am Esau* until asked, and the brothers said only
*"we have found this"* and handed their father a question** (37:32). **A true sequence with one pronoun
changed** is the most efficient lie in Genesis, and she is the third person in six chapters to use it.
See [[Deception]].

**Four things about how she tells it.**

**She tells the household first.** *"She called to the men of her house"* (39:14), and only at 39:16
does she keep the garment *"until his master came home."* **By the time Potiphar hears the charge it is
not his wife's word against a slave's — it is a story his whole staff has already been told**, with an
exhibit. Genesis reports the order without comment, and the order is a jury empanelled before a charge
is filed.

**She routes the blame to her husband, in both versions.** To the servants: *"**he** has brought a Hebrew
in to **us**"* — an unnamed *he*, most naturally Potiphar. To Potiphar: *"the Hebrew servant, whom **you**
have brought to us."* **In both tellings she includes herself with the household** — *to us*, *mock us* —
**speaking as one of the staff to the staff, and as the injured party to the master.**

**She calls him *a Hebrew*, twice, and never by name.** ***ʿIbrî*** entered Scripture at 14:13 as a
neutral identifier. **Genesis 39:14 is the first time it is spoken with contempt**, and the contempt is
the reason for using it: it marks the accused as foreign in front of an Egyptian household. From here
the word runs straight into Exodus — the Hebrew midwives, *"one of the Hebrews' children"*, *"the God of
the Hebrews"*. See [[Abram the Hebrew - The First Use of the Name]].

***"To mock us."*** ***Ləṣaḥeq bānû*** — **and the verb is the Isaac verb.** ***Ṣāḥaq*** is *to laugh*:
Abraham laughs (17:17), Sarah laughs (18:12), **[[Isaac]] is named for it** (21:6) — and at **26:8**
Abimelech looks out of a window and sees Isaac ***məṣaḥēq*** with Rebekah, **which is how he deduces she
is his wife and not his sister.** **That verse establishes the root's sexual sense in Genesis, and this
is its next appearance.** See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]] and [[Isaac in Gerar]].

***"I cried with a loud voice."*** **Said twice, and it is the one element of her account that reads
like construction rather than reversal.** **Deuteronomy 22:23–27 makes exactly this the legal test** for
an assault alleged inside a town — if she cried out she is innocent, and if she did not the silence
convicts her too. **She supplies the decisive fact, twice, about an incident she has just told us
happened in an empty house.** The law postdates the narrative and Genesis never refers to it; **what the
wiki records is that her story is built around the question a hearer would ask.**

### What Genesis withholds about her

**Everything.** She has **no name**, **no age**, **no origin**, **no children**, and **no motive the text
states.** She is not said to be unloved, neglected, or wronged; she is not said to be wicked either.
**Genesis passes no verdict on her at all** — the narration that declines to condemn ten brothers for a
slave sale declines to condemn her too.

**She does not appear again.** Not in the prison chapters, not at Joseph's rise, not when he marries
Asenath, and not when the brothers arrive. **Genesis 39 is the whole of her**, and it never says what
Potiphar concluded, whether she was believed, or what became of her. See [[Potiphar]] for the other half
of that silence.

### The names she was later given

**She is called *Zuleika* (Zulaykhā) in the Persian and later Islamic tradition**, where Yūsuf and
Zulaykhā became one of the great romance subjects — Jami's fifteenth-century poem is the best known —
and the Qur'an's own account (Sūrat Yūsuf 12:23–35) leaves her unnamed but adds a scene Genesis does not
have: **the torn shirt examined as evidence, and torn from behind, which acquits him** (12:25–28).
**Jewish tradition sometimes identifies her with the mother of Asenath**, Joseph's wife at 41:45, which
turns the chapter into a family reconciliation; *Joseph and Aseneth*, a Hellenistic romance, develops
the material further. **The wiki records all of this as reception history and adopts none of it.**
Genesis withholds the name on purpose, and **a wiki that supplies it is writing a different chapter.**

## Timeline / key events

- Sets her eyes on Joseph and says *"Lie with me"* — Genesis 39:7, [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]]
- Speaks to him *day by day*; he does not listen — Genesis 39:10
- Catches him by his garment in the empty house; he leaves it and runs — Genesis 39:12
- Tells the household, with the garment as evidence — Genesis 39:13–15
- Keeps the garment until evening and tells her husband — Genesis 39:16–18
- Joseph is imprisoned; she is never mentioned again in Scripture — Genesis 39:19–20

## Appears in

- [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]] — Genesis 39:7–20; **the chapter's antagonist and the speaker of more
  words in it than anyone else.** Two words to Joseph, said twice; two paragraphs about him, to two
  audiences, in a deliberate order. **She reverses a real sequence rather than inventing one**, supplies
  the fact a later law would make decisive, routes the blame to the man who bought him, and calls him
  *a Hebrew*. **Genesis gives her no name, no motive, and no verdict**

## Related people

- [[Joseph]] — her husband's steward, whom she propositions and then accuses; **he speaks to her once,
  at length, and never again**
- [[Potiphar]] — her husband, who hears her account and imprisons rather than executes the accused.
  **Genesis never says whether he believed her**, and never gives him a line of dialogue
- [[Tamar]] — the other woman in two consecutive chapters who uses a man's property as testimony about
  him. **Tamar's object tells the truth and saves her life; this one's tells a lie and costs a man
  thirteen years**, and Genesis puts the two chapters back to back
- [[Lot]]'s daughters, [[Bilhah]] — the other Genesis episodes of illicit sex inside a household, both
  reported without a stated verdict
- [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]], [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] — the foreign figures the book
  usually shows behaving *better* than the patriarchs about a wife. **She is the counter-case**, and
  Genesis draws no conclusion from either pattern
