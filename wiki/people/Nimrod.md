# Nimrod

## Overview

The only person in [[The Table of Nations]] who does anything. Genesis 10 is seventy names, an
ancestry apiece, and no verbs — and then it stops for five verses on Cush's son, gives him five,
quotes a proverb about him, and resumes as though nothing had happened.

> **8** Cush became the father of Nimrod. He began to be a mighty one in the earth.
> **9** He was a mighty hunter before Yahweh. Therefore it is said, “like Nimrod, a mighty hunter
> before Yahweh”.
> **10** The beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.
> **11** Out of that land he went into Assyria, and built Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah,
> **12** and Resen between Nineveh and the great city Calah.

That is the whole of him in Genesis. He is named twice more in Scripture — once in the Chronicler's
copy of the Table (1 Chronicles 1:10) and once by a prophet (Micah 5:6) — and never again.

**He is a *gibbōr*, and the word has a history.** *Hûʾ hēḥēl lihyôt gibbōr bāʾāreṣ* — *he began to be
a mighty one in the earth*. Genesis has used *gibbōr* once before, at 6:4, of the offspring of the
sons of God: *"the mighty men who were of old, men of renown"* — the sentence immediately before God
resolves to blot out humanity. The flood was supposed to have ended that world. Four verses into the
new world's roster of peoples, the first man given a description is described with its word. Genesis
does not comment; it simply reuses the term. See
[[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]] and [[The Nephilim]].

The verb matters too. *Hēḥēl*, **he began**, is how Genesis marks the opening of an era rather than
the start of an activity: men began to call on Yahweh's name (4:26), men began to multiply (6:1),
Noah began to be a man of the ground (9:20). Nimrod begins the age of kings.

**"A mighty hunter before Yahweh" is ambiguous, and the whole reception history turns on it.**
*Lipnê YHWH* can carry at least three senses:

1. **In Yahweh's estimation** — a superlative. Hebrew uses divine reference this way (*a great city to
   God*, Jonah 3:3), so the phrase would mean *an extraordinary hunter*, and the proverb would be
   praise.
2. **In Yahweh's presence** — neutral, the sense in which Noah is blameless *before* God (6:9, 7:1).
3. **In Yahweh's face** — in defiance. Hebrew *lipnê* can carry opposition, and this is what nearly
   all ancient interpreters heard.

Genesis states none of them and gives no verdict on the man. What it reports is that a saying about
him was current — **the only proverb quoted anywhere in the primeval history** — which means his
reputation was already old when the chapter was written.

**The first kingdom in the Bible is his.** *Rēʾšît mamlaktô*, *the beginning of his kingdom*, is the
first occurrence of *mamlākâ* in Scripture. Before this verse Genesis has cities (4:17), crafts
(4:20–22), and a sentence of servitude (9:25), but no realm and no ruler. Four cities in one clause —
[[Babel]], Erech (Uruk), Accad (Akkad), and Calneh — all of them in [[Shinar]], which is to say
southern Mesopotamia, and three of the four are among the oldest cities on earth. See
[[Kingship and Empire]].

**Whether he founded the Assyrian cities depends on how 10:11 is read**, and the Hebrew genuinely
allows both. *Min-hāʾāreṣ hahîʾ yāṣāʾ ʾaššûr* is either *"out of that land he went into Assyria"*
(the WEB, following the ancient versions) — Nimrod expanding north, and so the founder of
[[Nineveh]], Rehoboth Ir, Calah, and Resen as well as of Babel — or *"out of that land went forth
Asshur"*, with Asshur the son of Shem from 10:22 as the subject, which makes it a note about Assyrian
origins and leaves Nimrod in the south. Nothing settles it. On the first reading one man stands at
the head of both empires that will destroy Israel and Judah, and Micah 5:6 seems to read it that way,
pairing *"the land of Assyria"* with *"the land of Nimrod"* as the same place. See [[Assyria]] and
[[Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria]].

**What Genesis does not say about him.** It does not say he was wicked. It does not say he built the
tower — Genesis 11:1–9 names no one at all, and Nimrod's name appears nowhere in it. It does not say
he opposed God, hunted men rather than animals, or persecuted anybody. Every one of those is later
tradition, and the tradition is old and nearly unanimous: Josephus has him turn the people against God
so they would credit their prosperity to him rather than to heaven and has him build the tower out of
that (*Antiquities* 1.113–114); *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan*, *Genesis Rabbah*, and the Talmud make him
the archetypal tyrant and Abraham's antagonist; Jerome and Augustine pass the rebel reading into
Christian exegesis; Dante puts him in the ninth circle babbling. The etymology usually offered —
*nimrōd* from *mārad*, **to rebel**, so *let us rebel* — does not work as Hebrew morphology and is
better classed with the other folk etymologies Genesis itself does supply (Peleg, Babel) than treated
as evidence. Attempts to identify him with a historical figure — Sargon of Akkad, Naram-Sin,
Tukulti-Ninurta I, the god Ninurta — each explain part of the profile and none of it.

The wiki records the reception history as reception history and keeps it separate from the five
verses, because the difference matters: **Genesis's Nimrod is a builder of cities whom a proverb
remembers, and the rebel is an inference from two ambiguous words.**

## Timeline / key events

- Born to Cush, son of [[Ham]] — Genesis 10:8, [[The Table of Nations]]
- Begins to be *gibbōr* in the earth, and becomes proverbial as a hunter — Genesis 10:8–9
- Founds the first kingdom in Scripture: Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh in Shinar — Genesis 10:10
- Builds Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah, and Resen in Assyria, on the reading the WEB takes —
  Genesis 10:11–12
- Named again only in the Chronicler's Table (1 Chronicles 1:10) and in Micah 5:6, *"the land of
  Nimrod"*

## Appears in

- [[The Table of Nations]] — the chapter's one narrative, five verses long, in the middle of a list
- [[The Tower of Babel]] — **not in it.** Genesis 11:1–9 names nobody at all, and his name is absent
  from the passage that tradition has assigned to him for two thousand years. Now that the pericope is
  ingested, the point can be checked rather than asserted: the builders are *"they"* throughout, and
  *"the children of men"* once.
