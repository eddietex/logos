# Seth

## Overview

The third son of Adam and Eve, and the one the rest of the Bible descends from. He is given no
scene, no speech, and no act; what he is given is a position. Genesis 4 has spent twenty-four
verses following the line of the murderer to its worst man, and then stops, returns to the parents,
and starts the family over: *"Adam knew his wife again. She gave birth to a son, and named him
Seth"* (4:25). The verb and the phrasing are those of 4:1, which is the narrative's way of saying
that this is the real beginning of the line.

His mother names him and explains the name in the same breath: *"for God has given me another
child instead of Abel, for Cain killed him."* Two things are packed into that sentence. The gloss
plays on *šāt*, "he has set" or "appointed" — Seth is *the appointed one*, though as usual in
Genesis the wordplay is a sound-play rather than a real etymology. And the phrase WEB renders
*another child* is Hebrew *zeraʿ ʾaḥēr*, **another seed** — the word of Genesis 3:15. Eve has now
lost both sons she had, one to a grave and one to exile, and she names the third by the vacancy he
fills. See [[The Protoevangelium - Genesis 3.15 and the Seed of the Woman]].

He fathers Enosh, in whose time *"men began to call on Yahweh's name"* (4:26) — so the first thing
recorded of Seth's descendants is worship, set directly against the boast that ends Cain's line
one verse earlier. That contrast is the architecture of the chapter, and Seth is its hinge.

Genesis 5 will give him the formal treatment: born when Adam was 130, *"in his own likeness, after
his image"* (5:3) — a phrase lifted from 1:26–27 and applied for the first time to human descent —
and dead at 912. From him the line runs Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech,
Noah, and through Noah's son Shem to Abraham, and so to everything else.

The New Testament names him once, in Luke's genealogy, running Jesus's descent back through *"Enos,
the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God"* (Luke 3:38). That is the whole of his direct
attestation, and it is enough: every promise the Bible makes after Genesis 4 travels through a man
about whom nothing is narrated.
