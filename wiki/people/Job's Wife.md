# Job's Wife

## Overview

**Four Hebrew words, once, and no name.** *"Then his wife said to him, 'Do you still maintain your
integrity? Renounce God, and die'"* (Job 2:9). She is mentioned twice more — Job says at 19:17 that
*"My breath is offensive to my wife"*, and at 31:10 he swears a self-curse involving her — and she
is absent from the epilogue, which restores Job's fortunes, gives him ten more children, and does
not say who their mother is. On that foundation sits one of the largest interpretive traditions
attached to any minor figure in Scripture, almost all of it hostile, and a Greek translation that
rewrites her entirely.

**What she actually does in the text is quote two other people.** Her question uses God's exact
phrase from six verses earlier — *maḥăzîq bətummâ*, *maintains his integrity*, which Yahweh says of
Job at 2:3 — with the pronoun turned from third person to second. **Yahweh and Job's wife are the
only speakers in the book who use it.** Her imperative uses the accuser's verb: *bārēk ʾĕlōhîm*,
literally *bless God*, the scribal euphemism for cursing him that the prologue has already used at
1:5 and that the accuser used in both of his predictions, at 1:11 and 2:5. **She is the only human
being in the book who says the words the accuser said Job would say** — and she says them to Job,
not about him, and the accuser has already left the story and will never learn that they were
spoken. See [[The Second Test]] and
[[Renounce God and Die - The Euphemism and the Accuser's Prediction]].

**She is also the only other person who lost those ten children.** Nothing else in the book records
her grief, and the wiki's reading of her turns on whether that silence is a characterisation or an
omission.

## Timeline / key events

- **Job 2:9** — her one line, in the ashes, after the sores — [[The Second Test]]
- **Job 2:10** — Job's reply: her *speech* is convicted of *nəbālâ*, moral senselessness, and she is
  not; and his answer is in the **first person plural**, *shall **we** receive*
- **Job 19:17** — *"My breath is offensive to my wife, though I entreat for the children of my own
  body"*; she is still there, and he is still asking — [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]
- **Job 31:10** — in the oath of innocence, the self-curse attached to adultery is that his wife
  should grind for another and others stoop over her: the standard form of such an oath, and a
  reminder that she is assumed present throughout
- **Job 42:10–17** — absent by name from the restoration, which gives Job seven sons and three
  daughters and says nothing about their mother

## Appears in

- [[The Second Test]] — Job 2:1–10. Her whole speaking part, and its two quotations. **She arrives
  in the narrative at exactly the moment the accuser leaves it**, and says, in his vocabulary, what
  he twice predicted. Job answers the sentence and not the woman.

## Related people

- [[Job (person)]] — who does not call her foolish. *Kədabbēr ʾaḥat hannəbālôt*, *you speak as one
  of the foolish women would speak*, puts the weight on the utterance; **the Hebrew rebukes a class
  of speech and pointedly does not assign her to a class of person.** He then answers her in the
  plural, which is either courtesy or a shared grief and reads better as the second.
- [[Job's Children]] — hers as much as his, and the loss the book never lets her speak about.
- [[Satan]] — whose two predictions she voices without knowing he made them, and who is gone by the
  time she does. **If she is his instrument, she is one he never arranged, never sees, and never
  gets credit for** — the text gives him no scene with her and no knowledge of her.
- [[Eve]] — the parallel the tradition has always reached for: a woman's word standing between a man
  and God. The differences are large and are set out at
  [[Renounce God and Die - The Euphemism and the Accuser's Prediction]]; the shortest of them is
  that **Adam listens and Job does not.**

## Themes associated

- [[Integrity]] — *tummâ*, the word she uses as an objection and God uses as praise; the only thing
  Job has left, and the thing she asks him to drop.
- [[Marriage]] — the only conversation between husband and wife in the book, and the bleakest in
  Scripture.
- [[Suffering]] — hers is real and unnarrated; the book gives her no lament, no friends, and no
  seven days of silence.
- [[Language and Speech]] — a sentence built entirely of other people's words, and a reply that
  convicts the sentence rather than the speaker.
- [[Life and Death]] — *wāmūt*, *and die*. **She offers the one exit the accuser was forbidden to
  take at 2:6**, and the text does not say whether she knows it.
- [[Testing]] — read one way, she is part of the instrument; read another, she is collateral of it.

## Notes

- **The hostile tradition, and how old it is.** Augustine calls her ***diaboli adiutrix***, the
  devil's assistant (*De Civitate Dei* and elsewhere in the sermons); Chrysostom argues the accuser
  spared her deliberately, as the weapon he had left; Calvin calls her an *organum Satanae*, an
  instrument of Satan. The Latin and English exegetical tradition runs this way almost without
  interruption until the twentieth century, and it gets there in one step from the observation that
  her imperative is the accuser's prediction. **The step is real and the text invites it.** What the
  tradition adds, and the text does not, is a motive.

- **The Septuagint gives her a speech, and it changes everything.** Where the Hebrew has one line,
  the Greek has six verses. She describes her own ruin — wandering *"from place to place, and from
  house to house, waiting for the sun to set, that I may rest from the labours and griefs that now
  beset me"* — and names the children as *"the fruits of my womb, for whom I toiled with hardships
  in vain."* Only then does she say *"say some word against the Lord, and die."* **In the Greek she
  is a second victim making an exhausted request; in the Hebrew she is a voice with no history.**
  The expansion is almost certainly a translator's attempt to supply the motive the Hebrew omits,
  which is evidence that the omission was felt as a difficulty very early.

- **The *Testament of Job* names her Sitis**, makes her sell her hair to a bread-seller to feed Job,
  and has her die before the restoration — the source of the tradition that Job's later children
  were borne by a second wife. It is a first-century-ish pseudepigraphon and not evidence for the
  book, but it shows the same instinct as the Septuagint: **the Hebrew's silence about her is
  unbearable to readers, and every tradition fills it.**

- **The sympathetic reading, stated as strongly as it can be.** She has buried ten children in one
  afternoon, lost the household that was her life's work, and is now watching her husband rot on an
  ash-heap with a disease Deuteronomy 28 says cannot be healed. His *tummâ* is not saving him; it is
  the thing keeping him conscious inside it. On this reading *"renounce God, and die"* is not
  temptation but **mercy offered in the only currency available** — a wife telling a man in
  unrelievable agony that he is allowed to stop. It also explains the form of her sentence: she
  begins by asking whether he *still* holds on, which is the question of someone who has been
  watching, not the opening of a seduction.

- **What decides between the readings, and why the wiki does not.** The hostile reading has the
  vocabulary on its side: *bārēk* is the accuser's word, twice predicted and once delivered, and
  that is not a coincidence a careful author would produce accidentally. The sympathetic reading has
  the narrative on its side: she is never named as an agent, never appears with the accuser, and is
  never rebuked by God, **who at 42:7 rebukes the friends by name for exactly the kind of speech she
  is accused of and says nothing about her at all.** The wiki records both and adjudicates neither,
  and notes that the book's own habit — see [[The Second Test]] on *"you incited me against him"* —
  is to leave attributions of agency standing side by side without reconciling them.

- **The restoration's silence.** Job 42:13 gives him *"seven sons and three daughters"* again, and
  names the daughters — Jemimah, Keziah, Keren Happuch — in the book's only list of women's names.
  It does not name or mention their mother. **A book that is otherwise meticulous about doubling
  what was lost does not say whether she was there for it**, and the reader is left with 19:17 as
  the last thing the text records about the marriage.
