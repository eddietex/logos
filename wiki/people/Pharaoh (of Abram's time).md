# Pharaoh (of Abram's time)

## Overview

**A note on the name.** He has none. *Parʿōh* is not a name but a title — Egyptian *pr-ʿȝ*, *the
great house*, the palace used for its occupant, exactly as *the Crown* or *the Porte* is used — and
Genesis and Exodus never supply a personal name for any of the Pharaohs they narrate, though the
later historical books name Shishak, Neco, and Hophra without difficulty. This wiki disambiguates
the unnamed ones by the patriarch they meet, so this page is **Pharaoh (of Abram's time)** and the
Pharaoh of Joseph and the Pharaoh of the oppression will take their own pages when ingestion reaches
them.

### In this pericope — Genesis 12:14–20

He is told a woman is a man's sister, so he takes her; he is struck for it; he works out why; and he
gives her back.

His courtiers see Sarai, *"praised her to Pharaoh; and the woman was taken into Pharaoh's house"*
(12:15). That is a court procedure, not a seizure — the *śārîm*, the princes, do the seeing and the
recommending, and the acquisition is formal enough to carry a settlement with it: *"He dealt well
with Abram for her sake"* (12:16), a bride-price in sheep, cattle, donkeys, servants, and camels.
On the information he has been given, none of this is wrong. Genesis is careful to let him act
correctly on a false premise supplied by the man God chose.

**Then Yahweh afflicts him**, *"and his house, with great plagues because of Sarai, Abram's wife"*
(12:17) — struck for a marriage he was misled into and had no way of questioning. Genesis does not
say what the plagues were, and it does not say how he learned the cause; between 12:17 and 12:18
there is a gap the text simply steps over, where the parallel story at Genesis 20 puts a dream and a
divine speech.

**His three questions are the only moral speech in the passage.** *"What is this that you have done
to me? Why didn't you tell me that she was your wife? Why did you say, 'She is my sister,' so that I
took her to be my wife?"* (12:18–19). They are in the form Genesis has so far given only to God —
*"Where are you?"*, *"What have you done?"* — and they go unanswered. The man questioned says
nothing at all.

**And he gives everything back and asks for nothing.** *"Now therefore, see your wife, take her, and
go your way"* (12:19). He does not reclaim the livestock, does not punish, and does not detain;
12:20 has him detail an escort and put the household out of the country, and 13:2 has Abram leaving
*"very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold"*. A man who was lied to, plagued for it, and left
out of pocket is the nearest thing this pericope has to an injured party.

### What he is for, in the book

Two things, and the second is why he matters more than his eleven verses suggest.

**He is the first outsider to out-behave a patriarch** — a role Genesis will hand next to Abimelech
of Gerar (20:1–18) and then to Abimelech again with Isaac (26:6–11), and the pattern is consistent
enough to be deliberate: the foreign king is the one who talks about sin, integrity, and what ought
not to be done, and the chosen family is the one caught out. See [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].

**And he is the Exodus in miniature.** A Pharaoh whose house is struck with plagues *because of*
somebody Yahweh has claimed, who then sends the household out of Egypt with an escort and with the
country's wealth in their baggage, is the shape of Exodus 1–14 in eleven verses, four hundred years
early. See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

**Which dynasty he would be** is not answerable from the text and probably not meant to be. Readings
that place Abraham in the Middle Bronze Age put the episode in the Middle Kingdom or the Second
Intermediate Period; the Hyksos identification is a favourite of harmonizing chronologies and has no
support in Genesis, which gives no date, no dynasty, no capital, and no name.
