# Eliezer of Damascus

## Overview

Named once in Scripture, in the sentence in which he is disinherited.

*"Lord Yahweh, what will you give me, since I go childless, and he who will inherit my estate is
Eliezer of Damascus?"* (Genesis 15:2). He is Abram's steward, and in the absence of a son he is
Abram's heir. Two verses later Yahweh removes him: *"This man will not be your heir, but he who will
come out of your own body will be your heir"* (15:4). He never speaks, never acts, and is never
mentioned again.

**His name is a sentence, and the chapter makes nothing of it.** *ʾĔlîʿezer* is *my God is help* —
the same name Moses gives his second son, *"for the God of my father was my help"* (Exodus 18:4).
Genesis has just used *ʾĒl* four times in the Melchizedek scene and is about to spend a chapter on
what God gives; the one man in it whose name means *God helps* is the one the promise sets aside.

**The Hebrew of 15:2 is among the hardest in Genesis.** *Ben-mešeq bêtî hûʾ Dammeśeq ʾĔlîʿezer* —
roughly *and the son of possession(?) of my house is Damascus Eliezer*. The word *mešeq* occurs
nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, and the sound-play between it and *Dammeśeq* is either the verse's
wit or evidence that something in it has gone wrong in transmission. Ancient versions differ; the
WEB, like most, translates for sense — *he who will inherit my estate is Eliezer of Damascus* — and
leaves the play untranslated. What is not in doubt is the situation: a childless man's senior
servant stands to inherit.

**The institution behind it is ordinary.** A household head without a son adopts a dependent — often
a trusted servant — who cares for him in life, buries him, and takes the estate. The practice is
widely attested in second-millennium Mesopotamian law and family archives, and mid-twentieth-century
scholarship leaned hard on the Nuzi tablets to make Genesis 15:2 a precise legal parallel. That
particular argument has since been substantially walked back — the Nuzi documents turn out to be
doing something narrower — but the general custom is well enough attested that the verse needs no
special explanation. Abram is describing the arrangement any man in his position would have made.
Genesis 24:2 shows the same servant class still running his household: *"his servant, the elder of
his house, who ruled over all that he had."*

**Whether he is the servant of Genesis 24 is an old and unresolvable question.** The chapter that
sends a servant to Mesopotamia to find Isaac a wife — the longest chapter in Genesis, and the one
that gives its servant more dialogue and more interior life than almost any minor figure in the
book — never names him. Jewish tradition identifies him with Eliezer as a matter of course, and the
identification is reasonable on the arithmetic and on the description. It is also unsupported: the
narrator withholds the name across sixty-seven verses in which he had every opportunity to give it,
which reads more like a deliberate choice than an oversight. The wiki records the tradition and does
not adopt it.

**He is from [[Damascus]]**, which is the city Abram chased an army past one chapter earlier
(14:15). The two mentions of Damascus in Genesis are the pursuit and this man, four verses of
narrative apart.
