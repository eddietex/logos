# Lot

## Overview

An orphan attached to his grandfather's household, and — until the promise supplies a real one — the
nearest thing Abram has to an heir.

In this pericope he is introduced by his father's death. *"Haran became the father of Lot. Haran
died before his father Terah in the land of his birth, in Ur of the Chaldees"* (Genesis 11:27–28).
Two verses later he is on the travel list: *"Terah took Abram his son, **Lot the son of Haran, his
son's son**, and Sarai his daughter-in-law … They went from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land
of Canaan"* (11:31). Note how carefully the verse places him — *the son of Haran, his son's son* —
which is Genesis explaining, in an aside, why a grandson is travelling in a party that otherwise
consists of a man, his son, and his son's wife. He is there because his father is dead.

**He is the third member of the household and the only child in it.** Terah's party is a father, one
son and that son's barren wife, and a fatherless grandson. The arithmetic of a family without an
heir is set up in a single verse, and Genesis 15:2–3 will say it out loud when Abram complains that
*"one born in my house is my heir"*.

### In the next pericope — Genesis 12:1–9

*"Lot went with him"* (12:4), and then again in the inventory of 12:5. Four words, and they are a
small problem the text does not acknowledge: the call Abram has just obeyed said *"leave your
country, and your relatives, and your father's house"*, and the first thing he does is bring a
member of his father's house along. Genesis offers no comment, no permission, and no rebuke. Lot is
simply there, unbidden and unpromised — nothing in 12:2–3 is addressed to him or includes him.

That unexplained presence is what the next seven chapters are made of: the herdsmen's quarrel and
the separation (13), the war and the rescue (14), and Sodom (19). And it has a second function
immediately — a childless man of seventy-five who has just been promised *"a great nation"* is
travelling with the only young male relative he has. See [[The Call of Abram]].

### What his presence sets up

Everything that follows from Genesis 12 to 19 depends on his being here. He goes out of Haran with
Abram (12:4–5) and into Egypt and back; the two households grow until *"the land was not able to
bear them"* and they separate, Lot choosing the well-watered plain of the Jordan and pitching his
tent toward Sodom (13:5–13); he is captured in the war of the kings and Abram arms his household to
recover him (14:12–16); and he is dragged out of Sodom by angels on the morning of its destruction
(19:1–29), which the New Testament remembers twice — *"righteous Lot, very distressed by the lustful
life of the wicked"* (2 Peter 2:7), and *"Remember Lot's wife"* (Luke 17:32). He ends in a cave with
his two daughters, fathering Moab and Ammon (19:30–38), which makes him the ancestor of two of
Israel's nearest and most difficult neighbours — and, through Ruth the Moabitess, of David.

None of that is visible in Genesis 11. What Genesis 11 gives him is a dead father and a place in a
caravan.
