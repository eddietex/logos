# Esau

## Overview

Isaac's firstborn by minutes, the ancestor of Edom, and **the only person in his own birth narrative
whose name Genesis refuses to explain.**

**Two etymologies are given in the verse that names him, and neither is for *Esau*.** *"The first
came out red all over, like a hairy garment. They named him Esau"* (25:25). ***ʾAdmônî***, **red**,
seeds ***ʾĔdôm*** — the nation — which 25:30 then attaches to a bowl of red stew instead. ***Śēʿār***,
**hair**, seeds ***Śēʿîr*** — Seir, the mountain country he settles (32:3; 36:8). **Genesis derives
the people and the territory from the description of the baby and gives no derivation at all for the
man.** In a book that supplies an etymology for nearly every significant name it introduces, the
omission is conspicuous, and no proposed Hebrew derivation of *ʿĒśāw* has ever been accepted.

**He is the last of the book's passed-over elder brothers and the only one who is judged for it.**
Ishmael is sent away and blessed; Cain is protected; Esau is told before birth that he will serve his
younger brother, and then given the one evaluative sentence in the chapter: *"So Esau despised his
birthright"* (25:34). **The rest of the Bible follows that verdict** — Hebrews 12:16 calls him *"a
profane person"*, and Malachi 1:2–3 says *"Esau I hated"*, of the nation, centuries later. See
[[Esau's Birthright in Hebrews]] and
[[The Elder Will Serve the Younger - Genesis 25.23 in Malachi and Romans]].

**And Genesis is not finished with him.** The man who despises his birthright at 25:34 is the man who
at 33:4 *"ran to meet him, embraced him, fell on his neck, kissed him, and they wept"* — **the most
generous act by anyone in the Jacob cycle**, performed by the brother who had been cheated twice.
Deuteronomy 23:7 forbids Israel to abhor an Edomite *"for he is your brother."* The book's own
portrait is harder than the tradition's.

### In this pericope — Genesis 25:19–34

**He is characterised in twelve words and undone in six verses.**

***ʾÎš yōdēaʿ ṣayid, ʾîš śādeh*** — *"a skillful hunter, a man of the field"* (25:27). Competence, and
the outdoors; **neither is a criticism**, and Genesis has approved of a hunter before, after a
fashion, in [[Nimrod]] at 10:9. His father loves him, and the narrator gives the reason in a
subordinate clause that is faintly absurd: *"because he ate his venison"* (25:28). **A taste for game
is the only motive Genesis ever supplies for Isaac's preference**, and it is the same taste chapter 27
runs on.

**He asks for food in the syntax of an animal.** *"Please feed me with that same red stew"* —
***halʿîṭēnî nāʾ min-hā-ʾādōm hā-ʾādōm hazzeh***. The verb ***lāʿaṭ*** occurs **only here in the
Hebrew Bible** and its cognates are used of feeding beasts; and the object is ***hā-ʾādōm hā-ʾādōm***,
literally *this red, red* — an adjective doubled, with **no noun attached at all**. He does not say
what he wants and does not appear to know. And what he is given, after the sale, turns out to be
lentils — brown.

***"Behold, I am about to die. What good is the birthright to me?"*** (25:32). **He is not about to
die**, and there is bread in the house — 25:34 says so. Two readings, both available and neither
adopted by this wiki: hyperbole from a famished man, which makes the trade short-sighted; or a real
argument from a hunter's life expectancy, which makes it a considered judgment that an inheritance is
worth nothing to a dead man. **The narrator's own verdict — *he despised it* — fits the second
better.**

**He swears, eats, drinks, rises, and goes.** Four finite verbs in a row after the oath, with no
reaction reported, no regret, and no second thought. **The transaction of his life takes him less
time than the negotiation did.**

### What is still ahead

This page grows as those pericopes arrive; what follows is the shape of it, not a substitute for it.

He marries two Hittite women at forty, and *"They grieved Isaac's and Rebekah's spirits"*
(26:34–35). He comes in from the hunt to find the blessing gone and cries *"with an exceeding great
and bitter cry"* — *"Bless me, even me also, my father."* — and Isaac has nothing left to give him
but a hard prophecy and a yoke he will one day shake off (27:30–40). He plans to kill Jacob after the
mourning for their father (27:41), which is why Rebekah sends him away. He then marries Ishmael's daughter
Mahalath, joining the two non-covenant lines (28:9). Twenty years later he comes to meet Jacob with
four hundred men and embraces him instead (33:1–4), refuses the gift and then takes it, and goes back
to Seir. **He takes his household away from his brother because the land could not hold both of
them** (36:6–8) — the fourth and last time Genesis clears a brother off the stage — and his *toledot*
at chapter 36 gives Edom its chiefs and its kings, *"before any king reigned over the children of
Israel."*

## Related people

  the taker of his blessing, and the man he embraces at the Jabbok's far side
- [[Isaac]] — his father, who loves him for his hunting and blesses him second and with nothing left
- [[Rebekah]] — his mother, who receives the oracle against him and engineers the blessing away from

  Mahalath at 28:9, joining the two non-covenant lines
- [[Abraham]] — his grandfather, alive until he was fifteen on the book's own arithmetic
- [[God]] — who ranks him below his brother before he is born, and does not speak to him in Genesis
