# The Salt Sea

## Overview

The lowest body of water on the surface of the earth, and the place Genesis says a battle was fought
in a valley.

*"All these joined together in the valley of Siddim (also called the Salt Sea)"* (Genesis 14:3). The
gloss is the interesting part: the narrator names the battlefield by an old name and then translates
it for readers who know the site as a sea. Whether the parenthesis means the valley *lay where the
sea now is*, or simply *beside it*, is not settled by the Hebrew, and both readings have been used —
one to argue that the southern basin was dry land in Abram's day and flooded later, the other to
treat the gloss as ordinary geographical shorthand. See [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]].

***Siddim*** is of uncertain meaning; *fields* or *furrows* is the usual guess, from *sādeh*, and
nothing depends on it.

**The battle is lost in the ground.** *"Now the valley of Siddim was full of tar pits; and the kings
of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and some fell there, and those who remained fled to the hills"* (14:10).
The Hebrew doubles the noun for emphasis — *beʾĕrōt beʾĕrōt ḥēmār*, *pits, pits of bitumen* — and
the detail is exactly right for the place. The Dead Sea rift seeps asphalt; slabs of it floated to
the surface after earthquakes and were harvested and traded out of the region for millennia, which
is why classical writers called it *Lacus Asphaltites*. Genesis has used *ḥēmār* once before, as the
mortar of Babel: *"They had brick for stone, and they used tar for mortar"* (11:3). The same
substance builds the tower and swallows the army.

**The geology is the chapter's silent argument.** The basin sits on the Dead Sea Transform, one of
the most seismically active places on land, roughly 430 metres below sea level, with no outlet and
an evaporation rate that leaves the water some ten times saltier than the ocean. At its southern
end are the salt domes of Mount Sodom. It is a region where the ground genuinely does burn, subside,
and salt over, and Genesis sets its two stories of catastrophic loss — the four kings' victory here
and the destruction of the cities in chapter 19 — in the same depression.

**Later names.** The Hebrew Bible calls it *yām ha-melaḥ*, the Salt Sea (Numbers 34:3, 12; Joshua
15:2, 5; 18:19), *the sea of the Arabah* (Deuteronomy 3:17; Joshua 3:16), and *the eastern sea*
(Ezekiel 47:18; Joel 2:20; Zechariah 14:8). *Dead Sea* is Greek and later. Ezekiel 47:8–10 has the
river from the temple running down into it and healing it, so that fishermen stand along its shore —
an image that depends entirely on the reader knowing nothing lives in it.
