# Ai

## Overview

### The Gibeonite deception — Joshua 9:1–27

The city’s defeat joins Jericho’s as the immediate news prompting Gibeon’s ruse (3). Israel’s
successful stratagem in chapter 8 is followed by its susceptibility to another people’s deception.

See [[The Gibeonite Deception]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 9:1–27; comparisons as cited.


### The capture of Ai — Joshua 8:1–29

Now ingested. Yahweh promises *the king of Ai, with his people, his city, and his land* (1) and
orders an ambush behind the city (2). It is hidden west of Ai, between Ai and Bethel (9, 12); the
main army camps to the north across a valley (11). Every man of Ai and Bethel pursues the feigned
flight and leaves the city *open* (17). The ambush enters and fires it; twelve thousand, men and
women, die (25). Joshua makes it *a heap forever, even a desolation, to this day* (*tēl ʿôlām
šǝmāmâ*, WLC 8:28), the phrase of Deuteronomy 13:16 — the name *the ruin* and the verdict coinciding.
The king's body lies under a heap of stones at the gate (29). The narrative's numbers (thirty
thousand at 8:3, five thousand at 8:12, twelve thousand dead) sit awkwardly with the spies' *only a
few* (7:3); see [[The Capture of Ai]]. The archaeological problem below stands unchanged.

See [[The Capture of Ai]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 8:1–29; WLC (morphhb) where Hebrew is named.

### The sin of Achan — Joshua 7:1–26

Now ingested. Spies sent from Jericho judge Ai lightly defended; about three thousand men attack,
flee, and lose about thirty-six, chased from the gate to Shebarim (2–5). The hearts of the people
melted, and became like water — the idiom used of Canaan's fear in 2:11 and 5:1 now turns on Israel.
Ai is located beside Beth Aven, east of Bethel (2). The cause is Achan's theft, and the campaign
resumes only after the valley of Achor (8:1). The archaeological problem below is not addressed by
the narrative.

See [[The Sin of Achan]]. Source: stored WEB Joshua 7:1–26; WLC (morphhb) where Hebrew is named.

A small town east of [[Bethel]], mentioned in Genesis only to fix Abram's position — and later the
site of the only defeat Israel suffers in the conquest.

*"He left from there to go to the mountain on the east of Bethel and pitched his tent, having Bethel
on the west, and Ai on the east"* (Genesis 12:8). The verse is doing surveying, not storytelling:
Abram is on the ridge, with a town on either hand. Ai has no part in the pericope beyond being the
eastern marker.

**The name means "the ruin".** *Hāʿay*, always with the article — *the* Ai — from a root for a heap
of stones. A town whose name is *the ruin* is either named after an older mound it was built beside
or named retrospectively for what it became, and Joshua 8:28 makes the second reading hard to avoid:
*"Joshua burned Ai and made it a heap forever, even a desolation, to this day."*

**The archaeological problem is real and belongs on this page.** The traditional identification is
et-Tell, a mound just east of Beitin. Excavation there (Judith Marquet-Krause in the 1930s, Joseph
Callaway in the 1960s–70s) found a substantial Early Bronze city destroyed around 2400 BC, then an
abandonment of roughly a thousand years, then a small unwalled Iron Age I village — meaning no
fortified city stood at et-Tell at any plausible date for Joshua. Four responses circulate, and none
is clearly better than the others: that Ai is a different site nearby (Khirbet el-Maqatir has been
argued at length); that the Joshua 7–8 narrative attached itself to a spectacular pre-existing ruin
whose name already said what it was; that the identification of Bethel with Beitin is itself wrong,
which moves Ai with it; or that the conquest chronology needs revising. It is one of the sharper
points of friction between the biblical narrative and the excavated record, and this wiki's habit is
to state it rather than resolve it.

### Joshua 7–8

Ai's real weight in Scripture is a story of failure. After Jericho, Israel sends a small force
because Ai is small, and is routed — *"the hearts of the people melted, and became like water"*
(Joshua 7:5). The cause turns out to be Achan, who had taken devoted things from Jericho, and the
campaign does not resume until he is dealt with. The second assault succeeds by ambush, the city is
burned, its king hanged on a tree until evening (8:29), and immediately afterward Joshua builds an
altar on Mount Ebal and reads the blessings and the curses at [[Shechem (city)]] (8:30–35).

So the two places Abram stops between in Genesis 12 are, in Joshua, the site of the conquest's one
disaster and the site of its covenant renewal — and the order runs the same way both times.
