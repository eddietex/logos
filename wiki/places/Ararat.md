# Ararat

## Overview

Where the ark comes to rest, and the first place in Genesis that is somewhere on a map.

> **4** The ship rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, on Ararat’s
> mountains. — Genesis 8:4

The Hebrew is *hārê ʾărārāṭ*, **the mountains of Ararat** — plural, and a region rather than a
summit. Genesis does not name a mountain, and every English rendering that suggests it does is
adding something. What the verse gives is a mountain range, in a country, and the effect is
deliberate: after three consecutive pericopes in which Genesis names no location at all because the
arena is the whole flooded earth, one piece of the world comes back into view solid enough to be
called by name. See [[The Waters Recede]].

**Ararat is Urartu**, and it is a real kingdom with an external history. Assyrian royal inscriptions
name it from the thirteenth century BC onward as a rival power in the mountains north of Assyria,
centred on Lake Van in what is now eastern Turkey, with territory reaching into Armenia and
north-western Iran. It fought Assyria for three centuries, left its own inscriptions in a language
related to Hurrian, and fell in the early sixth century. The Hebrew consonants are the Assyrian name
with vowels supplied.

The rest of the Bible treats it as an ordinary geopolitical fact rather than a sacred site. When
Sennacherib is murdered by his sons, they *"escaped into the land of Ararat"* (2 Kings 19:37;
Isaiah 37:38) — the obvious refuge, being the great power on Assyria's northern frontier. When
Jeremiah summons the nations that will bring Babylon down, Ararat heads the list with Minni and
Ashkenaz (Jeremiah 51:27). Nobody in Scripture goes looking for the ark there, or anywhere.

**The single peak is a later idea.** The identification of Ağrı Dağı — the 5,137-metre volcano in
far eastern Turkey now called Mount Ararat in English — as *the* mountain of Genesis 8:4 is medieval
and European; the local Armenian and Turkish names for it are unrelated, and earlier traditions put
the landing elsewhere in the same broad region, including Jabal Judi in the Cudi range further south,
which is where the Qur'an places it (Hud 11:44) and where Syriac Christian tradition placed it for
centuries. Genesis itself is not in the business of locating it more precisely than a country.

**Why the location is the right one for the story.** The ark grounds high, in mountains at the far
north of the world Genesis's first readers knew, and everything that follows moves outward and
downward from there — the table of nations in Genesis 10, then Shinar and the tower on the southern
plain in Genesis 11, both still to be ingested. Humanity restarts on a mountain range at the edge of
the map and spreads from it. Mesopotamian tradition put its own flood mountain, Nimush, in the Zagros to the east; both
stories agree that the world begins again on high ground.

## Events here

- The ark comes to rest, in the seventh month on the seventeenth day, a hundred and fifty days after
  the flood began (8:4) — [[The Waters Recede]]
- The mountaintops become visible in the tenth month (8:5), and the earth is dry on the
  twenty-seventh of the second month of the following year (8:14)
- Noah, his family, and every creature disembark; Noah builds the first altar in Scripture and offers
  burnt offerings (8:15–20)
