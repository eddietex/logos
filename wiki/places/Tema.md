# Tema

## Overview

A great oasis of north-west Arabia, modern Tayma in Saudi Arabia, about 400 kilometres south-east of
the head of the Gulf of Aqaba. It sat on the incense road that carried frankincense and myrrh from
south Arabia — from Sheba — north to Edom, Gaza and Damascus, and its wells made it one of the road's
indispensable stops. Genesis names it as a son of Ishmael (25:15), the usual biblical pattern of a
people and their territory sharing an ancestor's name. **No separate person page is kept for Tema
son of Ishmael**: like Teman son of Eliphaz, he is a name in a list with no act and no word, and the
place is what the rest of Scripture refers to.

**Not to be confused with [[Teman]]**, the Edomite town of Eliphaz. The two names differ by one
letter in English and in Hebrew (*Têmāʾ*, *Têmān*), descend from different ancestors — Ishmael and
Esau — and lie in different countries. Both turn up in Job, five chapters apart.

Its one appearance in Job is as an image of disappointment. Job compares his friends to a wadi that
runs full in the wet season and vanishes in the heat: *"The caravans of Tema looked. The companies of
Sheba waited for them. They were distressed because they were confident. They came there, and were
confounded"* (6:19–20). The poet picks the two ends of the incense road — Tema in the north, Sheba
in the south — for caravans that leave the track for promised water and die. The image assumes an
audience that knows the desert trade, which fits the book's setting among *"the children of the
east"* (1:3).

Outside Job, Tema is the place whose people are told to bring water and bread to fugitives from
battle (Isaiah 21:14) — the opposite of the dry wadi — and one of the distant peoples who must drink
the cup of God's wrath in Jeremiah 25:23, listed as *"Dedan, Tema, Buz"*. Buz is the clan of Elihu,
*"the son of Barachel the Buzite"* (Job 32:2), so the book of Job's minor names keep returning to
the same corner of Arabia. Outside Scripture, Tayma is where the last Babylonian king, Nabonidus,
spent about ten years of his reign in the sixth century BC, leaving Belshazzar as regent in Babylon.
