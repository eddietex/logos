# Assyria

## Overview

Upper Mesopotamia, along the Tigris — and in [[The Table of Nations]] both a country and a man, which
is the difficulty the chapter's most disputed verse turns on.

> **11** Out of that land he went into Assyria, and built Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah,
> **12** and Resen between Nineveh and the great city Calah.

> **22** The sons of Shem were: Elam, Asshur, Arpachshad, Lud, and Aram.

*ʾAššûr* is one word doing three jobs in the ancient world: the god, the city on the Tigris that was
the country's first capital, and the country itself. Genesis uses it as a person at 10:22 and as a
territory at 10:11, and Hebrew does not mark the difference.

**Who founds the Assyrian cities is genuinely unsettled.** *Min-hāʾāreṣ hahîʾ yāṣāʾ ʾaššûr* can be
read two ways, and both are grammatical:

- ***"Out of that land he went into Assyria"*** — the WEB's rendering, and the one the Septuagint,
  Vulgate, and Targums support. [[Nimrod]] is the subject carried over from 10:8–10, and he expands
  north from [[Shinar]], founding [[Nineveh]], Rehoboth Ir, Calah, and Resen as well as Babel, Erech,
  and Accad. One man then stands at the head of both empires that will destroy Israel and Judah.
- ***"Out of that land went forth Asshur"*** — the KJV's rendering. Asshur, Shem's son from 10:22, is
  the subject, and the verse is a note about where Assyrians came from: a Semitic migration up the
  Tigris out of the Babylonian plain.

Nothing in the Hebrew decides it. Micah 5:6 puts *"the land of Assyria"* and *"the land of Nimrod"* in
parallel as designations of the same place, which reads most naturally on the first option, and it is
the only verse in Scripture that connects them. See
[[Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria]].

**What Assyria becomes.** The empire that destroys the northern kingdom and deports it (2 Kings
17:5–23), besieges Jerusalem under Sennacherib and fails (2 Kings 18–19; Isaiah 36–37), and is called
by Isaiah *"the rod of my anger"* — a tool God uses and then breaks for the arrogance of thinking
itself the agent (Isaiah 10:5–19). Nahum's three chapters are its funeral. Jonah is sent to its
capital and resents being sent. And Isaiah 19:23–25 ends with the most unexpected sentence about it in
the Bible: *"Blessed be Egypt my people, Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance."*
