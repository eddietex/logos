# Succoth

## Overview

**Named for the shelters Jacob builds there the day he arrives — a naming for what has just
happened, in the register this wiki has tracked since Beersheba and Peniel.**

*"Jacob traveled to Succoth, built himself a house, and made shelters for his livestock. Therefore
the name of the place is called Succoth"* (Genesis 33:17). ***Sukkōt*** is the plural of ***sukkâ***,
a temporary shelter or booth — the same word later given to the autumn pilgrimage festival,
*Sukkot*, the Feast of Booths (Leviticus 23:34).

**Where it is.** East of the Jordan, in the plain near the Jabbok's confluence with the river,
usually identified with Tell Deir ʿAlla. It is on the direct route from the Jabbok ford of
[[Wrestling at the Jabbok]] toward Shechem, and short of both Seir (where Esau is headed at the same
verse) and the Jordan crossing into Canaan proper — **the first stop after the reconciliation, and
not yet in the land the promise is about.**

**A name Genesis will use twice for two different places.** *Sukkōt* recurs at Exodus 12:37 as the
Israelites' first camp after leaving Rameses in Egypt — a different site entirely, on the opposite
side of the map from this one, sharing nothing with it but the Hebrew word for a booth. **The two
are not the same place**, and when Exodus is ingested this page and that site will need the
schema's disambiguating qualifiers (most likely `(Transjordan)` and `(Egypt)`, or a place-kind
qualifier if a clearer one presents itself) — the same mechanical rule the schema already applies to
`Mamre (place)` and `Enoch (city)`, flagged here in advance so the collision is not silent when it
arrives.

**1 Kings 7:46 later locates it precisely**: Solomon's bronze foundry works *"in the plain of the
Jordan… between Succoth and Zarethan"* — the same Transjordan site, still known by this name
centuries later. Judges 8:5–9, 14–16 gives it its other appearance: Gideon, pursuing the kings of
Midian, asks the town for bread for his exhausted men and is refused; he returns and *"taught the
men of Succoth"* with thorns and briers, the harshest reprisal any Israelite town suffers from
another in the book. [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] already cross-references the refusal as
*"the inverse"* of Melchizedek's unasked hospitality to Abram.
