# Beer Lahai Roi

## Overview

A well in the desert south of Canaan, on the road toward Egypt, named by a runaway Egyptian slave for
the God who found her there — **the only place in the Bible named by a woman**, and the only one whose
name preserves a name she gave God.

*Bəʾēr laḥay rōʾî* is usually rendered ***the well of the Living One who sees me***, taking *ḥay* as
the divine epithet *the Living One* and *rōʾî* as the participle *the one who sees* with a
first-person suffix. The alternative division — *the well of the living one who saw me*, or *the
well of the living after seeing* — trades on the same ambiguity that makes 16:13b hard, and the name
is at least as uncertain as the sentence it comes from. What is not uncertain is which root it is
built on: *rāʾâ*, to see, three times in two verses.

The narrator locates it *"between Kadesh and Bered"* (16:14) — Kadesh being the great oasis of the
northern Sinai, already named at 14:7 as *En Mishpat*, and **Bered** occurring nowhere else in the
Bible and never identified. The best guesses put the well somewhere in the desert west or southwest of
Beersheba; nothing is secure.

**What the wiki finds most worth saying about it is its afterlife.** Genesis mentions the place three
times, and the two later mentions have nothing to do with Hagar:

- *"Isaac came from the way of Beer Lahai Roi, for he lived in the land of the South"* (24:62) — he is
  coming from it when he first sees Rebekah crossing the field.
- *"After the death of Abraham, God blessed Isaac, his son. Isaac lived by Beer Lahai Roi"* (25:11).

**The son of the promise makes his home at the well named by the slave who was sent away.** Genesis
records this without a word of comment, which is its usual method, and the coincidence is either
nothing or the quietest thing in the Abraham cycle.

### In this pericope — Genesis 16:1–16

The well is not where the encounter begins — 16:7 calls it *a fountain of water in the wilderness, by
the fountain on the way to Shur*, using *ʿayin*, spring, the same word as *eye*. It becomes *bəʾēr*, a
well, only in the naming formula of 16:14, and the naming is the narrator's report of a name that
stuck: *"Therefore the well was called Beer Lahai Roi."*

The sequence in 16:13–14 has two namings in it. Hagar names **God** — *"You are a God who sees"* —
which is unique in Scripture. The place-name follows from it, and place-naming after a divine
encounter is the ordinary Genesis pattern (*Bethel*, 28:19; *Yahweh Will Provide*, 22:14; *Peniel*,
32:30). One of the two acts is conventional and one has no parallel anywhere in the Bible.
