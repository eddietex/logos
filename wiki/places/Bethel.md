# Bethel

## Overview

*Bêt-ʾĒl*, **house of God** — and the Bible's clearest case of a holy place that goes bad.

Abram reaches it second. *"He left from there to go to the mountain on the east of Bethel and
pitched his tent, having Bethel on the west, and Ai on the east. There he built an altar to Yahweh
and called on Yahweh's name"* (Genesis 12:8). He is not in the town; he is on the ridge between two
towns, in a tent, and what he does there is build and proclaim.

**The name is an anachronism at this point in the story.** The place is called *Luz* until Jacob
renames it after the dream of the ladder — *"He called the name of that place Bethel, but the name
of the city was Luz at the first"* (28:19). Genesis 12:8 uses the later name for the reader's
benefit, exactly as it uses *Ur of the Chaldees* for a city no Chaldean had yet reached. Two
narrator's conveniences in as many chapters, and neither is hidden.

**Where it is.** Usually identified with Beitin, about ten miles north of Jerusalem on the central
ridge road, at roughly 2,900 feet; [[Ai]] lies a mile or two east. The ridge between them is high
ground with a view in every direction, which is part of why the site is a sanctuary and part of why
armies keep passing through it.

### The house of God, and the house of nothing

- **Abram builds an altar and calls on the name** (12:8), and returns to the same altar after Egypt
  (13:3–4) — the only altar in Genesis a patriarch is said to come back to.
- **Jacob sleeps there and sees the ladder** (28:10–22), names the place, sets up the stone he had
  used as a pillow, and vows. *"This is none other than God's house, and this is the gate of
  heaven."* He is sent back to build an altar there twenty years later (35:1–15).
- **It becomes a sanctuary of the judges' period**, where the ark stands and Israel enquires of God
  (Judges 20:18, 26–28), and part of Samuel's circuit (1 Samuel 7:16).
- **And then Jeroboam puts a golden calf in it** (1 Kings 12:28–29), one of the two he makes so that
  the northern tribes need not go up to Jerusalem, with the line *"behold your gods, Israel, which
  brought you up out of the land of Egypt"* — the words of Exodus 32:4. From that point Bethel is
  the northern kingdom's royal sanctuary and, to the prophets, its chief scandal.
- **Amos preaches against it** and is thrown out by its priest (Amos 7:10–13), with the verdict
  *"Bethel will come to nothing"* (5:5) — a pun that Hosea sharpens by renaming the town **Beth
  Aven**, *house of trouble* (Hosea 4:15; 10:5).
- **Josiah tears down the altar and burns bones on it** (2 Kings 23:15–16), the last act of the
  reform.

The arc from 12:8 to 2 Kings 23 is the reason Bethel is worth a page in its own right. It is
Scripture's standing demonstration that a place where God really did appear can become the thing
most needing to be pulled down, and that the name it keeps the whole way through is *house of God*.
The arc is set out in full at [[Bethel from Jacob's Pillar to Jeroboam's Calf]].

**And the last word on it is a census number.** Ezra 2:28 counts two hundred and twenty-three men of
Bethel and Ai among the returnees, and Nehemiah 11:31 has Benjaminites living there. No sanctuary, no
altar, no comment: the gate of heaven finishes the Old Testament as a town on a ridge.

## Appears in

- [[The Call of Abram]] — Genesis 12:8, the ridge Abram pitches on and his second altar
- [[Abram and Lot Separate]] — Genesis 13:3–4, the altar he comes back to after Egypt and calls on
  Yahweh's name at a second time
- [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] — Genesis 28:10–22, **where it gets the name.** Jacob runs into the
  site after dark — ***wayyipgaʿ bammāqôm***, he does not choose it — sleeps on one of its stones,
  and wakes saying *"Surely Yahweh is in this place, and I didn't know it … This is none other than
  God's house, and this is the gate of heaven."* **The narrator has been calling it Bethel since
  12:8**, and this verse admits it: *"the name of the city was Luz at the first."* Six occurrences of
  ***māqôm*** in twelve verses, which is the Hebrew word for a sanctuary site, applied to a field
  before anybody knows it is one
