# Ur of the Chaldees

## Overview

The family's origin, named twice in four verses, and the first city in Genesis that anyone leaves.

*"Haran died before his father Terah in the land of his birth, in Ur of the Chaldees"* (11:28), and
*"They went from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of Canaan"* (11:31). It appears twice more
in the Bible — Genesis 15:7, where Yahweh identifies himself to Abram as *"Yahweh who brought you out
of Ur of the Chaldees"*, and Nehemiah 9:7, quoting it — and nowhere else. Four occurrences in total,
all of them about leaving it.

**The city is never described.** Genesis says nothing about what it looked like, what was worshipped
there, how large it was, or why the family left. That last silence is the important one: 11:31 gives
[[Terah]] a destination and no motive, and God does not appear in the chapter. What Ur *is*, in
Genesis, is a place someone died and a place a family walked out of.

Every previous city in Genesis was founded — Enoch by Cain (4:17), Babel and the cities of Shinar and
Assyria by Nimrod (10:10–12), the tower-city abandoned at 11:8. Ur is the first that simply exists
before the story starts, and the first whose narrative function is exit.

### Which Ur

*ʾÛr Kaśdîm*, "Ur of the Chaldeans". The qualifier is the problem.

**The Chaldeans are an anachronism.** *Kaldu* are an Aramean people attested in southern Mesopotamia
from the ninth century BC and dominant there only under the Neo-Babylonian kings from the late
seventh — several centuries after any proposed date for Abraham. So *of the Chaldees* is a later
identifier attached to an older name, in the way a modern writer says "in France" of a Roman site.
That much is agreed. What it identifies is not.

**The southern identification** — Tell el-Muqayyar in southern Iraq, the great Sumerian city of Ur,
capital of the Third Dynasty, excavated by Leonard Woolley between 1922 and 1934 — is the majority
view. Its strongest argument is the qualifier itself: *Ur of the Chaldees* is exactly how a
first-millennium writer or editor would specify *that* Ur, which lay in the heart of Chaldean
territory. Woolley's excavation gave the reading enormous popular currency, and his ziggurat of
Nanna, the moon-god, is the image most readers carry.

**The northern case** rests on everything else the family does. All their other geography is in the
far north, around [[Haran (city)]] on the Balikh — six hundred miles from southern Ur, and in the
opposite direction from Canaan if you start in the south, since a caravan from Tell el-Muqayyar to
Canaan would have no reason to pass through Haran except that the northern route is the only one that
avoids the desert. Joshua 24:2–3 places the fathers *"beyond the River"*, which reads most naturally
of the upper Euphrates. Genesis 24:4 and 10 call the Haran district Abraham's *"country"* and his
*"relatives"*, and send the servant there, not to Babylonia. And four names in the register —
[[Serug]], [[Nahor (son of Serug)]], [[Terah]], and less certainly [[Reu]] — correspond to towns in
that same small district. A northern Ur has been proposed at Urfa (ancient Edessa, modern Şanlıurfa),
where local tradition has long placed Abraham, and at a site *Ura* named in Hittite and Ugaritic
texts. The case is circumstantial and the position is a minority one.

The wiki records both and adopts neither. What both agree on is the direction of travel: out of
Mesopotamia, toward Canaan, and the family does not get there.

### The moon-god, and what can and cannot be said

Ur — the southern city — was the principal cult centre of **Nanna/Sin**, the moon-god, with a great
ziggurat still standing today. Haran was Sin's other principal centre, in the north. That two of Sin's
two main sanctuaries in the ancient Near East are exactly the two cities this family lives in is a
striking fact and it is regularly overstated. Joshua 24:2 says the fathers *"served other gods"* and
does not say which; Genesis says nothing at all; and no biblical text connects the family to Sin. The
honest statement is that the geography is suggestive, the accusation is generic, and the specific
identification is a modern inference. See [[Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call]].

**And God claims the departure as his own.** *"I am Yahweh who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldees,
to give you this land to inherit it"* (15:7). Genesis 11:31 had said *Terah took Abram his son*, with
no divine speech anywhere in the chapter; four chapters later Yahweh puts himself where Terah was.
Neither statement is withdrawn, and Joshua 24:3 takes the same line God does here: *"I took your
father Abraham from beyond the River."*

The form of the sentence matters as much as its content. *I am Yahweh who brought you out of X* is the
self-presentation formula the Decalogue opens with — *"I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the
land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage"* (Exodus 20:2) — and Genesis 15:7 is its first occurrence,
with **Ur where Egypt will be**. Israel's foundational memory is rehearsed on one man four centuries
early, in the chapter that predicts the Egypt version. See [[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].
