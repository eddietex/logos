# Sheol

*Filed under places rather than themes because the Hebrew Bible speaks of it as somewhere people
**go** — down to it, into it, through its gates — and because a reader following the geography of a
passage should find it where the other locations are. It is a realm, not a point on a map, and
nothing in Scripture locates it.*

## Overview

*Šəʾôl*, the common destination of the dead in the Hebrew Bible. It appears about sixty-five times,
and what is most striking about it to a reader arriving from the New Testament is how little it
does. Sheol is dark, dusty, silent, and downward. It is not a place of punishment — the wicked go
there, and so do the righteous, and so does everyone. It has no fire. It has no reward. Jacob
expects to go there mourning (Genesis 37:35); Job expects to go there and asks whether his hope is
coming with him.

Its recurring properties across the canon are consistent. It is **down** — one always *goes down*
to Sheol, never across or up. It is **dark** and dusty, *"the land of darkness and of the shadow of
death"* (Job 10:21–22). It has **gates** and bars, so it is imagined with a city's or a fortress's
architecture (Job 17:16, Isaiah 38:10, Psalm 9:13). It is **insatiable**, one of the four things
that never say *enough* (Proverbs 30:16). It is **silent**, and its silence is the thing the Psalms
protest most: *"The dead don't praise Yah, neither any who go down into silence"* (Psalm 115:17;
compare 6:5, 88:10–12, Isaiah 38:18). And it is **not outside God's reach** — *"If I make my bed in
Sheol, behold, you are there"* (Psalm 139:8) — though the texts that say so are unusual, and most
speak of it as the place where the relationship with God stops.

## In Job

Sheol is named eight times in Job, and its function in the argument is structural rather than
decorative. **Because Sheol offers no correction of earthly verdicts, Job's vindication has to
happen before he dies or not at all.** There is no assize down there in which the record is set
straight. That is the pressure behind the urgency of his appeals — the witness of 16:19 is needed
*now*, because 16:22 says *"when a few years have come, I shall go the way of no return"* — and it
is what makes the end of chapter 17 as bleak as it is.

Two passages carry the weight. At 14:13–15 Job entertains the one idea that would solve everything
and immediately drops it: *"Oh that you would hide me in Sheol, that you would keep me secret,
until your wrath is past, that you would appoint me a set time, and remember me!"* — a temporary Sheol, a
waiting room, with God calling and Job answering. He calls it a wish, not a hope. And at 17:13–16,
in [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]], he adopts it as a household: Sheol as his
house, his bed spread in the darkness, corruption addressed as *"You are my father"* and the worm as
*"My mother"* and *"my sister"*. The speech ends by asking whether his hope will go down with him to
the gates of Sheol, or descend together into the dust — and does not answer.

The apparent exception is 19:25–27, now ingested as [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]], where Job
seems to expect to see God *"after my skin is destroyed"*. The Hebrew of those verses is among the
most broken in the Bible — the disputed clause turns on whether *mibbəśārî* means *from within my
flesh* or *away from* it — and the wiki's reading is that Job most probably means vindication
before he dies, which is what the epilogue delivers. The verse cannot carry the weight the
resurrection tradition has put on it, and the pressure it registers is real; both are set out on
[[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].

Sheol is not named in Job 18–20, and one line in that stretch is worth marking anyway.
[[Bildad's Second Speech]] has the wicked man *"brought to the king of terrors"* (18:14) — a
passive, under escort, to a sovereign. Sheol in the rest of Job is a place without a ruler, dark
and dusty and administratively empty, and 18:14 is the one verse in the book that gives death a
throne. Whether *melek ballāhôt* is a figure, a superlative, or a demythologised borrowing from the
Canaanite Mot is discussed on [[Bildad's Second Speech]]; what it is not is the Sheol Job describes,
and the difference is the difference between a doctrine used to frighten and a place a man expects
to go.

## Appears in

- [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] — Job 17:13, 16, closing the speech.
- [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]] — not named, but the passage that most tests what the wiki says
  here: 19:25's Redeemer standing *"upon the earth"* is literally *upon dust*, the same word that
  ended the previous speech at 17:16.
- [[Bildad's Second Speech]] — not named either, and the source of the book's one crowned Death,
  *"the king of terrors"* (18:14).

## Related places

- **The dust** — the two are paired at 17:16, and the dust reaches back to Genesis 2:7 and 3:19,
  *"for you are dust, and to dust you shall return"*, on [[The Garden of Eden]] and [[The Fall]].
- **The pit** (*šaḥat*, *bôr*) and **Abaddon** (*destruction*, Job 26:6, 28:22, 31:12) — near
  synonyms used in parallel with Sheol.
- **Hades** — the Septuagint's rendering of *Sheol*, and the word the New Testament inherits; see
  the Notes.

## Events here

None narrated. No one in the Hebrew Bible describes Sheol from the inside, and the two apparent
exceptions prove the reticence rather than break it: the shade of Samuel comes up at 1 Samuel
28:8–19 and says only that he has been disturbed, and Isaiah 14:9–11 is an explicitly satirical
poem about the king of Babylon being greeted by the shades of dead kings.

## Notes

- **Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, and hell.** These four are not the same thing, and the King James
  Version's decision to render *Sheol* as *hell* thirty-one times has caused a great deal of
  confusion that modern versions, including the WEB, avoid by transliterating. **Sheol** is the
  Hebrew realm of all the dead, morally undifferentiated. **Hades** is the Septuagint's Greek
  equivalent, carried into the New Testament, where it is still primarily the realm of the dead —
  though Luke 16:23 gives it internal divisions. **Gehenna** is a different word entirely, from the
  Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, and it *is* a place of punishment; it is what Jesus means in
  Matthew 5:22, 29–30 and 10:28. **Hell** in later Christian usage corresponds to Gehenna, not to
  Sheol. Reading the developed doctrine back into Job 17 removes the problem the chapter exists to
  pose.
- **The etymology is unknown.** Proposals derive it from *šāʾal*, *to ask* or *to demand* (the
  insatiable pit that always asks for more, which suits Proverbs 30:16 and Isaiah 5:14), from a
  root meaning *to be hollow*, or from an unrecovered loanword. None commands agreement.
- **The development of the doctrine.** The undifferentiated Sheol of Job is where the Hebrew Bible
  mostly stands. The hope of resurrection appears late and sparsely — Isaiah 26:19, Daniel 12:2,
  and arguably Psalm 16:10, 49:15, and 73:24 — and becomes general only in the intertestamental
  period, which is why the Sadducees could still deny it in the first century (Mark 12:18, Acts
  23:8). Job's problem is therefore not that he lacks faith in an afterlife that the text assumes;
  it is that the resource later readers reach for on his behalf is not yet available to him. The
  wiki's practice is to let Job's despair stand at its own date rather than to answer it out of a
  later chapter of the canon, and to record the answers when the canon supplies them.
