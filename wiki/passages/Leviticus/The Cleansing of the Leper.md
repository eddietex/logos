# The Cleansing of the Leper

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 14:1–32

## Summary

Yahweh gives Moses the law for restoring a person whose leprosy has healed. The priest goes
outside the camp to examine him. Two clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet, hyssop, blood, and running
water belong to the first rite: one bird is killed, the person is sprinkled seven times and
pronounced clean, and the living bird is released. Washing, shaving, and bathing allow entry
to the camp, but the person must remain outside his own tent for seven days.

On the seventh day he shaves and washes again. On the eighth he brings offerings to the
Tent of Meeting. The priest applies trespass-offering blood and then oil to the right ear,
thumb, and big toe, with remaining oil on the head; sin, burnt, and meal offerings complete
the process. A separate provision reduces the cost for a poor person while preserving the
trespass-offering lamb, oil, bodily applications, and priestly atonement.

## Key verses

> **14:1** Yahweh spoke to Moses, saying,

> **14:3** and the priest shall go out of the camp. The priest shall examine him, and behold, if the plague of leprosy is healed in the leper,

> **14:7** He shall sprinkle on him who is to be cleansed from the leprosy seven times, and shall pronounce him clean, and shall let the living bird go into the open field.

> **14:8** “He who is to be cleansed shall wash his clothes, and shave off all his hair, and bathe himself in water; and he shall be clean. After that he shall come into the camp, but shall dwell outside his tent seven days.

> **14:9** It shall be on the seventh day, that he shall shave all his hair off his head and his beard and his eyebrows, even all his hair he shall shave off. He shall wash his clothes, and he shall bathe his body in water, then he shall be clean.

> **14:14** The priest shall take some of the blood of the trespass offering, and the priest shall put it on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot.

> **14:17** The priest shall put some of the rest of the oil that is in his hand on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot, upon the blood of the trespass offering.

> **14:19** “The priest shall offer the sin offering, and make atonement for him who is to be cleansed because of his uncleanness: and afterward he shall kill the burnt offering;

> **14:20** and the priest shall offer the burnt offering and the meal offering on the altar. The priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be clean.

> **14:21** “If he is poor, and can’t afford so much, then he shall take one male lamb for a trespass offering to be waved, to make atonement for him, and one tenth of an ephah of fine flour mingled with oil for a meal offering, and a log of oil;

> **14:22** and two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, such as he is able to afford; and the one shall be a sin offering, and the other a burnt offering.

> **14:32** This is the law for him in whom is the plague of leprosy, who is not able to afford the sacrifice for his cleansing.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh speaks to Moses and provides the law of restoration after healing, including a provision for poverty (1–32). He is the one before whom the person and offerings are presented.
- [[Moses]] — Moses alone is named as recipient of the new speech (1), unlike the joint address to Moses and Aaron at 13:1. Priestly examination and offerings are prescribed rather than narrated as actions Moses performs.
- [[Aaron]] — Aaron is not named in 14:1–32. The connection is his priestly office, named in 13:2: the officiant goes outside the camp, examines, and later offers for the healed person. The bodily blood application also recalls Aaron’s consecration in 8:23–24.
- The healed person and officiating priest are legal roles, not newly named individuals.

## Places

- [[Horeb]] — contextual Sinai setting (7:38), not named in this unit.
- Camp, personal tent, and Tent of Meeting are distinct locations; see [[The Dwelling of God]].

## Themes

- [[Clean and Unclean]] — Healing precedes the priest’s cleansing rite (3). Cleanness and access unfold in stages: sprinkling and washing permit camp entry, seven days outside the personal tent follow, and eighth-day offerings complete the prescribed process (7–20). The law supplies neither a cure nor an accusation that the disease was caused by a personal sin.
- [[Priesthood]] — The priest goes outside the camp to inspect the healed person, pronounces cleanness, and later presents him and his offerings at the Tent of Meeting (3, 7, 11). The office combines examination and restoration; it is not credited with causing the healing.
- [[Atonement]] — Atonement accompanies the oil application and offerings, ending in cleanness (18–20). Verse 19 expressly relates the sin offering to uncleanness. Unlike Leviticus 5:5–13, this unit supplies no confession of an identified offense or explicit forgiveness formula in the WEB.
- [[Blood]] — Bird blood is used with water, cedar, scarlet, and hyssop in the outside-camp rite (5–7). Trespass-offering blood later marks the right ear, thumb, and big toe; oil follows on those places and on the head (14–18). The poor person receives the same bodily applications (25–29).
- [[Sin]] — Sin and trespass offerings belong to restoration after healing (12, 19), without a specified personal offense, confession, or repayment. **Qualification retained:** the guilt and restitution of Leviticus 5:14–19 cannot be inferred merely from the shared trespass-offering label; this unit explicitly concerns uncleanness (14:19).
- [[Suffering]] — The social exclusion of Leviticus 13:46 is answered by a path back into the camp and eventually ordinary dwelling. Healing does not immediately end every restriction: the restored person remains outside his own tent seven days (14:8). No individual guilt is named as the cause of his suffering.
- [[Holiness]] — The trespass offering is most holy and belongs to the priest like the sin offering (13). Cleansing permits restored approach without appointing the person to priestly office; the parallel blood applications in Leviticus 8 and 14 do not erase that difference.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — The instruction distinguishes examination outside camp, the bird rite, washing and shaving, seven days outside the personal tent, and eighth-day offerings. The alternative for poverty changes specified materials while repeating blood and oil applications (21–32).
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — The ordinary eighth-day provision has two male lambs, a year-old ewe lamb, three tenths of an ephah of flour with oil, and a log of oil (10). The poor bring one male lamb, one tenth of flour with oil, a log of oil, and two birds (21–22). The retained lamb is the trespass offering; birds supply sin and burnt offerings.
- [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] — The poor person’s provision reduces the flour and substitutes birds for the sin and burnt offerings, but retains the trespass-offering lamb and log of oil (21–32). This is a specific accommodation, not a universal permission to replace every animal with birds or flour.
- [[The Dwelling of God]] — Restoration moves from outside the camp to camp entry, then to presentation before Yahweh at the Tent of Meeting’s door on the eighth day (3, 8, 11, 23). The text distinguishes a person’s own tent from the sanctuary and does not authorize unrestricted sanctuary entry.

## Notes

### Healing, cleansing, and access

Verse 3 assumes the plague has healed before the priest commands the bird rite. The law is
not a treatment for producing that healing. Nor does the first pronouncement of cleanness
collapse every subsequent stage: verse 8 permits camp entry but restricts the personal tent,
verse 9 requires renewed washing and shaving, and verses 10–20 prescribe eighth-day offerings.
The repeated clean declarations describe the process without explaining a theory behind each
stage. The title retains the WEB’s terminology without identifying a modern diagnosis.

### What the ritual does not explain

The living bird is released, but the text does not explicitly say that it carries away sin.
Cedar, scarlet, and hyssop receive no symbolic explanation here. Nor does the unit interpret
ear, hand, and foot as hearing, working, and walking obediently. Those are possible later
readings, not stated meanings. The comparison with priestly consecration rests on shared
bodily locations and materials, not a claim that the restored person becomes a priest.

The poverty provision retains one lamb: importing the flour-only provision of Leviticus 5
would change this law. Sin and trespass offering names likewise do not authorize inventing
a personal offense or restitution payment. The stated problem in verse 19 is uncleanness.
