# Holy Living in Field and Community

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 19:19–37

## Summary

The second half of the congregation's holiness address regulates mixtures of animals,
seed, and cloth, then a sexual offense involving an enslaved woman pledged to another
man (19–22). Punishment is required but execution excluded because she is not free;
the man brings a ram, and priestly atonement brings forgiveness.

New orchards have three forbidden years, a fourth year of holy fruit for praise, and
a fifth year for eating (23–25). Blood, sorcery, bodily practices, exploiting a daughter,
and consulting mediums are addressed beside Sabbath and sanctuary reverence (26–31).

Honor for elders is joined to fearing God. The resident foreigner must be treated
as native-born and loved as oneself, because Israel lived as foreigners in Egypt
(32–34). Just measures likewise rest on Yahweh's identity as deliverer from Egypt.
The unit closes by requiring observance of all his statutes and ordinances (35–37).


## Key verses

> **19:20** “‘If a man lies carnally with a woman who is a slave girl, pledged to be married to another man, and not ransomed, or given her freedom; they shall be punished. They shall not be put to death, because she was not free.

> **19:22** The priest shall make atonement for him with the ram of the trespass offering before Yahweh for his sin which he has committed: and the sin which he has committed shall be forgiven him.

> **19:24** But in the fourth year all its fruit shall be holy, for giving praise to Yahweh.

> **19:30** “‘You shall keep my Sabbaths, and reverence my sanctuary; I am Yahweh.

> **19:32** “‘You shall rise up before the gray head, and honor the face of an old man, and you shall fear your God. I am Yahweh.

> **19:34** The stranger who lives as a foreigner with you shall be to you as the native-born among you, and you shall love him as yourself; for you lived as foreigners in the land of Egypt. I am Yahweh your God.

> **19:36** You shall have just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin. I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh gives these commands within the address of 19:1–2 and identifies himself as the one who brought Israel out of Egypt (36). His authority joins sanctuary reverence, love of foreigners, and honest measures.
- [[Moses]] — The address entrusted to Moses in 19:1–2 continues through 37. He mediates these agricultural, ritual, and social commands; no new speech introduction occurs at 19.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — Egypt is remembered as the land of Israel’s foreign residence (34) and the place from which Yahweh brought them out (36). It grounds obligations to foreigners and in commerce, rather than locating this speech.
- [[Canaan]] — The land entered and planted with fruit trees (23) is Canaan in the wider context of 18:3. The name is not repeated in this unit, and entry is anticipated rather than narrated.
- [[Horeb]] — The Sinai setting follows the book’s context (7:38); no mountain is named in 19:19–37. Orchard planting anticipates settled life without relocating the current address.

## Themes

- [[Holiness]] — Distinct kinds in breeding, sowing, and clothing (19), holy fourth-year fruit (23–25), and reverence for the sanctuary (30) stand alongside love for the foreigner and honest measures (33–36). The chapter holds sacred distinctions and social obligations together.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — Keeping the statutes opens and closes this unit (19, 37). Its diverse commands address fields, bodies, worship, and commerce; the passage supplies no single explanation for every prohibition.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — The woman in 20 is enslaved, pledged to another man, and neither ransomed nor freed. Her unfree status is explicitly the reason the pair are not executed. This regulates an unequal legal institution rather than abolishing it; the WEB leaves the form of punishment unspecified.
- [[Sexual Sin]] — Intercourse with the pledged slave woman incurs punishment and requires the man’s ram and priestly atonement (20–22). Making a daughter a prostitute is forbidden (29). The first case does not specify consent or coercion, so neither should be supplied as a narrative fact.
- [[Marriage]] — The woman is pledged to another man but remains unfree (20). Betrothal and slavery jointly define this legal case; it is not a narrative of a marriage or a general penalty rule for every sexual offense.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — The man in the slave-woman case brings a ram as a trespass offering to the Tent of Meeting (21–22). Fourth-year fruit is holy for praise (24), but the passage gives no detailed fruit-offering rite.
- [[Atonement]] — The priest makes atonement with the ram for the man’s sin, and forgiveness is promised (21–22). Unlike the unknown-guilt case of 5:17–18, this case is not described in the WEB as unwitting. The promise cannot be generalized into a sacrificial remedy for every offense.
- [[Forgiveness]] — Forgiveness is explicitly promised to the man after priestly atonement with the ram (22). Verse 20 still prescribes punishment; forgiveness and the legal consequence are both retained.
- [[Sin]] — Verse 22 calls the man’s act sin and promises forgiveness after atonement. This case is not presented as accidental in the WEB; that does not establish that every deliberate offense has this remedy.
- [[Priesthood]] — An unnamed priest makes atonement for the man with the trespass-offering ram (22). No individual priest is identified, and the priest’s service does not cancel verse 20’s punishment.
- [[Judgment]] — The pair are punished but not put to death because the woman was not free (20). The form of punishment is unspecified in the WEB. Just judgment and honest measurements are commanded together (35–36).
- [[Festivals and First Fruits]] — New fruit trees have three years of forbidden fruit, a fourth year holy for praise, and a fifth year for eating (23–25). This is an orchard timetable, not the announcement of another annual festival.
- [[Praise and Song]] — All fourth-year fruit is holy for giving praise to Yahweh (24). Agricultural produce is directed toward praise; the text supplies neither a song nor a detailed performance.
- [[Blood]] — Eating meat with blood still in it is prohibited (26), continuing the food restriction already developed in Leviticus 17. Here it stands beside bans on enchantments and sorcery.
- [[Idolatry]] — Enchantments and sorcery are prohibited (26), and seeking mediums and wizards brings defilement (31). These are forbidden religious practices; the passage does not name a particular foreign deity or explain the origin of each custom.
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — Cuttings in the flesh for the dead are prohibited (28). This restricts a bodily mourning practice without forbidding every expression of grief; the neighboring tattoo prohibition should not be turned into an unverified account of its historical origin.
- [[Sabbath]] — Keeping Yahweh’s Sabbaths is paired with reverence for his sanctuary (30). Sacred time and sacred space remain part of the same community instruction as care for elders and foreigners.
- [[The Dwelling of God]] — The trespass offering is brought to the Tent of Meeting’s door (21), and reverence for the sanctuary is commanded (30). The speech prescribes approach without narrating a new manifestation of divine presence.
- [[The Fear of God]] — Rising before the gray head and honoring an old person are joined to fearing God (32). Reverence takes a public, embodied form toward the elderly.
- [[Love]] — The resident foreigner must be treated as native-born and loved as oneself (33–34). This explicitly extends the self-love measure of 19:18 to the foreigner; its narrower reference to one’s people is not an exclusion of outsiders.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — The orchard law anticipates entry into the land (23–25), while the foreigner within it must receive native-like treatment and love (33–34). Israel’s own foreign residence in Egypt grounds the obligation.
- [[Righteousness]] — Justice applies to measures of length, weight, and quantity as well as judgment (35–36). An accurate commercial standard is an obligation to Yahweh, who identifies himself as Israel’s deliverer.
- [[Integrity]] — Just balances, weights, ephah, and hin require reliable dealings (35–36). Honesty includes the measuring instrument, not merely the merchant’s words.
- [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] — Elders receive honor and foreigners protection and love (32–34); a father must not make his daughter a prostitute (29). The slave-woman case retains unequal status (20–22), so these protections should not be summarized as abolition of every legal inequality.
- [[The Names of God]] — Repeated divine self-identification grounds commands about produce, bodies, sanctuary, elders, foreigners, and measures. In 36 Yahweh identifies himself through the exodus, linking honest commerce to deliverance.
- [[Remembrance]] — Israel’s past as foreigners and its departure from Egypt supply reasons for present love and honest dealing (34, 36). This is a thematic appeal to remembered experience, without an assertion about a Hebrew verb.
- [[Redemption]] — The slave woman has not been ransomed or freed (20); those absent changes of status matter to the penalty. The man’s ram is an offering for his sin, not a payment said to purchase her freedom.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — Seeking mediums or wizards is said to defile (31). This is prohibited conduct, distinct from bodily impurity that can arise without wrongdoing in chapters 12–15.

## Notes

**Boundaries without a supplied theory.** Verse 19 states three mixture prohibitions
but does not explain them through a universal principle or identify every prohibited
fabric. No claim about exact Hebrew terminology or ancient ritual origins is needed
to record what the WEB actually says. The bodily prohibitions in 27–28 likewise should
not be expanded into a reconstructed history without sources.

**Status, punishment, and silence.** The slave-woman case names an unequal legal status,
not named individuals. It neither specifies consent nor explains the punishment's form
in the WEB. It assigns the ram and forgiveness to the man; it does not describe a
separate offering for the woman. Recording these limits avoids turning silence into
either an accusation against her or a claim that the case abolishes slavery.

**Love and unequal status.** Native-like treatment and love for the resident foreigner
(33–34) are explicit obligations. They coexist with the slave-status distinction in
20; the chapter should not be flattened into a claim of identical legal treatment for
everyone. Neither command cancels the other.

**Fruit and worship.** The fourth-year fruit is wholly holy for praise, while eating
is permitted in the fifth year. The text does not specify here how the fourth-year
fruit is transported or consumed. Those details are left open.
