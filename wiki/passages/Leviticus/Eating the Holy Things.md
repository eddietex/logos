# Eating the Holy Things

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 22:1–16

## Summary

Yahweh tells Moses to instruct Aaron and his sons to separate themselves from the holy things
Israel makes holy to him, so that they do not profane his holy name (1–2). A descendant of Aaron
who approaches the holy things with uncleanness on him is to be cut off from before Yahweh (3).
Skin disease, a discharge, contact with what is unclean by the dead, an emission of seed, a
creeping thing, or an unclean person each bars a priest from eating the holy things. The lesser
contacts last until evening and require bathing; after sunset he may eat again, "because it is
his bread" (4–7). He may not eat what dies of itself or is torn by animals (8), and the section
closes by warning that profaning the commandment brings sin and death (9).

The second half asks who belongs to the priest's table (10–13). A stranger, a foreigner living
with the priests, and a hired servant may not eat. A slave the priest has bought, and those born
in his house, may. A priest's daughter married to an outsider may not eat the heave offering; a
widowed or divorced daughter with no child who has returned to her father's house may again eat
her father's bread. A man who eats something holy unwittingly adds a fifth of its value and gives
the holy thing to the priest (14). The priests themselves must not profane Israel's holy things and
so make the people bear guilt by eating them (15–16).

The pericope follows directly from [[The Holiness of the Priests]]: chapter 21 left a descendant
with a defect his holy-food entitlement (21:22), and chapter 22 now names what does suspend that
entitlement — uncleanness, temporarily — and who outside the priest's own body shares it.

## Key verses

> **22:2** “Tell Aaron and his sons to separate themselves from the holy things of the children of Israel, which they make holy to me, and that they not profane my holy name. I am Yahweh.

> **22:7** When the sun is down, he shall be clean; and afterward he shall eat of the holy things, because it is his bread.

> **22:11** But if a priest buys a slave, purchased by his money, he shall eat of it; and such as are born in his house, they shall eat of his bread.

> **22:13** But if a priest’s daughter is a widow, or divorced, and has no child, and has returned to her father’s house, as in her youth, she may eat of her father’s bread: but no stranger shall eat any of it.

> **22:14** “‘If a man eats something holy unwittingly, then he shall add the fifth part of its value to it, and shall give the holy thing to the priest.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh speaks the instruction (1) and punctuates it with "I am Yahweh" (2, 3, 8) and "I am Yahweh who sanctifies them" (9, 16). The holy things are made holy *to him* (2–3), and profaning them profanes his name.
- [[Moses]] — Moses receives the speech and is told to pass it to Aaron and his sons (1–3). Unlike 21:24, this unit does not narrate its delivery.
- [[Aaron]] — Aaron and his sons are the addressees (2), and "the offspring of Aaron" are the subjects of the uncleanness rules (3–4). No individual son is singled out.

## Places

- [[Horeb]] — The Sinai setting is supplied by the book's context (Leviticus 7:38); chapter 22:1–16 names no place. The relevant locations are implied by the holy things themselves and the priest's household, where the "father's house" of verse 13 is the daughter's place of return.

## Themes

- [[Priesthood]] — The priest's portion is "his bread" (7), and it feeds a household, not only an officeholder: bought and house-born slaves eat it, a returned daughter eats it, and a hired servant or outsider-married daughter does not (10–13). Priests also bear responsibility not to let their eating make Israel bear guilt (15–16).
- [[Holiness]] — Holy things are things "which they make holy to me" (2); holiness is ownership by Yahweh, and the priests must "separate themselves" from it while unclean. Profaning the holy things is profaning the holy name (2), and Yahweh is the one "who sanctifies them" (9, 16).
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — The conditions of chapters 11–15 return as bars to eating: skin disease, discharges, corpse contact, an emission of seed, creeping things, unclean persons (4–5). The lesser cases close at evening after bathing (6–7). Approaching holy things unclean carries cutting off (3); the uncleanness itself is not called a sin.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — The holy things and the heave offering are offerings Israel gives that become priestly food (2, 12). Eating them is regulated by status, household, and the eater's condition, extending the portion rules of Leviticus 6–7 and 10:12–15.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — A slave "purchased by his money" and those "born in his house" eat the priest's holy food, while a hired servant and a foreigner living with the priests do not (10–11). Membership in the household, not free status, is the criterion. The text records the distinction without commenting on the institution.
- [[Marriage]] — A priest's daughter married to an outsider leaves the table; widowhood or divorce, no child, and return to her father's house restore it, "as in her youth" (12–13). Marriage moves a woman's household belonging, and its end can return it.
- [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] — The childless widowed or divorced daughter who returns home is fed from the holy things (13). Unlike the gleaning law of Leviticus 19:9–10, this provision runs through a specific household rather than to the poor at large.
- [[Theft and Restitution]] — Unwitting eating of something holy is repaid with the value plus a fifth, given with the holy thing to the priest (14), as at Leviticus 5:16. Verse 14 does not mention the ram of 5:15; this unit leaves that unresolved.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — "They shall therefore follow my commandment" (9) frames the uncleanness rules; the unwitting-eating rule distinguishes ignorance from defiance without excusing the loss (14).
- [[Life and Death]] — Corpse contact and carcasses defile (4, 8), and profaning the commandment risks that the priest will "die therein" (9). The death language is a warning attached to the commandment, not a narrated execution.
- [[Judgment]] — Cutting off "from before me" (3) and the possibility of dying for profanation (9) are divine sanctions; no human court is assigned to enforce them here.
- [[The Names of God]] — "My holy name" (2) is what priestly carelessness profanes, and the refrain "I am Yahweh who sanctifies them" (9, 16) names him by what he does to his priests.

## Notes

**Two kinds of exclusion, kept apart.** Chapter 21 barred a descendant of Aaron with a defect
from approaching the altar while letting him eat (21:22–23). Chapter 22 bars an *unclean*
descendant from eating until he is clean (4–7). The two chapters together separate a permanent
bodily condition that restricts service from a temporary ritual condition that restricts eating.
Neither is called a moral fault in these verses.

**Household membership, not ethnicity or freedom.** The slave purchased by the priest eats; the
hired servant does not (10–11). Verse 10's "stranger" sits beside a foreigner living with the
priests and a hired servant, and verse 13 repeats "no stranger shall eat any of it" after
restoring the daughter, so in context it marks someone outside the priestly household, including
other Israelites. This reading is drawn from the WEB context only; no source-language claim is
made about the Hebrew term behind "stranger" or "outsider". Leviticus 10:14 already included
Aaron's daughters at the breast and thigh; verse 12 now says marriage to an outsider ends that,
and verse 13 says the conditions under which it returns. The text does not say whether a widowed
daughter *with* a child may ever return to the table; the stated condition is "has no child".

**Unwitting eating and Leviticus 5.** Verse 14 requires the value plus a fifth, given with the
holy thing to the priest, as Leviticus 5:16 does for unwitting wrong in holy things. Leviticus
5:15 also required a ram; verse 14 does not mention one. The text here neither adds nor waives
it, and this page does not decide whether the earlier rule is assumed.

**Verses 15–16.** The WEB's "and so cause them to bear the iniquity that brings guilt, when they
eat their holy things" leaves the antecedents of "them" and "they" ambiguous: it may mean the
priests cause Israel to bear guilt by letting unqualified people eat, or by eating unclean. This
page does not resolve that from the Hebrew.

**Boundary.** The planned 22:1–16 boundary is kept: verse 17 opens a new speech, addressed to
Aaron, his sons, and all Israel, about acceptable victims.
