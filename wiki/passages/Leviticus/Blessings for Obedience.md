# Blessings for Obedience

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 26:1–13

## Summary

Two commands head the chapter: no idols, carved image, pillar, or figured stone in the land (1),
and keep Yahweh's Sabbaths and revere his sanctuary (2). Then comes the condition: *if you walk
in my statutes* (3). What follows is one long promise spoken by Yahweh in the first person.

Rain comes on time, the land and trees yield, and one harvest runs into the next, with bread to
the full and safety in the land (4–5). Peace comes next: lying down unafraid, evil animals
removed, and no sword through the land (6). Enemies fall before a few (7–8). Yahweh turns toward
Israel, makes them fruitful, establishes his covenant, and gives them so much that old stores are
cleared for new (9–10). The promise ends with his own presence: his tent among them, his walking
among them, *your God* and *my people* (11–12). The last verse names who is speaking. He is the God
who broke the bars of their yoke in Egypt and made them walk upright (13).

## Notes

**Does the unit start at 26:1?** The chapter break suits the content but cuts across a sentence
pattern: 25:55 ends *I am Yahweh your God*, and 26:1 ends the same way, followed by *I am Yahweh*
at 26:2. So verses 1–2 could close chapter 25 as easily as open chapter 26. They are kept here, as
planned, because they read as the heads of what Israel must keep before the *if* of verse 3.
MAM's paragraph break also falls after verse 2. The plan's unit end is sound: MAM has a paragraph break
after verse 13, and verse 14 turns with *But if you will not listen*.

**One condition, three verbs, and a long answer.** Verse 3 needs only a line: *walk*, *keep*, *do*.
Everything else is the answer. Grammatically, the promises run on Yahweh's *I will*: *wənātattî* (4,
6, 11), *wəhišbattî* (6), *ûpānîtî*, *wəhiprêtî*, *wəhirbêtî*, *wahăqimōtî* (9), *wəhithallaktî*,
*wəhāyîtî* (12). The people's own verbs here (*chase*, *eat*, *lie down*) are enjoyment rather than
achievement. Yet the condition is real. The text does not call these promises unconditional, and the
next unit makes the alternative explicit.

**The word *give*.** Israel is forbidden to *place* (*titənû*) a figured stone in the land (1), using
the verb Yahweh uses of giving rain (4), peace (6), and his dwelling (11); the land and tree also
*give* in verse 4. WEB's varied English (*place*, *give*, *yield*, *set*) hides the repetition.
The same kind of play occurs with *qûm*: Israel may not *raise up* (*tāqîmû*) a pillar (1), Yahweh
will *establish* (*wahăqimōtî*) his covenant (9), and *upright* (*qôməmiyyût*, 13) is generally taken
from the same root. Whether the writer intended these echoes cannot be shown; the repetition is
simply there in the Hebrew.

**Three walks.** Israel is to *walk* (*tēlēkû*) in statutes (3). Yahweh will walk about
(*wəhithallaktî*) among them (12). And he has made them walk (*wāʾôlēk*) upright (13), all from
*hālak*. Israel's walking in verse 3 rests on the release recalled by the third verb. The next
unit keeps the root with a different preposition: *walk contrary to me* (26:21, 23–24, 27–28).

**Turning and abhorring.** *I will have respect for you* (9) is *ûpānîtî ʾălêkem*, *I will turn to
you*, the verb of 19:4, *Don't turn to idols*: Israel is not to turn, and Yahweh turns. *My soul won't
abhor you* (11, *tigʿal*) is an odd promise until the next unit: it recurs of Israel's soul at 26:15
and of Yahweh's at 26:30, and the denial returns at 26:44. A blessing that bothers to say *I will not
abhor you* already knows the alternative. Each major gift here also has its counterpart ahead: rain
and yield (26:19–20), bread and fullness (26:26), evil animals (26:22), the sword (26:25), and
chasing (26:17, 36–37).

**Peace and the sword.** *Neither shall the sword go through your land* (6) stands beside *they shall
fall before you by the sword* (7–8). The text does not say where these battles are or who begins
them; the plainest reading is that the sword falls on enemies and does not cross Israel's land, but
this is inference. The unit promises victory without describing any campaign.

**Five and a hundred.** Five against a hundred is one to twenty; a hundred against ten thousand is
one to a hundred. The odds worsen as the numbers grow, a rhetorical figure, not a reckoning. The
text attributes nothing here to Israelite skill. See
[[Five Shall Chase a Hundred - Leviticus 26.8, Deuteronomy 32.30, and Joshua 23.10]].

**Old store.** *Yāšān* links verse 10 to 25:22, where *old store* is what Israel eats by promise
through the Jubilee gap. Here the old is not a reserve to live on but a problem of surplus. *Nôšān*
in *yāšān nôšān* reinforces the age, rendered by WEB as *long kept*.

**Fruitfulness and establishing.** *Wəhiprêtî ... wəhirbêtî* is the paired hiphil of Genesis 17:20,
said of Ishmael, and *wahăqimōtî ʾet-bərîtî* is the clause of Genesis 6:18 and 17:7. Leviticus 26:9
therefore speaks in the vocabulary of the earlier covenant promises. It calls the covenant *my
covenant* and does not state whether it means a new act or the keeping of an existing one. Compare
26:42, ahead, where the patriarchs are named.

**The presence is the summit, but not simply the reward.** The tent already stands (Exodus 40), and
Leviticus has been legislating from it. Verse 11's *I will set my tent among you* therefore cannot
describe a first building. It may promise continued residence, a settled residence in the land, or
both; the verse does not choose. What it does do is place God's presence after every material good.
Verse 12 shares *wəhāyîtî lākem lēʾlōhîm* with Exodus 6:7, where the people's half comes first; here
it comes second.

**The yoke in the last verse.** Verse 13 turns *slaves* (*ʿăbādîm*) from legal category to memory,
directly after a chapter on who may be held as one. *Bars* (*mōṭôt*) are commonly understood as
parts of the yoke, so *upright* would complete the image: a bowed animal or captive released to
stand. The context suggests that reading; the verse does not spell it out.

**A promise to a people, not a rule for individuals.** Every *you* in 3–13 is plural, and the
goods named (rain, land, peace, enemies) are collective. Job's friends use this language of one
man; see [[None Shall Make You Afraid - The Covenant Blessing of Security]]. **Tension left standing:**
the text ties national prosperity to obedience, and elsewhere in the canon the righteous suffer
(Job 1:1) and the wicked prosper (Ecclesiastes 8:14). This unit does not address those cases.
