# Thanksgiving, Vows, and the Peace Offering

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 7:11–21

## Summary

The peace-offering law now distinguishes thanksgiving from vow and freewill offerings.
Thanksgiving brings unleavened preparations together with leavened bread; one of each
is assigned to the priest who sprinkles the blood. Its meat must be eaten the same day.
Vow and freewill meat may also be eaten the next day, but third-day remains must burn.
Eating it then invalidates acceptance and brings liability on the eater.

Eligibility matters as well as timing. Contaminated meat must burn, while clean people
may eat uncontaminated meat. Eating Yahweh's peace-offering meat while unclean carries
the sanction of being cut off from one's people. The meal is shared under conditions;
neither offering it nor possessing it gives unrestricted permission to eat.

## Key verses

> **7:11** “‘This is the law of the sacrifice of peace offerings, which one shall offer to Yahweh.

> **7:12** If he offers it for a thanksgiving, then he shall offer with the sacrifice of thanksgiving unleavened cakes mixed with oil, and unleavened wafers anointed with oil, and cakes mixed with oil.

> **7:13** With cakes of leavened bread he shall offer his offering with the sacrifice of his peace offerings for thanksgiving.

> **7:14** Of it he shall offer one out of each offering for a heave offering to Yahweh. It shall be the priest’s who sprinkles the blood of the peace offerings.

> **7:15** The flesh of the sacrifice of his peace offerings for thanksgiving shall be eaten on the day of his offering. He shall not leave any of it until the morning.

> **7:16** “‘But if the sacrifice of his offering is a vow, or a freewill offering, it shall be eaten on the day that he offers his sacrifice; and on the next day what remains of it shall be eaten:

> **7:17** but what remains of the meat of the sacrifice on the third day shall be burned with fire.

> **7:18** If any of the meat of the sacrifice of his peace offerings is eaten on the third day, it will not be accepted, and it shall not be credited to him who offers it. It will be an abomination, and the soul who eats any of it will bear his iniquity.

> **7:19** “‘The meat that touches any unclean thing shall not be eaten. It shall be burned with fire. As for the meat, everyone who is clean may eat it;

> **7:20** but the soul who eats of the meat of the sacrifice of peace offerings, that belongs to Yahweh, having his uncleanness on him, that soul shall be cut off from his people.

> **7:21** When anyone touches any unclean thing, the uncleanness of man, or an unclean animal, or any unclean abomination, and eats some of the meat of the sacrifice of peace offerings, which belong to Yahweh, that soul shall be cut off from his people.’”

## Notes

### The bread and its destination

Verses 12–13 prescribe both unleavened and leavened bread with thanksgiving. Verse 14
assigns one out of each offering to the officiating priest. It does not direct burning
this leavened bread on the altar. The apparent tension with the yeast restriction in
2:11 and 6:17 must not be erased by calling all sacrificial bread unleavened: those
verses concern meal offerings, whereas 7:13 expressly requires leavened accompaniment
to thanksgiving. No inherent moral meaning for yeast is supplied in these instructions.

### Three kinds, two deadlines

Thanksgiving meat has a same-day deadline, with nothing retained until morning (15).
Vow and freewill meat may remain for the second day (16); on the third it must burn
(17). Verse 18 makes third-day eating an issue of acceptance, credit to the offerer,
and the eater's iniquity. This is not merely advice against food spoilage. The text
gives no hygienic rationale or explanation of why the deadlines differ.

Proverbs 7:14's speaker claims paid vows. The relevant deadline is therefore verse 16,
not the stricter thanksgiving rule. A meal invitation is a reasonable inference from
that setting; Proverbs does not explicitly cite this law or prove her claim true.

### Clean food and clean eaters

Verse 19 concerns meat that has touched an unclean thing; verses 20–21 concern an
unclean eater. Both matter. The permission for everyone clean to eat (19) differs
from the male-priest restriction on sin and trespass portions (6:29; 7:6); it does not
repeal it. Nor does the sanctifying contact language of 6:27 mean peace-offering meat
automatically cleanses an unclean eater. See the comparison page above.

**Qualification of ritual versus moral categories:** uncleanness is not identical with
moral wrongdoing, but eating this holy meat while unclean is expressly sanctioned.
This develops the qualification already recorded on [[Clean and Unclean]] from 5:2–6.
The WEB's cutting-off formula does not here specify an executor, mechanism, or time:
these verses alone do not settle whether the penalty means communal expulsion, death,
or another form of divine exclusion. They also prescribe no purification remedy here.
