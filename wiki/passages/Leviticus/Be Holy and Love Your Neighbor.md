# Be Holy and Love Your Neighbor

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 19:1–18

## Summary

Yahweh commands Moses to address the whole congregation: Israel is to be holy because
its God is holy (1–2). Respect for parents, Sabbath keeping, and rejection of idols lead
into the accepted peace-offering meal and its third-day prohibition (3–8).

Fields and vineyards must leave produce for the poor and foreigner (9–10). The laws
reject theft, deceit, false oaths, oppression, delayed wages, and abuse of deaf or blind
people (11–14). Courts must favor neither poverty nor greatness; slander and endangering
the neighbor’s life are forbidden (15–16).

The closing commands reach inward hatred and the handling of wrongs. Rebuke is required,
vengeance and grudges are forbidden, and love of the neighbor as oneself is commanded
(17–18). Repeated divine self-identification holds ritual and social obligations together.

## Key verses

> **19:2** “Speak to all the congregation of the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘You shall be holy; for I, Yahweh your God, am holy.

> **19:10** You shall not glean your vineyard, neither shall you gather the fallen grapes of your vineyard. You shall leave them for the poor and for the foreigner. I am Yahweh your God.

> **19:14** “‘You shall not curse the deaf, nor put a stumbling block before the blind; but you shall fear your God. I am Yahweh.

> **19:15** “‘You shall do no injustice in judgment. You shall not be partial to the poor, nor show favoritism to the great; but you shall judge your neighbor in righteousness.

> **19:17** “‘You shall not hate your brother in your heart. You shall surely rebuke your neighbor, and not bear sin because of him.

> **19:18** “‘You shall not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge against the children of your people; but you shall love your neighbor as yourself. I am Yahweh.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh grounds the whole congregation’s holiness in his own (2), and repeatedly names himself as the authority behind worship and neighbor-directed commands.
- [[Moses]] — Moses receives the charge to speak to all the congregation (1–2). The address includes worship, family respect, economic protections, justice, and love; it is not restricted to priests.

## Places

- [[Horeb]] — The Sinai setting is contextual from Leviticus 7:38; 19:1–18 names no mountain or settlement. Its fields and vineyards anticipate agricultural life without narrating a move from Sinai.

## Themes

- [[Holiness]] — The command to all Israel rests on Yahweh’s own holiness (2). Parent-respect, Sabbath, worship, harvest sharing, truthful dealing, and love belong within one address. Holiness here includes social conduct as well as sacred boundaries.
- [[Love]] — Love of the neighbor as oneself (18) stands beside the rejection of inward hatred, vengeance, and grudges (17–18). Rebuke is commanded too: love is compatible with truthful correction. This develops the family preferences of Genesis into an obligation toward the neighbor.
- [[Sabbath]] — Keeping Yahweh’s Sabbaths stands beside respect for mother and father (3). The address to the whole congregation brings sacred time into the same instruction as everyday treatment of neighbors.
- [[Idolatry]] — Israel must neither turn to idols nor make molten gods (4). The prohibition belongs to the call for the congregation to reflect Yahweh’s holiness, not only to regulations for priests.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — Peace offerings may be eaten on the offering day and the next; third-day remains are burned (5–8). A tension in scope remains: this general wording gives the window of the vow/freewill case in 7:16–18, while thanksgiving in 7:15 has a same-day deadline. Chapter 19 does not explicitly repeal that distinction.
- [[Judgment]] — Eating the third-day sacrifice brings liability and cutting off (7–8). Human judgment must favor neither the poor nor the great (15). Provision for poor people in 9–10 is compatible with impartial verdicts rather than permission to distort them.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — Harvest corners, gleanings, and fallen grapes must remain for poor people and foreigners (9–10). Possession of productive land carries obligations toward residents who need its produce; no named location is supplied.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — Field and vineyard owners must leave produce for the poor and foreigner (9–10), and employers must not keep wages overnight (13). Ownership does not authorize exhaustive collection or delayed payment.
- [[Work]] — The harvest includes provision for others (9–10), and a hired worker’s wages must not remain overnight until morning (13). Labor creates an obligation on the employer rather than merely an opportunity for gain.
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — Leaving food for the poor and foreigner and refusing to abuse deaf or blind people (9–10, 14) give protection practical form. These are thematic examples of care; no claim is made that a particular Hebrew mercy word occurs here.
- [[Integrity]] — Truthfulness, fair wages, and impartial judgment (11–15) make the holiness command concrete in dealings with neighbors. Worship and reliable conduct are required together.
- [[Deception]] — Lying and deceiving one another are explicitly forbidden beside stealing (11). Unlike narratives reporting deception without a verdict, this instruction directly prohibits it.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — False swearing by God’s name profanes that name (12). The rule prohibits false oaths; it does not here prohibit every oath.
- [[The Names of God]] — Yahweh’s repeated self-identification grounds the commands, and false swearing by his name is called profanation (12). The name belongs to the ethics of speech as well as worship.
- [[The Fear of God]] — The ban on cursing the deaf and placing a stumbling block before the blind ends with the demand to fear God (14). Reverence is expressed through restraint toward people vulnerable to abuse.
- [[Language and Speech]] — Lies, false oaths, cursing the deaf, and slander are forbidden (11–16); rebuking a neighbor is required (17). The passage distinguishes harmful speech from correction instead of treating silence as the only righteous response.
- [[Vengeance]] — Taking vengeance and bearing a grudge against one’s people are prohibited, and love of the neighbor is commanded (18). Private retaliation is rejected; the same passage still requires righteous judgment (15).
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — Hatred in the heart, vengeance, and grudges are rejected, while rebuke remains an obligation (17–18). Peace therefore need not mean concealing a wrong or refusing correction.
- [[Brotherhood]] — Brother, neighbor, and the children of one’s people appear together in the commands against hatred and vengeance (17–18). Responsibility for another reaches the heart and the handling of conflict, not only outward injury.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — Moses must address the whole congregation (1–2). Worship, parents, economic dealings, courts, and inner hatred are joined in one divine instruction; the passage does not separate neighbor-love from obedience.
- [[Sin]] — Eating the profaned offering incurs iniquity (8), and the rebuke command warns against bearing sin because of the neighbor (17). The latter clause does not specify every circumstance in which silence or correction creates liability.
- [[Righteousness]] — Judging the neighbor in righteousness excludes partiality both to the poor and to the great (15). Material provision for the poor (9–10) and impartial adjudication are complementary duties.
- [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] — The poor and foreigner receive harvest provision (9–10); hired workers, deaf people, and blind people receive explicit protections (13–14). Verse 15 rejects favoritism toward the poor in court, distinguishing support from a biased verdict.
- [[Theft and Restitution]] — Stealing, oppression, robbery, and keeping wages overnight are prohibited (11–13). This unit states obligations but supplies no new repayment multiplier, leaving the earlier restitution laws distinct.
- [[Life and Death]] — The WEB forbids endangering the neighbor’s life (16), adjacent to its slander prohibition. The passage protects life within ordinary communal dealings; it does not narrate a death.

## Notes

**One address.** The juxtaposition of holy meals, harvest provision, and love prevents
treating holiness here as only a priestly or ritual status. The audience is all Israel;
no individual poor person, foreigner, parent, or worker is named.

**Giving and judging.** The obligation to leave food for poor people (9–10) does not
contradict the refusal of partiality toward them in court (15). These govern different
actions: provision and verdict.

**Rebuke and love.** Verse 17’s final warning about bearing sin because of the neighbor
does not explain every case of responsibility. The paragraph rules out treating love
as either concealed hatred or automatic avoidance of correction.

**Scope.** Verse 18 explicitly mentions the children of one’s people; verse 10 already
protects foreigners. The later command to love the foreigner as oneself in 19:34 is
verified in the stored chapter but belongs to the next queued pericope. It is not
ingested here, and the narrower wording of 18 is not permission to harm outsiders.

**Offering-law tension.** The general two-day allowance in 19:5–8 must be read beside
the thanksgiving meal’s same-day limit in 7:15. Neither is silently replaced; the
connection page records their differing scope and sanctions.
