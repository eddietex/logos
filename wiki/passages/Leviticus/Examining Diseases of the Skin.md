# Examining Diseases of the Skin

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 13:1–46

## Summary

Yahweh gives Moses and Aaron rules for examining skin conditions. A person with a suspicious
swelling, scab, or bright spot is brought to Aaron or one of his priestly sons. The priest
examines the appearance, hair, raw flesh, and spread; uncertain cases may require seven days
of isolation and sometimes a second seven days before a ruling. A later change can reopen
a case already pronounced clean.

The law distinguishes chronic disease, whole-body whiteness and raw flesh, lesions after
healed boils or burns, head and beard conditions, harmless spots, and ordinary baldness.
Not every visible difference is unclean. The person pronounced leprous must wear torn
clothes, leave the hair loose, cover the upper lip, announce uncleanness, and dwell alone
outside the camp for as long as the plague remains.

## Key verses

> **13:1** Yahweh spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying,

> **13:2** “When a man shall have a rising in his body’s skin, or a scab, or a bright spot, and it becomes in the skin of his body the plague of leprosy, then he shall be brought to Aaron the priest, or to one of his sons, the priests:

> **13:6** The priest shall examine him again on the seventh day; and behold, if the plague has faded, and the plague hasn’t spread in the skin, then the priest shall pronounce him clean. It is a scab. He shall wash his clothes, and be clean.

> **13:11** it is a chronic leprosy in the skin of his body, and the priest shall pronounce him unclean. He shall not isolate him, for he is already unclean.

> **13:13** then the priest shall examine him; and, behold, if the leprosy has covered all his flesh, he shall pronounce him clean of the plague. It has all turned white: he is clean.

> **13:14** But whenever raw flesh appears in him, he shall be unclean.

> **13:37** But if in his eyes the itch is arrested, and black hair has grown in it; the itch is healed, he is clean. The priest shall pronounce him clean.

> **13:39** then the priest shall examine them; and behold, if the bright spots on the skin of their body are a dull white, it is a harmless rash, it has broken out in the skin; he is clean.

> **13:40** “If a man’s hair has fallen from his head, he is bald. He is clean.

> **13:45** “The leper in whom the plague is shall wear torn clothes, and the hair of his head shall hang loose. He shall cover his upper lip, and shall cry, ‘Unclean! Unclean!’

> **13:46** All the days in which the plague is in him he shall be unclean. He is unclean. He shall dwell alone. Outside of the camp shall be his dwelling.

## Themes
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — Visible skin change does not automatically mean uncleanness: dull white spots and ordinary baldness are clean (38–41). Whole-body whiteness can be clean while raw flesh is unclean (12–17). The specified signs and their development govern the ruling. Unlike the contact cases in 5:2–6, these rules name neither guilt nor confession. The distinction between ritual status and moral fault remains necessary without erasing the guilt clauses elsewhere.
- [[Priesthood]] — The priest examines depth, hair, raw flesh, and spread, sometimes after one or two seven-day periods. He pronounces status and orders isolation; the unit gives him no procedure for curing the disease. The teaching commission of 10:10–11 now includes case-by-case judgment, not only communication of household rules.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — The law differentiates skin eruptions, lesions after boils and burns, head or beard conditions, harmless spots, and baldness. An earlier clean verdict can require review if a lesion spreads (7–8, 35–36). Obedience includes returning for inspection and, for the person declared leprous, public warning and residence outside the camp (45–46).
- [[Suffering]] — The final rules add social separation to bodily affliction: torn clothes, loose hair, a covered upper lip, a warning cry, and solitary residence outside the camp (45–46). The unit does not name an offense that caused the disease. Its classification must not be turned into an accusation that every afflicted person deserves the affliction.

## Notes

### What the priest decides

This is a law of examination and status, not a narrated healing. The WEB calls the condition
leprosy, but this page does not identify it with a particular modern diagnosis. The text’s
own distinctions are more useful here than an imported medical label: white hair with a
deeper-looking lesion differs from dull white spots or uncomplicated baldness.

The whole-body case is especially important (12–17). Extensive whiteness can receive a
clean verdict, while raw flesh makes the person unclean. The chapter therefore cannot be
summarized as declaring every extensive visible skin change unclean. It gives no explanation
for that distinction. No modern theory of contagion is asserted as its stated rationale.

Verse 11’s instruction not to isolate the already-unclean person concerns the preliminary
observation used for uncertain cases; it does not grant residence inside the camp against
verse 46. The unit distinguishes waiting for a ruling from the restrictions following it.
In verses 6 and 34, washing clothes follows a clean ruling; the unit does not prescribe
an offering. The restoration rites of chapter 14 remain a separate queued pericope.

### Social cost and moral judgment

The requirements in verses 45–46 mark the condition publicly and impose separation. They
are not a confession of a specified sin. The torn clothes and loose hair also reverse
what Aaron and his sons were told during the crisis of 10:6–7; the contrast is explored
in [[Torn Clothes and Loose Hair - Leviticus 10 and 13]].
