# The Holiness of the Priests

**Book:** [[Leviticus]]
**Reference:** Leviticus 21:1–24

## Summary

Yahweh tells Moses to instruct Aaron’s priestly sons about death, mourning practices,
marriage, and holiness (1–9). Ordinary priests receive an exception for specified close
relatives to the rule against corpse defilement. The high priest’s anointing and garments
accompany stricter rules: no corpse defilement even for parents, no loose hair or torn
clothes, and marriage to a virgin of his own people (10–15).

A second speech regulates which descendants of Aaron may approach to offer (16–23).
The listed bodily defects exclude altar and veil approach, but the affected descendant
may still eat holy and most holy food. Moses delivers the instructions to Aaron, his sons,
and all Israel (24). The unit preserves both the exclusion from service and the provision
for those excluded.

## Key verses

> **21:6** They shall be holy to their God, and not profane the name of their God; for they offer the offerings of Yahweh made by fire, the bread of their God; therefore they shall be holy.

> **21:8** You shall sanctify him therefore; for he offers the bread of your God. He shall be holy to you; for I Yahweh, who sanctify you, am holy.

> **21:11** He must not go in to any dead body, or defile himself for his father, or for his mother.

> **21:22** He shall eat the bread of his God, both of the most holy, and of the holy.

> **21:23** He shall not come near to the veil, nor come near to the altar, because he has a defect; that he may not profane my sanctuaries, for I am Yahweh who sanctifies them.’”

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh grounds priestly obligations in his holiness and sanctifying action (6–8, 15, 23). He restricts approach by descendants with bodily defects while expressly preserving their holy-food portion (22).
- [[Moses]] — Moses receives two speeches, one for the priests and one addressed through him to Aaron (1, 16–17). He communicates the instructions to Aaron, his sons, and all Israel (24).
- [[Aaron]] — Aaron is named as the ancestor of the priestly line and recipient of the bodily-eligibility instruction (1, 17, 21, 24). The high-priest rules describe an anointed office (10–15); the chapter does not narrate a new anointing or identify a disabled descendant by name.

## Places

- [[Horeb]] — The Sinai setting is supplied by the book’s context (Leviticus 7:38), not a mountain named in chapter 21. The sanctuary, veil, and altar are the locations explicitly relevant to the approach restrictions (12, 23).

## Themes

- [[Priesthood]] — Ordinary priests may incur corpse defilement for specified close relatives (1–3), but the high priest may not do so even for parents (10–12). Bodily defects bar descendants of Aaron from altar and veil approach, yet not from eating holy and most holy food (17–23). Descent, service eligibility, and food entitlement are distinct.
- [[Holiness]] — Yahweh’s holiness and sanctifying action ground priestly conduct and sanctuary boundaries (6–8, 15, 23). The chapter restricts service by bodily condition without declaring that condition a personal sin; retained holy-food entitlement prevents equating every restriction with exclusion from the priestly household.
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — Priests may defile themselves for the close relatives enumerated in 2–3, while the high priest may not even for parents (11). Hair, beard, cutting, and torn-clothes rules regulate bodily practices (5, 10); they do not state that grief itself is forbidden. Leviticus 10:6–7 applies similar restrictions to Aaron and his surviving sons during the inauguration crisis.
- [[Marriage]] — Verse 7 prohibits priests from marrying a prostitute, a profane woman, or a divorced woman. The high priest must marry a virgin of his people and may not marry a widow (13–15). The widow restriction belongs explicitly to that office; these rules should not be restated as marriage bans for every Israelite.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — Corpse defilement has a limited close-kin exception for ordinary priests (1–3) and none for the high priest even for parents (11). Bodily defects in 17–23 restrict approach but do not receive a cleansing rite or a declaration of moral guilt. Verse 22 expressly permits holy food; defect, corpse impurity, and sin must not be collapsed.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — Priests offer the bread of God and offerings made by fire (6, 8, 21). A descendant with a defect cannot offer but may eat both holy and most holy food (22). This distinguishes serving at the altar from receiving the priestly portion.
- [[Life and Death]] — Death affects priestly access through corpse defilement and differentiated kinship exceptions (1–3, 11). Verse 9 also prescribes burning for a priest’s daughter who prostitutes herself; it is a legal sanction, not a narrated execution.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — The instructions distinguish ordinary priests, the high priest, and descendants barred from approach by bodily defects. Moses transmits them to priests and all Israel (24), so the community also knows the boundaries and obligations attached to priestly office.
- [[Sexual Sin]] — The priest’s daughter who prostitutes herself is said to profane her father and is sentenced to burning (9). Marriage eligibility is separately regulated in 7 and 13–15; widowhood or disability is not thereby identified as a sexual offense.
- [[Judgment]] — Leviticus 21:9 prescribes burning for a priest’s daughter who prostitutes herself. The chapter does not report an execution or identify an actual woman. The bodily restrictions of 17–23 are not described as punishments for personal wrongdoing.
- [[The Dwelling of God]] — The high priest must not leave or profane the sanctuary in the context of the death and mourning instructions (10–12). Descendants with defects may not approach the veil or altar (23), even though their right to holy food is retained (22). Sanctuary access and household provision have distinct boundaries.
- [[The Names of God]] — Priests must not profane the name of their God because they present his offerings (6). Yahweh identifies himself as holy and as the one who sanctifies the people, priest, and sanctuaries (8, 15, 23); the name accompanies concrete obligations.

## Notes

**Different scopes, not a silently erased tension.** Leviticus 10:6–7 restricts Aaron and
his surviving sons after the deaths of Nadab and Abihu; 21:1–3 permits ordinary priests
to defile themselves for certain close kin, while 21:10–12 gives the high priest stricter
rules. The earlier crisis and this standing differentiation are both retained. Chapter 21
does not explicitly explain the scope of every command in chapter 10.

**Bodily exclusion is real, but not a verdict of personal guilt.** Verses 17–23 exclude
people with the listed conditions from specified service. The text does not say they caused
their conditions by sin, prescribe healing, or remove their holy-food entitlement. This
is neither unrestricted participation nor total expulsion. The ancient categories in the
WEB list should not be converted into confident modern diagnoses.

**Limits of the wording.** Verse 4 is retained as the WEB’s chief-man formulation; no
source-language interpretation is asserted here. The close-relative list in 2–3 does not
explicitly name a wife. Verse 12’s prohibition on leaving the sanctuary occurs within
mourning instructions; the chapter does not establish that the high priest never leaves
for any purpose. The priest’s daughter in 9 is a hypothetical legal case, not a named figure.
Aaron’s sons are addressed collectively, so no individual son is assigned an action.
