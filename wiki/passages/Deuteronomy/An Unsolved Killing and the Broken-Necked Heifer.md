# An Unsolved Killing and the Broken-Necked Heifer

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 21:1–9

## Summary

Chapter 19 dealt with the killer who is known: the unwitting one who flees, and the murderer whom the
elders hand over. Chapter 21 opens with the case the courts of chapter 19 cannot reach — a body, and no
killer at all. *"If someone is found slain in the land which Yahweh your God gives you to possess, lying
in the field, and it isn't known who has struck him"* (1). There is no one to try, no witness to count
(19:15), no one to hand to the avenger (19:12). The blood has still been shed, and the law still holds that
it must be put away. The nine verses are what Israel does when justice has nothing to work on.

**Measuring (21:1–2).** *"Your elders and your judges shall come out, and they shall measure to the
cities which are around him who is slain"* (2). The first act is not an investigation of the crime but a
determination of responsibility by distance: which town is nearest to the body.

**The heifer and the valley (21:3–4).** The elders of that town take *"a heifer of the herd, which
hasn't been worked with, and which has not drawn in the yoke"* (3). They bring it down *"to a valley with
running water, which is neither plowed nor sown"*, and *"break the heifer's neck there in the valley"*
(4). Both the animal and the place are unused: a heifer never put to work, a valley never worked. The
Hebrew uses one verb for both, *ʿābad*, *work* (see Notes).

**The priests come near (21:5).** *"The priests the sons of Levi shall come near; for them Yahweh your
God has chosen to minister to him, and to bless in Yahweh's name; and according to their word shall every
controversy and every assault be decided"* (5). The verse says why they are there and gives them nothing
to do in the rite. Every act in it — measuring, fetching, breaking the neck, washing, speaking — belongs
to the elders or the judges. The priests' stated role is that of the court of 17:8–9 and 19:17, where the
hard case and the disputed testimony come *"before the priests"*.

**The washing and the oath (21:6–8).** *"All the elders of that city, who are nearest to the slain man,
shall wash their hands over the heifer whose neck was broken in the valley"* (6). They declare two
things: *"Our hands have not shed this blood, neither have our eyes seen it"* (7) — they did not do it,
and they did not witness it. Then a prayer: *"Forgive, Yahweh, your people Israel, whom you have redeemed,
and don't allow innocent blood among your people Israel"* (8). The verse ends with the result, not a
further command: *"The blood shall be forgiven them."*

**The purpose (21:9).** *"So you shall put away the innocent blood from among you, when you shall do that
which is right in Yahweh's eyes."* This is the phrase of 19:13, where the murderer was handed over and
Israel was to *"purge the innocent blood from Israel"*. There the blood was put away by the death of the
one who shed it. Here no one dies but a heifer.

**How the passage fits together.** The rite is built for the gap between two convictions the law holds
at once. The first is that a killing leaves a liability on the land and on the people, whether or not the
killer is found — 19:10 has already called it *"blood guilt on you"*. The second is that only the guilty
may be punished, and only on witnesses (19:15). An unsolved killing sets them against each other: the
blood claims someone, and there is no one the court may give it. The rite does not resolve the
contradiction by finding a culprit. It assigns responsibility by geography to a town that is not accused,
has the town swear that it is innocent and ignorant, and asks Yahweh to cover what the court cannot.

**Is it a sacrifice?** The text does not say so, and much of it points the other way. The heifer is not
brought to the place Yahweh chooses (12:5), it is not slaughtered (*šāḥaṭ*) but has its neck broken, no
blood is collected or sprinkled, there is no altar, and nothing is burned. The priests, who in Leviticus
do the sacrificial work, do none here. The verb *kipper*, which Leviticus uses of sacrifice, appears in
the prayer (8), but it is addressed to Yahweh as a petition, not stated as the effect of the heifer's
death. Readers have explained the heifer's death in at least three ways:

- *A re-enactment.* The killing is done again in the open field, this time officially, so that the town's
  elders can stand over it and disown it.
- *A substitute.* The heifer dies in the place of the unknown killer, whose death 19:13 and Numbers 35:33
  would otherwise require.
- *A removal.* The blood's liability is carried off to an untilled, unused place with water that does not
  stop, where it will touch no crop and no field.

The wiki records all three and adopts none. The text names only the outcome: the blood is *"forgiven"*
and *"put away"*.

**Numbers 35:33 and Deuteronomy 21:8.** Numbers states the rule without exception: *"No atonement can be
made for the land for the blood that is shed in it, but by the blood of him who shed it"* (35:33, stored
WEB). The Hebrew verb is *kipper* (WLC). Deuteronomy 21:8 asks Yahweh to *kipper* for his people when the
one who shed the blood cannot be found. The two texts are not reconciled in either book. One reading
takes Numbers to govern the case where the killer is known, and Deuteronomy 21 to supply the one case it
does not cover. Another notes that Numbers speaks of atonement *for the land* and Deuteronomy of atonement
*for your people* (WLC: *lǝʿammǝkā*). The difference is recorded here, not harmonized. See
[[Do Not Cover My Blood]].

**Where it sits.** The law stands between the law of war (20:1–20) and the law of the captive woman
taken in war (21:10–14). Nothing in 21:1–9 mentions war, and the body is found *"in the field"*, not on a
battlefield. The placement may reflect the subject of killing that both chapters share; the text gives no
reason for it.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh, who gives the land where the body is found (1), chose the priests to minister and
  bless in his name (5), redeemed his people Israel (8), and is asked to forgive them (8); the rite is
  *"that which is right in Yahweh's eyes"* (9).
- [[Moses]] — the speaker, addressing Israel in the second person.
- [[Levi]] — *"the priests the sons of Levi"* (5), chosen to minister, to bless, and to decide every
  controversy.
- The slain man, the unknown killer, the elders, and the judges — legal roles, not individuals. No pages.

## Places

- The land Yahweh gives (1), the field where the body lies (1), the surrounding cities (2), the nearest
  city (3), and the valley with running water (4) — unnamed. No pages.

## Notes

**"Slain."** *Ḥālāl* (21:1, WLC), *one pierced*, the usual word for a man killed by violence, and four
times in the passage (1, 2, 3, 6). In the Torah it is otherwise a word for the dead of violence and war —
the men of Shechem (Genesis 34:27, WEB *"the dead"*), the Midianite slain (Numbers 31:8, 19), and the slain of the sword whose
touch defiles in the open field (Numbers 19:16) — and recurs in Deuteronomy only in the Song, *"the blood
of the slain"* (32:42) (WLC lemma 2491, morphhb; Leviticus 21:7, 14 is a homograph, *profaned*, under the
same lemma number). The man of 21:1 has not died in war, but he has died the way men die in war.

**"Measure."** *Ûmādǝdû* (21:2, WLC), from *mādad*. In Deuteronomy the verb occurs only here (lemma
4058). The text does not say from which point of the body the distance is taken, or what is done if two
towns are equally near. Later Jewish law took up both questions; the Mishnah's tractate Sotah treats the
rite at length. That is described here from general knowledge of the tradition, not from a checked
source.

**"A heifer."** *ʿEglat bāqār* (21:3, WLC), *a heifer of the herd*. *ʿEglâ* occurs in the Torah only here
and at Genesis 15:9, where Abram is told to take *"a heifer three years old"* for the covenant between the
pieces (WLC lemma 5697). It is a different word from the red heifer of Numbers 19, *pārâ* (Numbers 19:2,
WLC), a full-grown cow. The two rites are often named together and share an unyoked animal (*ʿōl*, *yoke*,
in both: Deuteronomy 21:3; Numbers 19:2, WLC), but the animal, the place, the officiants, and the purpose
all differ. Numbers' heifer is burned for the water of cleansing; Deuteronomy's is killed and nothing is
said of its body.

**"Not worked."** The heifer *lōʾ-ʿubbad bāh*, *has not been worked* (21:3), and the valley *lōʾ-yēʿābēd
bô wǝlōʾ yizzārēaʿ*, *is not worked and is not sown* (21:4, WLC). Both use *ʿābad*, the verb of *serve*
and *work* (lemma 5647). WEB renders the first *"hasn't been worked with"* and the second *"neither
plowed"*. The same verb in the same sense governs the firstborn of the herd, *"You shall do no work with
the firstborn of your herd"* (15:19). In Deuteronomy *ʿōl*, *yoke*, occurs only here and at 28:48, the
*"iron yoke"* of the curse (WLC lemma 5923).

**"Running water."** *Naḥal ʾêtān* (21:4, WLC). *Naḥal* is a wadi, a valley with a stream; *ʾêtān* means
*enduring, perennial*. So the phrase is a valley whose stream does not dry up, which in the hill country
is the exception. In the Torah *ʾêtān* occurs only here, at Genesis 49:24 (*"his bow remained strong"*),
Exodus 14:27 (the sea returned *"to its strength"*), and Numbers 24:21 (*"Your dwelling place is strong"*)
(WLC lemma 386, morphhb; WEB stored). WEB's *"running water"* reads it of the stream, as most translations
do; some older translations read *ʾêtān* of the ground instead — the King James has *"a rough valley"*,
cited here from general knowledge, not a checked source — which fits *"neither plowed nor sown"*. Either way the place is untouched by human use. See [[The Crossing of the Sea]].

**"Break the heifer's neck."** *Wǝʿārǝpû* (21:4, WLC), from *ʿārap*, built on *ʿōrep*, *neck*. In the
Torah the verb occurs only here, at 21:6 (*hāʿărûpâ*, *the one whose neck was broken*), and in the law
of the unredeemed firstborn donkey (Exodus 13:13; 34:20) (WLC lemma 6202). In both laws the neck is broken
because the animal may not be offered in the normal way: the donkey because it has not been redeemed and no
law provides for offering it, the heifer because this is not a sacrifice at the altar. See
[[Break Its Neck - Exodus 13.13, 34.20, and Deuteronomy 21.4]].

**"Every controversy and every assault."** *Kol-rîb wǝkol-nāgaʿ* (21:5, WLC). *Negaʿ* is *a stroke*, *a
blow*; it is the third of the hard cases of 17:8, *"between stroke and stroke"* (WLC: *bên negaʿ
lānegaʿ*), and elsewhere in Deuteronomy only at 24:8, where it is the *"plague of leprosy"* (WLC lemma
5061). *Rîb*, *controversy*, is the word of 17:8 (*dibrê rîbōt*) and 19:17 (*hārîb*). 21:5 thus restates
the jurisdiction of the priests in the vocabulary of the central court.

**"To minister to him, and to bless in Yahweh's name."** *Lǝšārǝtô ûlǝbārēk bǝšēm YHWH* (21:5, WLC). The
pair is 10:8, *lǝšārǝtô ûlǝbārēk bišmô* (WLC), where Levi was set apart *"to minister to him, and to bless
in his name"*. See [[To Bless in His Name - Numbers 6, Deuteronomy 10, and 2 Samuel 6]].

**"Wash their hands."** *Yirḥǎṣû ʾet-yǝdêhem* (21:6, WLC). *Rāḥaṣ* occurs in Deuteronomy only here and at
23:11 (WLC 23:12), where a man unclean by night *"shall bathe himself in water"* before he comes back into
the camp (WLC lemma 7364; stored WEB 23:11). The text does not say that water is used in 21:6, though the
valley has a stream; it says only that the hands are washed *"over the heifer"*.

**"Our hands have not shed."** The consonantal text of 21:7 has *šāpǝkâ*, a feminine singular (*has not
shed*, with *hands* as a collective), and the Masoretic margin reads *šāpǝkû*, plural (*have not shed*).
Morphhb records both, the written form unvocalized and the read form vocalized (WLC 21:7). WEB follows
the read form. The meaning is unaffected.

**"Neither have our eyes seen it."** The declaration has two parts, and the second is the one the town
can actually vouch for. It disclaims knowledge as well as action: had anyone in the town seen the killing,
the case would have a witness and would go to the court of 19:15.

**"Forgive."** *Kappēr* and *wǝnikkappēr* (21:8, WLC), from *kipper*. WEB translates *"Forgive"* and
*"shall be forgiven"*; elsewhere it usually renders this verb *make atonement*, as at 32:43, *"will make
atonement for his land and for his people"* (stored WEB). In Deuteronomy *kipper* occurs only at 21:8
(twice) and 32:43 (WLC lemma 3722). The English *forgive* here does not translate *sālaḥ*, the usual verb
for forgiving. Whether *kipper* in 21:8 means *cover*, *wipe away*, or *ransom* is a question the wider
debate on the verb has not settled; Deuteronomy does not define it. See [[Atonement]].

**"Whom you have redeemed."** *ʾăšer pādîtā* (21:8, WLC), from *pādâ*. The elders' words are Moses' own
from his intercession at Horeb: *"your people and your inheritance, that you have redeemed"* (9:26,
stored WEB; WLC *ʾăšer pādîtā*, the identical form). The verb recurs in Deuteronomy for the redemption from
Egypt (7:8; 13:5; 15:15; 24:18; WLC lemma 6299). The ground of the petition is the same in both places: the
people belong to Yahweh because he bought them out.

**"Innocent blood."** *Dām nāqî* (21:8) and *haddām hannāqî* (21:9, WLC), as at 19:10, 13 (WLC lemma
5355). At 27:25 it is the cursed man who *"takes a bribe to kill an innocent person"* (stored WEB; WLC
*dam nāqî*). At 19:10 the innocent blood is the unwitting killer's, which the cities of refuge protect; at
19:13 and here it is the victim's, which must be put away.

**"Put away."** *Tǝbaʿēr* (21:9, WLC), from *bāʿar*, the verb of the formula *"so you shall remove the evil
from among you"* (13:5; 17:7, 12; 19:19; 21:21; and later), and of 19:13, where it also takes *"the
innocent blood"* as its object (WLC lemma 1197). See
[[Remove the Evil from Among You - Deuteronomy 13.5 and 1 Corinthians 5.13]].

**Washing the hands in the New Testament.** Matthew 27:24 has Pilate take water and wash his hands
*"before the multitude, saying, 'I am innocent of the blood of this righteous person'"*, and the people
answer, *"May his blood be on us, and on our children!"* (27:25; WEB, bible-api.com). Whether Matthew
intends an allusion to Deuteronomy 21:6–8 is debated: Pilate is a Gentile, hand-washing as a sign of innocence
is said to be known outside Israel as well (general knowledge, not checked here), and Matthew does not cite
the law. The Greek of Matthew 27 was not checked here. See
[[Washing the Hands of Innocent Blood - Deuteronomy 21, Psalms 26 and 73, and Matthew 27]].

**What the passage does not settle.** Whether the heifer's death is a re-enactment, a substitute, or a
removal. How 21:8 relates to Numbers 35:33's denial of any atonement without the shedder's blood. What
the priests do. What happens if the killer is found afterwards. Later Jewish tradition answered the last
of these — the killer, if found, is still tried — and records that the rite ceased when murders became too
many for it; both are reported here from general knowledge of the Mishnah, tractate Sotah, not from a
checked source. Each is recorded, none adopted.
