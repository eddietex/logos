# The Covenant in Moab and the Hidden Things

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 29:1–29

## Summary

Moses summons the whole assembly in Moab to an oath that establishes them as Yahweh’s people,
in continuity with the promise to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and in addition to Horeb’s covenant.
Egypt’s signs, wilderness preservation, and the eastern victories ground the appeal, yet the
people have not received a heart to know. Women, children, resident foreigners, and those absent
are included. No individual or tribe may take belonging as permission to turn secretly to idols.

The imagined apostate blesses himself despite the warning; Yahweh instead assigns him the book’s
curse. Future children and foreign observers see a land as ruined as Sodom and ask why. The answer
is covenant abandonment and rival worship. The closing distinction assigns secret things to God
and revealed instruction to the community and its children, so that they may obey.

> **29:10** All of you stand today in the presence of Yahweh your God; your heads, your tribes, your elders, and your officers, even all the men of Israel,

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh establishes Israel as his people by oath (12–13), yet has not given a knowing heart to this day (4). He judges the self-assured apostate (20–21); hidden things belong to him while revealed law belongs to the community (29).
- [[Moses]] — Moses summons all Israel to the covenant in Moab (1–2). His speech joins remembered rescue, an inclusive assembly, warnings against secret apostasy, and the duty to do the revealed law.
- [[Pharaoh (of the Exodus)]] — The king and his servants are recalled as recipients of the witnessed deeds in Egypt (2–3). No personal name or dynasty is supplied.
- [[Sihon]] — His defeat alongside Og is recalled as the prelude to the eastern inheritance (7–8). He is identified as king of Heshbon, not a participant in the present assembly.
- [[Og]] — The king of Bashan is recalled with Sihon as defeated before the eastern land was allotted (7–8). No new battle takes place.
- [[Abraham]] — Named with Isaac and Jacob as a recipient of the oath underlying Israel’s establishment as Yahweh’s people (13). This is ancestral remembrance, not presence in Moab.
- [[Isaac]] — Named with Abraham and Jacob in the ancestral oath (13). The covenant assembly inherits a promise made before its own generation.
- [[Jacob]] — Named with Abraham and Isaac among the fathers to whom Yahweh swore (13). Jacob the ancestor is distinguished from the assembled people Israel.
- [[Reuben]] — The Reubenites receive a share of the defeated kings’ land (8). The reference is to the tribe, not the patriarch personally.
- [[Gad]] — The Gadites receive a share of the eastern inheritance (8). This recalls the descendants’ allotment rather than the patriarch’s own action.
- [[Manasseh]] — The half-tribe of the Manassites shares the eastern inheritance (8). The statement does not assign the whole tribe east of the Jordan.

## Places

- [[Moab (place)]] — The covenant is made in Moab in addition to the covenant at Horeb (1). The location distinguishes this assembly without making it a covenant with the Moabites.
- [[Horeb]] — The earlier covenant at Horeb is expressly distinguished from the covenant commanded in Moab (1). The verse does not say the earlier covenant is abolished.
- [[Egypt]] — The assembly remembers witnessed signs against Pharaoh (2–3), former residence and encountered idols (16–17), and the covenant made at the exodus (25).
- [[Heshbon]] — Sihon is identified as king of Heshbon in the recollection of victory (7). The present assembly is in Moab, not a new battle here.
- [[Bashan]] — Og is named as its king when the two defeated rulers and the eastern inheritance are recalled (7–8).
- [[Sodom]] — Its overthrow is explicitly made a comparison for Israel’s threatened devastated land (23). Covenant membership does not make Israel immune from a fate associated with foreign cities.
- [[Gomorrah]] — Named with Sodom, Admah, and Zeboiim as an example of overthrow (23). The comparison concerns Israel’s future land, not another destruction of Gomorrah.
- [[Canaan]] — The devastated and abandoned land of verses 22–28 is the promised land in context; Canaan is not named. Exile is explained as covenant abandonment and rival worship, not failed divine ownership.
- [[Admah]] — Admah is one of the four cities whose overthrow illustrates the threatened devastation of Israel’s land (Deuteronomy 29:23). The verse names it alongside Sodom and Gomorrah; Genesis 19:24–25 narrates the overthrow of the cities and plain but does not name Admah individually. No precise modern location is assigned here.
- [[Zeboiim]] — Zeboiim is one of the four cities whose overthrow illustrates the threatened devastation of Israel’s land (Deuteronomy 29:23). The verse names it alongside Sodom and Gomorrah; Genesis 19:24–25 narrates the overthrow of the cities and plain but does not name Zeboiim individually. No precise modern location is assigned here.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — The Moab covenant is additional to Horeb (1), grounded in the ancestral oath (13), and embraces those present and absent (14–15). Belonging includes women, children, and resident foreigners (10–11); it gives no immunity to secret apostasy.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — Keeping and doing frame the address (9, 29). Hidden things belong to God, but revealed law obligates the community and its children; uncertainty is not an excuse to disregard what has been disclosed.
- [[Hardening of the Heart]] — Witnessed signs coexist with the absence of a divinely given knowing heart (2–4). The warning also holds the stubborn apostate responsible (18–21). The tension between divine gift and human responsibility is stated, not resolved by equating verse 4 with a deliberate hardening act.
- [[Remembrance]] — Egypt’s signs, forty years of preserved clothing, and the victories over Sihon and Og ground the present summons (2–9). Remembered experience does not itself guarantee understanding (4).
- [[Providence]] — Clothing and shoes endure forty years, and life without bread, wine, or strong drink teaches dependence on Yahweh (5–6). The point is sustained provision; the passage does not say Israel ate nothing.
- [[The Wilderness]] — Forty years of preserved clothing and unusual provision become evidence for knowing Yahweh (5–6). The wilderness past grounds the covenant obligation now.
- [[Signs and Wonders]] — The people saw the signs and wonders in Egypt (2–3), yet lack a knowing heart (4). This stands in tension with Deuteronomy 4:35, where showing has knowledge as its purpose: visible evidence and effective understanding are not treated as identical.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — The entire assembly enters Yahweh’s covenant and oath (10–15), grounded in his oath to the fathers. This is communal allegiance across generations, not an optional private vow.
- [[Idolatry]] — A hidden turning of any individual, household, or tribe toward other gods becomes a poisonous root (16–18). Future observers explain national ruin by abandoned covenant and worship of other gods (25–28).
- [[Curse]] — The person who hears the curse and inwardly claims peace is singled out for the book’s sanctions (19–21). The national devastation that follows exposes the emptiness of that private self-blessing.
- [[Judgment]] — An individual apostate’s erased name and a land made barren stand together (20–28). Sodom’s overthrow becomes the explicit analogy for Israel’s possible fate, explained to future generations as covenant breach.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — Resident foreigners stand within the covenant assembly (11), while a visiting foreigner later witnesses the ruined land (22). These are distinct roles. Possession can give way to uprooting into another land (28).
- [[The Nations]] — Israel has encountered other nations’ idols (16–18); later nations ask why Yahweh devastated Israel’s land (24–28). Their question receives a covenant explanation rather than an account of Yahweh’s defeat.
- [[Wisdom]] — The secret belongs to Yahweh, while revelation is entrusted to the people and their children for obedience (29). Unlike royal investigation in Proverbs 25:2, this verse emphasizes the practical obligation arising from what is known.
- [[Forgiveness]] — Yahweh refuses pardon to the apostate who presumes peace while persisting in stubbornness (19–20). The warning targets defiant self-assurance; it is not a universal denial that repentant sinners may be forgiven.
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — The inward claim of peace despite stubbornness (19) is a form of presumptuous security. Hearing a covenant warning while privately exempting oneself defeats its intended response.

## Notes

**Boundary and voices.** The planned 29:1–29 unit is retained. The covenant notice introduces
the assembly; the future observer’s speech (24–28) interprets the threatened ruin. Its closing
“as it is today” belongs to that quoted explanation in the WEB narrative frame; it does not by
itself settle the date of composition. The shift to divine first-person speech in 5–6 occurs
inside Moses’ address and is retained without smoothing it away.

**Belonging across time.** Those absent in 15 are not individually identified. The references
to later children (22, 29) support an intergenerational reading, but do not restrict the absent
to one named future generation. Tribal names refer to descendants, not resurrected patriarchs;
generic officeholders, workers, and foreigners receive no invented personal identities.

**Tensions retained.** Witnessing and understanding diverge in 2–4 despite the stated purpose
of disclosure in 4:35. Human responsibility remains in 9 and 18–21. The refusal to pardon in
20 addresses defiant presumption. Neither clause is made a universal explanation of every
person’s ignorance or a denial of every possibility of forgiveness. The obscure moist/dry
phrase in 19 is preserved as the WEB gives it; its precise image is left unresolved.

**Sodom as warning.** Verse 23 explicitly names four overthrown cities; Genesis 19:24 names
Sodom and Gomorrah and 19:25 extends the overthrow to the plain. Admah and Zeboiim receive
place pages here without assigning speculative locations. The analogy threatens Israel
itself, not merely its neighbors. Canaan is a contextual identification, not a name in this unit.
