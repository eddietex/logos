# Judges, Worship, and Difficult Cases

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 16:18–17:13

## Summary

After the calendar of the place, the law turns to the gates. It sets up courts in every town, lists
three offences against Yahweh's worship, gives a procedure for the death penalty, and sends the cases
the towns cannot decide up to the chosen place.

**Judges in every gate (16:18–20).** *"You shall make judges and officers in all your gates, which
Yahweh your God gives you, according to your tribes; and they shall judge the people with righteous
judgment"* (16:18). Three prohibitions follow: *"You shall not pervert justice. You shall not show
partiality. You shall not take a bribe, for a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise, and perverts the
words of the righteous"* (19). The goal is life in the land: *"You shall follow that which is
altogether just, that you may live, and inherit the land"* (20).

**Three things Yahweh will not accept (16:21–17:1).** No *"Asherah of any kind of tree"* is to be
planted beside Yahweh's altar (16:21). No *"sacred stone which Yahweh your God hates"* is set up
(22). No ox or sheep *"in which is a defect, or anything evil"* is sacrificed, *"for that is an
abomination to Yahweh your God"* (17:1).

**The worshipper of other gods (17:2–7).** A man or woman in any of the towns who has *"served other
gods, and worshiped them, or the sun, or the moon, or any of the stars of the sky, which I have not
commanded"* (2–3) is not put to death on report alone. *"You shall inquire diligently. Behold, if it
is true, and the thing certain"* (4), the person is brought out *"to your gates"* and stoned (5). The
rule of evidence is stated here for the first time in Deuteronomy: *"At the mouth of two witnesses,
or three witnesses, he who is to die shall be put to death. At the mouth of one witness he shall not
be put to death"* (6). The witnesses' hands are first, then the hands of all the people, *"So you
shall remove the evil from among you"* (7).

**The case too hard (17:8–13).** *"If there arises a matter too hard for you in judgment, between
blood and blood, between plea and plea, and between stroke and stroke"*, the parties *"go up to the
place which Yahweh your God chooses"* (8). There they come *"to the priests who are Levites, and to
the judge who shall be in those days"* and receive the verdict (9). That verdict is binding: *"You
shall not turn away from the sentence which they shall show you, to the right hand, nor to the
left"* (11). The man who *"does presumptuously, in not listening to the priest … or to the judge,
even that man shall die"* (12), and *"All the people shall hear, and fear, and do no more
presumptuously"* (13).

The unit links the local and the central. Chapters 12–16 brought worship to the one place. This
section keeps justice in every town, but makes the place the court of last resort. The same place
that receives the tithe and the feasts also gives the final ruling.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh, who gives the towns (16:18; 17:2), hates the sacred stone (16:22), counts the
  defective offering an abomination (17:1), and before whom the priest stands to minister at the
  place he chooses (17:8, 10, 12).
- [[Moses]] — the speaker, unnamed here. At 17:3 his voice becomes Yahweh's, *"which I have not
  commanded"*; see the note *Which I have not commanded*.
- The judges, officers, witnesses, *"the priests who are Levites"*, and *"the judge who shall be in
  those days"* are offices, not named people; none gets a page. Levi's tribe is in view in *"the
  priests who are Levites"* (17:9), but the passage names no person.

## Places

- *"All your gates"* (16:18; 17:2, 5, 8) — Israel's towns, where the courts sit and where the
  condemned is stoned. No place page.
- The chosen place (17:8, 10) — the court for cases too hard for the towns, still unnamed. No place
  page; see [[The Place Yahweh Will Choose]].

## Notes

### Judges and officers

*Šōpəṭîm wəšōṭərîm*, *judges and officers* (WLC 16:18, morphhb). In the Torah *šōṭēr* first names
the Israelite foremen in Egypt, *"whom Pharaoh's taskmasters had set over them"* and who *"were
beaten"* (Exodus 5:14; also 5:6, 10, 15, 19). It then names the elders' officers at Numbers 11:16,
the tribal officers of Deuteronomy 1:15, the officers who address the army at 20:5, 8–9, and the
officers who stand in the covenant assembly (29:10 WEB, 29:9 Hebrew) and are summoned at 31:28 (WLC
lemma search across Genesis–Deuteronomy, morphhb). Their task is not defined here. Many readers take
them as clerks or court officers who carry out the judges' decisions, but the verse names the role
without describing it.

The judges are appointed *"according to your tribes"* (16:18), as the heads were at 1:13 and 1:15. The
text does not say who makes the appointment. The verb *titten*, *you shall give* or *set*, is
singular and addressed to Israel as a whole.

### Pervert, show partiality, take a bribe

*Lōʾ-taṭṭeh mišpāṭ lōʾ takkîr pānîm wəlōʾ-tiqqaḥ šōḥad* (WLC 16:19, morphhb). The three clauses repeat
earlier law. *Lōʾ taṭṭeh mišpaṭ* is Exodus 23:6. *Hakkîr pānîm*, *to recognize faces*, is the idiom
of Deuteronomy 1:17, where it is plural, *lōʾ-takkîrû*, because Moses addresses the judges. Here it is
singular, addressed to Israel. The bribe clause comes from Exodus 23:8 almost word for word. The
difference is who is blinded: *piqḥîm*, *those who have sight* (WEB, Exodus 23:8), or *ʿênê
ḥăkāmîm*, *the eyes of the wise*. The verb *ʿiwwēr*, *blinds*, occurs in the Torah only in these two
verses (WLC lemma search, morphhb). Exodus says the bribe blinds someone who can see. Deuteronomy says
it blinds someone who is wise. **The bribe does not need a corrupt judge to work. It corrupts a good
one.**

*Šōḥad*, *bribe*, occurs in the Torah only at Exodus 23:8 (twice), Deuteronomy 10:17, 16:19 (twice),
and 27:25 (WLC lemma search, morphhb). At 10:17 Yahweh is the one who *"doesn't respect persons, nor
takes reward"*, and the WEB's *reward* there is *šōḥad*. At 27:25 *"Cursed is he who takes a bribe
to kill an innocent person"*. So the bribe is first something God refuses, then something the judge
must refuse, and then something the assembly curses.

### Justice, justice

*Ṣedeq ṣedeq tirdōp*, *justice, justice you shall pursue* (WLC 16:20, morphhb). The WEB renders the
doubling *"that which is altogether just"*. **This is the only verse in the Torah where *ṣedeq*
occurs twice** (WLC lemma search across Genesis–Deuteronomy, morphhb; the noun's other Torah uses are
Leviticus 19:15, 36; Deuteronomy 1:16; 16:18; 25:15; 33:19). The verb *rādap* is the ordinary word for
pursuing an enemy. Readers explain the doubling in different ways: as emphasis, as justice in both
the verdict and the means of reaching it, or as justice for both parties. The verse does not say
which. Its stated motive is *"that you may live, and inherit the land"*, so justice is a condition of
entering and keeping the land, as obedience is at 4:1, 6:18, and 11:8.

### An Asherah planted, a stone set up

*Lōʾ-tiṭṭaʿ ləkā ʾăšērâ kol-ʿēṣ*, *you shall not plant for yourself an Asherah, any tree* (WLC 16:21,
morphhb). The verb is *nāṭaʿ*, *plant*, which in Genesis is used of Abraham's tamarisk at Beersheba
(*wayyiṭṭaʿ*, Genesis 21:33, morphhb). *ʾĂšērâ* occurs in the Torah only four times: Exodus 34:13;
Deuteronomy 7:5; 12:3; and here (WLC lemma search, morphhb). The first three command Israel to cut
down or burn *their* Asherah poles. **Only here is Israel forbidden to make one, and to make it
beside Yahweh's own altar.** That last detail suggests a real temptation to worship Yahweh with the
Asherah alongside, not a fear of converting to another god. *Kol-ʿēṣ*, *any tree* or *any wood*,
leaves it open whether the Asherah was a living tree or a carved pole. The planting verb points to a
tree; the destruction verbs of 7:5 and 12:3 (*cut down*, *burn*) fit either. The name is also the
name of a goddess in texts outside the Bible. The verse does not mention her, and the wiki does not
build on that here.

*Wəlōʾ-tāqîm ləkā maṣṣēbâ ʾăšer śānēʾ YHWH*, *you shall not raise for yourself a sacred stone, which
Yahweh hates* (WLC 16:22, morphhb). The WEB has *"sacred stone"* here and *"pillar"* elsewhere for the
same noun. *Maṣṣēbâ* is the word for Jacob's pillars at Bethel and Galeed and over Rachel's grave
(Genesis 28:18, 22; 31:13, 45, 51–52; 35:14, 20), and for the twelve pillars Moses set up at Sinai
(Exodus 24:4) (WLC lemma search, morphhb). **Deuteronomy 16:22 is the only place in the Torah that says
Yahweh *hates* the object.** The other prohibitions command destruction or forbid setting one up.
Here the reason is his hatred. The same verb was used at 12:31 for *"every abomination to Yahweh,
which he hates"*. How this fits with Jacob's pillars is set out, with three readings and none chosen,
on [[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]].

### A defect, or anything evil

*Mûm kōl dābār rāʿ*, *a defect, any evil thing* (WLC 17:1, morphhb). *Mûm* is Leviticus' word for
the blemish that bars an animal or a priest (Leviticus 21:17–23; 22:20–25) and was applied to the
firstling at 15:21. The rule here is general: any ox or sheep. It is called *tôʿăbat YHWH*, *an
abomination to Yahweh*, **the word repeated three verses later for the worship of the sun and stars
(17:4).** *Tôʿēbâ* occurs in sixteen verses of Deuteronomy, from the nations' idols (7:25–26) and their
rites (12:31; 18:9, 12) to unclean food (14:3), false weights (25:16), and the image set up in secret
(27:15) (WLC lemma search, morphhb). By joining the cheap sacrifice and the foreign god under one word,
the law treats an offering of contempt as close to worship of another god.

### The three verses that interrupt

16:21–17:1 has no clear tie to the judges around it, and readers divide over why it is here. Some
take the three offences as examples of what the local court must judge, leading into 17:2–7. Others
see a list of worship laws joined to the court laws by a shared idea, that justice and pure worship
together keep the land. Others think the verses have been moved from the worship laws of chapters
12–16. The text gives no link. The wiki keeps the verses in this unit because 17:2 continues the same
subject, the worship of what Yahweh did not command, and because the court procedure that follows
applies to exactly this kind of offence.

### Which I have not commanded

*ʾĂšer lōʾ-ṣiwwîtî*, *which I have not commanded* (WLC 17:3, morphhb). The verb is first person
singular. Moses is the speaker, but the *I* who did not command the worship of the heavens can only
be Yahweh. **The voice of the lawgiver passes into the voice of God without notice.** Deuteronomy
18:20 does the same within Yahweh's quoted speech: *"which I have not commanded him to speak"*. Here
there is no quotation marker. Some read it as Moses speaking for God as his prophet; others as a
sign that the law code is God's speech carried through Moses. The verse does not explain it.

The sun, moon, and stars were named at 4:19, where Israel is warned not to worship them, *"which
Yahweh your God has allotted to all the peoples under the whole sky"*. Chapter 4 placed them in the
nations' worship; chapter 17 imagines them in Israel's towns.

### Two or three witnesses

*ʿAl-pî šənayim ʿēdîm ʾô šəlōšâ ʿēdîm yûmat hammēt lōʾ yûmat ʿal-pî ʿēd ʾeḥād* (WLC 17:6, morphhb).
Numbers 35:30 had made the same rule for murder: *"one witness shall not testify against any person
that he die"*. Deuteronomy 17:6 extends it to the worship of other gods, and 19:15 will make it
general, *"for any iniquity, or for any sin"*. Verse 4 has already required an inquiry before the
witnesses are counted. *"If it is true, and the thing certain"* — *ʾĕmet nākôn haddābār* — is word
for word the test for the apostate town at 13:14 (13:15 Hebrew) (WLC, morphhb).

**The witnesses throw first** (17:7). At 13:9 the enticed relative's hand was *"first on him"*. That
relative was the witness, and the enticer had spoken *"secretly"* (13:6), so the two-witness rule
could not have been met in the same way. The two laws stand side by side, and the text does not
reconcile them. Here, the witnesses who caused the death must also begin it, so a false witness would
become the killer. 19:16–19 will make the punishment for false testimony explicit.

The condemned is brought out *"to your gates"* (17:5) — *ʾel-šəʿārêkā* (WLC, morphhb). The gate is
both where the court sits and where the sentence is carried out, which Deuteronomy repeats for the
rebellious son and the bride (21:19; 22:15, 24).

### The case too hard

*Kî yippālēʾ mimməkā dābār lammišpāṭ*, *if a matter is too hard for you for judgment* (WLC 17:8,
morphhb). The verb is *pālāʾ* in the niphal, *to be beyond*, *to be wonderful*. **The form
*yippālēʾ* with the noun *dābār* occurs in the Torah only here and at Genesis 18:14,** *hăyippālēʾ
mêYHWH dābār*, *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"* (WLC, morphhb; the niphal of *pālāʾ* elsewhere in
the Torah is Exodus 3:20; 34:10, *wonders*, and Deuteronomy 30:11, *"not too hard for you"*). In
Genesis nothing is too hard for Yahweh. Here something is too hard for the court in the town, and it
goes up to Yahweh's place. The connection is lexical; the text does not draw it.

Deuteronomy 1:17 and Exodus 18:26 described the same procedure with another word: *qāšâ*, *hard*,
*"the case that is too hard for you, you shall bring to me"* (WLC, morphhb). In 1:17 the hard case
went to Moses. In chapter 17 it goes to the place, to *"the priests who are Levites, and to the judge
who shall be in those days"*. **Moses' role passes to an institution that will outlast him.** See
[[The Case Too Hard - Exodus 18, Deuteronomy 1.17, and Deuteronomy 17.8]].

The three kinds of case are *bên-dām lədām bên-dîn lədîn ûbên negaʿ lānegaʿ* (WLC 17:8, morphhb). *Dām*
is blood: homicide, or a question of bloodguilt. *Dîn* is a legal claim. *Negaʿ*, *"stroke"* in the WEB,
is in Leviticus 13–14 the *plague* or mark of skin disease, and at Deuteronomy 24:8 *"the plague of
leprosy"*, where the people are to do *"according to all that the priests the Levites teach you"*. It
can also mean a blow struck in assault. Readers who take it as assault see three civil and criminal
categories. Readers who take it as skin disease see a reason for the priests' presence. The verse
does not say which.

### The priests the Levites, and the judge

*Hakkōhănîm halwiyyim*, *the priests the Levites* (WLC 17:9, morphhb). The phrase occurs in the Torah
only in Deuteronomy, at 17:9, 17:18, 18:1, 24:8, and 27:9 (WLC search for the two nouns in sequence,
morphhb). Deuteronomy 18:1 defines it: *"The priests the Levites, all the tribe of Levi"*. Whether
Deuteronomy distinguishes priests from other Levites, as Numbers does, or treats all Levites as
eligible for priesthood, is a long-standing question; 18:1–8 is its main text, now ingested as
[[The Portion of the Priests and Levites]], which records the readings and adopts none. See [[The Priests Who Kept Their Land and the Levites Who Got None]].

*"The judge who shall be in those days"* (9) is a single officer, not a council. Verse 12 also
narrows *"the priests"* to *"the priest who stands to minister there before Yahweh"*. The text does
not say how priest and judge divide the work, whether by kind of case or together on every case.

### Teach, and the right hand or the left

*ʿAl-pî hattôrâ ʾăšer yôrûkā*, *according to the instruction which they instruct you* (WLC 17:11,
morphhb; the WEB has *"the decisions of the law which they shall teach you"*). *Tôrâ* and the verb
*yārâ*, *to teach*, share a root, so the court's ruling is called *tôrâ*, the word Deuteronomy uses for
itself (1:5; 4:44). *"You shall not turn away from the sentence which they shall show you, to the
right hand, nor to the left"* (11) uses the phrase of 5:32, *"You shall not turn away to the right hand
or to the left"*, which there applied to God's commands, and which 17:20 will apply to the king and
28:14 to the covenant. **The court's verdict is put in the same terms as the commandments.**

### Presumption

*Bəzādôn*, *presumptuously* (WLC 17:12, morphhb). The noun *zādôn* occurs in the Torah only here and
at 18:22, of the prophet who speaks a word Yahweh did not speak (WLC lemma search, morphhb). The
related verb *zîd* describes Israel at Kadesh, who *"were presumptuous, and went up into the hill
country"* (1:43), the man who *"schemes and comes presumptuously on his neighbor to kill him"* and is taken
even from the altar (Exodus 21:14), and the prophet of 18:20 (morphhb lemma 2102; the same lemma also covers
Genesis 25:29, where Jacob *boils* stew). Verse 13 uses the verb: *wəlōʾ yəzîdûn ʿôd*, *"and do no more
presumptuously"*. In each case the offender knows the word and acts against it. The death penalty
is for refusal of the verdict, not for losing the case.

*"All the people shall hear, and fear"* (13) repeats 13:11, *"All Israel shall hear, and fear"*, and
anticipates 21:21. The sentence is meant to teach.

### Boundary

The planned boundary (16:18–17:13) is kept. The title is written *Judges, Worship, and Difficult
Cases*, with the commas the plan's draft lacked. 16:18 opens a new subject after the summary of
16:16–17. 17:14 turns from courts to the king, with its own opening, *"When you have come to the land"*.
