# Captives, Firstborn Sons, and Public Shame

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 21:10–23

## Summary

Four short laws, each opening with *"if"* or *"when"*, each about a person whose position in a household
or a town makes them easy to wrong or easy to discard: a woman taken in war, the son of an unloved wife,
a son who will not listen, and the body of an executed man. The first three are household laws; the
fourth is about the land. What runs through all four is a limit set on a power the law does not take
away — the captor's, the father's, the parents', and the executioner's.

**The captive woman (21:10–14).** *"When you go out to battle against your enemies, and Yahweh your God
delivers them into your hands, and you carry them away captive, and see among the captives a beautiful
woman, and you have a desire to her, and desire to take her as your wife"* (10–11). The law assumes the
plunder of 20:14, where *"the women, the little ones"* of a distant city are taken, and the soldier's
desire. It does not forbid the marriage. It puts a month between the desire and the marriage. *"You shall
bring her home to your house. She shall shave her head and trim her nails. She shall take off the
clothing of her captivity, and shall remain in your house, and bewail her father and her mother a full
month"* (12–13). Only *"after that"* may he go in to her and be her husband. If he then has *"no delight
in her"*, he must let her go *"where she desires"*: *"you shall not sell her at all for money. You shall
not deal with her as a slave, because you have humbled her"* (14).

Three things are given to her that a captive does not otherwise have: time, grief for her parents, and,
if the marriage ends, freedom rather than sale. Three things are not given: consent to the marriage, a
choice to stay or go before it, and any means of return to her people. The law records both, and so
does this page. The reason it gives for her release — *"because you have humbled her"* — is the reason
of 22:29, where a man who lies with an unbetrothed girl must marry her and *"may not put her away all
his days"*, *"because he has humbled her"*. The verb is the one Genesis 34:2 uses of Shechem and Dinah
(see Notes, and [[Took Her and Humbled Her - Genesis 34 and Deuteronomy 21.10-14]]).

**The firstborn of the hated wife (21:15–17).** *"If a man has two wives, the one beloved, and the other
hated, and they have borne him children … and if the firstborn son is hers who was hated"* (15), then
when he divides his inheritance he *"may not give the son of the beloved the rights of the firstborn
before the son of the hated, who is the firstborn"* (16). He must *"acknowledge the firstborn … by giving
him a double portion of all that he has; for he is the beginning of his strength. The right of the
firstborn is his"* (17). The father may love whom he likes; he may not let that decide who is firstborn.
The law describes the household of Genesis 29–30 term for term, and forbids what that household does
(see [[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]]).

**The stubborn and rebellious son (21:18–21).** A son *"who will not obey the voice of his father or the
voice of his mother, and though they chasten him, will not listen to them"* (18) is brought by both
parents *"to the elders of his city, and to the gate of his place"* (19). Their charge is short: *"This
our son is stubborn and rebellious. He will not obey our voice. He is a glutton and a drunkard"* (20).
*"All the men of his city shall stone him to death with stones. So you shall remove the evil from among
you. All Israel shall hear, and fear"* (21). The law is the harshest in the pericope, and it is also a
limit. The father does not judge and does not execute. Both parents must agree and bring the charge, the
elders hear it in the gate, and the town carries out the sentence. Exodus 21:15, 17 and Leviticus 20:9
already put to death the son who strikes or curses a parent; this law adds the son who persistently will
not listen, and requires a public hearing first.

**The hanged man (21:22–23).** *"If a man has committed a sin worthy of death, and he is put to death,
and you hang him on a tree; his body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall surely bury
him the same day; for he who is hanged is accursed of God; that you don't defile your land which Yahweh
your God gives you for an inheritance."* The order matters: he is put to death, *and then* hung. The
hanging is the display of a body already dead, not the means of death (see Notes). The law does not
forbid the display; it limits it to one day. The body must be buried before night, because a body left
hanging defiles the land.

**How the passage fits together.** The laws are joined by what they share rather than by a heading. The
first two are both about wives and what a husband's preference may not do — dismiss a captive wife as a
slave, or transfer a son's right by love. The second and third are both about sons and what a father's
power may not do — move the birthright, or put a son to death himself. The third and fourth are both
about death under the law, and both end with the land and the people: *"remove the evil from among you"*
(21) and *"that you don't defile your land"* (23). The pericope begins where chapter 20 left off, in
war, and ends with the land that 21:1–9 also had to keep clean of blood.

**Who is protected, and how far.** The captive is protected from sale, not from marriage. The firstborn
is protected from his father's preference, not from his own conduct — Reuben, who loses his portion in
Genesis 49:4 for his own act at 35:22, is a case the law does not address. The rebellious son is
protected from his father alone, not from the town. The hanged man is protected from exposure, not from
death. In each case the law takes a practice it does not abolish and sets a boundary inside it. The wiki
records the boundaries and does not soften what lies on the far side of them.

**The rebellious son and Israel.** The pair of words in 21:18, 20, *sôrēr ûmôreh*, *stubborn and
rebellious*, uses for its second word the verb (*mārâ*) Deuteronomy uses of Israel against Yahweh: at
Kadesh (1:26, 43), in the retrospect of the calf (9:7, 23, 24), and in Moses' last forecast, *"you have
been rebellious against Yahweh … How much more after my death?"* (31:27). Deuteronomy also calls Israel
Yahweh's son, carried *"as a man does bear his son"* (1:31) and disciplined *"as a man disciplines his
son"* (8:5), with the verb (*yāsar*) the parents use at 21:18. Whether the law is meant to be read
against that portrait of the nation is not said; the shared vocabulary is recorded here, not built into
an argument.

**Galatians 3:13.** Paul quotes 21:23 of the crucifixion: *"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the
law, having become a curse for us. For it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree'"* (WEB,
bible-api.com). Deuteronomy's law is about a body displayed after execution, and Roman crucifixion is
not that practice. Paul's wording also differs from the WEB of 21:23, which says *"accursed of God"*;
the Greek of Galatians and of the Septuagint was not checked here, so whether Paul is following a
Greek form of the verse or adapting it is left open. Acts 5:30 uses the same language, *"whom you
killed, hanging him on a tree"* (WEB, bible-api.com), and John 19:31 has the bodies taken down so they
*"wouldn't remain on the cross on the Sabbath"* (WEB, bible-api.com). See
[[Hanged on a Tree - Genesis 40, Deuteronomy 21, and Galatians 3]].

## Notes

**"Carry them away captive."** *Wǝšābîtā šibyô* (21:10, WLC), verb and noun from *šābâ*. The noun for
the captives as a group in 21:11, 13, *šibyâ*, occurs in Deuteronomy otherwise only in the Song, *"the
blood of the slain and the captives"* (32:42; WLC lemma 7633; stored WEB).

**"A beautiful woman."** *ʾēšet yǝpat-tōʾar* (21:11, WLC), *a woman beautiful of form*. *Tōʾar*, *form*,
occurs in the Torah only here and at Genesis 29:17 (Rachel, *yǝpat-tōʾar*), 39:6 (Joseph), and 41:18–19
(Pharaoh's cows) (WLC lemma 8389). The phrase the law uses of the captive is the narrator's phrase for
Rachel.

**"You have a desire to her."** *Wǝḥāšaqtā bāh* (21:11, WLC), from *ḥāšaq*, *to be attached to, to long
for*. In the Torah the verb is used of a person only four times: Shechem's soul *"longs for"* Dinah
(Genesis 34:8), Yahweh *"set his love on"* Israel (Deuteronomy 7:7) and *"had a delight in"* the fathers
(10:15), and here (WLC lemma 2836; the other uses, Exodus 27:17; 38:17, 28, are a homograph meaning
*filleted*, of the tabernacle's posts). So the same verb names a captor's desire, a rapist's, and God's
election. The text draws no line between them; the wiki notes it and draws none either.

**"Shave her head and trim her nails."** *Wǝgillǝḥâ ʾet-rōʾšāh wǝʿāśǝtâ ʾet-ṣippǝrnêhā* (21:12, WLC), *she
shall shave her head and do her nails*. *Do* is the plain verb *ʿāśâ*, and whether it means *trim* or
*let grow* is an old question; WEB reads *trim*. *Gillaḥ*, *shave*, occurs in the Torah at Genesis 41:14
(Joseph shaves before he comes in to Pharaoh), Leviticus 13:33 and 14:8–9 (the cleansing of skin
disease), 21:5 (forbidden to priests), Numbers 6:9, 18–19 (the Nazirite's vow), and here (WLC lemma 1548).
In most of those it marks the passage from one state into another. It is not the word of 14:1, which
forbids *qorḥâ*, *baldness*, *"for the dead"* (WLC; stored WEB). The text does not say whether the
captive's shaving is a rite of mourning, of purification, or of leaving her people; each has been
proposed, and none is adopted here.

**"The clothing of her captivity."** *Śimlat šibyāh* (21:13, WLC), the clothes she was taken in. Joseph
too *"shaved himself, changed his clothing, and came in to Pharaoh"* (Genesis 41:14, stored WEB) — two
of the same three acts, at another change of household. The resemblance is noted, not argued.

**"A full month."** *Yeraḥ yāmîm* (21:13, WLC), *a month of days*. In the Torah *yeraḥ* occurs only here,
at Exodus 2:2 (Moses hidden *"three months"*), and at Deuteronomy 33:14 (WLC lemma 3391). Israel mourned
Aaron *"thirty days"* (Numbers 20:29) and will mourn Moses the same (Deuteronomy 34:8, stored WEB), so a
month is the length of a full public mourning; the captive is given that for parents who are alive or
dead, the text does not say which.

**"No delight in her."** *Lōʾ ḥāpaṣtā bāh* (21:14, WLC), *you did not delight in her*. The law does not
say what ends the marriage or whether a formal divorce is meant; 24:1–4 is the law code's divorce law.

**"You shall not deal with her as a slave."** *Lōʾ-titʿammēr bāh* (21:14, WLC), from *ʿāmar* in the
hithpael. In the Torah the verb occurs only here and at 24:7, where the kidnapper who
*"deals with him as a slave, or sells him"* dies (WLC lemma 6014; stored WEB 24:7). In both the verb is
paired with *mākar*, *sell*. Its exact sense — *treat as a slave*, *treat with violence*, or *profit by*
— is not settled; WEB's rendering follows the first.

**"Because you have humbled her."** *Taḥat ʾăšer ʿinnîtāh* (21:14, WLC), from *ʿinnâ*, *to afflict, to
humble, to force*. It is the verb of Shechem and Dinah (*wayǝʿannehā*, Genesis 34:2), of Sarai with Hagar
(16:6), of Egypt with Israel (Exodus 1:11–12), and later in this code of the betrothed woman and the
unbetrothed girl (Deuteronomy 22:24, 29) (WLC lemma 6031). Here the man has married her, and the law still
uses it. Whether it describes the marriage itself, the taking in war, or the dismissal is not said; the
consequence is that he has lost the right to sell her.

**"The one beloved, and the other hated."** *Hāʾaḥat ʾăhûbâ wǝhāʾaḥat śǝnûʾâ* (21:15, WLC). The idiom is
comparative, as Genesis 29:30–31 shows: Jacob *"loved also Rachel more than Leah"*, and the next verse says
Leah *"was hated"* (stored WEB). The last word of 21:15 is spelled *laśśǝnîʾâ*, a variant of the same
participle (WLC; lemma 8146 rather than 8130 in morphhb's tagging). See [[Love]].

**"Give … the rights of the firstborn."** *Lǝbakkēr* (21:16, WLC), the verb *bikkēr*, *to treat as
firstborn*. In the Torah it occurs only here and at Leviticus 27:26, of the firstborn animal *"which is
made Yahweh's firstborn"* (WLC lemma 1069; stored WEB). The father cannot make a son firstborn; he can
only acknowledge (*yakkîr*, 17) the one who is.

**"A double portion."** *Pî šǝnayim* (21:17, WLC), *a mouth of two*, two shares. It is the phrase of
Elisha's request, *"a double portion of your spirit"* (2 Kings 2:9, WEB, bible-api.com); the Hebrew of 2
Kings was not checked here, and the identity of the phrase is reported from general knowledge, not a
checked source.

**"The beginning of his strength."** *Rēʾšît ʾônô* (21:17, WLC), the phrase Jacob uses of Reuben,
*rēʾšît ʾônî*, *"the beginning of my strength"* (Genesis 49:3, WLC; stored WEB) — in the speech that takes
Reuben's preeminence away. *"The right of the firstborn"* is *mišpaṭ habbǝkōrâ*; *bǝkōrâ* is the word of
Esau's sold birthright (Genesis 25:31–34; 27:36; WLC lemma 1062). See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

**"Stubborn and rebellious."** *Sôrēr ûmôreh* (21:18, 20, WLC). *Sôrēr* (*sārar*, *to be stubborn*) occurs
in the Torah only in these two verses (WLC lemma 5637). *Môreh* (*mārâ*, *to rebel*) is used elsewhere in
Deuteronomy only of Israel's rebellion against Yahweh: 1:26, 43; 9:7, 23, 24; 31:27 (WLC lemma 4784). The
pair recurs outside the Torah — Psalm 78:8 and Jeremiah 5:23 are commonly cited — but neither was checked
here.

**"Though they chasten him."** *Wǝyissǝrû ʾōtô* (21:18, WLC), from *yāsar*, *to discipline*. Deuteronomy
uses it of Yahweh's discipline of Israel: *"as a man disciplines his son, so Yahweh your God disciplines
you"* (8:5, stored WEB), and of the voice from heaven, *"that he might instruct you"* (4:36); and later of the elders who *"chastise"*
the husband who slanders his wife (22:18) (WLC lemma 3256).

**"Take hold of him."** *Wǝtāpǝśû bô* (21:19, WLC), both parents. The verb is the one Potiphar's wife uses
on Joseph's garment (*"She caught him by his garment"*, Genesis 39:12) and Moses on the tablets (9:17)
(WLC lemma 8610; stored WEB). The law's point is that the father and the mother act together.

**"A glutton and a drunkard."** *Zôlēl wǝsōbēʾ* (21:20, WLC). In the Torah neither word occurs anywhere
else (WLC lemmas 2151, 5435). Proverbs 23:20–21 has the same pair in reverse order, as read from
Sefaria's Masoretic text on the connection page; Proverbs was not rechecked against WLC here. See
[[A Glutton and a Drunkard - Proverbs 23.20-21, Deuteronomy 21.20, and Matthew 11.19]].

**"Stone him to death."** *Ûrǝgāmuhû … bāʾăbānîm* (21:21, WLC), from *rāgam*. In the Torah *rāgam* is used
of the Molech worshipper and the medium (Leviticus 20:2, 27), the blasphemer (24:14, 16, 23), the
Sabbath-breaker (Numbers 15:35–36), the congregation's threat against Joshua and Caleb (14:10), and here
(WLC lemma 7275). The stoning of 13:10 (WLC 13:11); 17:5; 22:21, 24 uses another verb, *sāqal* (WLC lemma 5619).

**"Hear, and fear."** *Wǝkol-yiśrāʾēl yišmǝʿû wǝyirāʾû* (21:21, WLC). The formula, with small variations,
closes the laws at 13:11 (WLC 13:12), 17:13, and 19:20: the sentence is public so that others are deterred.

**"Put to death, and you hang him."** *Wǝhûmāt wǝtālîtā ʾōtô ʿal-ʿēṣ* (21:22, WLC): first the death, then
the hanging, joined by *and*. *Tālâ* occurs in the Torah only at Genesis 40:19, 22; 41:13 (the baker) and
here (WLC lemma 8518). Genesis 40:19 has the same order, the head lifted off and then the body hung *"on a
tree"* for the birds. Deuteronomy 28:66, *"Your life will hang in doubt"*, uses a different word (lemma
8511, *tǝlūʾîm*).

**"Shall not remain all night."** *Lōʾ-tālîn nibltô ʿal-hāʿēṣ* (21:23, WLC). *Lîn*, *to lodge*, is the verb
of 16:4, where nothing of the Passover may *"remain all night until the morning"*. *Nǝbēlâ*, *body*, is
otherwise in the Torah the word for an animal carcass — the unclean animal (Leviticus 11), the beast that
dies of itself (Deuteronomy 14:21) — and in Deuteronomy for the *"dead body"* left to the birds in the
curse (28:26) (WLC lemma 5038; stored WEB). The man is called by the word for a carcass.

**"Accursed of God."** *Qilelat ʾĕlōhîm tālûy* (21:23, WLC), *a curse of God is the hanged one*. The
genitive can be read *cursed by God* or *an insult to God*, and both readings are old; the wiki adopts
neither. *Qǝlālâ* is otherwise in Deuteronomy the word of *"the blessing and the curse"* set before Israel
(11:26–29; 30:1, 19), of Balaam's curse, which Yahweh *"turned … into a blessing"* (23:5, WLC 23:6), and of the curses of chapter
28 (28:15, 45) (WLC lemma 7045). See [[Curse]].

**"Defile your land."** *Wǝlōʾ tǝṭammēʾ ʾet-ʾadmātǝkā* (21:23, WLC), from *ṭimmēʾ*. In the Torah the verb
is overwhelmingly about persons and things (Leviticus 11–15; Numbers 19); the land is its subject or
object at Leviticus 18:25, 27–28 (*"The land was defiled"*; *"when you defile it"*) and Numbers 35:34
(WLC lemma 2930; stored WEB). Numbers 35:34 — *"You shall not defile the land which you
inhabit"* (stored WEB) — closes the law of blood, which links this verse to 21:1–9.

**What the passage does not settle.** Whether the captive consents to anything. Whether her shaving and
change of clothes are mourning, purification, or a sign of leaving her people. Whether *"no delight"*
means a formal divorce. How the law of the firstborn bears on a firstborn who forfeits by his own act, as
