# Laws for Going to War

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 20:1–20

## Summary

Chapter 19 protected life, land, and a good name inside Israel. Chapter 20 turns outward, to the one
setting in which the law expects Israelites to kill on purpose: battle. It is the law code's only
chapter on war, and it is arranged as a campaign — the march out (1), the priest's address before the
fight (2–4), the officers' dismissals (5–8), the appointment of commanders (9), the approach to a
city (10–18), and the long siege (19–20).

**Do not be afraid (20:1).** *"When you go out to battle against your enemies, and see horses,
chariots, and a people more than you, you shall not be afraid of them; for Yahweh your God is with you,
who brought you up out of the land of Egypt."* The enemy Israel is told to picture is the one it
already faced at the sea, where *"all the horses and chariots of Pharaoh"* overtook it (Exodus 14:9).
The ground of courage is the exodus: the God who beat that army is *"with you"*.

**The priest speaks (20:2–4).** Before the battle *"the priest shall approach and speak to the
people"*. His speech opens like the Shema, *"Hear, Israel"* (3; compare 6:4), and stacks four
prohibitions: *"Don't let your heart faint! Don't be afraid, nor tremble, neither be scared of them"*
(3). The reason is a single clause: *"Yahweh your God is he who goes with you, to fight for you against
your enemies, to save you"* (4). It is what Moses said at Kadesh, *"he will fight for you"* (1:30), which
that generation refused to believe.

**The officers send men home (20:5–8).** Then the officers, not the priest, ask four questions, each
ending *"Let him go and return to his house"*:

| Who goes home | Why |
|---|---|
| a man who has built a new house and not dedicated it (5) | *"lest he die in the battle, and another man dedicate it"* |
| a man who has planted a vineyard and not used its fruit (6) | *"lest … another man use its fruit"* |
| a man pledged to a wife and not yet married (7) | *"lest … another man take her"* |
| a man *"fearful and faint-hearted"* (8) | *"lest his brother's heart melt as his heart"* |

The first three are men with something begun and not yet enjoyed: a house, a vineyard, a marriage.
The law will not have another man finish what they started. The fourth is different in kind. The
fearful man is sent home not for his own sake but for his brother's, because fear spreads. Then the
officers *"appoint captains of armies at the head of the people"* (9).

**A city far off (20:10–15).** *"When you draw near to a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace
to it"* (10). If it answers with peace and opens its gates, *"all the people who are found therein shall
become forced laborers to you, and shall serve you"* (11). If it refuses and makes war, Israel besieges
it, and *"when Yahweh your God delivers it into your hand, you shall strike every male of it with the edge
of the sword"* (12–13). The women, the little ones, the livestock, and the plunder are taken (14). *"Thus
you shall do to all the cities which are very far off from you, which are not of the cities of these
nations"* (15).

**The cities of the land (20:16–18).** For the cities *"that Yahweh your God gives you for an
inheritance"* there is no offer and no exception: *"you shall save alive nothing that breathes; but you
shall utterly destroy them: the Hittite, the Amorite, the Canaanite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the
Jebusite; as Yahweh your God has commanded you"* (16–17). The command referred to is 7:2. The reason
given is religious, not military: *"that they not teach you to follow all their abominations, which they
have done to their gods; so would you sin against Yahweh your God"* (18).

**The trees (20:19–20).** In a long siege Israel may not take an ax to the city's fruit trees, *"for you
may eat of them. You shall not cut them down; for is the tree of the field man, that it should be besieged
by you?"* (19). Only trees *"that you know are not trees for food"* may be cut for siege works *"against the
city that makes war with you, until it falls"* (20).

**How the passage fits together.** The chapter is a series of limits placed on an activity it assumes
Israel will undertake. It limits who fights: not the man with an unfinished life at home, not the man
whose fear will spread. It limits how a distant city is approached: peace is offered first. It limits
what a siege may destroy: not the trees that feed people. And it states, in the middle of all this
restraint, the one case in which no limit applies at all. The law of the land's cities (16–18) is the
chapter's harshest command, and it stands beside its mildest ones. The wiki records them together, as
the text does, and does not soften either.

**Two kinds of city, and one question the text does not answer.** The distinction between *"cities
which are very far off"* (15) and *"the cities of these peoples"* (16) is the hinge of the chapter. What
is less clear is whether the offer of peace (10) applies to both kinds or only to the first. Read in
order, 10–15 describe the distant city and end by saying so (15), and 16–18 then set the land's cities
apart; on that reading the land's cities get no offer. Joshua 11:19–20 can be read the other way: it says
*"There was not a city that made peace with the children of Israel, except the Hivites, the inhabitants of
Gibeon"*, and that *"it was of Yahweh to harden their hearts"*, which implies that peace was at least
conceivable (WEB, bible-api.com). Jewish interpretation has long held that the offer was made to every
city; that is a tradition of reading, not a statement of Deuteronomy 20. The wiki records both readings
and adopts neither.

**Deuteronomy 7 and Deuteronomy 20.** 7:1–2 commanded that the seven nations be utterly destroyed, with
*"no covenant"* and no mercy. 20:16–18 repeats the command and adds its ground, the teaching of
abominations. It also restates the list with six names instead of seven: the Girgashite, present at 7:1,
is absent at 20:17 (stored WEB; the Hebrew has the same six, WLC). The text gives no reason for the
omission. The list of six also occurs elsewhere (Exodus 3:8, 17; 23:23), so the shorter list is not
unique to this chapter.

## Notes

**"Is the tree of the field man?"** *Kî hāʾādām ʿēṣ haśśādeh lābōʾ mippānêkā bammāṣôr* (20:19, WLC). The
consonants can be read as a statement, *for man is the tree of the field*, or as a question; the
Masoretic vowels, and morphhb's morphology, which tags the *hā-* on *ʾādām* as the interrogative particle,
read a question, as WEB does. The question expects *no*: the tree is not an enemy combatant who can flee
into the city or come out to fight, so it is not to be treated as one. The statement reading, *man is a
tree of the field*, was known to ancient and medieval readers as a moral saying and has a long life in
Jewish preaching; it is not what the Masoretic text says here. The wiki follows the vocalized text.

**"Do not destroy."** *Lōʾ-tašḥît ʾet-ʿēṣāh* (20:19, WLC), from *šāḥat*, *ruin*, *destroy*. Later Jewish law
built a general principle on this verse, *bal tašḥît*, *do not destroy*, extending it from fruit trees to
needless waste of anything useful. That principle is rabbinic, not stated in Deuteronomy, which forbids
only the felling of food trees in a siege. The wiki records it as the verse's later history.

**Trees for food.** *ʿĒṣ maʾăkāl* (20:20, WLC) is the phrase of Leviticus 19:23, *kol-ʿēṣ maʾăkāl*, *"all
kinds of trees for food"* (WLC). There the newly planted tree's fruit is forbidden three years and holy in
the fourth (Leviticus 19:23–25). The vineyard exemption at 20:6 uses the word *ḥillēl*, *make common*, of
the planter who has not yet *"used its fruit"*. Taken together, the two laws suggest that the vineyard
man's waiting may have been a matter of years, not months. That is an inference from the two laws, which
do not refer to one another.

**"Dedicated."** *Wəlōʾ ḥănākô* (20:5, WLC), from *ḥānak*, the root of *ḥănukkâ*, *dedication*. In
Deuteronomy the verb occurs only here (lemma 2596), and elsewhere it is used of Solomon's temple (1 Kings
8:63), so the word is not in itself cultic. The text does not say what dedicating a house involved. See
[[The Dedication of the Altar - Numbers 7, 2 Chronicles 7, and Ezra 6]].

**"Used its fruit."** *Wəlōʾ ḥilləlô* (20:6, WLC), literally *not made it common*. In Deuteronomy the same
verb in the same sense occurs again only at 28:30, *wəlōʾ təḥallənennû*, *"you will plant a vineyard, and
not use its fruit"*. At 2:24–25, 31; 3:24; and 16:9 morphhb tags the same lemma, there in its sense
*begin*.

**The exemptions, and the curse that reverses them.** 28:30 takes the three exemptions in a different
order and turns each into a loss: *"You will betroth a wife, and another man shall lie with her. You will
build a house, and you won't dwell in it. You will plant a vineyard, and not use its fruit."* The Hebrew
uses the verbs of 20:5–7: *ʾāraś*, *betroth*, *bānâ*, *build*, *nāṭaʿ*, *plant*, and *ḥillēl* (WLC). What the
law of war protects, the covenant curse takes away. See
[[Let Him Return to His House - Deuteronomy 20, 24.5, 28.30, Judges 7, and Luke 14]].

**"Melt."** *Wəlōʾ yimmas ʾet-ləbab ʾeḥāyw kilbābô* (20:8, WLC), *lest his brothers' heart melt as his
heart*. The verb *māsas* occurs in Deuteronomy only here and at 1:28, where the people complain that *"our
brothers have made our heart melt"* with the spies' report (WLC; lemma 4549). The Kadesh story is the case
this law exists to prevent: ten frightened men turned a whole generation back. The consonants of
*ʾeḥāyw* are plural, *his brothers*; WEB renders *"his brother's"*.

**Four words for fear.** 20:3 uses *rākak*, *be soft* (*"faint"*), *yārēʾ*, *fear*, *ḥāpaz*, *be alarmed*
(*"tremble"*), and *ʿāraṣ*, *dread* (*"be scared"*). *Rākak* and *ḥāpaz* occur in Deuteronomy only here
(lemmas 7401, 2648). *ʿĀraṣ* occurs at 1:29, *"Don't dread"*; 7:21, *"You shall not be scared of them"*;
and 31:6, *"Don't be afraid or scared of them"*, where Moses charges Israel before the crossing (WLC; lemma
6206). 20:8 then names the man *rak hallēbāb*, *soft of heart* — the adjective of the same root as the verb
in 20:3.

**"Goes with you" and "fights for you."** 20:4, *hahōlēk ʿimmākem ləhillāḥēm lākem* (WLC), joins two
phrases Moses has used before: *"who goes before you, he will fight for you"* (1:30) and *"himself fights
for you"* (3:22). 31:6 will say *hahōlēk ʿimmāk*, *"who goes with you"*, again in Hebrew the same
participle as 20:4 (WLC).

**"Proclaim peace."** *Wəqārāʾtā ʾēlêhā ləšālôm* (20:10, WLC). Moses had already done this in the
retrospect: he sent Sihon *dibrê šālôm*, *"words of peace"* (2:26, WLC). Sihon lay outside the land
promised to the fathers, so his case fits 20:10–15, and his refusal ended in the destruction of
*"every inhabited city, with the women and the little ones"* (2:34). That goes beyond what 20:13–14 allows
for a distant city, which spares the women and children. The difference is recorded, not harmonized:
Deuteronomy 2 narrates a past war under a specific command to *"contend with him in battle"* (2:24), and
20 legislates for future ones. The wiki's [[Edom and Sihon - Refused Passage and Different Outcomes]]
already notes that Sihon's war rests on that territorial command, not on a general rule.

**"Forced laborers."** *Yihyû ləkā lāmas waʿăbādûkā* (20:11, WLC). *Mas* is the corvée, conscripted
labor; in Deuteronomy it occurs only here (lemma 4522). Joshua 16:10 uses the same phrase, *ləmas-ʿōbēd*
(WLC), for the Canaanites of Gezer whom Ephraim *"didn't drive out"* — that is, for the people of the
land, to whom 20:16–18 had denied the option. 1 Kings 9:20–21 makes Solomon's levy out of the peoples
Israel *"were not able utterly to destroy"* (WEB, bible-api.com). The law's terms for distant cities
became, in the narrated history, the fate of the land's own peoples.

**"Every male."** *Kol-zəkûrāh* (20:13, WLC). The noun *zəkûr* occurs in the Torah only here and in the
law of the three pilgrimage feasts, where *all your males* appear before Yahweh (Exodus 23:17; 34:23;
Deuteronomy 16:16; WLC lemma 2138). The same word names the men of Israel who appear before God and the
men of the distant city who are put to the sword.

**"Nothing that breathes."** *Lōʾ təḥayyeh kol-nəšāmâ* (20:16, WLC). *Nəšāmâ*, *breath*, occurs in
Deuteronomy only here (lemma 5397). Joshua uses it in the narrative of fulfilment: Joshua *"utterly
destroyed all that breathed, as Yahweh … commanded"* (Joshua 10:40), *kol-hannəšāmâ heḥĕrîm*, with the
verb of 20:17; and *"They didn't leave any who breathed"* (11:14), *kol-nəšāmâ* (WLC; WEB, bible-api.com).
Joshua 11:14 also takes the plunder and livestock. Whether *"nothing that breathes"* (20:16) includes
animals, Deuteronomy does not say; in the retrospect Israel kept the livestock of Sihon's and Og's
cities (2:35; 3:7).

**"Utterly destroy."** *Kî-haḥărēm taḥărîmēm* (20:17, WLC), the infinitive absolute with the finite verb,
the construction of 7:2, *haḥărēm taḥărîm ʾōtām*. See
[[Devoted to Destruction - Leviticus 27.28-29, Deuteronomy 7, Joshua 6-7, and 1 Samuel 15]].

**"Siege" and "bulwarks."** *Māṣôr* is both the siege in 20:19 (*bammāṣôr*) and the siege works built in
20:20 (*ûbānîtā māṣôr*), WLC. In the rest of Deuteronomy the word occurs only in the curses: *"in the siege
and in the distress with which your enemies will distress you"* (28:53, and again at 55 and 57), where
Israel is the besieged city and eats its own children (WLC lemma 4692). The siege Israel lays in chapter 20
is laid against it in chapter 28. *"Until it falls"* is *ʿad ridtāh*, *until it comes down* (20:20, WLC).

**The ax.** *Linədōaḥ ʿālāyw garzen* (20:19, WLC), *to swing an ax against it*. *Garzen*, *ax*, occurs in
Deuteronomy only here and at 19:5, the ax that flies off the handle and kills a neighbour unawares, with
the same verb *nādaḥ* (WLC; lemmas 1631, 5080). The ax that kills by accident in chapter 19 is forbidden
against a fruit tree in chapter 20.

**What the passage does not settle.** Whether the offer of peace in 20:10 extends to the cities of the
land. Why the Girgashite is missing from the list in 20:17. What *dedicating* a house involved. Whether
the officers' four questions were meant to be applied as strictly as they read, in a society where most
men would qualify under one of them — later Jewish law restricted them to discretionary wars, which is a
later distinction, not Deuteronomy's. Each is recorded, none adopted.
