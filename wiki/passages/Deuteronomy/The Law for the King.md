# The Law for the King

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 17:14–20

## Summary

After the judges and the higher court, Deuteronomy turns to the one office Israel does not yet
have. It is the only law in the Torah about a king, and it is written as a concession to a request
that has not yet been made.

**The request and the permission (17:14–15).** The law begins after the conquest: *"When you have
come to the land which Yahweh your God gives you, and possess it, and dwell in it"*, Israel may say,
*"I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me"* (14). The request is quoted
in Israel's own voice and is not rebuked. The answer is permission with two conditions: *"you shall
surely set him king over yourselves, whom Yahweh your God chooses"*, and he must be *"one from among
your brothers. You may not put a foreigner over you, who is not your brother"* (15).

**Three things he shall not multiply (17:16–17).** *"Only he shall not multiply horses to himself,
nor cause the people to return to Egypt, to the end that he may multiply horses; because Yahweh has
said to you, 'You shall not go back that way again'"* (16). *"He shall not multiply wives to
himself, that his heart not turn away. He shall not greatly multiply to himself silver and gold"*
(17). Horses, wives, silver and gold — strength in war, a large household, and treasure — are the usual marks
of a king. Each is limited, not banned: the text forbids *multiplying*. The horses are tied to the
road back to Egypt, the wives to the king's heart, *"that his heart not turn away"*; silver and
gold are given no reason of their own.

**What he shall do (17:18–20).** The only positive duty is a book. *"When he sits on the throne of
his kingdom, … he shall write himself a copy of this law in a book, out of that which is before the
priests the Levites"* (18). *"It shall be with him, and he shall read from it all the days of his
life; that he may learn to fear Yahweh his God, to keep all the words of this law and these
statutes, to do them"* (19). The reading has two aims: *"that his heart not be lifted up above his
brothers, and that he not turn away from the commandment, to the right hand, or to the left"*.
Then comes the promise, *"that he may prolong his days in his kingdom, he and his children, in the
middle of Israel"* (20).

**What the law leaves out.** The king is given no task. He does not judge, which 16:18–17:13 has
given to judges and priests; he does not lead in war, which chapter 20 gives to priests and
officers; and he offers no sacrifice. The law assumes that he will rule, but every clause it writes
is a limit or a duty to read. The king it describes is first of all an Israelite under the same
law as his brothers.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh, who gives the land (14), chooses the king (15), and has said *"You shall not go
  back that way again"* (16). The king is to learn to fear him (19).
- [[Moses]] — the speaker, unnamed here, giving a law for an office that will not exist for
  generations after his death.
- The king — unnamed and in the future. No person page: the law describes an office, not a man.
  Solomon, whose reign 1 Kings describes in terms that match every clause, has no page yet; see
  the notes.
- The priests the Levites — keepers of the law the king copies (18). No new page; see
  [[Priesthood]].

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — the place the king must not send the people back to for horses (16).

## Themes

- [[Kingship and Empire]] — the Torah's only law of the king: permitted, chosen by Yahweh, limited,
  and placed under the book.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — the king's one positive duty is to copy and read *"this law"* all his life
  (18–19), so as not to turn aside right or left (20).
- [[The Fear of God]] — the reading is so that *"he may learn to fear Yahweh his God"* (19), the
  phrase already used of the tithe meal (14:23).
- [[Brotherhood]] — the king is *"one from among your brothers"* (15), and his heart is not to be
  lifted *"above his brothers"* (20).
- [[The Nations]] — the request is to be *"like all the nations"* (14), and the foreigner may not be
  king (15).
- [[Call and Election]] — the king is the one *"whom Yahweh your God chooses"* (15), the verb used
  of the place throughout chapters 12–17.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — horses, silver, and gold are not to be multiplied (16–17).
- [[Marriage]] — many wives are forbidden to the king, *"that his heart not turn away"* (17).
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — *"that his heart not be lifted up above his brothers"* (20), the
  warning of 8:14 applied to the throne.

## Notes

**A law for a request.** Most of the code is command: *you shall*, *you shall not*. Here the law
begins with what Israel will say, and grants it. The request is the same as the one the elders make
to Samuel: *"make us a king to judge us like all the nations"* (1 Samuel 8:5, WEB checked at
bible-api.com). In the WLC both verses use the verb *śîm*, *set*, with *melek*, *king*, and both end
*kəkol-haggôyim*, *like all the nations* (17:14 *ʾāśîmâ ʿālay melek kəkol-haggôyim*; 1 Samuel 8:5
*śîmâ-lānû melek … kəkol-haggôyim*; WLC, morphhb). Deuteronomy does not call the request sinful;
1 Samuel 8:7 has Yahweh say the people have rejected him. How the two fit is one of the old
questions about the monarchy. Readers have taken Deuteronomy as a law that allows a king (a
permission), as a law that commands one (17:15 opens with the emphatic *śôm tāśîm*, *you shall
surely set*), or as a law written with knowledge of the kings and set against them. The wiki records
the three readings and adopts none. See
[[Like All the Nations - Deuteronomy 17.14 and 1 Samuel 8.5, 20]].

**Chosen like the place.** *"Whom Yahweh your God chooses"* (15) uses *bāḥar* (WLC lemma 977), the
verb of *"the place which Yahweh your God chooses"* (12:5; 14:23; 17:8, 10). The people may ask for
a king and set him up, but the choice of the man is not theirs. The law does not say how the choice
is made known; the later narratives use a prophet's anointing.

**A brother, not a foreigner.** *ʾîš nokrî … ʾăšer lōʾ-ʾāḥîkā hûʾ* (15, WLC), *"a foreigner … who
is not your brother"*. *Nokrî* is the word already used of the outsider who is not released from
debt (15:3) and who may buy what dies of itself (14:21). The king is defined by the same line: he
is inside the brotherhood. The law does not say why a foreign king is a danger. The surrounding
chapters suggest one reason, other gods; the text does not state it.

**"You shall not go back that way again."** The quoted saying, *lōʾ tōsipûn lāšûb baddereḵ hazzeh
ʿôd* (16, WLC), is not found in these words in the stored Torah. The nearest is Moses at the sea:
*"the Egyptians whom you have seen today, you shall never see them again"* (Exodus 14:13), *lōʾ
tōsîpû lirʾōtām ʿôd*. Deuteronomy 28:68 looks back to a saying with the same shape: Yahweh will
bring Israel into Egypt again *"by the way of which I told to you that you would never see it
again"*, *lōʾ-tōsîp ʿôd lirʾōtāh*. All three use *yāsap* with *ʿôd*, *add again* (WLC lemma search).
Whether 17:16 cites Exodus 14:13, an unrecorded word, or a saying Deuteronomy itself coins is not
settled. The point is clear either way: horses from Egypt would be a road back to the house of
bondage. *"Cause the people to return to Egypt"* may mean trade, sending men in exchange for
horses, or alliance; the text does not say which.

**Three times "multiply".** *Lōʾ yarbeh lô*, *he shall not multiply to himself*, is said of horses,
of wives, and of silver and gold (16–17), with *harbôt sûs* between (WLC; *rābâ*, lemma 7235). *Lô*,
*to himself*, is repeated each time. The fault is not having horses, a wife, or silver; it is
accumulating them for himself. The same verb stands three times in 8:13, *"when your herds and your
flocks multiply, and your silver and your gold is multiplied"*, just before the heart is lifted up
(8:14; WLC). And when Israel goes to war it is told not to fear the enemy's *"horses, chariots, and a
people more than you"* (20:1): Israel's strength in battle is not to be horses.

**The heart.** Two of the reasons concern the king's heart: *wəlōʾ yāsûr ləbābô*, *that his heart
not turn away* (17), and *ləbiltî rûm-ləbābô mēʾeḥāyw*, *that his heart not be lifted up above his
brothers* (20). The verb *sûr*, *turn aside*, returns in the same verse for the commandment: *ûləbiltî
sûr min-hammiṣwâ yāmîn ûśəmōʾwl* (20). *Rûm* with *lēbāb* is the phrase of 8:14, *wərām ləbābekā*,
*"then your heart might be lifted up, and you forget Yahweh"*, said there of the people when silver
and gold multiply (8:13). The law of the king applies to one man the warning first given to all
(WLC lemma search for 5493, 7311, 3824).

**"A copy of this law."** *Mišnê hattôrâ hazzōʾt* (18, WLC). *Mišneh* means a second or double, and
here a copy. The book's Greek name, *Deuteronomion*, is usually traced to the Septuagint's rendering
of this phrase as *"this second law"*. That rendering is reported in standard reference works and
was not checked against a Greek edition for this page. The Hebrew asks for a copy of a law already
given, not a second law. *"This law"* most naturally means Deuteronomy's own teaching, though how
much of it is meant is not stated.

**Reading as the king's work.** The copy is to be *"with him"*, and he reads it *"all the days of
his life"* (19). The goal, *ləmaʿan yilmad ləyirʾâ*, *that he may learn to fear*, uses the same
words as the tithe meal of 14:23 (WLC; lemmas 3925, 3372). What every household learns at the
yearly meal, the king learns by daily reading. Deuteronomy 4:10 says the same of Horeb: Yahweh
gathered the people *"that they may learn to fear me all the days that they live on the earth"*.

**Solomon.** 1 Kings describes Solomon's reign in terms that touch every clause: *"the weight of gold
that came to Solomon in one year was six hundred sixty-six talents of gold"* (10:14); *"The king made
silver as common as stones in Jerusalem"* (10:27); *"The horses which Solomon had were brought out of
Egypt"* (10:28); *"He had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines; and his
wives turned away his heart"* (11:3); *"When Solomon was old, his wives turned away his heart after
other gods"* (11:4). All WEB, checked at bible-api.com (`translation=web`), 2026-11-07; 1 Kings is
not stored. Whether 1 Kings was written with Deuteronomy 17 in view, or Deuteronomy 17 with
Solomon in view, is debated. The match itself is in the English, and no claim is made here that
the Hebrew verb for *turned away* in 1 Kings 11 is the *sûr* of 17:17; that was not checked.

**What he does not do.** The king has no judicial role here, and no military or priestly one. The
people of 1 Samuel 8:20 want a king who will *"judge us, and go out before us, and fight our
battles"* (WEB checked); Deuteronomy gives the judging to the court (16:18–17:13) and the battle to
the priest and officers (20:2–9, not yet ingested). The law's king is placed in a list of offices —
judges, king, priests, prophet (16:18–18:22) — and he is not at its head.

**Not resolved here.** Whether the law permits or commands a king; whether *"return to Egypt"* means
trade or alliance; what *"this law"* covers; whether 17:16 quotes Exodus 14:13; whether the law was
shaped by the history of the kings. Each is recorded, none adopted.
