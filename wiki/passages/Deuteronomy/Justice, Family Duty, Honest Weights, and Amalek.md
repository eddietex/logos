# Justice, Family Duty, Honest Weights, and Amalek

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 25:1–19

## Summary

The last chapter of the casuistic laws before the closing ritual of chapter 26. It has six laws, and
they look unrelated: the judges must acquit the righteous and condemn the wicked, and a beating is
capped at forty strokes (1–3); the ox is not muzzled while it threshes (4); a man must marry his dead
brother's childless widow, or be shamed at the gate if he refuses (5–10); a wife who seizes her
husband's assailant by the genitals loses her hand (11–12); weights and measures must be honest
(13–16); and Israel must remember Amalek, and blot out its memory (17–19).

In the Hebrew, chapter 25 has the same verse numbers as the WEB.

**The judge and the beaten man (25:1–3).** *"If there is a controversy between men, and they come to
judgment"* (1). The *"controversy"* is *rîb*, the word for the dispute that goes up to the central
court in 17:8 and stands *"before Yahweh"* in 19:17. The judges *"shall justify the righteous, and
condemn the wicked"*: *wǝhiṣdîqû ʾet-haṣṣaddîq wǝhiršîʿû ʾet-hārāšāʿ*, both verbs in the hiphil,
*declare righteous* and *declare guilty*. In the Torah the hiphil of *ṣādaq* occurs only here and at
Exodus 23:7, where Yahweh says, *"I will not justify the wicked"*; the hiphil of *rāšaʿ* only here and
at Exodus 22:9 (WLC 22:8), *"He whom God condemns shall pay double"* (see Notes). What Yahweh does and
refuses to do in Exodus, the human judges are to do in Deuteronomy. Proverbs 17:15 states the
reverse: *"He who justifies the wicked, and he who condemns the righteous, both of them alike are an
abomination to Yahweh."* See
[[A Bribe Blinds - Proverbs 17.23, Exodus 23.8, and Deuteronomy 16.19]].

The verdict leads straight to a sentence. *"If the wicked man is worthy to be beaten"* (2), literally
*if the wicked man is a son of beating*, the judge makes him lie down and has him beaten *"before his
face"*, *"according to his wickedness, by number"*. Three limits are set on the punishment. It is
counted, *bǝmispār*; it is done in the judge's presence; and it has a ceiling: *"no more than forty
stripes"* (3). The reason is not the man's guilt but his standing: *"lest … your brother will be
degraded in your sight"*. The verb is *wǝniqlâ*, the niphal of *qālâ*, *be made light, be
dishonored*. Its only other Torah occurrence is the hiphil at 27:16, *"Cursed is he who dishonors his
father or his mother"* (see Notes). The convicted man is called *"the wicked"* in verse 2 and
*"your brother"* in verse 3. The sentence may punish him; it may not make him less than a brother.
See [[Forty Stripes - Deuteronomy 25.3 and 2 Corinthians 11.24]].

**The ox (25:4).** *"You shall not muzzle the ox when he treads out the grain."* Four Hebrew words,
*lōʾ-taḥsōm šôr bǝdîšô*. The verb *ḥāsam* occurs only here in the Torah (see Notes). The threshing ox
walked over the cut sheaves on the threshing floor, and the law lets it eat from what it treads
(that the practice was trampling by oxen is reported from general knowledge). The ox has been in
Deuteronomy's care before: it rests on the Sabbath (5:14), and a brother's fallen ox is lifted up
(22:4). Here the animal is a worker, and the law treats it like the hired man of 24:14–15, who is
paid the same day, and the neighbor in the vineyard of 23:24, who may eat what he picks. Paul quotes
the verse twice, once to ask *"Is it for the oxen that God cares?"* (1 Corinthians 9:9) and once
beside *"The laborer is worthy of his wages"* (1 Timothy 5:18). See
[[Do Not Muzzle the Ox - Deuteronomy 25.4, 1 Corinthians 9, and 1 Timothy 5]].

Why the verse stands here, between the beaten man and the widow, is not said. Two readings are
current, and the wiki adopts neither. It may be a stray proverb set among laws about persons; or it
may be placed on purpose, the ox's right to eat preparing for the brother's duty to build a house
for another. Some Jewish and Christian readers have taken the verse that way (reported from general
knowledge; not checked against any particular commentary).

**The brother-in-law (25:5–10).** The levirate law. *"If brothers dwell together, and one of them dies,
and has no son"* (5), the widow is not to marry *"outside to a stranger"*; *"Her husband's brother
shall go in to her, and take her as his wife, and perform the duty of a husband's brother to her."*
The Hebrew noun for the brother-in-law is *yābām*, the noun for the widow *yǝbēmet*, and the verb
*yibbēm*, *to perform the brother-in-law's duty*. In the Torah the verb occurs only here (5, 7) and at
Genesis 38:8, where Judah tells Onan, *"perform the duty of a husband's brother to her"*. The two
nouns occur only in this passage (see Notes). The law gives a name to what Genesis 38 already
assumed.

The condition is *"brothers dwell together"*, *yēšǝbû ʾaḥîm yaḥdāw*. The law applies to brothers
living on the same holding, not to every brother (the text says no more about it). Its purpose is
stated in verse 6: *"the firstborn whom she bears shall succeed in the name of his brother who is
dead, that his name not be blotted out of Israel."* The verb is *yimmāḥeh*, the niphal of *māḥâ*,
*be wiped out*. It is the same verb as verse 19, where Israel is to *"blot out (timḥeh) the memory of
Amalek"*. One chapter uses it twice: once to keep a dead man's name, and once to erase a people's.
See [[Blot Out - The Flood, Amalek, and God's Book]].

The man may refuse. *"If the man doesn't want to take his brother's wife"* (7). The verb is *ḥāpēṣ*,
*desire*. In the Torah it occurs only here (7, 8) and at 21:14, the captive wife the husband *"take[s]
no delight in"* (see Notes). Refusal is permitted, and it is public. The widow herself *"shall go up
to the gate to the elders"*; she speaks first, and in the first person: *"My husband's brother
refuses to raise up to his brother a name in Israel"* (7). The elders summon him and speak to him
(8). If he stands by his refusal, she comes to him *"in the presence of the elders, and loose[s] his
shoe from off his foot, and spit[s] in his face"* (9).

She acts three times and speaks twice. The verb for loosing the shoe is *ḥālaṣ*. Elsewhere in the
Torah its passive participle is the WEB's *"armed"*, the men of Reuben and Gad who *"pass over armed"*
before their brothers (Numbers 32:21, 27, 29–32; Deuteronomy 3:18). The piel is used of stones
*"taken out"* of a diseased house (Leviticus 14:40, 43) (WLC lemma search; see Notes). Whether the
same verb means *to equip* and *to strip* is a lexical question this page does not settle. The spitting
is *yāraq*, which occurs elsewhere only at Numbers 12:14, *"If her father had but spit in her face"*.
See [[Spit in Her Face - Numbers 12.14 and Deuteronomy 25.9]]. Her words are a sentence on him:
*"So shall it be done to the man who does not build up his brother's house"* (9). The man who would
not *build* (*bānâ*) a house now has a name, and his house is named for what was done to him: *"The
house of him who had his shoe removed"* (10), *bêt ḥălûṣ hannāʿal*.

The law does not compel the brother. It gives the widow standing at the gate, a ceremony, and a
permanent name of shame. The refusal is legal, and its cost is that it is made in front of the
town. Genesis 38 shows what happens with none of this: Onan's refusal is secret and Judah's is
silence, and Tamar has no court to go to. In Ruth 4, the nearer kinsman refuses openly at the gate,
and *"took off his shoe"* (4:8). See
[[Levirate Marriage - Genesis 38, Deuteronomy 25, and Ruth]].

**Two laws in tension.** Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 forbid a man to take his brother's wife; verse 5
commands it when the brother has died without a son. The tension is recorded on the levirate
connection and on [[Forbidden Unions and the Defiled Land]]; this page adds only that Deuteronomy
states the case narrowly, *"has no son"*, *"dwell together"*, and gives a purpose, the name. It does
not mention Leviticus.

**The wife in the fight (25:11–12).** *"When men strive against each other"* (11). The verb is
*yinnāṣû*, the niphal of *nāṣâ*. The same form opens Exodus 21:22, *"If men fight and hurt a pregnant
woman"*; the participle is the two Hebrews *"fighting with each other"* at Exodus 2:13, and the
narrative form Leviticus 24:10, where the blasphemer and a man of Israel *"strove together"* (WLC; see
Notes). This case reverses
Exodus 21:22. There a woman is hurt when she comes near two
men fighting; here a woman comes near to *"deliver her husband"* and herself does the injury. She
*"takes him by his private parts"*, *mǝbūšāyw*, a word that occurs only here in the Torah (see Notes).
*"Then you shall cut off her hand. Your eye shall have no pity"* (12).

It is the only law in the Torah that prescribes a mutilation as a penalty. That is reported from
general knowledge; the wiki has not surveyed every penalty. The verb *qāṣaṣ*, *cut off*, occurs
elsewhere in the Torah only in the piel at Exodus 39:3, where gold is *"cut … into wires"*. *"Your eye
shall have no pity"* is the formula of 7:16 (the nations), 13:8 (WLC 13:9, the inciter), and 19:13,
21 (the murderer and the false witness). The law gives no reason. Two readings are current, and the
wiki adopts neither. It may protect the man's ability to father children, which ties it to the
levirate law just before, where a man's name depends on offspring. Or it may punish a shameful act
against a stranger's body. Some later Jewish interpretation read the hand as a monetary penalty
(reported from general knowledge, not checked). The text itself says *"cut off"*.

**Weights and measures (25:13–16).** *"You shall not have in your bag diverse weights, one heavy and
one light. You shall not have in your house diverse measures, one large and one small"* (13–14). The
Hebrew is *ʾeben wāʾāben*, *a stone and a stone*, and *ʾêpâ wǝʾêpâ*, *an ephah and an ephah*. The
same doubled *ephah* stands in Proverbs 20:10, *"Differing weights and differing measures"* (WLC; see
Notes). The law forbids owning the second weight, not only using it. It is *"in your bag"* and *"in
your house"*; the fraud begins when the merchant packs.

*"You shall have a perfect and just weight"* (15) is *ʾeben šǝlēmâ wāṣedeq*, *a whole stone, and
righteous*. *Šālēm* is the adjective of the altar's *"uncut stones"* at 27:6 (WEB), *ʾăbānîm šǝlēmôt*. *Ṣedeq* is the word of *"righteous judgment"* in 16:18 and of
*"that which is altogether just"* in 16:20. The weight, the judge, and the court share one word. The
promise is *"that your days may be long in the land"* (15), the promise of the fifth commandment
(5:16) and of 4:40. *"For all who do such things, all who do unrighteously, are an abomination to
Yahweh your God"* (16). *ʿĀwel*, *unrighteousness*, is the word of Leviticus 19:35, *"You shall do no
unrighteousness in judgment, in measures of length, of weight, or of quantity"*. In the Torah it
occurs only at Leviticus 19:15, 35 and Deuteronomy 25:16; 32:4 (see Notes). Deuteronomy 32:4 says of
God that he is *"without iniquity"*. See
[[Honest Weights - Proverbs 11.1, the Torah, and Job 31.6]].

**Amalek (25:17–19).** *"Remember what Amalek did to you by the way as you came out of Egypt"* (17).
The Hebrew begins *zākôr ʾēt ʾăšer-ʿāśâ*, word for word the opening of 24:9, *"Remember what Yahweh
your God did to Miriam"*. Both verses end *baddere(k) bǝṣēʾtǝkem mimmiṣrāyim*, *"by the way as you
came out of Egypt"*, the phrase of 23:4 (WLC 23:5) for Ammon and Moab, who *"didn't meet you with
bread and with water"*. Deuteronomy has three wilderness memories in three chapters: what Ammon and
Moab did not do, what Yahweh did to Miriam, and what Amalek did. See
[[Remember What Yahweh Did to Miriam - Numbers 12, Deuteronomy 24, and Micah 6]].

The charge is new. Exodus 17:8 says only that *"Amalek came and fought with Israel in Rephidim"*.
Deuteronomy says *"how he met you by the way, and struck the rearmost of you, all who were feeble
behind you, when you were faint and weary; and he didn't fear God"* (18). Three rare words carry it.
*"Struck the rearmost"* is *wayzannēb*, a verb built on *zānāb*, *tail*. It occurs in the Torah only
here, and elsewhere in the books searched only at Joshua 10:19, *"attack them from the rear"*.
*"Feeble"* is *neḥĕšālîm*, which occurs only here in the books searched. *"Faint"* is *ʿāyēp*, the
word for Esau, *"famished"*, coming in from the field in Genesis 25:29–30. In the Torah it occurs
only in those two verses and here (see Notes). Amalek is Esau's grandson (Genesis 36:12). Esau was
the weary man who sold his birthright; his grandson's people fell on Israel's weary. This is a
verbal link, not a claim the text makes. Deuteronomy draws no line to Esau.

*"And he didn't fear God"* is *wǝlōʾ yārēʾ ʾĕlōhîm*. The subject is most naturally Amalek. Some
readers take it to be Israel, *you were faint and weary, and did not fear God*. The Hebrew allows
both, since *yārēʾ* is third person masculine singular and the preceding clause is in the second
person (*wǝʾattâ ʿāyēp*). The wiki follows the WEB in reading Amalek as the subject and records the
other. Abraham in Gerar said *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place"* (Genesis 20:11). The
Hebrew midwives *"feared God"* (Exodus 1:17). *Not fearing God* in these narratives means having no
restraint toward the weak. That is the wiki's reading of the pattern, not a statement of the text.

**"Blot out … You shall not forget" (25:19).** The command waits for rest: *"when Yahweh your God has
given you rest from all your enemies all around"*. The phrase is the rest of 12:10, which also comes
before a command, the one place of worship. *"You shall blot out the memory of Amalek from under the
sky"* repeats Exodus 17:14 with one change. There Yahweh said *"I will utterly blot out"*; here
Israel is told *"you shall blot out"*. The noun *zēker*, *memory*, is the word of both verses. It is
also the word of Exodus 3:15, *"this is my memorial (zikrî) to all generations"*. The tension between
the two verses is recorded on [[The Battle with Amalek]] and on the Amalek thread; this page does
not resolve it.

The chapter ends *lōʾ tiškāḥ*, *"You shall not forget"*. Israel is to blot out a memory and is
forbidden to forget it. The law asks for both at once. The memory to be erased is Amalek's place
under the sky; the memory to be kept is what Amalek did. The wiki records this reading and does not
press it further. 1 Samuel 15:2–3 is the command carried out: *"I remember what Amalek did to
Israel, how he set himself against him on the way, when he came up out of Egypt. Now go and strike
Amalek."* See [[Amalek - From a Concubine's Son to Israel's First Enemy]].

**What holds the chapter together.** The plan's title lists four subjects, and the chapter does not
announce a unity. Read together, the laws have one concern: who may be degraded and who may not.
The convicted man may be beaten, but not until *"your brother"* is degraded. The ox may work, but not
be starved. The dead brother may not have his name blotted out, and the living one who refuses may
be named in contempt. The merchant may not give the buyer less than his due. The weary may not be
struck from behind. That is an observation, offered as a reading and not adopted as the text's own
structure. 26:1 begins the first-fruits ceremony, *"when you have come in to the land"*. The boundary
is the plan's, kept as drawn.

## People

- [[Moses]] — the speaker, addressing Israel in the second person.
- [[Amalek]] — the people under their ancestor's name; struck the stragglers *"by the way"* and are
  to be blotted out (17–19).
- The judges, the beaten man, the brothers, the widow (*yǝbēmet*), the elders, the wife in the fight,
  and the merchant: legal categories, not individuals. No pages.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — *"as you came out of Egypt"* (17).
- None other named. *"The land which Yahweh your God gives you for an inheritance"* (19) is Canaan,
  not named here; the gate (7) is the town's.

## Notes

**Verse numbering.** WEB 25:1–19 is WLC 25:1–19. The boundary is the plan's, kept as drawn, with
commas added to the title.

**"Justify … condemn."** *Wǝhiṣdîqû* and *wǝhiršîʿû* (WLC 25:1), hiphil of *ṣādaq* (lemma 6663) and
*rāšaʿ* (lemma 7561). In Genesis–Deuteronomy the hiphil of *ṣādaq* occurs only at Exodus 23:7 and
here; the lemma otherwise at Genesis 38:26 (qal, *"She is more righteous than I"*) and 44:16
(hithpael, *"how shall we clear ourselves?"*). *Rāšaʿ* occurs only at Exodus 22:8 (WEB 22:9) and here
(WLC lemma search). *Rîb* (lemma 7379) in Deuteronomy: 1:12; 17:8; 19:17; 21:5; 25:1.

**"Son of beating."** *ʾim-bin hakkôt hārāšāʿ* (WLC 25:2). *"By number"*, *bǝmispār*.

**"Forty."** *ʾarbāʿîm yakkennû lōʾ yōsîp* (WLC 25:3), *forty he may strike him; he shall not add*.
That later Jewish practice gave thirty-nine strokes, to stay inside the limit, is reported from
general knowledge (Mishnah *Makkot* 3:10 is the usual citation; not checked). 2 Corinthians 11:24 says
*"forty stripes minus one"* (WEB at bible-api.com). The Greek was not checked.

**"Degraded."** *Wǝniqlâ* (WLC 25:3), niphal of *qālâ* (lemma 7034). In Genesis–Deuteronomy the lemma
occurs only here and at Deuteronomy 27:16 (*maqleh*, hiphil participle) (WLC lemma search).

**"Muzzle."** *Taḥsōm* (WLC 25:4), qal of *ḥāsam* (lemma 2629); in Genesis–Deuteronomy only here.
*Bǝdîšô*, infinitive of *dûš* (lemma 1758), *threshing*; also only here in Genesis–Deuteronomy (WLC
lemma search).

**The levirate vocabulary.** *Yibbēm* (lemma 2992), piel: Genesis 38:8; Deuteronomy 25:5, 7. *Yābām*
(lemma 2993): Deuteronomy 25:5, 7. *Yǝbēmet* (lemma 2994): Deuteronomy 25:7 (twice), 9. All in
Genesis–Deuteronomy (WLC lemma search). Ruth 1:15 uses *yǝbēmet* of Orpah, *"your sister-in-law"*
(reported from general knowledge; the Hebrew of Ruth was not checked).

**"Blotted out."** *Yimmāḥeh* (WLC 25:6), niphal of *māḥâ* (lemma 4229); *timḥeh* (25:19), qal. In
Deuteronomy the verb also at 9:14 and 29:19 (WEB 29:20) (WLC lemma search).

**"Doesn't want."** *Yaḥpōṣ* (25:7) and *ḥāpaṣtî* (25:8), qal of *ḥāpēṣ* (lemma 2654). In
Genesis–Deuteronomy five verses, Deuteronomy's three being 21:14; 25:7, 8 (WLC lemma search).
*"Refuses"* in 25:7 is a different verb, *lōʾ ʾābâ* (lemma 14), the word of 1:26, *"you would not go
up"*, and 2:30; 10:10; 23:5.

**"Loose his shoe."** *Wǝḥālǝṣâ naʿălô* (WLC 25:9); *bêt ḥălûṣ hannāʿal* (25:10). *Ḥālaṣ* (lemma 2502)
in Genesis–Deuteronomy: Leviticus 14:40, 43 (piel); Numbers 31:3, 5; 32:17, 20–21, 27, 29–30, 32;
Deuteronomy 3:18; 25:9–10. morphhb gives one lemma number to all of them. Whether these are one verb
or two homonyms is a lexicon question not settled here. *Naʿal* (lemma 5275) in Genesis–Deuteronomy:
Genesis 14:23; Exodus 3:5; 12:11; Deuteronomy 25:9–10; 29:4 (WEB 29:5). The WEB renders it *"sandal"*
at Genesis 14:23 and Exodus 3:5 and *"shoe"* here and at 29:5. Ruth 4:7–8 has a man take off *"his
shoe"* (WEB). [[Levirate Marriage - Genesis 38, Deuteronomy 25, and Ruth]] quoted verse 10 as *"sandal
removed"*; the WEB has *"shoe removed"*, and that page is corrected in this pass.

**"Spit."** *Wǝyārǝqâ bǝpānāyw* (WLC 25:9), qal of *yāraq* (lemma 3417); elsewhere in
Genesis–Deuteronomy only Numbers 12:14 (WLC lemma search).

**"Build up his brother's house."** *Lōʾ-yibneh ʾet-bêt ʾāḥîw* (WLC 25:9). Ruth 4:11 blesses Ruth to be
*"like Rachel and like Leah, which both built the house of Israel"* (WEB at bible-api.com). That Ruth
uses *bānâ* there is reported from general knowledge; the Hebrew of Ruth was not checked.

**"Private parts."** *Bimǝbušāyw* (WLC 25:11), lemma 4016; in Genesis–Deuteronomy only here. That it
occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible is reported from general knowledge. *Yinnāṣû* (25:11) is the
niphal of *nāṣâ* (lemma 5327); in Genesis–Deuteronomy the niphal occurs at
Exodus 2:13; 21:22; Leviticus 24:10; Deuteronomy 25:11, and the hiphil at Numbers 26:9 (WLC lemma
search).

**"Cut off."** *Wǝqaṣṣōtâ* (WLC 25:12), qal of *qāṣaṣ* (lemma 7112); elsewhere in Genesis–Deuteronomy
only Exodus 39:3, piel (WLC lemma search). *Lōʾ tāḥôs ʿênekā*: *ḥûs* (lemma 2347) at Genesis 45:20;
Deuteronomy 7:16; 13:9 (WEB 13:8); 19:13, 21; 25:12.

**"Bag," "weights," "measures."** *Kîs* (lemma 3599): in Genesis–Deuteronomy only Deuteronomy 25:13;
in Proverbs at 1:14; 16:11; 23:31. *ʾÊpâ wǝʾêpâ*: Deuteronomy 25:14 and Proverbs 20:10 (WLC). *ʾÊpâ*
(lemma 374) also at Exodus 16:36; Leviticus 5:11; 6:13 (WEB 6:20); 19:36; Numbers 5:15; 28:5.

**"Perfect and just."** *Šǝlēmâ wāṣedeq* (WLC 25:15). *Šālēm* (lemma 8003) in Genesis–Deuteronomy:
Genesis 15:16; 33:18; 34:21; Deuteronomy 25:15; 27:6. *Ṣedeq* (lemma 6664) in Deuteronomy: 1:16;
16:18, 20; 25:15; 33:19 (WLC lemma search).

**"Unrighteously."** *ʿĀwel* (lemma 5766): Leviticus 19:15, 35; Deuteronomy 25:16; 32:4 (WLC lemma
search, Genesis–Deuteronomy).

**"Remember."** *Zākôr* (infinitive absolute, lemma 2142) in Deuteronomy: 7:18; 24:9; 25:17 (WLC).
24:9 and 25:17 share *zākôr ʾēt ʾăšer ʿāśâ … baddere(k) bǝṣēʾtǝkem mimmiṣrāyim*; 23:5 (WEB 23:4) shares
the last three words.

**"Met you."** *Qārǝkā* (WLC 25:18), qal of *qārâ* (lemma 7136), *happen upon*. It is the verb of God
*"meeting"* Balaam (Numbers 23:3–4, 15–16, niphal).

**"Struck the rearmost."** *Wayzannēb* (WLC 25:18), piel of *zānab* (lemma 2179): in
Genesis–Deuteronomy only here; in Joshua at 10:19 (WLC lemma search, Genesis–Deuteronomy and Joshua).
That these are its only two occurrences in the Hebrew Bible is reported from general knowledge.

**"Feeble."** *Hanneḥĕšālîm* (WLC 25:18), niphal participle of *ḥāšal* (lemma 2826); in
Genesis–Deuteronomy only here. That it is a hapax legomenon of the Hebrew Bible is reported from
general knowledge.

**"Faint and weary."** *ʿĀyēp* (lemma 5889): Genesis 25:29, 30; Deuteronomy 25:18. *Yāgēaʿ* (lemma
3023): only Deuteronomy 25:18 in Genesis–Deuteronomy (WLC lemma search).

**"He didn't fear God."** *Wǝlōʾ yārēʾ ʾĕlōhîm* (WLC 25:18); *yārēʾ* is parsed by morphhb as qal
perfect 3ms. The alternative subject (Israel) is reported from general knowledge as a reading some
interpreters take.

**"Rest."** *Bǝhānîaḥ* (WLC 25:19), hiphil infinitive of *nûaḥ* (lemma 5117); the hiphil of giving
rest also at Deuteronomy 3:20; 12:10.

**"Memory."** *Zēker* (lemma 2143) in Genesis–Deuteronomy: Exodus 3:15; 17:14; Deuteronomy 25:19;
32:26. *"You shall not forget"*, *lōʾ tiškāḥ* (lemma 7911).

**What the passage does not settle.** What the forty strokes were given with. Why the ox stands
among laws about persons. Whether *"dwell together"* means a shared household or a shared
inheritance. Why the refusing brother is shamed but not compelled, and how the law relates to
Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21. Why the wife's hand is cut off, and whether the penalty was ever applied
literally. Whether the subject of *"he didn't fear God"* is Amalek or Israel. How Israel blots out a
memory it may not forget. Each is recorded, none adopted.
