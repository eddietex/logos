# Curses for Disobedience

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 28:15–68

## Summary

Moses turns from blessing to the consequences of refusing Yahweh’s commands. Ordinary life is
cursed in matching terms, then the warning expands through disease, drought, defeat, dispossession,
captivity, and economic dependence (15–46). Joyful service in abundance gives way to enemy service
under an iron yoke (47–48).

A distant nation lays siege to the fortified towns. Hunger destroys even the most tender family
bonds, with parents consuming their children (49–57). Further disease, numerical decline,
uprooting, and scattering leave survivors without rest or assurance of life. The closing image is
return to Egypt by ship and attempted self-sale with no buyer (58–68). This is a conditional
covenant warning delivered before entry, not a narrative naming a particular siege or exile.

## People

- [[Moses]] — Moses continues the address with conditional sanctions, not a report of their fulfillment (15, 45). The future king and invader remain unnamed.
- [[God]] — Yahweh is explicitly the agent of disease, defeat, exile, and terrifying insecurity (20–28, 63–68). Verse 63 places rejoicing over destruction beside rejoicing to do good; this disturbing claim must remain beside the promises of covenant preservation in Leviticus 26:44–45.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — Egypt supplies the named diseases (27, 60) and the final destination by ship (68). Return and attempted self-sale reverse the exodus; the WEB ends with nobody buying, not a completed slave transaction.
- [[Canaan]] — The given land is the scene of crop failure, siege, and eventual uprooting (21, 38–42, 52, 63). Canaan is the contextual identification; it is not named in this unit.

## Themes

- [[Curse]] — The opening curses reverse city, field, basket, offspring, and coming and going from the blessing list (15–19). The expanded sanctions overtake disobedience and reach exile and return to Egypt (45–68).
- [[Covenant]] — Land, multiplication, freedom, and national standing are all threatened with reversal (36, 43–44, 62–68). Destruction language stands in tension with Leviticus 26:44–45, where Yahweh refuses utter destruction and covenant cancellation; the witnesses are retained without making this unit a final declaration of abandonment.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — Failure to listen and do frames the sanctions (15, 45, 58, 62). Verse 58 expressly speaks of the words written in this book; the warning concerns enacted allegiance, not merely hearing a reading.
- [[War]] — Defeat reverses 28:7, and a distant, unnamed nation besieges trusted walls without sparing old or young (25, 49–57). These are threatened horrors, not commands authorizing siege atrocities.
- [[Famine and Plenty]] — Failed rain, insects, confiscated harvests, and siege hunger undo plenty (23–24, 33, 38–42, 51–57). The children themselves become food in the warning; the text describes familial devastation rather than approving it.
- [[Idolatry]] — Exile brings service to gods of wood and stone (36, 64), the very allegiance the covenant forbids. The threatened outcome is not permission to worship them.
- [[Retribution]] — The sanctions explicitly follow national disobedience (15, 45), but their symptoms cannot prove individual guilt. Job 2:3 affirms blamelessness amid ruin, and 2:7 describes a similarly extensive affliction; the tension rules out reversing the conditional.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — Failure to serve Yahweh joyfully gives way to serving enemies in deprivation under an iron yoke (47–48). Return to Egypt culminates in attempted self-sale without a buyer in the WEB (68), a reversal of liberation.
- [[The Nations]] — An unnamed distant nation invades, the king is deported, and Israel becomes a byword among peoples (36–37, 49–50, 64). The text does not identify a particular later empire, so no identification is imposed here.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — The land remains Yahweh’s gift even when its inhabitants are uprooted (52, 63). Scattering brings no secure resting place (64–65); possession does not make covenant accountability disappear.
- [[Suffering]] — The threatened suffering includes bodily disease, loss of children, hunger, and unremitting fear (27–35, 53–67). Job 2:3 explicitly resists applying this explanation to every sufferer; similar bodily symptoms do not establish similar guilt.
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — Verses 47–48 contrast joyful service in abundance with enemy service in deprivation. They address covenant ingratitude, not a general accusation against those who experience grief or cannot feel cheerful.
- [[The Fear of God]] — Failure to fear Yahweh’s glorious name accompanies disobedience (58); exile then brings fearful insecurity morning and evening (65–67). Reverence and debilitating terror have distinct roles in the warning.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — The king whom Israel will appoint shares the people’s deportation (36). Royal office offers no immunity from covenant judgment; neither the king nor the invading empire is named.
- [[Work]] — House-building, planting, sowing, and tending vineyards fail to yield enjoyment (30, 33, 38–40). The curse includes labor whose benefit goes to others, not simply the absence of effort.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — The resident foreigner rises and lends while Israel becomes dependent (43–44), reversing 28:12–13. This national sanction does not establish that each poor or indebted person is disobedient.
- [[Blessing]] — The blessings of 28:3–6 and 12–13 are reversed in 28:16–19 and 43–44. Both halves retain their conditional setting, rather than making election an unconditional guarantee of prosperity.
- [[Language and Speech]] — The invader’s language is not understood (49), intensifying alienation and powerlessness. No language or nation is identified by name.
- [[Judgment]] — Disease, dispossession, siege, and scattering are presented as divine sanctions (20–68). Their terrible effects on children are explicit; describing judgment must not conceal the cost or turn the warning into permission for cruelty.
- [[Marriage]] — Betrothal gives way to another man taking the woman (30); the siege destroys even affection between spouses (54–57). The protected beginnings of household life in Deuteronomy 20:5–7 are reversed.
- [[Signs and Wonders]] — The curses themselves become a sign and wonder to Israel and its offspring (46). Signs here expose covenant failure rather than guaranteeing rescue.
- [[Healing]] — The repeated inability to be healed intensifies the threatened disease (27, 35, 59–61). It is not a medical classification or a warrant for diagnosing sufferers as covenant-breakers.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 28:15–68 unit is retained. Repeated statements of disobedience
(15, 45, 58, 62) bind its expanding sanctions together. No further pericope is ingested.

**Reversal, not identification.** The blessings become curses; the protected household becomes
a dispossessed household; rescue from Egypt becomes return to Egypt. Neither the king of verse 36
nor the nation of verse 49 is named. Associating the eagle image with a particular empire would
require additional evidence. Generic rulers, foreigners, towns, and family members receive no
invented individual identities.

**Unresolved tensions.** Verse 63 speaks of Yahweh rejoicing to destroy, while Leviticus 26:44–45
refuses utter destruction and covenant cancellation. Deuteronomy itself describes survivors
(62, 65), so destruction is not simply interchangeable with the extinction of every person.
The promise and warning remain side by side. Job 2:3, 7 also prevents treating the threatened
symptoms as a universal test of personal guilt. This unit does not explain every later atrocity
or authorize blame against its victims.
