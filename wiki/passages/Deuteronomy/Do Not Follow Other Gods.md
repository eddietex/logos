# Do Not Follow Other Gods

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 12:29–13:18

## Summary

When Yahweh has cut off the nations and Israel lives in their land, Israel must not be ensnared
into asking *"How do these nations serve their gods? I will do likewise"* (12:29–30). *"You shall
not do so to Yahweh your God"*: every abomination Yahweh hates they have done for their gods,
*"for they even burn their sons and their daughters in the fire to their gods"* (31). Whatever
Moses commands is to be kept, with nothing added and nothing taken away (32).

Three cases follow, each a voice saying *"Let us go … and serve other gods"* (13:2, 6, 13):

1. **A prophet or dreamer of dreams** whose sign or wonder actually comes to pass (13:1–5). Israel
   must not listen, *"for Yahweh your God is testing you, to know whether you love Yahweh your God
   with all your heart and with all your soul"* (3). Israel is to walk after Yahweh, fear him, keep
   his commandments, obey his voice, serve him, and cling to him (4). The prophet is put to death
   for speaking rebellion against the God who brought Israel out of Egypt (5).
2. **A close relative or friend** who entices in secret — a brother, son, daughter, *"the wife of
   your bosom"*, or *"your friend, who is as your own soul"* (6–11). There is to be no consent, no
   pity, no concealment. The one enticed strikes first, then all the people, with stones, so that
   *"all Israel shall hear, and fear"* (11).
3. **A whole town** drawn away by *"certain wicked fellows"* (12–18). Israel must first inquire,
   search, and ask diligently (14). If the report is true, the town is struck and devoted to
   destruction with its livestock; its plunder is burned in its square, and it stays *"a heap
   forever"* (15–16). Nothing devoted is to cling to Israel's hand, so that Yahweh may turn from his
   anger, show mercy, and multiply Israel as he swore to the fathers (17–18).

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh, who cuts off the nations (12:29), hates what they did for their gods (31),
  tests Israel through a prophet's fulfilled sign (13:3), brought Israel out of Egypt (5, 10), and
  turns from anger to mercy (17).
- [[Moses]] — the speaker, whose command may not be added to or reduced (12:32; 13:18).
- The prophet or dreamer, the enticing relative or friend, and the *"wicked fellows"* of 13:13 are
  hypothetical figures in case law; no individual is named.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — *"the house of bondage"* from which Yahweh redeemed Israel (13:5, 10), the ground of
  the death sentence in both the prophet's and the relative's case.
- [[Canaan]] — not named; the nations' land that Israel will dwell in (12:29), and *"your cities,
  which Yahweh your God gives you"* (13:12).

## Notes

### The second half of the worship law

12:1–28 dealt with *where* Israel worships: one chosen place, not the nations' many shrines.
12:29–31 turns to *how*, and the same sentence governs both: *"You shall not do so to Yahweh your
God"* (12:4, 31). The danger is presented as curiosity after victory. The nations are already
destroyed (30), and the question *"How do these nations serve their gods?"* comes from a settled
Israel wanting to do *"likewise"* — for Yahweh, not necessarily for the other gods. The text
answers by naming the worst of their practices: burning sons and daughters.

Deuteronomy 12:31 says *burn … in the fire* (the verb *śārap*, WLC 12:31, morphhb). Deuteronomy
18:10 speaks instead of making a son or daughter *"pass through the fire"*, and the Molech laws of
Leviticus 18:21 and 20:2 of *giving* a child. This page records the difference in wording and does not decide whether the
phrases describe the same rite; see
[[Child Sacrifice and the Redemption of the Firstborn]].

### Add nothing, take nothing away

12:32 repeats 4:2 nearly word for word in Hebrew. Both use *lōʾ tōsēp* and *lōʾ tigraʿ* with the
same object, *the thing/word that I command you* (WLC Deuteronomy 4:2 and 13:1, morphhb). In the
Hebrew Bible this verse is 13:1, so it opens the chapter of the three cases. In the WEB it closes
chapter 12. Either way it joins them. The fulfilled sign of 13:2 is the clearest case of something
that would *add* to the command — a new revelation, apparently confirmed, that adds another god.

### The prophet whose sign comes true

The first case assumes the hardest version: the sign *"comes to pass"* (13:2). Deuteronomy does
not explain how a prophet of other gods can produce a fulfilled sign. It says only what the
fulfilment means for Israel: *"Yahweh your God is testing you"* (3). The verb is *nāsâ* (WLC 13:4,
morphhb), the same verb used in 8:2 of the forty years — *"testing you, to know what was in your
heart"*. Here the test asks whether Israel loves Yahweh *"with all your heart and with all your
soul"*, the first two phrases of 6:5.

The content of the message decides, not the power behind it. Deuteronomy 18:21–22 later gives a
second test: a word in Yahweh's name that does not happen was not spoken by Yahweh. The two do not
contradict. Failure exposes a false prophet; success does not prove a true one. See
[[Two Tests of a Prophet - Deuteronomy 13.1-5 and 18.20-22]].

Verse 4 piles up six verbs of loyalty — walk after, fear, keep, obey, serve, cling. The last,
*dābaq* (WLC 13:5, morphhb), returns at 13:17 (WLC 13:18): Israel clings to Yahweh, and nothing of
the devoted thing is to cling to Israel's hand. The wiki notes the repetition as it stands in the
Hebrew and in the WEB's *cling*.

### The enticer in the family

The second case moves from public sign to private speech. The list climbs toward intimacy: brother,
*"the son of your mother"*, son, daughter, *"the wife of your bosom"*, *"your friend, who is as
your own soul"* (6). The verb of 13:6 is *sût* (WLC 13:7, morphhb), *to incite* or *entice*, the
verb Job 2:3 uses of the accuser inciting God; see [[The Second Test]].

The penalty clauses refuse every means by which family protects its own: *"you shall not consent
to him, nor listen to him; neither shall your eye pity him, neither shall you spare, neither shall
you conceal him"* (8). *"Your hand shall be first on him"* (9). Unlike the third case, this one has
no instruction to inquire. Deuteronomy 17:2–7, which also stones an idolater, does require
diligent inquiry and *"two witnesses, or three witnesses"*, and puts *the hand of the witnesses*
first. Here the one enticed is himself the witness. The wiki records both laws and does not
harmonize them; whether 17:6's witness rule governs this case is a question the text leaves to
the reader.

Exodus 32:26–29 has a narrative counterpart: after the calf, the sons of Levi go through the camp,
*"every man kill his brother, and every man his companion, and every man his neighbor"* (27), and
Moses says they have consecrated themselves *"every man against his son, and against his
brother"* (29). See [[The Golden Calf]]. Matthew 10:37 sets loyalty to Jesus above father, mother,
son, and daughter, without a penalty attached.

### The apostate town

The third case is the only one with a stated investigation: *"you shall inquire, and make search,
and ask diligently"* (14), and only *"if it is true, and the thing certain"* does anything follow.
The people who draw the town away are *bənê bəliyyaʿal* (WLC 13:14, morphhb), which the WEB
translates *"certain wicked fellows"*.

What follows treats an Israelite town as the conquest treats the nations' towns. *"Destroying it
utterly"* (15) is the verb *ḥāram*, and *"the devoted thing"* (17) is *ḥērem* (WLC 13:16, 18,
morphhb) — the vocabulary of Deuteronomy 7:2, 26 and of Jericho in Joshua 6–7. Its plunder is not
divided but burned *"to Yahweh your God"*, and the town is never rebuilt (16). See
[[Devoted to Destruction - Leviticus 27.28-29, Deuteronomy 7, Joshua 6-7, and 1 Samuel 15]].

Mercy appears in this unit only at this point, and it is Israel that receives it: if nothing devoted
is kept, Yahweh will *"turn from the fierceness of his anger, and show you mercy, and have
compassion on you, and multiply you"* (17). The mercy is to the nation, after the town has been
destroyed.

### The severity, stated plainly

All three cases end in death, and the second forbids pity in so many words. The wiki does not soften
this. Four things in the text bear on how it is read, and they are recorded here without being
offered as a resolution:

- The offence is not private belief but active recruitment. Each case has someone saying *"Let us
  go … and serve other gods"* (13:2, 6, 13).
- The stated reason in each case is the exodus: rebellion against *"Yahweh your God, who brought
  you out of the land of Egypt, and redeemed you out of the house of bondage"* (5, 10). The law
  treats enticement as treason against the God who freed Israel.
- The stated aim is deterrence: *"All Israel shall hear, and fear"* (11).
- The town case requires diligent inquiry and certainty first (14).

Whether these laws were enforced, and how often, is a historical question this page does not try
to answer.

### "Remove the evil from among you"

13:5 is the first use of a formula that recurs throughout Deuteronomy's laws: 17:7, 17:12, 19:19,
21:21, 22:21, 22:22, 22:24, and 24:7 (stored WEB, searched by English phrase). The verb is *bāʿar*,
*to burn* or *purge* (WLC 13:6, morphhb). Paul quotes the formula in 1 Corinthians 5:13, *"Put away
the wicked man from among yourselves"* (WEB, verified from bible-api.com), and applies it to
expulsion from the church rather than death. See
[[Remove the Evil from Among You - Deuteronomy 13.5 and 1 Corinthians 5.13]].

### Boundary

The planned boundary (12:29–13:18) is kept. 12:29 changes subject from the place of worship to
the danger of imitating the nations, and 14:1 begins the laws of mourning and food. The WEB chapter
break at 13:1 falls one verse later than the Hebrew (WLC 13:1 = WEB 12:32); this page cites WEB
verse numbers throughout and gives the WLC numbers in parentheses where it cites the Hebrew.
