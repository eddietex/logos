# A Holy People and Its Food

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 14:1–21

## Summary

Moses opens with who Israel is: *"You are the children of Yahweh your God"* (14:1). Because of
that, they are not to cut themselves or make baldness *"between your eyes for the dead"*. Because
they are *"a holy people to Yahweh your God"*, chosen *"to be a people for his own possession,
above all peoples who are on the face of the earth"* (2), they are not to eat *"any abominable
thing"* (3).

What follows is a food list in four parts:

1. **Land animals** (4–8). Ten permitted animals are named, from the ox to the chamois (4–5). Then
   comes the rule: an animal that parts the hoof, has it cloven in two, and chews the cud may be
   eaten (6). The camel, hare, and rabbit chew the cud but do not part the hoof. The pig parts the
   hoof but does not chew the cud. All four are unclean, and *"you shall not touch their carcasses"*
   (7–8).
2. **Water creatures** (9–10). Whatever has fins and scales may be eaten. Anything else is unclean.
3. **Birds** (11–18). *"Of all clean birds you may eat"* (11). There follows a list of twenty-one
   birds that may not be eaten, from the eagle to the hoopoe, with the bat at the end.
4. **Winged swarming things** (19–20). *"All winged creeping things are unclean to you"* (19), and
   then the list closes as it opened: *"Of all clean birds you may eat"* (20).

Verse 21 adds three more rules. Israel is not to eat an animal that died of itself. It may be given
to *"the foreigner living among you who is within your gates"*, or sold to *"a foreigner"*, for
*"you are a holy people to Yahweh your God"*. And: *"You shall not boil a young goat in its
mother's milk."*

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh, whose children Israel are (14:1), who has chosen them as *"a people for his own
  possession"* (2), and to whom they are holy (2, 21).
- [[Moses]] — the speaker, not named in these verses; the address of 12:1 onward continues.
- The foreigner within the gates and the foreigner to whom carcass meat may be sold (21) are
  categories of person in law, not named individuals.

- No place is named. *"Within your gates"* (21) assumes Israel settled in its towns in the land.

## Notes

### Identity first, rules second

The unit begins and ends with Israel's status. *"You are the children of Yahweh your God"* (1)
opens it; *"you are a holy people to Yahweh your God"* stands at 2 and again at 21. The food list
sits between them. As in Leviticus 11:44–45, holiness is not earned by the diet. The diet follows
from a holiness already given. Deuteronomy's ground is election (*"Yahweh has chosen you"*, 2),
where Leviticus 11:45 grounds it in the exodus.

*Children* is *bānîm*, *sons* (WLC 14:1, morphhb). Deuteronomy has already used the image twice:
Yahweh carried Israel *"as a man does bear his son"* (1:31) and disciplines Israel *"as a man
disciplines his son"* (8:5). Exodus 4:22 calls Israel *"my son, my firstborn"*. Here, for the
first time in the book, the image becomes a direct statement of who Israel is, and the first
consequence drawn from it concerns the dead.

### Cutting and baldness for the dead

*"You shall not cut yourselves"* is the verb *gādad* in its reflexive form (*titgōdədû*, WLC 14:1,
morphhb). The same form describes the prophets of Baal at Carmel, who *"cut themselves in their way
with knives and lances"* (1 Kings 18:28; WEB verified from bible-api.com; WLC *wayyitgōdədû*,
morphhb). *Baldness* is *qorḥâ*, the noun Leviticus 21:5 uses for the priests. The text gives
no reason for the ban beyond the identity clause before it. It does not say these were foreign rites,
though 1 Kings 18:28 shows self-cutting in the worship of Baal. Leviticus 19:28 forbids the same
thing to all Israel with a different word (*śereṭ*, WLC, morphhb); Leviticus 21:5 lays it on the
priests in particular. Deuteronomy gives the ban to the whole people, called *"a holy people"* (2).

The ban is on cutting and shaving, not on grief. Deuteronomy records thirty days of weeping for
Moses at 34:8 without censure. Jeremiah 16:6 later names cutting and baldness as ordinary mourning
in Judah (WEB verified from bible-api.com), which suggests the law was not always kept.

### Deuteronomy's list beside Leviticus 11

The two lists agree on the tests — hoof and cud, fins and scales — and on most of the forbidden
animals. The differences:

- **Deuteronomy names what may be eaten.** Ten permitted land animals open the list (4–5).
  Leviticus 11 names only forbidden ones.
- **The camel, hare, and *shāpān*.** Both chapters forbid the same three cud-chewers by the same
  Hebrew names; *šāpān* is the same word at Deuteronomy 14:7 and Leviticus 11:5 (WLC, morphhb). The
  WEB renders it *rabbit* here and *cony* in Leviticus. The difference is in the English, not the
  source. The wiki does not identify the animal zoologically.
- **The birds.** Deuteronomy's list has one name more in the WEB (21 against 20), and the Hebrew
  differs at one point: Deuteronomy 14:13 has *rāʾâ*, *ʾayyâ* and *dayyâ*, where Leviticus 11:14
  has *dāʾâ* and *ʾayyâ* (WLC, morphhb). Whether *rāʾâ* and *dāʾâ* are one bird, and how the
  English names map onto the Hebrew, are questions the wiki leaves open.
- **No edible insects.** Leviticus 11:20–23 calls winged swarming things that go on all fours an
  abomination, then permits four kinds that hop: locust, katydid, cricket, grasshopper. Deuteronomy
  14:19 uses the same phrase, *šereṣ hāʿôp* (WLC, morphhb), and makes no exception. The tension is
  recorded, not resolved. One reading turns on 14:20, which repeats 14:11 but uses a different word
  for *bird*: 14:11 has *ṣippôr*, 14:20 has *ʿôp*, the word in *šereṣ hāʿôp* just before it (WLC,
  morphhb). The WEB translates both as *birds*. On that reading, 14:20's *clean ʿôp* could include
  Leviticus' permitted insects. The text does not say so.
- **Carcasses.** Leviticus 11:24–40 gives long rules for touching carcasses and the uncleanness that
  follows. Deuteronomy has one clause: *"you shall not touch their carcasses"* (8). Leviticus 11:8
  has the same clause after the camel, hare, cony, and pig. In Deuteronomy it follows the pig
  sentence directly; the plural *their* (*ûbənibəlātām*, WLC 14:8, morphhb) may take in all four
  animals or the pig alone, and the wiki does not decide which.

See [[Two Lists of Clean Animals - Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14]].

### *"Any abominable thing"*

*Abominable thing* is *tôʿēbâ* (WLC 14:3, morphhb), the word Deuteronomy has just used for what
the nations did for their gods (12:31) and for the idolatrous town (13:14; WLC 13:15). Leviticus 11 uses a
different word, *šeqeṣ*, for its forbidden creatures (WLC Leviticus 11:20, morphhb). Deuteronomy
puts unclean food under the same word as idolatry. It does not say that eating the pig is
idolatry, and the wiki does not infer it.

### The animal that dies of itself

Verse 21 forbids Israel to eat *nəbēlâ*, an animal that died without being slaughtered. It may be
given to the *gēr*, *"the foreigner living among you"*, or sold to a *nokrî*, *"a foreigner"*
(WLC, morphhb). The WEB translates both words *foreigner*; the Hebrew distinguishes the resident
alien from the outsider.

**This is in tension with Leviticus 17:15.** That verse requires *"every person that eats what dies
of itself, … whether he is native-born or a foreigner"* (*gēr*, WLC, morphhb) to wash, bathe, and be
unclean until evening. Leviticus treats the *gēr* who eats *nəbēlâ* as needing cleansing.
Deuteronomy gives the *gēr* *nəbēlâ* to eat, without mentioning cleansing. Exodus 22:31 is a third
form: torn meat goes to the dogs. Each text is recorded as it stands. A common reconciliation is
that the *gēr* in Deuteronomy is not bound by the holiness given to Israel, while Leviticus
regulates the impurity that eating still causes. That is an interpretation, and neither text
states it. See [[Torn Flesh and Cleansing - Exodus 22 and Leviticus 11 and 17]].

The ground given is the same as at 14:2: *"for you are a holy people"*. The law is not about what
is wholesome; the meat may be given away or sold. It is about who Israel is.

### The young goat

The last clause, *"You shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk"*, is the third occurrence
of this rule (Exodus 23:19; 34:26). In Exodus it stands next to first fruits; here it closes the
food laws. No reason is given in any of the three. The wiki does not adopt any reason for it. See
[[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]].

### The New Testament

Mark 7:19 says, in the WEB, that food *"doesn't go into his heart, but into his stomach, then into
the latrine, thus purifying all foods"* (verified from bible-api.com). English versions differ on
whether the last clause is Jesus' words or the narrator's comment; the wiki has not checked the
Greek and makes no claim about it. In Acts 10:14, Peter refuses the vision's command: *"I have
never eaten anything that is common or unclean"* (WEB, verified from bible-api.com). The vision's
point, Acts 10:28 says, is about people, not food. See
[[All Foods Clean - Leviticus 11, Deuteronomy 14, Mark 7, and Acts 10]].

### Boundary

The planned boundary (14:1–21) is kept. 14:1 begins a new subject after the apostate town of
13:12–18, and 14:22 turns from food that may not be eaten to the tithe that is eaten before
Yahweh.
