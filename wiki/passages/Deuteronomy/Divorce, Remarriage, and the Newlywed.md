# Divorce, Remarriage, and the Newlywed

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 24:1–5

## Summary

Two laws about the beginning and end of a marriage. A man who sends his wife away with a written bill
of divorce may not take her back after she has married another man and that marriage has ended, by
divorce or by death (1–4). A man newly married is not sent with the army or given any public duty for
a year, so that he may *"cheer his wife whom he has taken"* (5).

In the Hebrew, chapter 24 has the same verse numbers as the WEB. The offset of chapter 23 has ended.

**One long sentence (24:1–4).** Verses 1–3 are all condition, and the law itself comes only in verse 4.
*If* a man marries a woman, *and* she finds no favor in his eyes, *and* he writes her a bill of divorce
and sends her out, *and* she marries another man, *and* the second man hates her and divorces her or
dies — *then* *"her former husband, who sent her away, may not take her again to be his wife"* (4). The
divorce is assumed, not commanded. Moses does not say that a man must divorce, or may, or on what
grounds; he says what follows once a divorce and a remarriage have happened. Everything a modern reader
might want to know — what *"some unseemly thing"* is, whether the wife can initiate, whether the first
divorce was just — lies in the conditions, where the law does not rule on it.

The WEB starts the *then* earlier: *"then it shall be … that he shall write her a bill of divorce"*
(1). Read that way, verse 1 commands the bill. The Hebrew is a chain of clauses joined by *and*, and
where the condition ends is a matter of reading. Many modern translations hold the *then* until verse
4; that is reported from general knowledge. The two readings matter for the Gospel exchange, where
Moses is said both to have *"command[ed]"* and to have *"allowed"* the bill (see Notes).

**The ground of the divorce.** *"If she finds no favor in his eyes, because he has found some unseemly
thing in her"* (1). *"Finds no favor"* is *ḥēn*, the word of Genesis 6:8, *"Noah found favor in
Yahweh's eyes"*. In Deuteronomy *ḥēn* occurs only here (see Notes). *"Some unseemly thing"* is *ʿerwat
dābār*, literally *the nakedness of a thing*. The same phrase was the *"unclean thing"* in the camp of
23:14, which Yahweh must not *"see … in you, and turn away from you"*. The two verses are the only
places it occurs in the books searched (see Notes). In the camp it was excrement left uncovered; what it
means in a wife the text does not say. It is less than adultery, which 22:22 punishes with death, and it
is something the husband *"has found"*. The later rabbinic dispute over this phrase is recorded in
Notes and not adopted.

**The bill of divorce.** *"He shall write her a bill of divorce, and put it in her hand, and send her out
of his house"* (1). Three acts: writing, giving, sending. The second husband repeats all three in the
same words (3). The phrase is *sēper kǝrîtut*, *a document of cutting off*. It is put *"in her hand"*:
the document is hers, and the next verse says what it is for: *"When she has departed out of his house,
she may go and be another man's wife"* (2). Deuteronomy does not say the bill protects her. But the
law assumes she will marry again and treats the second marriage as lawful. The bill is the evidence
that she is free to do so (an observation, not a statement of the text).

The verb for *"send her out"* is *šillaḥ*, the piel of *šālaḥ*. Deuteronomy has used it already for the
captive wife who is *"let go where she desires"* (21:14), and twice for a power taken away: the
slandering husband and the man who seized a virgin *"may not put her away all his days"* (22:19, 29).
Chapter 22 removed the power to send away; chapter 24 limits what happens after it is used. Both
chapters keep the woman from being taken up and put down at a man's pleasure.

**The second husband.** *"If the latter husband hates her"* (3). The verb is *śānēʾ*, *hate*. It is the
verb of the *"hated"* wife whose son keeps the double portion (21:15–17) and of the husband who *"hates"*
his bride and slanders her (22:13, 16). In Deuteronomy's marriage law *hate* is the word for a husband
turning from a wife, not necessarily an emotion; the text does not define it further. The second
marriage can also end in death (3). The law treats both endings alike: in neither case may the first
husband take her back.

**"After that she is defiled" (24:4).** The prohibition is *lōʾ-yûkal*, *he is not able*. The same
words limited the father who would move the firstborn's portion (21:16) and the two husbands who may not
divorce (22:19, 29). This is the fourth limit in four chapters on what a man may do with the members of
his household.

The reason given is *ʾaḥărê ʾăšer huṭṭammāʾâ*, *"after that she is defiled"*. The verb is *ṭāmēʾ* in a
rare passive stem, the hotpaal: she *has been made unclean*. In Deuteronomy the stem occurs only in this
verb, and *ṭāmēʾ* occurs elsewhere in the book only at 21:23, *"that you don't defile your land"* (see
Notes). Who or what defiled her the text does not say. Three readings are current, and the wiki adopts
none:

1. **The second marriage defiles her with respect to the first husband.** Leviticus 18:20 forbids lying
   with a neighbor's wife *"and defile yourself with her"*, and Numbers 5:13–14, 29 says the wife who
   goes astray *"is defiled"*. On this reading, a return to the first husband would make the second
   marriage retrospectively like adultery.
2. **The defilement is the first husband's own doing.** He declared her unfit by his *"unseemly
   thing"*; having done so, he may not reclaim her after another man has had her. The passive verb
   would then describe her status in his eyes, and the law would protect the wife from being reclaimed.
3. **The law protects the second marriage.** If the first husband can take her back, the second husband
   is exposed to a rival, and divorce becomes a means of exchanging wives. Forbidding the return makes
   the first divorce final.

*"For that is abomination before Yahweh"* (4). This is *tôʿēbâ hiwʾ lipnê YHWH*, not Deuteronomy's
usual construct *tôʿăbat YHWH*, *"an abomination to Yahweh"* (18:12; 22:5; 23:18). In the Torah this
is the only verse where *tôʿēbâ* is said to be *before Yahweh* (see Notes).

*"You shall not cause the land to sin, which Yahweh your God gives you for an inheritance"* (4). The
verb is the hiphil of *ḥāṭāʾ*, *to cause to sin*. In the Torah it occurs only here and at Exodus 23:33,
where the nations *"make you sin against me"*. Here the object is the land. The phrase *"which Yahweh
your God gives you for an inheritance"* closed the law of the hanged man (21:23) and the cities of
refuge (19:10). Each time, a private act, a body left on a tree, innocent blood, a wife reclaimed,
reaches the land. Leviticus 18:25, 28 says *"the land was defiled"* and *"vomited out her inhabitants"*;
Numbers 35:33–34 says *"blood pollutes the land"* and *"You shall not defile the land … in the middle of
which I dwell"*. Deuteronomy makes the land able to sin.

**The newlywed (24:5).** *"When a man takes a new wife, he shall not go out in the army, neither shall he
be assigned any business. He shall be free at home one year, and shall cheer his wife whom he has
taken."* It opens with the same four Hebrew words as 24:1 and 22:13, *kî-yiqqaḥ ʾîš ʾiššâ*, *when a man
takes a wife* (WLC). Three laws begin with a man taking a wife. In 22:13 he hates her and slanders her;
in 24:1 he finds no favor in her and sends her out; in 24:5 he stays home and makes her glad.

The law extends 20:7, where the man *"who has pledged to be married a wife, and has not taken her"* is
sent home from battle *"lest he die in the battle, and another man take her"*. 20:7 covers the betrothed
man; 24:5 covers the man just married. The *"new"* wife is *ḥădāšâ*, the adjective of the *"new house"*
not yet dedicated (20:5) and the *"new house"* that needs a railing (22:8). See
[[Let Him Return to His House - Deuteronomy 20, 24.5, 28.30, Judges 7, and Luke 14]].

*"Neither shall he be assigned any business"* is, literally, *nothing shall pass over him for any
matter*. The exemption goes beyond war to any public burden. *"Free"* is *nāqî*, the adjective of
*"innocent blood"* in 19:10, 13 and 21:8–9, and of Genesis 24:41, where Abraham's servant will be
*"clear from my oath"*. Here it means released from obligation, *"at home"* (literally *to his house*)
*"one year"*.

*"Shall cheer his wife"* is *wǝśimmaḥ ʾet-ʾištô*, the piel of *śāmaḥ*: he shall *make his wife glad*.
Everywhere else in Deuteronomy the verb is qal, Israel rejoicing *"before Yahweh your God"* at the
feasts (12:7, 12, 18; 14:26; 16:11, 14; 26:11; 27:7), and its object is never a person. This is the
only piel of *śāmaḥ* in the Torah (see Notes). The year is given for her gladness, not his. Where the
divorce law limits what a husband may do to a wife, the newlywed law commands what he is to do for her.

**How the passage fits together.** The divorce law and the newlywed law share the man who *"takes a
wife"*, and they face each other: one marriage ending twice, one marriage beginning. The divorce law has
no command to love; the newlywed law has no penalty. Both put limits on other powers, the husband's to
reclaim and the army's to conscript, for the sake of a marriage. 24:6 turns to pledges. The boundary is
the plan's, kept as drawn.

**What later texts do with it.** Jeremiah 3:1 quotes the case of verse 4: *"If a man puts away his wife,
and she goes from him, and becomes another man's, should he return to her again? Wouldn't that land be
greatly polluted?"* Then Yahweh says to Israel, who has *"played the prostitute with many lovers"*, *"yet
return again to me"*. Jeremiah 3:8 has Yahweh give Israel *"a bill of divorce"*. Isaiah 50:1 asks,
*"Where is the bill of your mother's divorce, with which I have put her away?"* See
[[A Bill of Divorce - Deuteronomy 24.1-4, Isaiah 50.1, and Jeremiah 3]]. In the Gospels, Pharisees ask
Jesus whether divorce is lawful, and the exchange turns on what Moses *"commanded"* and what he
*"allowed"* (Matthew 19:3–9; Mark 10:2–12; compare Matthew 5:31–32; Luke 16:18). See
[[Because of the Hardness of Your Hearts - Deuteronomy 24.1-4 and the Gospels]].

## Notes

**Verse numbering.** WEB 24:1–5 is WLC 24:1–5. The boundary is the plan's, kept as drawn, with commas
added to the title: 24:5 shares the opening of 24:1 and the subject of marriage, and 24:6 begins the
pledge laws.

**"Favor."** *Ḥēn* (lemma 2580) occurs in Deuteronomy only at 24:1 (WLC lemma search). It occurs 27
times in Genesis–Deuteronomy in all.

**"Some unseemly thing."** *ʿErwat dābār* (WLC 24:1; lemma 6172 with 1697). In Deuteronomy *ʿerwâ*
occurs only at 23:15 (WEB 23:14) and 24:1. The earlier ingest found *ʿerwat dābār* only in these two
verses in the books it searched. This pass searched Genesis–Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea, and
Malachi; the full canon was not searched. Reported from general knowledge and not checked: the Mishnah
(*Gittin* 9:10) records the school of Shammai reading the phrase as sexual misconduct, and the school of
Hillel as anything that displeases the husband. The wiki adopts neither.

**"Bill of divorce."** *Sēper kǝrîtut* (WLC 24:1, 3). The noun *kǝrîtut* (lemma 3748) occurs in the
books searched only at Deuteronomy 24:1, 3; Isaiah 50:1; and Jeremiah 3:8 (WLC lemma search,
Genesis–Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea, Malachi). That these are its only occurrences in the
Hebrew Bible is reported from general knowledge. That *kǝrîtut* is from *kārat*, *to cut off*, is the
standard lexical view; morphhb gives it its own lemma number.

**"Send her out."** *Wǝšillǝḥāh* (WLC 24:1, 3) and *šillǝḥāh* (24:4), piel of *šālaḥ* (lemma 7971).
Deuteronomy's other piels with a wife as object are 21:14; 22:19, 29 (WLC). Isaiah 50:1 and Jeremiah
3:1, 8 use the same piel. Leviticus 21:7, 14; 22:13 and Numbers 30:9 (WLC 30:10) describe a divorced woman with a different
verb, the qal passive participle of *gāraš* (lemma 1644), *driven out* (WLC lemma search). Deuteronomy
never uses it of a wife. [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]] calls *gāraš* *"the Torah's
word for what happens to a divorced wife"*; that is true of Leviticus and Numbers, and that page now
notes Deuteronomy's *šillaḥ*.

**Where the condition ends.** In the WLC, verses 1–3 run *wǝhāyâ ʾim-lōʾ … wǝkātab … wǝnātan …
wǝšillǝḥāh … wǝyāṣǝʾâ … wǝhālǝkâ … wǝhāyǝtâ … ûśǝnēʾāh*. Each is a perfect with *wǝ-*, and nothing in
the grammar of verse 1 marks where the protasis ends. Matthew 19:7 has the Pharisees say Moses
*"command[ed] us to give her a bill of divorce"*; Mark 10:3–4 has Jesus ask *"What did Moses command
you?"* and the Pharisees answer *"Moses allowed"* (WEB). The Greek was not checked.

**"Hates."** *Ûśǝnēʾāh* (WLC 24:3), qal of *śānēʾ* (lemma 8130). In Deuteronomy's family law: 21:15–17
(the *"hated"* wife, four times) and 22:13, 16 (WLC).

**"May not."** *Lōʾ-yûkal* (WLC 24:4), qal imperfect of *yākōl* (lemma 3201). The same form, *yûkal*,
3ms, with *lōʾ*, occurs in Deuteronomy at 21:16; 22:19, 29; and 24:4 (WLC lemma search).

**"After that she is defiled."** *Huṭṭammāʾâ* (WLC 24:4), tagged in morphhb as the hotpaal of
*ṭāmēʾ* (lemma 2930). In the books searched, the hotpaal stem occurs once in Deuteronomy (this verse),
twice in Leviticus, three times in Numbers, and once in Isaiah, each time with another verb; this is
the only hotpaal of *ṭāmēʾ* (morphhb morphology search, Genesis–Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea,
Malachi). *Ṭāmēʾ* occurs in Deuteronomy otherwise only at 21:23, in the piel (WLC). The hotpaal is a
passive-reflexive stem; whether this form is best read *she has been made unclean* or *she has let
herself be made unclean* is a grammatical question this page does not settle.

**"Abomination before Yahweh."** *Tôʿēbâ hiwʾ lipnê YHWH* (WLC 24:4). A co-occurrence search of
*tôʿēbâ* (lemma 8441) with *pānîm* (lemma 6440) in Genesis–Deuteronomy found four verses: Leviticus
18:27, 30 and Deuteronomy 18:12 use *pānîm* of the nations *before you*, not of Yahweh. Deuteronomy 24:4
is the only verse in the Torah that puts an abomination *before Yahweh* (WLC, read verse by verse).

**"Cause the land to sin."** *Wǝlōʾ taḥăṭîʾ ʾet-hāʾāreṣ* (WLC 24:4), hiphil of *ḥāṭāʾ* (lemma 2398).
In the Torah the hiphil occurs only here and at Exodus 23:33 (WLC morphology search). Jeremiah 3:1
uses another verb, *ḥānap*, *to be polluted* (lemma 2610), whose only Torah occurrences are Numbers
35:33, twice (WLC lemma search).

**"New."** *Ḥădāšâ* (lemma 2319). In Deuteronomy: 20:5; 22:8; 24:5; 32:17 (WLC).

**"Any business."** *Wǝlōʾ-yaʿăbōr ʿālāyw lǝkol-dābār* (WLC 24:5), *and nothing shall pass over him
for any matter*.

**"Free."** *Nāqî* (lemma 5355). In the Torah: Genesis 24:41; 44:10; Exodus 21:28; 23:7; Numbers
32:22; Deuteronomy 19:10, 13; 21:8, 9; 24:5; 27:25 (WLC lemma search).

**"Cheer his wife."** *Wǝśimmaḥ* (WLC 24:5), piel perfect of *śāmaḥ* (lemma 8055). In Deuteronomy the
verb otherwise occurs in the qal: 12:7, 12, 18; 14:26; 16:11, 14; 26:11; 27:7; 33:18. In
Genesis–Deuteronomy this is the only piel (WLC morphology search). Some translations take the clause as
*rejoice with his wife*; that requires reading the verb differently from the Masoretic pointing, and is
reported from general knowledge, not checked against any ancient version.

**What the passage does not settle.** What *ʿerwat dābār* is. Whether a wife could initiate divorce.
Who or what defiled the wife, and why the return is an abomination when the remarriage is not. Whether
the law's purpose is to protect the woman, the second marriage, or the land. Whether 24:5 also exempts
the man from labor at home, or only from public service. Each is recorded, none adopted.
