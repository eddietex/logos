# Care for the Neighbor and Ordered Distinctions

**Book:** [[Deuteronomy]]
**Reference:** Deuteronomy 22:1–12

## Summary

Ten short laws, most of a single verse, with no narrative and no heading to join them. They fall into
two groups that overlap rather than divide cleanly. The first is about what one Israelite owes another
when he comes upon something by chance — a stray animal, a lost coat, an animal fallen on the road, a
bird's nest, a roof someone could fall from. The second is about keeping apart what belongs apart —
men's and women's clothing, two kinds of seed, an ox and a donkey under one plow, wool and linen in one
cloth — and it ends with the fringes on the four corners of the cloak. The order of the pericope moves
back and forth between the two, so the pattern is recorded here and not imposed.

**The brother's animal and the brother's lost thing (22:1–4).** *"You shall not see your brother's ox
or his sheep go astray, and hide yourself from them. You shall surely bring them again to your
brother"* (1). If the owner is far off or unknown, the finder takes the animal *"home to your house,
and it shall be with you until your brother comes looking for it, and you shall restore it to him"*
(2). The same holds *"with his donkey … with his garment … with every lost thing of your brother's,
which he has lost, and you have found. You may not hide yourself"* (3). And if the animal has fallen
on the road, *"You shall surely help him to lift them up again"* (4).

The law is the Exodus law of the enemy's ox, retold. Exodus 23:4–5 has *"your enemy's ox or his
donkey going astray"* and *"the donkey of him who hates you fallen down under his burden"*; Deuteronomy
has *"your brother"* throughout. Two things change with the word. Exodus reached across a quarrel;
Deuteronomy reaches across distance and anonymity, to an owner who *"isn't near to you"* or whom *"you
don't know"* (2), and so it adds the instruction Exodus does not have, to keep the animal at home until
he comes. And Deuteronomy names the temptation Exodus does not: to *"hide yourself"*, to look away and
let it be someone else's matter. The verb is said three times in four verses (see Notes). The law asks
for a small cost — a stall, some fodder, a delay — from someone who did nothing to cause the loss.
See [[Help for an Enemy - Exodus 23, Proverbs 25, and Matthew 5]].

**Men's and women's clothing (22:5).** *"A woman shall not wear men's clothing, neither shall a man
put on women's clothing; for whoever does these things is an abomination to Yahweh your God."* The
reason is *tôʿēbâ*, *abomination*, Deuteronomy's word for what Yahweh will not have among his people:
images (7:25–26), the Canaanite rites (12:31; 18:9–12), unclean food (14:3), a blemished sacrifice
(17:1), and dishonest weights (25:16). What the law has in view is not stated. The first clause does
not use the ordinary word for clothes but *kǝlî geber*, *a man's article* — a word that elsewhere covers
vessels, tools, and weapons — and the second uses *śimlâ*, the cloak of 22:3. Readings that take the
law as about cultic disguise, about war gear, or about keeping the sexes visibly distinct have each been
proposed; the text supports the last most directly and does not exclude the others, and the wiki adopts
none.

**The mother bird (22:6–7).** *"If you come across a bird's nest on the way, in any tree or on the
ground, with young ones or eggs, and the hen sitting on the young, or on the eggs, you shall not take
the hen with the young. You shall surely let the hen go, but the young you may take for yourself; that
it may be well with you, and that you may prolong your days."* It is a law about chance — the nest
*"you come across … on the way"* — and about restraint at the moment of chance. The eggs and young
may be taken; the mother, who stays on the nest rather than fly, may not. The promise attached is the
promise of the fifth commandment, *"that your days may be long, and that it may go well with you"*
(5:16): the same two verbs, in the other order (see Notes). Deuteronomy attaches that promise to
honoring father and mother and to this. Why is not said. That the law protects the source of future
young, or that it forbids taking advantage of a mother's care for her offspring, or both, are the usual
readings.

**The parapet (22:8).** *"When you build a new house, then you shall make a railing around your roof,
so that you don't bring blood on your house if anyone falls from there."* The flat roof was living
space, and the owner is responsible for the fall he could have prevented. *"Blood"* is *dāmîm*, the
plural of bloodguilt, the word of 19:10, where innocent blood shed in the land would leave *"blood
guilt on you"*. The previous chapter's unsolved killing required a rite to put blood away; here the
law requires a railing so that there is none to put away.

**Two kinds (22:9–11).** *"You shall not sow your vineyard with two kinds of seed, lest all the fruit
be defiled, the seed which you have sown, and the increase of the vineyard. You shall not plow with an
ox and a donkey together. You shall not wear clothes of wool and linen woven together."* Leviticus
19:19 has three laws of the same kind — no crossbreeding of animals, no field sown with two kinds, no
garment of two kinds — and Deuteronomy's three differ from them at two points: a vineyard in place of
a field, and an ox and donkey yoked together in place of animals bred together. The two key words,
*kilʾayim*, *two kinds*, and *šaʿaṭnēz*, the mixed cloth, occur in the Torah only in these two places
(see Notes). The consequence in 22:9 is unusual: what WEB renders *"be defiled"* is the verb *qādaš*,
*become holy* — the crop is forfeited, though to whom the text does not say. See
[[Two Kinds - Leviticus 19.19 and Deuteronomy 22.9-11]].

The laws give no reason except for the vineyard, and the vineyard's reason is forfeiture, not
hygiene. The ox is a clean animal that may be offered; the donkey is not, and its firstborn must be
redeemed with a lamb (Exodus 13:13). The two also differ in strength and gait. Whether the law means
kindness to the weaker animal, the separation of clean and unclean, or a sign of Israel's own
separateness, the text does not say. Wool and linen were
worn separately by Israelites; Leviticus 13:47 speaks of *"a woolen garment, or a linen garment"* side
by side. That some priestly fabrics combined them is often argued from Exodus 28 and 39; that argument
is reported here from general knowledge and was not checked against the Hebrew in this pass.

**The fringes (22:12).** *"You shall make yourselves fringes on the four corners of your cloak with
which you cover yourself."* The law is the shortest statement of Numbers 15:37–41, which gives the
fringes a blue cord and a purpose — *"that you may look on it, and remember all Yahweh's commandments,
and do them"* (15:39). Deuteronomy gives neither the cord nor the purpose, and uses a different word for
the fringe (*gǝdilîm*, not Numbers' *ṣîṣit*). Placed here, after three laws against mixing, it ends
the sequence on what the Israelite *is* to wear: a sign on the cloak that the wearer belongs to the one
who gave the law. See [[Fringes on the Four Corners - Numbers 15.37-41 and Deuteronomy 22.12]].

**How the passage fits together.** The laws come between the hanged man of 21:22–23 and the marriage
laws of 22:13–30, and both neighbors share words with them: the *śimlâ*, *cloak*, of 22:3, 5 returns
in 22:17, where the parents *"spread the cloth before the elders"*, and the *"blood"* on the house in 22:8
looks back to the blood of chapter 21. Within the pericope two lines run side by side. One is
attention: do not hide yourself from a neighbor's loss, do not take the mother bird, build so that no
one falls. The other is order: keep male and female, seed and seed, ox and donkey, wool and linen, each
to its own kind. Both are framed as things Yahweh notices in the ordinary run of a day — on the road, in
a tree, on a roof, in a field, in what one puts on in the morning. Whether one principle underlies both
lines is a question the passage raises and does not answer.

## Notes

**"Your brother."** *ʾāḥîkā* (22:1–4, WLC), six times in four verses. Exodus 23:4 has *ʾōyibkā*,
*your enemy*, and 23:5 *śōnaʾăkā*, *the one who hates you* (WLC). The command form is the same:
*hāšēb tǝšîbēm*, *returning you shall return them* (22:1), as *hāšēb tǝšîbennû* (Exodus 23:4) (WLC).
Deuteronomy's *brother* is its word for any fellow Israelite (15:2–3, 7, 12; 17:15; 19:18–19), so
the change narrows the law's reach on one side — the enemy is no longer named — and widens it on the
other, to the stranger *"you don't know"* (2). Whether Deuteronomy means to exclude the enemy or to
assume him among the *brothers* is not said.

**"Hide yourself."** *Wǝhitʿallamtā* (22:1, 4) and *lǝhitʿallēm* (22:3), the hithpael of *ʿālam*, *to
hide*. In the Torah the verb occurs in that stem only in these three verses. Its other Torah uses are
the niphal of something *"hidden"* from a person's knowledge (Leviticus 4:13; 5:2–4; Numbers 5:13) and
the hiphil of Leviticus 20:4, where the people *"hide their eyes"* from the man who gives his child to
Molech (WLC lemma 5956). The word names a deliberate not-seeing.

**"Every lost thing."** *Kol-ʾăbēdat ʾāḥîkā* (22:3, WLC). *ʾĂbēdâ*, *lost thing*, occurs in the Torah
only here, at Exodus 22:8 (WEB 22:9), in the dispute over *"any kind of lost thing"*, and at Leviticus
5:22–23 (WEB 6:3–4), of the man who *"has found that which was lost, and dealt falsely therein"* (WLC
lemma 9; stored WEB). Exodus and Leviticus deal with the lost thing once it is in dispute or denied;
Deuteronomy deals with it at the moment it is found.

**"Fallen down by the way."** *Nōpǝlîm baddārek* (22:4, WLC). Exodus 23:5's donkey is *rōbēṣ taḥat
maśśāʾô*, *lying down under its load* (WLC), rendered *"fallen down"* by the WEB. The help is different
too: Exodus *ʿāzōb taʿăzōb ʿimmô*, a much-discussed verb usually rendered *help* or *release*,
Deuteronomy *hāqēm tāqîm ʿimmô*, *raising you shall raise with him* (WLC). In both, *ʿimmô*, *with
him*: the owner is there and the finder works beside him.

**"Men's clothing."** *Kǝlî-geber* (22:5, WLC), *a man's article*. *Kǝlî* is the common word for any
vessel, tool, weapon, or implement; *geber* is a man, often with a note of strength. The woman's side
uses *śimlat ʾiššâ*, *a woman's cloak*. The asymmetry is in the Hebrew; WEB's *"men's clothing"*
smooths it.

**"An abomination to Yahweh your God."** *Tôʿăbat YHWH ʾĕlōhêkā* (22:5, WLC). *Tôʿēbâ* occurs in sixteen
verses of Deuteronomy (7:25, 26; 12:31; 13:15; 14:3; 17:1, 4; 18:9, 12; 20:18; 22:5; 23:19; 24:4; 25:16;
27:15; 32:16, by WLC verse numbers) and in Genesis–Numbers otherwise only of what Egyptians abhor
(Genesis 43:32; 46:34; Exodus 8:22) and of the sexual sins of Leviticus 18 and 20 (WLC lemma 8441). The
word is broad: it covers worship, food, sacrifice, sexual conduct, and business.

**"A bird's nest."** *Qan-ṣippôr* (22:6, WLC). *Qēn* occurs in the Torah only here, at Genesis 6:14
(the ark's *qinnîm*, *rooms*), Numbers 24:21 (Balaam's pun on the Kenite's *"nest"* in the rock), and
Deuteronomy 32:11, *"As an eagle that stirs up her nest, that flutters over her young"* — Yahweh's care
for Israel told in the figure of a bird over its nest (WLC lemma 7064; stored WEB). *ʾEprōaḥ*, *young
bird*, and *bêṣâ*, *egg*, occur in the Torah only in this verse (WLC lemmas 667, 1000).

**"Sitting on the young."** *Rōbeṣet* (22:6, WLC), the verb of the donkey *"lying"* under its load in
Exodus 23:5 (WLC lemma 7257). The mother on the nest and the beast under its burden have the same
posture in the Hebrew.

**"You shall surely let the hen go."** *Šallēaḥ tǝšallaḥ* (22:7, WLC), the piel of *šālaḥ*, *to send
away, to release*. It is the verb of 21:14, *wǝšillaḥtāh*, *"you shall let her go"*, of the captive wife
(WLC). The two laws are a chapter apart and the wiki notes the shared verb without arguing a link.

**"That it may be well with you, and that you may prolong your days."** *Lǝmaʿan yîṭab lāk
wǝhaʾăraktā yāmîm* (22:7, WLC). Deuteronomy 5:16 has *lǝmaʿan yaʾărîkun yāmêkā ûlǝmaʿan yîṭab lāk*,
*that your days may be long, and that it may go well with you* (WLC; stored WEB) — the same two verbs,
*ʾārak* and *yāṭab*, in the reverse order. Long days are promised elsewhere in Deuteronomy for keeping
the commandments in general (4:40; 5:33; 6:2; 11:9) and for one other single law, honest weights
(25:15). A lemma search of Deuteronomy for verses containing both *ʾārak* and *yāṭab* finds only 4:40,
5:16, and here (WLC lemmas 748, 3190), so of the single laws only the fifth commandment and the nest
carry both. Verses that express *well with you* with another word (5:33 among them) are not counted by
that search.

**"A railing."** *Maʿăqeh* (22:8, WLC), occurring nowhere else in the Torah (WLC lemma 4624). *"Blood"*
is the plural *dāmîm*, which in Deuteronomy occurs only here and at 19:10 (WLC lemma 1818, plural
forms); the plural is the usual form for *bloodguilt*.

**"Two kinds."** *Kilʾayim* (22:9, WLC), a dual noun. In the Torah it occurs only at Leviticus 19:19
(three times) and here (WLC lemma 3610). Deuteronomy uses it once, of the vineyard; for the ox and
donkey it says only *yaḥdāw*, *together* (22:10), and for the cloth it uses the other term of Leviticus
19:19, *šaʿaṭnēz*, adding *ṣemer ûpištîm yaḥdāw*, *wool and linen together* (22:11). *Šaʿaṭnēz* occurs
only in those two verses (WLC lemma 8162); its etymology is uncertain, and it is often taken as a loan
word, reported here from general knowledge.

**"Lest all the fruit be defiled."** *Pen-tiqdaš hammǝlēʾâ* (22:9, WLC), *lest the full yield become
holy*. The verb is *qādaš* in the qal, *to be holy*, and *mǝlēʾâ*, *fullness*, occurs in the Torah only here, at Exodus
22:28 (WEB 22:29, *"your harvest"*, which may not be withheld from Yahweh), and at Numbers 18:27 (WLC
lemma 4395). WEB's *"defiled"* translates the effect rather than the
word: the crop passes out of the owner's use. Whether it goes to the sanctuary or is simply forbidden
the text does not say.

**"Plow with an ox and a donkey together."** *Lōʾ-taḥărōš bǝšôr-ûbaḥămōr yaḥdāw* (22:10, WLC). *Ḥāraš*,
*to plow*, occurs in the Torah only here in that sense; its other Torah uses are a homograph meaning *to
be silent* (Genesis 24:21; 34:5; Exodus 14:14; Numbers 30) (WLC lemma 2790). The ox may be offered;
the donkey's firstborn must be redeemed with a lamb (Exodus 13:13, stored WEB).

**"Wool and linen."** *Ṣemer ûpištîm* (22:11, WLC). Outside this verse the pair occurs in the Torah only
in the law of mold in garments, Leviticus 13:47–59, where the two are named as alternatives (WLC lemmas
6785, 6593).

**"Fringes."** *Gǝdilîm* (22:12, WLC), from a root meaning *to twist*; it occurs nowhere else in the
Torah (WLC lemma 1434). Numbers 15:38–39 uses *ṣîṣit* (WLC lemma 6734). Both laws put the fringes on the
*kǝnāpôt* / *kanpê*, *corners* — the word for a bird's *wing* (Deuteronomy 32:11) and a garment's
*skirt* (22:30, WLC 23:1, *"his father's skirt"*) (WLC). Whether the two words name the same object is
assumed by Jewish practice and by most readers; the texts themselves do not equate them.

**"Your cloak with which you cover yourself."** *Kǝsûtǝkā ʾăšer tǝkasseh-bāh* (22:12, WLC). *Kǝsût*,
*covering*, occurs in the Torah only at Genesis 20:16 (*"a covering of the eyes"*), Exodus 21:10 (the
wife's clothing that may not be diminished), Exodus 22:26 (WEB 22:27, *"his only covering"*, the cloak
taken in pledge), and here (WLC lemma 3682; stored WEB). It is the garment one sleeps in.

**The New Testament fringes.** The woman with the issue of blood *"touched the fringe of his garment"*
(Matthew 9:20), the sick of Gennesaret begged *"that they might just touch the fringe of his garment"*
(14:36), and the scribes and Pharisees *"enlarge the fringes of their garments"* (23:5) (all WEB,
bible-api.com). That the Greek word in these verses is the Septuagint's word for the fringes of Numbers
15 and Deuteronomy 22 is commonly said; neither the Greek New Testament nor the Septuagint was checked
here, so the identification is reported, not verified.

**What the passage does not settle.** What 22:5 forbids: ordinary dress, cultic disguise, or war gear.
Why the mother bird carries the promise of the fifth commandment. Whether the laws of two kinds have a
reason beyond themselves — kindness, cleanness, or Israel's separateness — and why Deuteronomy's list
differs from Leviticus'. Whether the forfeited vineyard goes to the sanctuary. Whether *gǝdilîm* and
*ṣîṣit* are the same thing. Whether Deuteronomy's *brother* excludes the enemy of Exodus 23:4–5. Each
is recorded, none adopted.
