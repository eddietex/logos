# Naomi Returns to Bethlehem

**Book:** [[Ruth]]
**Reference:** Ruth 1:19–22

## Summary

> **19** So they both went until they came to Bethlehem. When they had come to Bethlehem, all the city was
> excited about them, and they asked, "Is this Naomi?"
> **20** She said to them, "Don't call me Naomi. Call me Mara; for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with
> me.
> **21** I went out full, and Yahweh has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since Yahweh
> has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?"
> **22** So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned out of the
> country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest.

[[Naomi]] and [[Ruth (person)]] walk on together to [[Bethlehem]]. The whole town is stirred at their
arrival, and the women ask, *Is this Naomi?* (19). Naomi answers by refusing her own name. *Naomi* sounds
like *pleasant*; she asks to be called *Mara*, *bitter*, because *the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with
me* (20). She went out full and Yahweh has brought her back empty; Yahweh has *testified against* her and
the Almighty has *afflicted* her (21). The narrator closes the chapter with a summary: Naomi returned, and
Ruth the Moabitess with her, *who returned out of the country of Moab*, and they arrived *in the beginning
of barley harvest* (22).

**What is going on.** Four verses end the first act. The road scene of 1:6–18 was about who would go; this
one is about how Naomi arrives. The town's question is short and open: it could be surprise, pity, or
simply recognition after ten years. Naomi's answer is the longest speech she makes in the chapter about
herself, and it is an accusation in the form of a renaming. She uses the two divine names, *Shaddai* and
*Yahweh*, twice each, and makes God the subject of all four verbs: dealt bitterly, brought back, testified
against, afflicted. The narrator does not answer her, and does not correct her. The last verse quietly
supplies what her speech left out. She says she came back *empty*, and the narrator says she came back with
Ruth. She speaks of loss, and the narrator dates the arrival to the start of the harvest.

**What is notable.**

- **All the city was stirred.** *Wattēhōm kol-hāʿîr* (19, WLC), from *hûm* (lemma 1949), *to be in
  uproar* or *murmur*. In the WLC files the wiki has read (Genesis through Ruth, 1 Samuel, Job), the niphal
  occurs only here and at 1 Samuel 4:5, where the ark came into the camp *so that the earth rang again*. The
  WEB's *excited* is mild; the word is loud. The question that follows is spoken by women: *wattōʾmarnâ*,
  *they said*, is feminine plural (WLC). The women of Bethlehem will speak again at the end of the book,
  over Naomi's grandson (4:14–17; not yet ingested).
- **Is this Naomi?** *Hăzōʾṯ noʿŏmî* (19). The question is about recognition, and Naomi takes it as a
  question about her name.
- **Call me Mara.** *Qərɛʾnā lî mārāʾ* (20, WLC). The name is spelled with a final *ʾālep*, *mārāʾ*,
  rather than the usual *-â* ending, and the WLC files list it under its own number (lemma 4755); it occurs
  only here. The meaning, *bitter*, is not in doubt. Israel's first camp after the sea was named *Marah* for
  its bitter water (Exodus 15:23), a different spelling and number (lemma 4785). See [[Marah]] and
  [[Call Me Mara - Marah, Naomi, and Job 27.2]].
- **The Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.** *Kî-hēmar šadday lî məʾōḏ* (20, WLC). The verb is the
  hiphil of *mārar* (lemma 4843), *to make bitter*. In the files read the WLC tags three hiphil forms of it:
  here, Job 27:2, and Exodus 23:21 (*Don't provoke him*, of the angel). With *Shaddai* as subject it occurs
  only here and at Job 27:2: *the Almighty, who has made my soul bitter* (*wəšadday hēmar napšî*). Naomi had already used the plain verb at 1:13, *mar-lî*, *it is bitter for me*. See
  [[Suffering]].
- **Shaddai.** Naomi calls God *Shaddai*, *the Almighty*, in 1:20 and 1:21, and these are the only two uses
  in Ruth. In the WLC files read, the name occurs six times in Genesis, once in Exodus (6:3), twice in
  Numbers (24:4, 16), and thirty-one times in Job. It is the name of the patriarchal promises, and in Job it
  is the name of the God a sufferer accuses. Naomi pairs it with *Yahweh* in parallel lines (21). See
  [[God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus]] and [[The Names of God]].
- **Full and empty.** *ʾĂnî mələʾâ hālaḵtî wərêqām hĕšîḇanî YHWH*, *I went out full, and Yahweh has brought
  me home again empty* (21, WLC). *Rêqām*, *empty-handed* (lemma 7387), is the word of Exodus 3:21 (*you
  shall not go empty-handed*) and Deuteronomy 16:16 (*They shall not appear before Yahweh empty*). In the
  files read it occurs ten times, and twice in Ruth: here, and at 3:17, where Boaz sends Ruth home with six
  measures of barley because *Don't go empty to your mother-in-law* (not yet ingested). Eliphaz accuses Job
  of having *sent widows away empty* (Job 22:9). Naomi's *full* is an odd word for a woman who left in a
  famine; she means her husband and sons, not her store. See
  [[You Shall Not Go Empty-Handed - The Plunder of Egypt]].
- **Yahweh brought me back.** *Hĕšîḇanî* (21) is the hiphil of *šûb*, the chapter's key word (see
  [[Ruth Clings to Naomi]]). Everyone else in chapter 1 *returns*; only here is Yahweh the one who *brings
  back*. The same causative form returns at 4:15, where the women call Naomi's grandson *a restorer of life*,
  *ləmēšîḇ nep̄eš*, literally *one who brings back the soul* (WLC; not yet ingested). Job says Shaddai has
  made his *nep̄eš* bitter (27:2); the child will bring Naomi's back.
- **Yahweh has testified against me.** *Wayhwh ʿānâ ḇî* (21, WLC). The verb is *ʿānâ*, *to answer*, and
  with *bə-* against a person it is the language of the witness in court: *You shall not give false
  testimony against your neighbor* (*lōʾ-ṯaʿăneh ḇərēʿăḵā*, Exodus 20:16, WLC). The WLC files tag both under
  the same lemma (6030 b). Some ancient and modern translations instead read *has afflicted* or *humbled*
  me, from a verb of the same consonants (reported, not verified here). The WEB's courtroom reading fits
  Naomi's speech: she presents herself as the defendant in a case God has brought.
- **The Almighty has afflicted me.** *Wəšadday hēraʿ lî* (21, WLC), the hiphil of *rāʿaʿ*, *to do harm* or
  *treat badly*. Moses uses the same form in two complaints to Yahweh: *why have you brought trouble on this
  people?* (*hărēʿōṯâ*, Exodus 5:22) and *Why have you treated your servant so badly?* (Numbers 11:11). Naomi
  stands in a line of speakers who bring a complaint to God, not against him behind his back. See
  [[Murmuring]] and [[Mourning and Lament]].
- **Ruth is not counted.** Naomi says she came back *empty* with Ruth beside her. The narrator lists Ruth in
  the very next verse. The book does not say whether Naomi's silence about Ruth is grief, despair, or
  embarrassment about a Moabite daughter-in-law, and the page does not guess.
- **Ruth the Moabitess, who returned.** *Wərûṯ hammôʾăḇiyyâ kallāṯāh ʿimmāh haššāḇâ miśśəḏê môʾāḇ* (22,
  WLC). This is the first time the book calls her *the Moabitess* (1:4 had *women of Moab*, plural); the
  tag recurs at 2:2, 6, 21; 4:5, 10.
  *Haššāḇâ*, *who returned*, is used of Ruth, who has never been to Bethlehem. The WLC tags it as a perfect
  with the article. Grammatically it could also describe Naomi; the same form recurs of Naomi at 4:3 (*Naomi,
  who has come back*), and the overseer uses a participle form of it of Ruth at 2:6 (*the Moabite lady who
  came back*). Ruth's *return* is to a place she has not been. See [[The Nations]].
- **The beginning of barley harvest.** *Biṯḥillaṯ qəṣîr śəʿōrîm* (22). Barley is the first grain to ripen;
  Exodus 9:31 has the barley *in the ear* while the wheat is not yet up. The phrase *biṯḥillaṯ qəṣîr* occurs
  only here in the files read. It looks forward: chapter 2 runs *to the end of barley harvest and of wheat
  harvest* (2:23). The book opened with a famine in *the house of bread* (1:1); chapter 1 ends with the
  harvest beginning there. See [[Famine and Plenty]] and [[Festivals and First Fruits]].

**How it connects.** The four verses are a hinge. They close the account of loss (1:1–21) and open the
account of the harvest (chapters 2–3). Naomi's speech is the book's answer to the question the narrator
never answered in 1:3–5 — why did it happen? — and it is only Naomi's answer. The narrator's own claims
about God in chapter 1 are two: that Yahweh *visited his people in giving them bread* (1:6), and nothing
else. The rest of the book will test Naomi's word *empty*. Boaz will refuse to send Ruth back *empty*
(3:17); the women will say Yahweh has *not left* Naomi without a redeemer, and the child will *bring back*
her life (4:14–15). The reversal is built from the words of this scene. Behind the scene stand Marah, the
first bitter water after the Exodus (Exodus 15:23), and Job, who uses the same name for God and the same
verb of his own soul (Job 27:2).

## Notes

**Judgment calls.** The boundary 1:19–22 is kept as planned: the arrival, Naomi's speech, and the narrator's
summary that ends the chapter. A new connection page links Marah, Naomi's *Mara*, and Job 27:2, because the
hiphil of *mārar* with *Shaddai* as subject occurs only in Ruth 1:20 and Job 27:2 in the files read, and
Naomi takes a name from the same root as the place (see also [[Merari]], whose page notes the root). No page was created for the women of
Bethlehem; they speak as a group here and at 4:14–17, and the town's page records them.

**On Naomi's theology.** Naomi says God has acted against her four times in two verses. The narrator neither
endorses nor rebukes her. The wiki keeps the two voices apart, as on [[Ruth Clings to Naomi]]: these are
Naomi's claims about God, made in grief and addressed to God by name. Later readers often compare her to
Job (reported, not verified here); the textual overlap is the name *Shaddai* and the verb *hēmar*.

**On *testified against me*.** The WLC files tag *ʿānâ* here with the same lemma as Exodus 20:16's *give
testimony against*. The alternative reading *afflicted / humbled* is reported from translation practice and
not checked against the Septuagint or a lexicon in this pass.
