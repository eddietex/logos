# The Covenant with Noah

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 9:1–17

## Summary

Eight people step off a boat onto a planet with nothing left alive on it that was not in the boat
with them, and God gives them a world to live in. Genesis 9:1–17 is the charter of that world: two
speeches, the first telling humanity how it may now live, the second telling it what God has
decided never to do again. Between them they are the only legislation in the primeval history and
the first covenant in Scripture that anyone is actually told about.

The pericope is built as a matched pair, and the pairing is the argument.

- **9:1–7** is addressed to people and is full of obligations. It reissues the creation blessing,
  alters the terms of human life among the animals, opens a diet, closes a diet, and lays down one
  law.
- **9:8–17** is addressed to people *and to every living creature*, and contains no obligation at
  all. God speaks about himself: what he establishes, what he will not do, what he will look at,
  what he will remember.

The first half tells humanity what to do. The second half tells humanity what God will do, and asks
nothing in return. That asymmetry is the shape of every covenant in Genesis and it starts here.

### The blessing reissued, and what is missing from it

> **1** God blessed Noah and his sons, and said to them, “Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the
> earth.

This is Genesis 1:28 spoken a second time, and Genesis expects the comparison. The Hebrew is
identical — *pərû ûrəbû ûmilʾû ʾet-hāʾāreṣ*, *be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth* — and it
is the first time humanity has been blessed since the sixth day. The blessing went missing for the
whole length of the flood: [[Noah and the Flood]] gives Noah instruction and a covenant promise but
never a blessing, and [[The Waters Recede]] restores it to the animals at 8:17 and holds humanity's
turn back until here. See [[Blessing]].

**What 9:1 does not contain is as loud as what it does.** Genesis 1:28 ran: *"Be fruitful, multiply,
fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky,
and over every living thing that moves on the earth."* Two verbs are gone. *Subdue* — **kābaš**,
to tread down — is not repeated. *Have dominion* — **rādâ**, to rule — is not repeated. The
mandate is reissued minus its two words of mastery, and the next verse supplies what stands in
their place.

### Dominion becomes terror

> **2** The fear of you and the dread of you will be on every animal of the earth, and on every bird
> of the sky. Everything that moves along the ground, and all the fish of the sea, are delivered
> into your hand.

*Fear* and *dread* are **môrāʾ** and **ḥat**, and the second is the stronger: the root behind
*ḥittâ*, terror, shattering. The four categories — beasts, birds, creeping things, fish — are the
four of 1:26 and 1:28 in the same order, which makes the substitution exact. Where the man once
ruled the animals he now frightens them, and Genesis states this as a fact about the new world
rather than as a grant. It is the same relationship the flood established in practice: humanity
survived and the animals survived only by being carried.

*Delivered into your hand* is **nātan bəyādkem**, and it is the standard Hebrew idiom for handing a
defeated enemy over. The verse is not a promise of harmony. It describes a peace kept by fear, which
is what peace between unequal parties usually is, and Genesis does not pretend otherwise.

### A diet opened

> **3** Every moving thing that lives will be food for you. As I gave you the green herb, I have
> given everything to you.

This is a change, and the verse itself points back to the arrangement it changes: *"as I gave you
the green herb"* is a quotation of Genesis 1:29–30, where God assigns *"every herb yielding seed…
and every tree, which bears fruit yielding seed"* to humanity and *"every green herb"* to the
animals, and assigns nothing else to anyone. Genesis 1 does not say the word *vegetarian* and does
not forbid meat; it simply never mentions it, in a chapter that is otherwise exhaustive about who
eats what. Here the omission is filled in and the permission is total: *kol-remeś ʾăšer hûʾ ḥay*,
every moving thing that lives.

Two things follow. The first is that the clean/unclean distinction, which [[Noah and the Flood]]
assumed without explanation at 7:2 and [[The Waters Recede]] spent at the altar in 8:20, is **not**
applied to food here. Nine verses after Noah sorted the animals into clean and unclean for
sacrifice, God gives him all of them to eat. The dietary use of that boundary waits for Leviticus 11
and Deuteronomy 14, and the New Testament's argument about it (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:9–16; Romans
14:14) is in part an argument about which of these two settlements is the older and more basic one.
See [[Clean and Unclean]].

The second is that the prophets read the Genesis 1 diet as the picture of a healed world and expect
it back: *"The wolf and the lamb will feed together, and the lion will eat straw like the ox"*
(Isaiah 65:25; cf. 11:6–9). On that reading 9:3 is a concession to a world that has just been
drowned for violence, not a description of how things are supposed to be.

### A diet closed

> **4** But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat.

The single restriction, and the first food law in the Bible. *Bāśār bənapšô dāmô* — *flesh with its
**nepeš**, its blood*. **Nepeš** is the word Genesis 2:7 uses when the man becomes *a living being*,
and the equation the verse makes — life *is* blood — is the premise the whole sacrificial system
will be built on. Leviticus 17:11 states it as doctrine and draws the conclusion: *"For the life of
the flesh is in the blood. I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your souls; for
it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life."* Blood is withheld from the human table
because it belongs on the altar, and it belongs on the altar because it is the life.

So the permission of 9:3 comes with a reservation attached, and the reservation is the point: you
may kill for food, and you may not treat the life you took as though it were merely food. Killing an
animal is now allowed and is not now casual. See [[Blood]] and [[Life and Death]].

Rabbinic tradition reads 9:4 more narrowly and more vividly as *ʾēver min haḥay*, **a limb from a
living animal** — the prohibition of eating flesh cut from a beast still alive — and makes it one of
the seven Noahide commandments binding on all humanity (*b. Sanhedrin* 56a–b). The Jerusalem council
of Acts 15:20, 29 asks Gentile believers to abstain from *"what is strangled, and from blood"*,
which is this verse and Leviticus 17 rather than anything in the law of Moses proper, and is the one
piece of the Mosaic food code that the earliest church asked of people who were not under it.

### One law, and it is about blood

> **5** I will surely require your life’s blood. At the hand of every animal I will require it. At
> the hand of man, even at the hand of every man’s brother, I will require the life of man.
> **6** Whoever sheds man’s blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image.

The verb in 9:5 is **dāraš**, to seek out, investigate, demand an accounting, and it is used three
times in one verse. God is not describing a principle; he is announcing that he will come looking.
The same root produces the psalmist's title for him: *dōrēš dāmîm*, *"he who avenges blood"* (Psalm
9:12).

Three defendants are named, in a widening then narrowing sweep. **Every animal** — which is not
rhetoric: Exodus 21:28 has the goring ox stoned, and its flesh not eaten. **Man**. And then, with no
new clause, *"even at the hand of every man's brother"* — **ʾîš ʾāḥîw**. Genesis has an interest in
that word. The Bible's first killing was a brother's (4:8), and the Bible's first recorded lie was
*"Am I my brother's keeper?"* (4:9). Here God answers Cain's question in the form of a statute: you
are, and I will ask you for him. Homicide is defined at its first legal appearance as fratricide,
which is what it always is once Genesis 5 has established that everyone descends from the same man.

**Genesis 9:6a is the most tightly built sentence in the primeval history.** In Hebrew it is six
words, and they mirror:

> *šōpēk* — *dam* — *hāʾādām* ‖ *bāʾādām* — *dāmô* — *yiššāpēk*
> *shedder — blood of — the man* ‖ *by the man — his blood — shall be shed*

The line turns on its own axis: shedding, blood, man ‖ man, blood, shedding. The punishment is the
crime read backwards, and the syntax performs the *lex talionis* before the law ever states it. It
is one of the few places in Genesis where the form of a sentence carries its content.

The preposition **bā-** in *bāʾādām* is where the interpretation is decided, and it can bear more
than one weight. Read instrumentally — *by man* — the verse licenses human beings to carry out the
sentence, and it becomes the Bible's first delegation of judicial authority to people, the seed of
the courts that the rabbinic tradition counts as the seventh Noahide law and of the sword that
Romans 13:4 says the magistrate does not bear in vain. Read as a *beth* of price — *in exchange for
the man* — it states the equivalence without naming an executioner. Read locally — *among men*, in
the presence of witnesses — it points at due process. The instrumental reading is the majority one
and fits the contrast the passage is drawing, since 9:5 has just said God will require it and 9:6
says how; but the ambiguity is real, and it matters, because a very large argument about capital
punishment has been conducted on top of this preposition.

**What the law rests on is the image of God**, and that is the verse's most consequential half:
*kî bəṣelem ʾĕlōhîm ʿāśâ ʾet-hāʾādām*, *for God made man in his own image*. Three things at once.
It settles a question [[The Fall]] left open — the image is still in force after the fall, after
Cain, and after a flood sent for total corruption; God grounds a permanent statute in it in the same
speech where he calls the human heart evil from its youth (8:21). It makes murder a crime against
God rather than against society, because what is destroyed in a killing is a likeness of him — which
is why the penalty is not compensable, and why Numbers 35:31 forbids taking a ransom for a murderer
when the law allows one for almost everything else. And it makes the protection universal: the image
belongs to *hāʾādām*, humanity as such, with no qualification by nation, righteousness, or standing.
James 3:9 still argues from it, against cursing people *"who are made in the likeness of God"*. See
[[Image of God]].

Set 9:6 beside the two other places Genesis has counted blood. [[Cain and Abel]] has God protecting
a murderer with a sevenfold threat (4:15); [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] has Lamech
boasting of seventy-sevenfold for a wound (4:24). Here the ratio is one to one, it is not a boast,
and it is a limit rather than a licence. The escalation that ran through Cain's line is stopped by
being made into arithmetic. See [[Vengeance]].

And there is a reason this is the one law given. The flood came because *"the earth was filled with
violence"* — **ḥāmās** — twice stated (6:11, 6:13). The new world's single statute addresses exactly
what destroyed the old one. God has just promised not to send another flood; the alternative he puts
in its place is human justice.

> **7** Be fruitful and multiply. Increase abundantly in the earth, and multiply in it.”

The speech closes where it opened, and the repetition is a frame rather than an afterthought: a
paragraph about killing, bracketed on both sides by a command to make more life. It is also the last
word before the covenant, and it puts the emphasis where 9:5–6 put it — on the value of a human
life, which is worth both protecting by law and producing in quantity.

### The covenant established

> **8** God spoke to Noah and to his sons with him, saying,
> **9** “As for me, behold, I establish my covenant with you, and with your offspring after you,

*As for me* — **waʾănî** — is emphatic and it changes the speaker's footing. The first speech told
Noah what he must do; this one is about what God is doing, and the pronoun announces it.

The verb is **hēqîm**, *establish*, the same one used at 6:18 — *"But I will establish my covenant
with you"* — spoken to Noah before the water came. That promise is now kept, which means the flood
narrative has been carrying an unredeemed pledge since chapter 6 and discharges it here. Genesis
never uses *kārat*, **to cut**, of this covenant, and there is no ceremony, no oath sworn by a human
party, no animals divided, and no meal. Nobody agrees to anything. Noah does not speak in this
pericope any more than he has spoken in the three before it. See [[Covenant]].

> **10** and with every living creature that is with you: the birds, the livestock, and every animal
> of the earth with you, of all that go out of the ship, even every animal of the earth.
> **11** I will establish my covenant with you: All flesh will not be cut off any more by the waters
> of the flood. There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth.”

**The animals are parties to it.** This is the single most unusual feature of the Noahic covenant and
the text will not let it pass unnoticed: the beneficiaries are named five separate times in ten
verses (9:10, 12, 15, 16, 17), each time as *every living creature* or *all flesh*, and 9:13 widens
the scope further still to *"between me and the earth"*. No later biblical covenant does this. Sinai
is with a nation, David's with a dynasty, the new covenant with the house of Israel and Judah; this
one is with the biosphere, and the human parties are simply the ones being spoken to.

The clumsy repetition in 9:10 — *"every animal of the earth with you, of all that go out of the
ship, even every animal of the earth"* — is in the Hebrew too, and it reads like a legal document
straining to leave no creature out.

Two honesties are worth recording. The first is that the covenant is with animals who, eight verses
earlier, were delivered into human hands as food and told to be afraid. Both things are said in one
speech, and Genesis does not reconcile them. The second is that the promise is **negative**: not
that the world will flourish, but that it will not be drowned. A guarantee of the floor, not of the
ceiling. That is what a promise looks like when its content is a limit God places on himself, and
[[Judgment]] is what it is a limit on.

### The sign

> **12** God said, “This is the token of the covenant which I make between me and you and every
> living creature that is with you, for perpetual generations:
> **13** I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between me and the
> earth.

The word translated *rainbow* is **qešet**, and Hebrew has no other word for a rainbow because
*qešet* is not a word for a rainbow. It is the word for a **bow** — the weapon. It occurs some
seventy-five times in the Hebrew Bible and everywhere else it means the thing an archer draws:
Ishmael's bow, Jonathan's bow, the bows of the Philistines, and God's own bow when the prophets and
the psalms put him in the field as a warrior — *"You uncovered your bow"* (Habakkuk 3:9), *"He has
bent his bow, and made it ready"* (Psalm 7:12), *"He has bent his bow like an enemy"* (Lamentations
2:4).

So the sentence is *I have set my bow in the cloud*, and what it describes is a weapon put down. The
arc of light hangs in the sky slack and pointed away from the earth, at the end of a war God fought
against the world with water. Whether the writer intended the martial sense cannot be proved from
the verse alone, but the vocabulary is unambiguous and Hebrew offered no neutral alternative; the
reading is old, it is widely held, and nothing in the text resists it. It also explains why the sign
is a bow rather than, say, a star.

> **14** When I bring a cloud over the earth, that the rainbow will be seen in the cloud,
> **15** I will remember my covenant, which is between me and you and every living creature of all
> flesh, and the waters will no more become a flood to destroy all flesh.
> **16** The rainbow will be in the cloud. I will look at it, that I may remember the everlasting
> covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth.”

**The sign is not for us.** Every other covenant sign in the Bible is something the human party does
or keeps — circumcision cut in the flesh (Genesis 17:11), the Sabbath observed (Exodus 31:13, 17) —
and this one is something God looks at. *"I will look at it, that I may remember"*: the subject of
both verbs is God, twice over, and the beneficiary of the reminder is God. Nobody is asked to do
anything with a rainbow. It is a knot tied in the divine handkerchief, and the only reason we know
about it is that we were told.

The verb is **zākar**, the hinge-word of the flood. [[The Waters Recede]] turns on *"God remembered
Noah"* (8:1) and [[Remembrance]] sets out what the word does: never recollection after a lapse,
always action on a commitment. The rainbow is a standing trigger for that action, which is a
strange and rather tender thing for a text to say about God — that he has arranged the weather so
that the very sight of a gathering storm will call the promise to his mind.

*Everlasting covenant* — **bərît ʿôlām** — is here for the first time in Scripture, and it will
attach itself to the covenants that follow: to Abraham's (17:7, 13, 19), to the Sabbath (Exodus
31:16), to David's (2 Samuel 23:5), and to the new covenant in the prophets (Jeremiah 32:40; Ezekiel
37:26; Isaiah 55:3), and finally in Hebrews 13:20 to the blood of Jesus. The first thing in the
Bible called an everlasting covenant is one nobody agreed to and nobody can break, because only one
party is under obligation.

> **17** God said to Noah, “This is the token of the covenant which I have established between me
> and all flesh that is on the earth.”

The formula of 9:12 repeated, with the scope at its widest — *all flesh that is on the earth* — and
the verb now in the perfect: *established*. It is done. Nine verses in Hebrew have used the word
*bərît* seven times, which in a narrative this fond of sevens is unlikely to be an accident.

### What has and has not changed

The world Noah walks into is not Eden. Humanity is blessed and fruitful, and it is feared by the
animals rather than ruling them. It may eat anything, and it may not eat blood. It bears God's image
and it needs a law against murder. The earth is guaranteed against destruction by a covenant it did
not ask for and cannot forfeit, and the guarantee is grounded in nothing about human character
except the verdict, delivered in the previous pericope, that the human heart is evil from its youth
(8:21).

The safety is total and the improvement is nil. That is the settlement, and Genesis tests it
immediately: the next thing that happens is that the man who received it plants a vineyard and gets
drunk, and the first words he ever speaks in Scripture are a curse (9:20–25).

## People

- [[God]] — he is *ʾĕlōhîm* throughout, in all seventeen verses, with no occurrence of *Yahweh*. The
  seam that ran through Genesis 8 — *ʾĕlōhîm* for the cosmic and administrative material, *Yahweh*
  from the altar onward — closes here on the *ʾĕlōhîm* side, which suits a speech addressed to all
  flesh rather than to a worshipper. What he does in this pericope is unusual in three ways. He
  legislates, for the first time since 2:16–17, and the law he gives is one law. He binds his own
  future conduct in public, having already resolved on it privately at 8:21. And he gives himself a
  reminder: the only covenant sign in the Bible whose stated function is to prompt God's own memory
  (9:16).
- [[Noah]] — spoken to, blessed, legislated to, and covenanted with, and silent in all of it, as he
  has been since 6:9. Four consecutive pericopes now contain no line of dialogue from him. He is not
  asked whether he accepts the covenant, because the covenant is not the kind of thing this book
  offers for acceptance. His first recorded words are three verses away and they are a curse.
- [[Shem]], [[Ham]], and [[Japheth]] — named collectively as *"his sons"* at 9:1 and 9:8, and
  included as parties to both the blessing and the covenant. They are still without an act or a word
  of their own; that changes at 9:18, where the narrative names them again and immediately begins
  telling them apart. What this pericope gives them is standing: the covenant is *"with you, and
  with your offspring after you"*, and Genesis 10 will make those offspring the whole human race.
- **Noah's wife, and the wives of Shem, Ham, and Japheth** — absent from both speeches. Every
  boarding and disembarking list in the flood narrative counted the eight; the blessing and the
  covenant are addressed to *"Noah and his sons"*. The command that follows is *be fruitful and
  multiply*, which is not a thing four men do by themselves, so the address is a convention of the
  text rather than a statement about who is included — 9:9's *"your offspring after you"* has to run
  through them. Still no page for any of the four; still nothing to put on one.

## Places

None. As with the flood narrative itself, the arena is *hāʾāreṣ* — the earth, the whole of it — and
the covenant's stated scope (*"between me and the earth"*, 9:13) is the reason no smaller location
would do. Geography returns in the next pericope, with [[Ararat]] behind them and Canaan named for
the first time at 9:18.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the pericope is the theme's decisive text. *Bərît* seven times in ten verses; the
  promise of 6:18 discharged; *hēqîm* rather than *kārat*, with no ceremony and no human obligation;
  the first *bərît ʿôlām* in Scripture; and a scope — every living creature, the earth itself —
  that no later covenant matches.
- [[Blessing]] — 1:28 reissued at 9:1 and repeated at 9:7, after an absence that lasted the entire
  flood. Reissued shorn of *subdue* and *have dominion*.
- [[Image of God]] — 9:6 is the doctrine's load-bearing use: the image survives the fall, the first
  murder, and the flood, and is strong enough after all three to ground a capital statute. Its
  transmission was asserted at 5:3; here its consequence is legislated.
- [[Blood]] — the theme's first legislation, and both halves of it: blood withheld from the table
  (9:4) because it is the life, and blood required by God for blood shed (9:5–6). Everything
  Leviticus does with blood is downstream of these three verses.
- [[Life and Death]] — *nepeš* equated with blood at 9:4, human life priced at exactly one human
  life at 9:6, and the command to multiply framing both.
- [[Vengeance]] — the escalation of Genesis 4 brought to a stop by being made proportionate. One for
  one, stated as law, with God as the one who *requires* it and human beings as the ones through
  whom it is exacted.
- [[Judgment]] — formally renounced in one mode. *"There will never again be a flood to destroy the
  earth"* is the first promise in Scripture in which God rules out a form of judgment permanently,
  and the human court of 9:6 is what he puts in its place.
- [[Remembrance]] — *zākar* twice (9:15, 9:16), with God as subject, attached to a physical sign for
  the first time. What 8:1 did once, the rainbow is arranged to do perpetually.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — conspicuously not applied. The distinction operative at 7:2 and 8:20 is
  not carried over into the diet of 9:3, which permits every moving thing without qualification.
- [[Creation]] — the re-creation completed. The blessing of 1:28, the four animal categories of
  1:26, and the food grant of 1:29 are all restated with alterations, which is the clearest signal
  Genesis gives that chapter 9 is a second chapter 1 rather than a continuation.
- [[Sin]] — unmentioned and everywhere. A world washed clean is given a homicide statute in its
  first paragraph, on the explicit grounds of 8:21.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — anticipated rather than present. 9:4's reservation of blood is the
  premise Leviticus 17:11 turns into the logic of atonement.

- Isaiah 24:5 — *"they have broken the everlasting covenant"*: the one verse that appears to charge
  humanity with breaking a covenant of this kind.
- Isaiah 54:9–10 — *"this is like the waters of Noah to me"*: the Noahic oath named and used as the
  guarantee of a greater one.
- Jeremiah 31:35–36; 33:20–26 — *"my covenant of the day and my covenant of the night"*: the fixed
  order of creation as the measure of an unbreakable promise, the same argument 8:22 and 9:11 set
  up.
- Ezekiel 1:28 — *"As the appearance of the rainbow that is in the cloud in the day of rain"*: the
  glory of God described by the sign of this covenant.
- Hosea 2:18 — *"In that day I will make a covenant for them with the animals of the field"*: the
  only other covenant in Scripture with animals as parties.
- Psalm 9:12 — *dōrēš dāmîm*, *"he who avenges blood"*: 9:5's verb as a divine title.
- Matthew 26:52 — *"all those who take the sword will die by the sword"*, which is 9:6's structure
  in Jesus' mouth, spoken to stop a killing rather than to require one.
- Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25 — the apostolic decree's *"what is strangled, and… blood"*.
- Romans 13:1–4 — the magistrate who *"doesn't bear the sword in vain"*, the New Testament's nearest
  thing to a comment on 9:6b.
- James 3:9 — cursing people *"who are made in the likeness of God"*: 9:6's ground applied to speech.
- 2 Peter 3:5–7 — the world destroyed by water, and the next judgment moved to fire, which is what
  9:11 makes necessary.
- Revelation 4:3 — *"a rainbow around the throne"*; and 10:1, an angel *"with a rainbow on his
  head"*.

## Notes

- **The bow, and the Babylonian bow.** Two Mesopotamian texts put a token in the sky after a
  cosmic conflict. In *Enūma Eliš* VI, after Marduk kills Tiamat, Anu takes up his bow, blesses it,
  and sets it in heaven as the Bow-star, where it is fixed among the gods as a memorial of the
  victory. And in *Gilgamesh* XI — the tablet that also supplies the bird-sending and the smelled
  sacrifice noted in [[The Waters Recede]] — the mother goddess lifts up the lapis-lazuli necklace
  Anu made for her and swears by it: *"I shall not forget these days… let the gods come to the
  offering."* A divine ornament raised in the sky as a permanent reminder of a flood is not a motif
  Genesis invented. What Genesis does with it is change who needs reminding and of what: not a
  trophy of a victory but a weapon laid down, and a memorial not of the deed but of the promise not
  to repeat it.

- **Why the covenant signs come in a set of three.** The rainbow (9:12–17), circumcision (17:11),
  and the Sabbath (Exodus 31:13, 16–17) are the only three things the Hebrew Bible calls an *ʾôt*
  of a covenant, they are attached to Noah, Abraham, and Moses respectively, and each is called or
  associated with *ʿôlām*, everlasting. Source-critical scholarship has long taken this as one of
  the clearest structural fingerprints of the Priestly material, which organizes the Pentateuch as
  a series of covenants each with its sign. One does not have to accept the source analysis to
  notice the pattern, and the pattern has a shape worth noticing on its own: the signs move
  inward, from the sky, to the body, to time — and only the first of them requires nothing of the
  people it is given to.

- **Was the pre-flood world vegetarian?** Genesis 1:29–30 assigns plants to humans and to animals
  and mentions no other food; 9:3 then grants *"every moving thing that lives"* as though it were
  new. The straightforward inference is that meat-eating is permitted here for the first time, and
  it is the reading behind Isaiah 11 and 65's picture of restoration as a return to the older diet.
  Two cautions. Genesis 1 states a grant, not a prohibition, and an argument from silence in a text
  this compressed is not strong on its own. And 4:2–4 has Abel keeping sheep and offering *"the
  firstborn of his flock and of its fat"*, which shows animal husbandry and the use of animal fat
  well before the flood, though not necessarily the eating of meat. What can be said without strain
  is that 9:3 is presented by the text as a change, and that the text itself flags it as one by
  quoting 1:29 in the same breath.

- **9:6 and capital punishment.** The verse is the principal biblical text on both sides of a very
  old argument, and it is worth being clear about what is and is not settled in it. Settled: a
  murderer's life is forfeit, the reason is the image of God rather than social utility, and the
  ratio is one to one rather than the escalating vengeance of Genesis 4. Not settled by this verse
  alone: whether *bāʾādām* names human beings as the executioners (*by man*) or states an
  equivalence (*in exchange for the man*); and whether the sentence is a command, a permission, or a
  grim observation about how homicide propagates — the Hebrew is a participle plus an imperfect,
  which will carry all three. The later law removes some of the doubt by legislating in detail
  (Exodus 21; Numbers 35), and it also builds in the safeguards that 9:6 says nothing about:
  cities of refuge for the unintentional killer, the requirement of two witnesses, and the
  prohibition of ransom. Anyone reading 9:6 as a standalone rule is reading it without the system
  the Torah attaches to it.

- **The animals are parties, and the animals are food.** The two most striking features of the
  pericope point opposite ways, and they are eight verses apart. 9:2–3 delivers every creature into
  human hands and makes them edible; 9:9–17 makes them covenant partners whose survival God
  guarantees. Genesis does not harmonize this and neither should a summary of it. What can be said
  is that the covenant is what constrains the grant: the same speech that lets humanity kill animals
  puts their continued existence under a promise humanity has no power over, and reserves their
  blood. The nearest later parallel is Hosea 2:18, and the nearest theological development is Paul's
  in Romans 8:19–22, where creation's fate is bound to humanity's without being at humanity's
  disposal.

- **"Never again by water" is a narrow promise, and the New Testament reads it narrowly.** 9:11
  rules out one instrument — *"by the waters of the flood"* — and 8:21 rules out one scope, *"every
  living thing"*. Neither rules out judgment. 2 Peter 3:5–7 takes exactly this line and draws the
  obvious conclusion, keeping the promise intact by changing the element: the world that then was
  *"perished, being flooded with water"*, and *"the heavens that exist now… have been stored up for
  fire."* Whatever one makes of the eschatology, the exegesis is careful — it is a reading of 9:11
  that respects the wording rather than expanding it.
