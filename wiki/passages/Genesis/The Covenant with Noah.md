# The Covenant with Noah

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 9:1–17

## Summary

The world has been made a second time and now it has to be given terms. Genesis 9:1–17 is God's
first speech to humanity since the sentence in Eden, and it does two things in two movements: it
reissues the blessing of the sixth day with the wording changed (9:1–7), and it establishes the
covenant promised back at 6:18, with a sign (9:8–17). Between them they are the charter of the world
everybody actually lives in — a world that is permanently safe from one particular catastrophe,
permanently violent in a way the first world was not supposed to be, and held in place by nothing
except a promise God has made to himself.

**Noah still does not speak.** Seventeen verses, three divine speeches (9:1, 9:8, 9:12), and not one
human word in reply. He has now been silent for four chapters. The next thing out of his mouth is
9:25, and it is a curse.

### 9:1–7 — the blessing reissued, with the terms altered

The unit is bracketed by the same sentence: *"Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the earth"* (9:1)
and *"Be fruitful and multiply. Increase abundantly in the earth, and multiply in it"* (9:7).
Everything between the brackets is the fine print, and every clause of it is a change to Genesis 1.

|  | Genesis 1:26–30 | Genesis 9:1–7 |
|---|---|---|
| Blessing | *"God blessed them"* (1:28) | *"God blessed Noah and his sons"* (9:1) |
| Mandate | be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth | be fruitful, multiply, replenish the earth |
| Rule over creatures | *"subdue it. Have dominion over…"* (1:28) | **absent** |
| Relation to creatures | dominion | *"The fear of you and the dread of you"* (9:2) |
| Food | *"every herb yielding seed… every tree"* (1:29) | *"Every moving thing that lives will be food for you"* (9:3) |
| The one limit | the tree in the middle of the garden (2:17) | *"flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat"* (9:4) |
| The image of God | the ground of dominion (1:26–27) | the ground of a death sentence (9:6) |

Read down the right-hand column and the shape of the change is unmistakable. The mandate survives
intact; everything attached to it is worse.

**Dominion is gone, and terror has replaced it.** Genesis 1:28 uses two strong verbs — *kābaš*, to
subdue, and *rādâ*, to rule — and neither appears here. What appears instead is *môrāʾ* and *ḥat*,
**fear** and **dread**, two nouns that elsewhere describe what an army feels approaching a stronger
one. And the animals are *"delivered into your hand"*, which is the standing formula of conquest
(*nātan bəyad*: Deuteronomy 20:13, Joshua 6:2, Judges 3:10). The relationship between humanity and
the other creatures has been re-founded on fear rather than on office. This is not the same grant
weakened; it is a different kind of grant. Whatever the man who named the animals in Genesis 2:19–20
had, this is not it. See [[Image of God]] and [[Creation]].

**Meat is permitted, and the text says so as an explicit amendment.** *"Every moving thing that lives
will be food for you. As I gave you the green herb, I have given everything to you"* (9:3). The
second sentence quotes 1:29–30 in order to extend it — the old grant is named, and then the category
is widened from vegetation to everything alive. Genesis's first world is one in which nothing eats
anything that breathes: 1:29 gives plants to humans and 1:30 gives plants to the animals too. That
world ends in this verse. Isaiah will imagine it back (11:6–9; 65:25, where the wolf and the lamb
feed together and the lion eats straw), which is a good indication that the arrangement of 1:29–30
was understood as the intended one rather than as a passing detail.

An honest complication, since the text supplies it: animals were certainly *killed* before the
flood. [[Abel]] keeps sheep and brings God *"the fat of it"* (4:4), and Noah loads seven pairs of
every clean animal (7:2) so that he can burn some of them on an altar (8:20). Genesis nowhere says
anyone ate them. The distinction the text actually maintains is between sacrifice, which is old, and
diet, which changes here.

**Then the first prohibition since the garden.**

> **4** But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat.

The Hebrew is *bāśār bənapšô dāmô* — flesh *with its nepeš, its blood*. **Nepeš** is the word
Genesis 2:7 uses when the man becomes *"a living soul"*; here it is used of an animal and then
apposed directly to its blood. The equation is not metaphor and Leviticus 17:11 will state it
flatly: *"the life of the flesh is in the blood."* So the permission and the restriction are one
thought. You may take the flesh; the life is not yours to take, because it was never yours to give.
Every animal killed for food is a life returned to the God who owns it, and draining the blood is
how that is acknowledged. This is the first dietary law in Scripture, it is given to all humanity
rather than to Israel, and it is the single Old Testament food rule the New Testament reimposes on
Gentiles (Acts 15:20, 29). See [[Blood]] and [[Life Is in the Blood]].

**And then a reckoning demanded, three times over.**

> **5** I will surely require your life’s blood. At the hand of every animal I will require it. At
> the hand of man, even at the hand of every man’s brother, I will require the life of man.

The verb is *dāraš*, to seek out or demand an accounting, and it is repeated three times in one
verse — from the animal, from the man, from *"every man's brother"*. That last phrase is not
decoration. The Bible's only murder so far was committed by a brother on a brother, and 4:9's *"Am I
my brother's keeper?"* is answered here, four chapters late, by a God who says he will require the
life of a man from his brother's hand. See [[Cain and Abel]].

**9:6 is a poem, and its shape is the argument.**

> **6** Whoever sheds man’s blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image.

In Hebrew the first half is six words in perfect mirror:

```
šōpēk    dam     hā-ʾādām
bā-ʾādām dāmô    yiššāpēk
```

*shedder — blood of — the man // by the man — his blood — shall be shed.* The verb *šāpak* opens the
line and closes it, once active and once passive; the word for blood stands second from each end;
and *ʾādām* sits at the centre twice, facing itself. The sentence enacts the equivalence it
legislates. It is also the first poetry in Genesis since [[Lamech (son of Methushael)]]'s
sword-song at 4:23–24, and that is not a coincidence either.

**Which is the key to the hardest problem in the passage.** Genesis 4:15 has God *protect* the first
murderer: *"whoever slays Cain, vengeance will be taken on him sevenfold,"* with a mark to keep
anyone from striking him. Genesis 9:6 requires the murderer's death. The two verses give opposite
instructions about the same act, and a wiki that smoothed that over would be lying. Two readings
are worth holding side by side, and they are not exclusive:

- **The subject changes.** 4:15 is about *private* blood-vengeance — the family avenger, the man who
  "finds" Cain — and God suppresses it. 9:6 institutes a *requital* that is universal, stated as
  law, and grounded in a reason rather than in the avenger's grief. On this reading 9:6 is not the
  reversal of 4:15 but the alternative to it.
- **The multiplier is abolished.** Line up the three: Cain avenged **sevenfold** (4:15), Lamech
  boasting of **seventy-sevenfold** (4:24), and then 9:6 — one life for one life, and no more.
  Between Cain and Lamech, vengeance inflates; that is what the sword-song is bragging about. The
  world that fills up with *ḥāmās*, **violence**, until God ends it (6:11, 13) is the world that
  ran on escalating private revenge. Genesis 9:6 caps it. Read this way the verse is a *restraint*
  on bloodshed rather than a licence for it, which is also how the *lex talionis* of Exodus 21:23–25
  functions in its own setting.

See [[Vengeance]], [[Retribution]], and [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]].

**The reason clause is the most important six words in the chapter.** *"For God made man in his own
image."* The prohibition of murder is not grounded in social order, deterrence, or the victim's
worth to anyone. It is grounded in whose likeness the victim bears — which means the image of
Genesis 1:27 has survived Genesis 3 and Genesis 6 and the flood, intact enough to still be the
reason a human life may not be taken. James 3:9 is still arguing from it in the New Testament.
Genesis 5:3 had already shown the image passing from father to son; 9:6 shows it surviving the
judgment of the whole race. See [[Image of God]] and [[From Adam to Noah]].

### 9:8–17 — the covenant established, and its sign

The second movement drops the imperatives entirely. Nothing is required of anybody; from 9:8 to
9:17 God speaks only about what he will do.

> **9** “As for me, behold, I establish my covenant with you, and with your offspring after you,
> **10** and with every living creature that is with you…

**Six things about this covenant, in the order the text gives them.**

**It keeps a promise.** *"I establish my covenant"* is *hēqîm bərît*, the exact phrase of 6:18,
where the covenant was announced in the middle of a sentence of universal death and then left
hanging for three chapters. It is not *cut* — *kārat bərît*, the ordinary idiom, with animals halved
and the parties walking between them, which is what Genesis 15 will stage. There is no ceremony
here, no blood, and no oath sworn by a human party. See [[Covenant]].

**The animals are party to it.** *"Every living creature that is with you: the birds, the livestock,
and every animal of the earth"* (9:10), repeated at 9:12, 9:15, and 9:16, and finally widened at
9:17 to *"all flesh that is on the earth."* No other covenant in Scripture has non-human parties.
The flood's victim list was *"every living thing"* (7:21–23), and the covenant's beneficiary list is
the same list. This also cashes out 8:1, where what God remembered was *"Noah, all the animals, and
all the livestock that were with him."*

**Nothing is asked in return.** Noah is not told to keep it, obey it, or agree to it, and he is not
recorded as saying anything at all. Compare Genesis 17:1 (*"Walk before me, and be blameless"*) or
Exodus 24:7 (*"All that Yahweh has spoken we will do"*). This is the least conditional arrangement
in the Bible, and the only one whose continuation depends on nothing whatever that a creature does.

**The promise is negative.** *"All flesh will not be cut off any more by the waters of the flood.
There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth"* (9:11). God is not undertaking to do
something; he is undertaking not to. What the covenant delivers is a permanent limit on divine
action, which is a strange thing for a covenant to be and is exactly what the passage says it is.
Note also what is *not* promised: no second flood, which is not the same as no judgment. 2 Peter
3:5–7 reads the limit precisely — the world that perished by water, and the present one *"reserved
for fire."* See [[Judgment]].

**The word itself is repeated seven times.** *Bərît* occurs in 9:9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, and 17 —
seven times in ten verses, in a book that counts sevens on purpose. At 9:16 it becomes *bərît
ʿôlām*, **everlasting covenant**, the phrase's first appearance in Scripture; it will attach next to
[[Covenant]]'s other great unconditional grant, Genesis 17:7.

**And it has a sign, which is a weapon.**

> **13** I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between me and the
> earth.

The Hebrew is **qešet**, and it is worth being precise about what that word does. Biblical Hebrew
has no separate word for a rainbow. *Qešet* appears some seventy times in the Old Testament and
everywhere else it means the **war bow** — Esau's bow, Jonathan's bow, the bow that Israel's enemies
bend. When God is a warrior, this is the weapon he carries: *"You uncovered your bow"* (Habakkuk
3:9), *"He has bent his bow like an enemy"* (Lamentations 2:4), *"He has bent his bow, and made it
ready"* (Psalm 7:12). So a Hebrew hearing 9:13 hears God announcing what he has done with his bow:
he has set it in the cloud. The war is over, and the weapon is hung up where everyone can see it.
See [[The Bow in the Cloud]].

**The sign is for God to look at, not for humanity.**

> **16** The rainbow will be in the cloud. I will look at it, that I may remember the everlasting
> covenant…

This is the detail that makes the Noahic covenant sign unlike every other one. Circumcision is cut
into the human party (17:11); the Sabbath is kept by the human party (Exodus 31:13, 17). Here the
stated function of the sign runs the other way: *I will look, I will remember.* The verb is *zākar*,
the verb of 8:1 that turned the whole flood — and, as [[Remembrance]] sets out, it never means
recall after a lapse; it means acting on a commitment. God has not built a memory aid. He has bound
himself in public, and put the binding somewhere he cannot fail to see it.

Which is why the last verse of the pericope is a repetition rather than a conclusion: *"This is the
token of the covenant which I have established between me and all flesh that is on the earth"*
(9:17). The chapter ends the way it has proceeded — God talking, Noah listening, and a world whose
continuance rests entirely on the reliability of the one who is speaking.

**One last thing about where this sits.** [[The Waters Recede]] ended with Noah building an altar
and worshipping in ignorance: 8:21–22 was said *in God's heart*, to nobody, and he did not hear it.
Here he hears it. The same promise is made twice — once as a private resolve and once as a public
covenant with a sign — and the gap between the two is the whole distance between a decision and a
covenant. The order matters and the narrative arranged it deliberately: the worship comes first,
without the assurance; the assurance comes after, unasked for.

## People

- [[Noah]] — addressed, blessed, given terms, and made the first party to a ratified covenant, and
  he answers none of it. This is the fourth consecutive chapter in which he is spoken to and does
  not speak; his silence ends nine verses later at 9:25, with a curse. Note also what he is *not*
  asked to do: nothing in 9:8–17 is a stipulation, so the covenant that bears his name is the only
  one in Scripture the human party could not break if he tried.
- [[God]] — *ʾĕlōhîm* throughout, without exception. The seam that ran through the last two
  pericopes holds: the personal, interior, altar-side material at 8:20–22 was *Yahweh*, and this
  formal, universal, covenantal speech is *God*. Three separate speech formulas structure the unit
  — *"God blessed Noah and his sons, and said"* (9:1), *"God spoke to Noah and to his sons"* (9:8),
  *"God said"* (9:12) — and what is most striking about the God of this passage is the direction of
  the binding. He requires nothing and undertakes everything, and he puts the sign of it where *he*
  will see it.
- [[Shem]], [[Ham]], and [[Japheth]] — for the first time addressed rather than listed. Both the
  blessing (9:1) and the covenant (9:8) are given to *"Noah and his sons"*, which matters because
  Noah is nine hundred and fifty years old and dies at the end of the chapter: the covenant is
  spoken to the men through whom it will actually run. They are still given no act and no word.
  Everything that individuates them starts at 9:18.
- [[Adam]] — not named, but 9:1–7 is unintelligible except as a rewriting of what was said to him.
  Every clause of the blessing is a clause of 1:28–30 either repeated, dropped, or amended.
- [[Cain]] and [[Lamech (son of Methushael)]] — also unnamed, and 9:5–6 is addressed to what they
  did. The reckoning *"at the hand of every man's brother"* answers 4:9, and the strict
  one-for-one of 9:6 answers the sevenfold and seventy-sevenfold of 4:15 and 4:24.

## Places

No location is named. The arena is still *hāʾāreṣ*, the earth entire, and the covenant's stated
scope is *"between me and the earth"* (9:13) and *"all flesh that is on the earth"* (9:17) — the
last passage in Genesis whose terms apply to everybody before the narrative starts narrowing at
chapter 10 into nations, at chapter 11 into a family, and at chapter 12 into one man. [[Ararat]] is
somewhere underfoot and goes unmentioned.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the Bible's first covenant actually established, and the template against which
  every later one reads as a variation: unilateral, unconditional, everlasting, universal in scope,
  signed, and impossible for the human party to break.
- [[Blood]] — introduced here as a category in its own right. 9:4–6 makes blood the seat of *nepeš*,
  God's to require, forbidden as food and forbidden to spill, in five verses that govern both the
  sacrificial system and the ethics of homicide for the rest of the canon.
- [[Image of God]] — 9:6 is the proof text that the image survived the fall and the flood, and the
  first passage to draw an ethical consequence from it rather than a vocational one.
- [[Blessing]] — 1:28's blessing reissued, and the only element of Genesis 1 that comes through the
  flood unaltered. Everything attached to it is changed for the worse; the blessing itself is not.
- [[Remembrance]] — *zākar* twice more (9:15, 9:16), now with a covenant as its object and a sign
  built to trigger it. The verb that turned the flood at 8:1 is the verb that guarantees there will
  not be another.
- [[Life and Death]] — the passage legislates both directions at once: life may be taken from
  animals for food, life may not be taken from humans at all, and the reason for the second is
  supplied while the first is left as a bare permission.
- [[Judgment]] — formally limited for the first time. *"There will never again be a flood to destroy
  the earth"* is God constraining his own future action and attaching a public sign to the
  constraint.
- [[Creation]] — Genesis 1's charter re-issued in a damaged form. Dominion is not restored; fear is
  installed in its place, and the vegetarian order of 1:29–30 is revoked.
- [[Vengeance]] and [[Retribution]] — 9:6 sets an exact equivalence where 4:15 and 4:24 had
  multipliers, and grounds it in the victim rather than in the avenger.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — a distinction that was already operating at 7:2 and 8:20 is conspicuously
  *not* applied here: *"every moving thing that lives"* is food, with no exceptions listed. Leviticus
  11 will make the exceptions, for Israel only.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — 9:4's reservation of the blood is the premise the whole altar system
  runs on. Leviticus 17:11 quotes the logic of this verse to explain why blood atones.
- [[Sin]] — 8:21 declared the human *yēṣer* incurably evil; 9:5–6 is what a world governed on that
  assumption needs. The provision for homicide comes before any homicide is reported.
- [[Curse]] — absent, pointedly. This is the first divine speech to humanity in Genesis with no
  curse in it: 3:14–19 cursed the serpent and the ground, 4:11 cursed Cain, and 9:1–17 curses
  nothing. Noah supplies one himself at 9:25.

## Notes

- **The two accounts of the same promise, and the seam between them.** Genesis 8:20–22 and 9:1–17
  say substantially the same thing twice in two entirely different registers. The first is *Yahweh*,
  an altar, an aroma, a resolve spoken *in his heart*, and no sign; the second is *ʾĕlōhîm*, a
  formal *bərît* repeated seven times, a public speech, and a sign in the sky. Source critics assign
  the first to J and the second to P with unusual unanimity, and Genesis 9 is one of the clearest
  places to see what P's covenant scheme looks like: two covenants, Noah's and Abraham's, both
  *hēqîm* rather than *kārat*, both called *ʿôlām*, each with a physical sign (rainbow,
  circumcision), and no Sinai covenant of the same type. As with the flood account itself — see
  [[Noah and the Flood]] — the finished text is what this wiki reads, and the finished text gains
  something from the doubling: the promise is made once where only God and the reader can hear it,
  and once where Noah can. The worship happens in the gap.

- **The bow, and how far the image can be pushed.** That *qešet* is the war-bow is a lexical fact and
  not an interpretation; Hebrew simply has one word for both things. What is genuinely a reading, and
  a much later one, is the further step — popular in medieval and modern preaching — that the bow
  hangs *unstrung* and *pointed away*, curve toward the earth and string toward heaven, so that God
  has aimed his weapon at himself. That is not in the verse and should not be presented as though it
  were. What the verse says is that God has put his bow in the cloud and will look at it. The
  disarmament reading needs no more than that, and the ancient Near Eastern background supports it
  without the flourish: in *Enūma Eliš* VI, Marduk hangs his bow in the sky after defeating Tiamat
  and the gods make it a constellation. A victorious warrior storing his weapon in the heavens was
  an available image, and Genesis uses it to mean the war has ended rather than that it was won.

- **What "replenish" does and does not mean.** WEB's *"replenish the earth"* at 9:1 follows KJV, and
  a modern reader naturally hears *re*-fill, which suits the context so well that the point is easy
  to miss: the Hebrew is *mālʾû*, plain **fill**, the identical form used at 1:28. "Replenish" meant
  simply "fill" in 1611 English. Nothing in the Hebrew of 9:1 marks it as a second filling; the
  repetition of the sixth-day wording does that work by itself.

- **The Noahide laws.** Rabbinic tradition derives from this chapter the seven commandments held to
  bind all humanity rather than Israel alone — the *sheva mitzvot b'nei Noach* (b. *Sanhedrin*
  56a–60a; t. *Avodah Zarah* 8:4): prohibitions of idolatry, blasphemy, murder, sexual immorality,
  theft, and eating flesh torn from a living animal, plus the positive requirement to establish
  courts of justice. Only two of the seven are actually stated in Genesis 9 — the blood prohibition
  of 9:4, from which the rule against *ʾēver min ha-ḥay* is drawn, and the homicide provision of
  9:5–6, from which the courts are inferred. The rest are derived by exegesis from Genesis 2:16. The
  derivation is looser than the doctrine; the doctrine is nonetheless one of the most consequential
  things built on this passage, because it is the framework in which Judaism can say that a
  non-Jew is righteous without being a Jew.

- **Acts 15, and why the blood rule is the one that carried.** The Jerusalem council imposes four
  things on Gentile believers: abstinence from pollution of idols, from sexual immorality, from
  things strangled, and from blood (15:20, 29; 21:25). Two of the four are the rule of 9:4 —
  strangling kills an animal without draining it. There are two standard explanations and they lead
  to different places. One is that the council kept precisely the requirements that predate Sinai
  and therefore bind everyone, which makes the list essentially Noahic. The other is that the four
  correspond to the laws of Leviticus 17–18 that apply to the resident alien living among Israel,
  and are therefore about table fellowship in mixed congregations rather than about universal
  morality. The second has the tighter textual fit; the first explains better why these four and not
  others. Either way the prohibition of 9:4 is the reason the one dietary rule in the New Testament
  is a rule about blood, and it is why some churches — Orthodox, and notably the Jehovah's Witnesses
  in the matter of transfusion — have continued to treat it as binding.

- **What 9:6 is taken to authorize, and what it plainly does not settle.** The verse is the oldest
  and most-cited biblical warrant for capital punishment, and the argument over it turns partly on a
  preposition. *Bā-ʾādām* can be read instrumentally, *"by man"* — human beings are the agents, which
  is how WEB and nearly every English version render it, and which makes the verse a delegation of
  judicial authority. It can also be read as a *bet* of price, *"in exchange for the man"* — a life
  for a life, with the agent unspecified, which leaves the sentence stating an equivalence without
  saying who executes it. Both readings are ancient; Targum Onqelos resolves the question in a third
  direction by supplying judges and witnesses. Beyond the grammar, the verse is a statement in a
  narrative rather than a statute with procedure, and the Torah's actual homicide law — which
  distinguishes intent, requires multiple witnesses, and provides cities of refuge (Numbers 35;
  Deuteronomy 19) — is considerably more careful than the maxim. Readers who take 9:6 as
  establishing the state's authority over lethal force and readers who take it as a restraint on
  private vengeance are both working from something the text supports; readers who take it as a
  complete penal code are not.

- **The covenant's beneficiaries, and what the chapter refuses to say.** It is worth noticing what a
  strange document this is by the standards of everything that follows it. The parties are God on one
  side and, on the other, every human being who will ever live and every animal that breathes. There
  is no elect group, no condition, no obligation, and no way to forfeit it. Nothing in the rest of
  the Bible is like this, and the passages that most explicitly build on it — Isaiah 54:9–10 above
  all — use it precisely because it cannot be broken: the argument runs *this promise is as
  unconditional as the one about the water*. The one prophetic text that turns it around, Isaiah
  24:5, accuses the earth of having *"broken the everlasting covenant"*, which cannot mean the
  Noahic covenant on its own terms — nobody was given anything to keep — and is usually read as the
  created order or the moral obligations of 9:4–6 rather than the promise of 9:11.
