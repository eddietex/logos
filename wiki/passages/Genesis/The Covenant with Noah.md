# The Covenant with Noah

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 9:1–17

## Summary

Everything promised since 6:18 arrives here, and it arrives in two halves that do not obviously
belong together. The first seven verses are a **blessing** — the creation mandate of Genesis 1:28
handed back to humanity, with the terms visibly altered. The last ten are a **covenant** — the
*bərît* first mentioned inside a sentence of universal death, finally established, in public, with a
sign. Between them stands the pericope's real subject: what kind of world is safe to restart, and
what God is willing to bind himself to in order to have one.

[[The Waters Recede]] ended with God resolving never to strike all life again and telling nobody.
This is where he says it out loud.

### The mandate reissued, and what is missing from it (9:1–7)

> **1** God blessed Noah and his sons, and said to them, “Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the
> earth.

That is Genesis 1:28's opening clause, word for word, spoken to eight people standing on a planet
that has just been emptied. [[Blessing]] returns to humanity here; it was restored to the animals
five verses earlier, at 8:17, and has been absent from the narrative since the flood began.

But 1:28 was longer, and the difference is the point:

> *God blessed them. God said to them, “Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have
> dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that
> moves on the earth.”* (1:28)

**The dominion language is gone.** *Kābaš* (subdue) and *rādâ* (have dominion) do not appear in
Genesis 9, and nothing in the chapter replaces them with a synonym. What replaces them is this:

> **2** The fear of you and the dread of you will be on every animal of the earth, and on every bird
> of the sky. Everything that moves along the ground, and all the fish of the sea, are delivered
> into your hand.

The four categories are 1:28's four categories — beasts, birds, creeping things, fish — and the
relation to them has changed from **rule** to **terror**. *Môrāʾ* and *ḥat*, fear and dread, are the
words used later for what falls on Israel's enemies (Deuteronomy 11:25, *"the fear of you and the
dread of you"*, the identical pair). And *"delivered into your hand"* — *nātan bəyedkem* — is the
standard Hebrew formula for handing a city over to an invading army. Humanity's authority over the
creatures survives the flood; the peaceable form of it does not. What was a viceroy's commission at
1:28 is a state of armed truce at 9:2, and the text records the change without a word of comment.
See [[Image of God]], where dominion is the strongest evidence for the functional reading of the
image, and note that Genesis 9 keeps the image (9:6) while dropping the dominion.

**The diet changes with it.**

> **3** Every moving thing that lives will be food for you. As I gave you the green herb, I have
> given everything to you.

This is the first permission to eat meat in the Bible, and it cites the verse it is amending: *"as I
gave you the green herb"* points back to 1:29–30, where the food assigned to humanity is
seed-bearing plants and fruit, and the food assigned to the animals is green herb. Whether 1:29 was
ever meant as a prescription is arguable — [[Abel]] keeps sheep at 4:2 and offers *"some of the
firstborn of his flock and of its fat"* at 4:4, so animals are being killed long before anyone is
told they may be eaten — but the two verses are plainly in conversation, and 9:3 is the concession.
The world after the flood is one in which creatures eat each other with permission.

**And then the single restriction, which is not about species:**

> **4** But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat.

The Hebrew is *ʾak bāśār bənapšô dāmô lōʾ tōʾkēlû* — flesh *in its nephesh*, its blood, you shall
not eat. **The life is in the blood**, and blood is therefore not food. This is the Bible's first
dietary law, it precedes Sinai by centuries, and it is the one command in Scripture given to all
humanity rather than to a covenant people. Leviticus 17:11 supplies the reason the Torah works from
— *"the life of the flesh is in the blood"*, and *"I have given it to you on the altar to make
atonement for your souls"* — and the prohibition is repeated more often than almost any other in the
Pentateuch (Leviticus 3:17, 7:26–27, 17:10–14; Deuteronomy 12:16, 23–25). See [[Blood]].

**The prohibition then turns from animal blood to human blood, and the tone changes.**

> **5** I will surely require your life’s blood. At the hand of every animal I will require it. At
> the hand of man, even at the hand of every man’s brother, I will require the life of man.

*Require* is *dāraš*, to seek out, to demand an accounting. It is said three times in one verse, and
the third occurrence names the paradigm case: *"at the hand of every man's brother"*. That is
[[Cain and Abel]], and it is deliberate. God has been requiring the blood of a murdered brother
since 4:10, when *"the voice of your brother's blood cries to me from the ground"* — and until this
verse he has done so with no rule in existence and no human agency involved. The new world gets the
rule.

> **6** Whoever sheds man’s blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image.

The Hebrew is six words and a reason clause, and the six words are a perfect chiasm:

```
šōpēk   dam   hā-ʾādām        ba-ʾādām   dāmô    yiššāpēk
shedder blood of-the-man      by-the-man his-blood shall-be-shed
   A      B        C              C'        B'        A'
```

The sentence turns on its own axis: the form enacts the content, and *ʾādām* stands at the centre on
both sides of the pivot — the victim and the executor are the same word. It is the first line of
poetry in the Bible spoken by God about law rather than about creation or curse.

And the ground given for it is the one thing Genesis has never withdrawn: *"for God made man in his
own image"*. The image was granted at 1:26–27, transmitted from father to son at 5:3, and here —
after a fall, a murder, a corrupted earth, and a flood — it is still in force and still
load-bearing. It is what makes a human death different in kind from an animal death in the same
paragraph that has just handed the animals over as food. Homicide is a crime against God's likeness,
which is why James 3:9 can still make the same appeal about *cursing* people, and why the
prohibition is universal rather than tribal.

> **7** Be fruitful and multiply. Increase abundantly in the earth, and multiply in it.”

The blessing repeated, closing the unit where it opened. Between the two statements of *be fruitful*
sit a diet, a prohibition, and a law about killing — which is Genesis's way of saying that the
command to fill the earth now runs through a world where filling it and emptying it are both things
people do.

### The covenant established (9:8–17)

> **8** God spoke to Noah and to his sons with him, saying,
> **9** “As for me, behold, I establish my covenant with you, and with your offspring after you,
> **10** and with every living creature that is with you: the birds, the livestock, and every animal
> of the earth with you, of all that go out of the ship, even every animal of the earth.
> **11** I will establish my covenant with you: All flesh will not be cut off any more by the waters
> of the flood. There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth.”

The word *bərît* occurs **seven times in these ten verses** (9:9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17), which in
a narrative this spare is not an accident. The verb is *hēqîm*, **establish** — *cause to stand* —
and not the ordinary idiom *kārat bərît*, to *cut* a covenant. No animals are divided, no parties
pass between the pieces, nobody swears. The difference is discussed on [[Covenant]]; what can be
said from this passage alone is that the covenant of 6:18, announced before the flood, is here made
to stand, in the same verb, and that Genesis never uses *cut* of it.

Four features, each of which the rest of the Bible's covenants will lack at least one of:

**It is universal in scope.** *"With every living creature that is with you"*, said twice in verse
10 and repeated at 12, 15, 16, and 17 — *"every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth"*.
The animals are parties to this covenant. No later covenant in Scripture includes a non-human party,
and Genesis has prepared the ground: 8:1 put the animals in the object list of what God remembered,
and the ark narrative counted them by name. The Bible's first ratified covenant is made with the
biosphere.

**It is unconditional.** There is no stipulation on the human side. Nothing is asked of Noah — not
obedience, not a sign to keep, not a response. Compare Genesis 17:1, where the covenant with Abraham
opens *"Walk before me, and be blameless"*, and Exodus 24:7, where the people answer *"All that
Yahweh has spoken will we do, and be obedient."* Here, nobody answers, because there is nothing to
answer. Noah does not speak in this pericope, as he has not spoken since 6:9; his first recorded
words in Scripture are still eight verses away and they are a curse.

**Its content is negative.** *"There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth."* The
covenant does not promise land, offspring, kingship, or presence. It promises an omission. This is
the form a promise takes when it is a limit God places on his own conduct, and it is the first such
limit in Scripture — 8:21's *"I will never again strike every living thing"* was the resolve, and
this is the resolve made binding.

**It has a sign, and the sign is not for us.**

> **12** God said, “This is the token of the covenant which I make between me and you and every
> living creature that is with you, for perpetual generations:
> **13** I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between me and the
> earth.

*ʾÔt*, sign or token — the third in Genesis, after the lights of 1:14 set *"for signs"* and the
protective mark given to Cain at 4:15. The Hebrew word rendered *rainbow* is **qešet**, and it is
not a special word for the meteorological phenomenon; Hebrew has no such word. *Qešet* is the
ordinary noun for a **war bow**, used some seventy times in the Hebrew Bible and almost always for
the weapon (Genesis 21:16, 27:3; 1 Samuel 18:4; Psalm 7:12; Zechariah 9:10). What God says, in the
plainest reading of the Hebrew, is *I have set my bow in the cloud* — the weapon of the divine
warrior hung up in the sky, unstrung, pointing away from the earth. Hebrew has no separate term to
hide behind, and the metaphor is available whether or not the narrator pressed it. See
[[The Bow in the Cloud]].

Note also what the verse does **not** say. It does not say God makes a rainbow, or creates one, or
that one had never been seen. The verb is *nātan*, to give or to set, and what is given is a
*meaning*: an ordinary thing in the sky is assigned a job. This is the same move as the mark on Cain
— an existing sign made to carry a promise.

> **14** When I bring a cloud over the earth, that the rainbow will be seen in the cloud,
> **15** I will remember my covenant, which is between me and you and every living creature of all
> flesh, and the waters will no more become a flood to destroy all flesh.
> **16** The rainbow will be in the cloud. I will look at it, that I may remember the everlasting
> covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth.”

**The sign is a memory aid for God.** Every clause of its purpose has God as subject: *I bring*, *I
will remember*, *I will look at it*, *that I may remember*. Nothing is said about the human party
seeing it, being comforted by it, or being obliged to keep it. Set that against circumcision, which
Abraham's household must perform and carry (17:10–14), and against the Sabbath, which Israel must
observe as its sign (Exodus 31:13, 17): those are signs the covenant partner keeps. This one, God
keeps.

*Remember* is **zākar** again, the verb that turned the flood at 8:1 — never recollection after
forgetting, always action on a commitment. See [[Remembrance]]. Which makes the rainbow the visible
form of the same act the whole flood narrative pivots on: it is 8:1 made permanent and put in the
sky.

And *"everlasting covenant"* — *bərît ʿôlām* — is its first occurrence in Scripture, a phrase that
will attach afterwards to Abraham (17:7, 13, 19), to the Sabbath (Exodus 31:16), to David (2 Samuel
23:5), and to the new covenant of Jeremiah 32:40 and Ezekiel 37:26.

> **17** God said to Noah, “This is the token of the covenant which I have established between me
> and all flesh that is on the earth.”

The last verse repeats the twelfth almost exactly, and closes the unit the way the blessing closed
at 9:7 — by saying the same thing twice with the substance in between.

### What this pericope settles, and what it does not

It settles that the world will not be un-made again by water, and it settles it unilaterally. It
does not claim that the world has improved: 8:21 named the human heart as evil *from its youth* and
gave that as the reason for the promise, and 9:5–6 immediately legislates for murder, which is a
strange thing to do in a world one expects to behave. The rule about bloodshed and the covenant
about the flood are two halves of the same realism. Humanity will kill; God will not.

That realism is confirmed eight verses later. The man who has just been blessed plants a vineyard,
gets drunk, and curses his own grandson — see
[[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]], where the first words Noah ever speaks in Scripture undo a
good deal of the mood of this one.

## People

- [[God]] — he speaks for sixteen of the seventeen verses and does nothing else in them. This is the
  longest uninterrupted divine speech in Genesis so far, and its content is entirely about limits:
  what humanity may eat, what it may not, what will be required of it, and what he himself will
  never do again. Two things are new. **He legislates** — 9:4 and 9:6 are the first standing rules
  in Scripture addressed to humanity as such, as against the single prohibition given to one man in
  a garden. And **he ties his own memory to an object**: *"I will look at it, that I may remember."*
  He is *ʾĕlōhîm* throughout, without exception, which continues the pattern of 8:1–19 and breaks
  from the *Yahweh* of the altar scene at 8:20–21.
- [[Noah]] — blessed, addressed, legislated to, and covenanted with, and he says nothing. He is not
  asked to agree, is given no obligation, and is not even told to look at the sign. The silence that
  has run since 6:9 runs through the most important speech ever addressed to him. Everything he
  receives here is unearned in the strict sense: the covenant is established with *"you, and with
  your offspring after you, and with every living creature"* — that is, on terms that would be
  identical if he were not righteous.
- [[Shem]], [[Ham]], and [[Japheth]] — *"Noah and his sons"* (9:1), *"Noah and to his sons with
  him"* (9:8). They are addressed directly for the first time in Scripture: every earlier mention
  has them as items in a boarding list. They are also covenant parties in their own right, and the
  phrase *"your offspring after you"* makes every human being who has ever lived one. They still do
  not speak, and they are individuated nine verses later.
- **Every living creature** — the birds, the livestock, and every animal that came off the ship,
  named as parties to the covenant five separate times (9:10, 12, 15, 16, 17). No page, because they
  are not a character; recorded here because the wiki should not let the most unusual feature of the
  Bible's first ratified covenant pass without saying so. The animals are simultaneously handed over
  as food (9:3) and included in a covenant (9:10), in the same speech, twelve verses apart.
- **Noah's wife and his sons' wives** — not mentioned. The blessing at 9:1 is addressed to *"Noah
  and his sons"*, and *be fruitful and multiply* is said to a group of four men. The four women who
  are half of the eight and are indispensable to the command are absent from the sentence, as they
  have been from every speech in the flood narrative. Genesis 1:28 was addressed to *them*, male and
  female.

## Places

None. The pericope has no geography at all: its arena is *hāʾāreṣ*, the earth, which occurs nine
times in seventeen verses and is the covenant's second party at 9:13. Genesis 5:1–7:24 ran three
pericopes without a named location; [[Ararat]] broke the run at 8:4, and this pericope goes back to
having none. That is the primeval history's frame at full extent — a covenant whose parties are God,
all flesh, and the earth needs no map. Geography returns at Genesis 10.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the theme's decisive passage. *Bərît* occurs seven times in ten verses; the
  covenant promised at 6:18 is established; and the four features that make it unlike every later
  covenant are all here: universal scope (the animals are parties), no condition, negative content,
  and a sign God keeps rather than the human party. Also the first *bərît ʿôlām*, everlasting
  covenant, in Scripture.
- [[Blood]] — introduced as a theme here. *Dām* occurs four times in two verses, and the pericope
  makes two claims that the rest of the canon runs on: the life is in the blood, and therefore blood
  is not food (9:4); and human blood is required by God from whoever sheds it (9:5–6). Both are
  pre-Sinai and both are addressed to all humanity.
- [[Image of God]] — the status survives the flood and does legal work. 9:6 is the only verse in the
  Torah to give the image as the *reason* for a command, and the command it grounds is the
  prohibition of homicide. Note the asymmetry with what is dropped: the image is retained and the
  dominion language of 1:28 is not.
- [[Blessing]] — restored to humanity at 9:1 after its longest absence in Genesis, and framed at
  both ends of the unit (9:1, 9:7). It comes back shorn of *subdue* and *have dominion* and with
  *fear and dread* in their place.
- [[Life and Death]] — the whole pericope is about which deaths are permitted. Animals may now be
  killed and eaten (9:3); their blood may not be consumed (9:4); human life is required back from
  anyone who takes it, animals included (9:5); and the world will not be killed again (9:11).
- [[Vengeance]] — the theme's regulation. Cain's sevenfold protection (4:15) and Lamech's
  seventy-sevenfold boast (4:23–24) were the Bible's first two statements on returning harm, and
  both were unlegislated. 9:6 finally supplies a rule, and it is exactly proportionate — one life
  for one life — which is *lex talionis* four books before Exodus states it.
- [[Judgment]] — bounded. God gives up the flood as an instrument and says so under oath, which is
  the first time in Scripture he limits what he will do rather than what people may do. And he
  delegates: 9:6 hands the requiring of blood, until now his own act (4:10–15), to human hands.
- [[Remembrance]] — *zākar* twice (9:15, 9:16), attached to a physical object. The verb that turned
  the flood at 8:1 is here made permanent and given a trigger.
- [[Creation]] — Genesis 1 quoted and revised. 1:28's blessing at 9:1 and 9:7, 1:29–30's diet at
  9:3, 1:26–27's image at 9:6, and 1:14's *signs* in the vocabulary of 9:12. The new world is the
  old world re-issued with amendments, and the amendments are all concessions to violence.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — conspicuously *not* invoked. 7:2 assumed a clean/unclean distinction with
  no definition, and 8:20 used it at the altar; 9:3 then hands over *"every moving thing that
  lives"* as food without any qualification at all. Genesis does not reconcile the two, and
  Leviticus 11 will restrict for Israel what Genesis 9 grants to humanity.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — indirectly, and importantly. 9:4's reason for withholding blood is
  the reason Leviticus 17:11 gives for putting it on the altar: it is the life. The dietary
  prohibition and the sacrificial system rest on the same sentence.
- [[Curse]] — absent, which is worth recording. This is the first substantial divine speech in
  Genesis since chapter 1 that contains no curse, and the next human speech in the book (9:25) is
  one — see [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].

## Notes

- **On *hēqîm* and the missing ceremony.** Genesis never says God *cut* (*kārat*) a covenant with
  Noah. The verb throughout 6:18 and 9:9–17 is *hēqîm*, to cause to stand, and there is no ritual —
  no divided animals, no meal, no oath sworn by either party, no blood. Dumbrell's argument that
  *hēqîm bərît* always confirms a pre-existing arrangement, and that the Noahic covenant therefore
  renews an implicit covenant with creation, is set out and weighed on [[Covenant]]; the objection
  is that the same verb appears at 17:7 and 17:19 of a covenant Genesis 15 and 17 are visibly
  inaugurating. What is safe to say from this passage: the absence of ceremony fits the absence of a
  human party's obligation. There is nothing for Noah to swear to, so there is nothing to swear
  over.

- **Is 9:6 law or prediction?** The Hebrew has no imperative and no addressee: it is a participial
  clause followed by an imperfect — *the one shedding the blood of the man, by man his blood shall
  be shed*. That will support either reading. As **institution**, it is the founding text of
  judicial capital punishment, and the Torah proceeds on that basis (Exodus 21:12, Leviticus 24:17,
  Numbers 35:16–21), with Numbers 35 adding the procedural apparatus Genesis lacks — witnesses,
  intent, cities of refuge, and a prohibition on ransom. As **prediction**, it is a statement about
  how violence propagates, and Matthew 26:52 reads it that way in the plainest sense: *"all those
  who take the sword will die by the sword"*. The two readings are not exclusive, and Genesis 9
  gives no court, no penalty scale, and no procedure — which is why it can found a legal tradition
  without settling a single question of how to run one. The wiki records both and does not
  adjudicate.

- **The bow, and what the parallels do and do not show.** Reading *qešet* as a warrior's weapon laid
  aside is old (it is in the medieval Jewish commentators and in Luther) and is supported by the
  noun's ordinary usage, by the divine-warrior imagery of Habakkuk 3:9–11 and Lamentations 2:4, and
  by the fact that Hebrew simply has no other word available. It is worth adding that the
  Mesopotamian flood tradition puts a memorial sign in the sky too. In *Enūma Eliš* VI, Anu takes up
  Marduk's bow after the victory over Tiamat and sets it in heaven as a constellation; in
  *Gilgamesh* XI, immediately after the flood, Ishtar lifts the lapis-lazuli necklace of her neck
  and swears *"I shall not forget these days… I shall remember them and never forget."* A deity, a
  sky-object, an oath, and an act of divine remembering after a flood — the configuration is the
  same as Genesis 9:13–16, and the differences are the ones [[The Waters Recede]] noted at 8:21: in
  the Akkadian the goddess is reproaching the gods for having caused it, and the oath binds nobody.
  What the parallels establish is a shared repertoire; what they do not establish is a direction of
  borrowing. See [[The Bow in the Cloud]].

- **The Noahide commandments.** Rabbinic tradition derives from Genesis 2:16 and 9:1–7 the *sheva
  mitzvot b'nei Noach*, seven commandments binding on all humanity rather than on Israel:
  prohibitions of idolatry, blasphemy, murder, sexual immorality, theft, and eating flesh from a
  living animal, plus the requirement to establish courts (Tosefta *Avodah Zarah* 8:4; *Sanhedrin*
  56a). Only two of the seven are actually stated in Genesis 9, and the number is a construction.
  But the impulse is textual, not invented: this is the one body of divine command in the Hebrew
  Bible addressed to every human being, given before any nation exists, and it is the natural place
  for a tradition needing a universal ethic to look. The category of the *ger toshav*, the resident
  alien who keeps the Noahide laws without converting, is built on it. See
  [[The Noahic Commandments and the Council of Jerusalem]].

- **Vegetarianism before the flood.** Genesis 1:29–30 assigns plants to humans and green herb to
  animals, and says nothing about eating meat; 9:3 grants *"every moving thing that lives"*. The
  straightforward reading is that the primeval diet was vegetarian and the flood changed it, and
  Isaiah 11:6–9 and 65:25 imagine the reversal — the wolf and the lamb, the lion eating straw — as
  part of the restored world. Three complications. Abel keeps flocks and offers their fat (4:2, 4:4)
  with no suggestion the meat is wasted. The clean/unclean distinction of 7:2 has no function except
  sacrifice unless it also has one at the table. And 1:29–30 is a grant, not a prohibition — it says
  what is given, not what is withheld. The text supports the reading; it does not compel it.

- **Source criticism.** Genesis 9:1–17 is assigned to P by every standard analysis, and it reads
  like it: the vocabulary of 1:1–2:3 (*ṣelem*, *bərît*, *ʾôt*, the fourfold animal categories, *be
  fruitful and multiply*), the formal repetition, the interest in covenant and sign, and the divine
  name *ʾĕlōhîm* without exception. 8:20–22, immediately before, is the classic J passage — an
  altar, a sacrifice, *Yahweh*, and God speaking in his heart. So the two promises never to flood
  the earth again sit adjacent and are attributed to different hands, which is the tidiest
  illustration in the primeval history of what source analysis is responding to. The finished text
  makes the doubling do work: the same undertaking is given twice, once as a private resolve nobody
  hears and once as a public covenant with a sign, and the gap between them is where Noah builds an
  altar without knowing. See the notes on [[Noah and the Flood]] for the fuller account of the
  flood's source problem.

- **What the covenant does not promise.** It is worth being exact, because the verse is often quoted
  loosely. God undertakes that there will not be another *flood* (*mabbûl*) to destroy the earth,
  and 9:11 says so twice. He does not undertake that there will be no further judgment, no local
  catastrophe, and no end. 2 Peter 3:5–7 makes precisely this reading — the world that then was
  perished by water, and *"the heavens that now are and the earth… have been stored up for fire"* —
  and it is not an evasion of Genesis 9 but an attentive reading of what Genesis 9 specifies.
