# The Covenant with Noah

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 9:1–17

## Summary

This is the charter of the world the rest of the Bible happens in, and the world its readers live
in. Eight people step off a boat onto a planet that has been emptied, and God gives them terms.

The unit is two speeches with a hinge between them, and the division is exact. **9:1–7** is about
what humanity may and may not do — a blessing, a diet, and a law. **9:8–17** is about what God will
not do — a covenant, its scope, and its sign. The first half places an obligation on people; the
second half places one on God, and on nobody else. Read together they answer the two questions the
flood leaves open: *how are we supposed to live now*, and *is this going to happen again*.

The pericope's centre of gravity is that neither half is a return to Eden. Everything in 9:1–7 is a
quotation of Genesis 1:28–30 with something altered, and every alteration is downward.

**The blessing comes back, and the dominion does not.**

> **1** God blessed Noah and his sons, and said to them, “Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the
> earth.

Set that beside the original: *"Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion
over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the
earth"* (1:28). The first three verbs survive word for word. **Subdue** (*kābaš*) and **have
dominion** (*rādâ*) do not. They are simply absent, and this is the only place in Scripture where
1:28 is repeated and they are dropped. This is the first blessing on humanity since Genesis 1, and
[[Blessing]] has been withheld through the whole flood narrative — it returns to the animals at 8:17
and to people here, on the far side of a year of water.

What replaces the dominion is not nothing. It is worse.

> **2** The fear of you and the dread of you will be on every animal of the earth, and on every bird
> of the sky. Everything that moves along the ground, and all the fish of the sea, are delivered
> into your hand.

The roll of creatures is the roll of 1:28, in the same order, and the relation to them has changed
from rule to terror. *Fear* (*môrāʾ*) and *dread* (*ḥat*) are not administrative words; the second is
the vocabulary of a routed army. And *"delivered into your hand"* — *nətunîm bəyedkem* — is the
standing biblical idiom for handing over a defeated enemy: *"I have given Jericho into your hand"*
(Joshua 6:2), *"Yahweh has delivered your enemies the Moabites into your hand"* (Judges 3:28). Adam
named the animals; Noah's sons are told the animals will be afraid of them. Whatever authority
humanity keeps over the creation after the flood, Genesis describes it as the authority of a
conqueror rather than of a steward. See [[Image of God]] and [[Creation]].

**And then the diet changes.**

> **3** Every moving thing that lives will be food for you. As I gave you the green herb, I have
> given everything to you.

Genesis 1:29–30 gave seed-bearing plants and fruit to humanity and green plants to the animals, and
gave nothing else to anyone. Whether that constitutes a prohibition or merely a grant is arguable —
the text of 1:29 does not forbid meat, it simply does not mention it — but 9:3 unmistakably treats
itself as an extension, and it makes the comparison itself: *as I gave you the green herb, I have
given everything*. The vegetarian world of chapter 1 is over, and the sentence that ends it is
constructed to make the reader look back at the verse it is enlarging.

Two things follow. Predation, which the reader has been watching among the animals since long before
this, is now formally licensed for people; and the [[Clean and Unclean]] distinction that Genesis
7:2 assumed and 8:20 used is conspicuously **not** applied here — *"every moving thing that lives"*
is given without qualification, which is one of the reasons Leviticus 11's food laws read as
Israel's particular discipline rather than as a rule for humanity.

**One thing is withheld, and it is the first food law in the Bible.**

> **4** But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, you shall not eat.

The Hebrew is *bāśār bənapšô dāmô* — *flesh with its life, its blood*. **Nepeš** is the word Genesis
2:7 uses of the man when the breath goes into him (*nepeš ḥayyāh*, a living being), and here it is
predicated of the animal and identified with its blood. The rule is not hygienic and it is not about
cruelty. It rests on a claim about what blood *is*: the life, which belongs to God and not to the
eater. Leviticus 17:11 states the premise the prohibition assumes — *"the life of the flesh is in the
blood… for it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life"* — and joins it to the altar.
Note where this rule is given: to Noah, before Abraham, before Israel, before any law. It is the one
dietary restriction in Scripture addressed to the whole human race, which is why it is also the one
the Jerusalem council keeps for Gentile believers (Acts 15:20, 29). See
[[The Life Is in the Blood]].

**Then the same word, blood, turns to human blood, and the pericope's hardest verses arrive.**

> **5** I will surely require your life’s blood. At the hand of every animal I will require it. At
> the hand of man, even at the hand of every man’s brother, I will require the life of man.
> **6** Whoever sheds man’s blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image.

*Require* is **dāraš**, to seek out, investigate, demand an accounting — and it is said three times in
one verse. The accounting is demanded from an **animal** that kills a person, which is not a throwaway
(Exodus 21:28 has the goring ox stoned and its flesh not eaten), and from a **man**, and then, in a
phrase the narrative has earned, *"at the hand of every man's brother"*. Genesis has one story about
a man and his brother's blood, and God's question in it was *"the voice of your brother's blood cries
to me from the ground"* (4:10). The law of 9:5 is written in the vocabulary of [[Cain and Abel]].

Verse 6 is poetry set in prose. In Hebrew it is six words arranged as a mirror:

> *šōpēk dam hāʾādām* — **bāʾādām** — *dāmô yiššāpēk*
> (shedder / blood-of / the-man — **by-man** — his-blood / shall-be-shed)

The first three elements come back in reverse, the verb *šāpak* opens and closes it, and the word at
the centre is *ʾādām*, **man**. The form enacts the content: what is done comes back on the doer, and
the pivot on which it comes back is a human being. Which is also the disputed part — *bāʾādām* can be
read as agency (*by* a human hand, so a court executes the sentence) or as exchange (*for* the man,
a life for a life). Nearly every translation takes the first, and the rest of the Torah's homicide
law assumes it, but the ambiguity is real and the notes below set it out.

**The ground given for the rule is the image of God**, and it is the first mention of the image since
Genesis 5:3. That placement decides a question chapter 3 raised and never answered. If the *tselem*
had been forfeited in the fall, or dissolved in a world that had grown so violent God drowned it,
this sentence would have no premise. Genesis 9:6 is written after the fall, after Cain, after the
flood, and it still says *"God made man in his own image"* in the present tense of a standing fact —
which is exactly how James 3:9 will use it. Murder is capital not because life is valuable in general
but because the victim is a likeness of God; the crime is closer to iconoclasm than to theft. See
[[Image of God]] and [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]].

And notice what has happened to [[Vengeance]] across nine chapters. God protected Cain from being
killed and reserved the reckoning to himself (4:15). Lamech seized the reckoning and inflated it to
seventy-sevenfold, for a wound (4:23–24). Now the reckoning is neither withheld nor privately
claimed: it is required by God, capped at one life for one life, and placed in human hands. The
first delegation of judicial authority in the Bible happens here, four verses after the flood that
[[Noah and the Flood]] says came because the earth was *filled with violence*.

**The mandate closes where it opened.**

> **7** Be fruitful and multiply. Increase abundantly in the earth, and multiply in it.”

The repetition brackets 9:2–6 inside the blessing, which is the ordinary way Hebrew marks a unit —
and it is doing something else too. *Increase abundantly* is **šāraṣ**, the swarming verb Genesis
1:20–21 uses of the sea creatures and 8:17 has just used of the animals leaving the ship. This is the
only place in the Bible it is said of human beings. Set beside 6:1 — *"when men began to multiply on
the surface of the ground"*, the verse that opens the disaster — the command to swarm is not a small
thing to give a race God has just finished drowning.

---

**Then God speaks again, and stops talking about people.**

> **8** God spoke to Noah and to his sons with him, saying,
> **9** “As for me, behold, I establish my covenant with you, and with your offspring after you,

*As for me* — *waʾănî* — is emphatic, and it turns the pericope over. Everything from 9:1 to 9:7 was
a set of obligations laid on human beings. From 9:9 the only party under obligation is God, and there
is no *if* anywhere in the ten verses that follow.

**Seven times in nine verses the word is *bərît*** (vv. 9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17), which is more
occurrences than any other passage of comparable length in Scripture. The verb is **hēqîm**, *cause
to stand*, establish — the verb of the promise at 6:18 and not the ordinary idiom *kārat bərît*, to
**cut** a covenant, which Genesis 15 will stage with animals split in half and a self-curse walked
between the pieces. Nothing is cut here. No animal dies, no oath is sworn by either party, nobody
passes between anything, and Noah is not asked whether he accepts. See [[Covenant]].

**The scope is the startling part**, and Genesis states it with a redundancy that is clearly
deliberate:

> **10** and with every living creature that is with you: the birds, the livestock, and every animal
> of the earth with you, of all that go out of the ship, even every animal of the earth.

The verse says *every animal of the earth* twice and enumerates the categories in between. Across
9:9–17 the phrase *every living creature* or its equivalent appears five times, and God twice calls
the arrangement one *"between me and the earth"* (9:13) and *"between me and all flesh"* (9:17). The
Bible's first covenant is not with a person, a family, or a nation. It is with the biosphere, and the
human parties are named alongside the livestock rather than above them — which is a striking way to
follow four verses that have just handed every creature over to human hands in fear and dread. The
same speech subordinates the animals and makes them covenant partners.

**The content is a negative**, which is what a promise looks like when it is a limit God places on
himself:

> **11** I will establish my covenant with you: All flesh will not be cut off any more by the waters
> of the flood. There will never again be a flood to destroy the earth.”

Said twice, in both directions: no more cutting off of flesh, no more destroying of the earth. What
is *not* promised is worth stating as plainly as what is. No promise is made that the world will be
spared judgment — only that it will not be this judgment, by this means. 2 Peter 3:5–7 reads the
clause exactly that closely: the world that was *"overflowed with water, perished"*, and the present
one is *"reserved for fire"*.

**And there is a sign.**

> **12** God said, “This is the token of the covenant which I make between me and you and every
> living creature that is with you, for perpetual generations:
> **13** I set my rainbow in the cloud, and it will be a sign of a covenant between me and the earth.

The word is **qešet**, and everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible — some seventy-five occurrences — it
means a **war-bow**. Esau's hunting bow (27:3), Ishmael's (21:16, 20), *"his bow abides in strength"*
of Joseph (49:24), *"he has bent his bow"* of God in Psalm 7:12. There is no separate Hebrew word for
a rainbow; the meteorological sense exists in the Bible only here and at Ezekiel 1:28, and in both
places it is context that supplies it. So the sentence a Hebrew reader meets is *I have set my bow in
the cloud* — a weapon put down, pointed away from the earth, in the sky of the one who has just
finished using it. The reading is old and widely held and it is an inference: the text does not say
the bow is unstrung, hung up, or laid aside, and the case rests on the vocabulary rather than on any
statement. It is set out with its counter-arguments in the notes, and traced across the canon in
[[The Bow in the Cloud]].

**The sign is not for the people. It is for God.** This is the detail that makes the passage
peculiar, and Genesis says it three times:

> **15** I will remember my covenant … **16** The rainbow will be in the cloud. I will look at it,
> that I may remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh
> that is on the earth.”

Every other covenant sign in Scripture is borne by the human party and functions as their reminder:
circumcision is cut into Abraham's household (17:11), the Sabbath is kept by Israel (Exodus 31:13,
17), the Passover is eaten. Here God installs a sign in his own field of view and states its purpose
as his own remembering. The verb is **zākar**, the verb of 8:1 — *"God remembered Noah"* — which
never means recollection after a lapse but always action on a commitment (see [[Remembrance]]). The
promise is not that God will avoid forgetting. It is that when the clouds gather over a world whose
heart he has already declared incorrigible (8:21), there is something in the sky that he has bound
himself to look at. The rainbow appears at the moment the fear would.

**Two more words in verse 16.** *Everlasting covenant* — *bərît ʿôlām* — is its first occurrence in
Scripture, and it will become the standing formula for the covenants that cannot be revoked:
circumcision (17:7, 13, 19), the Sabbath (Exodus 31:16), David's throne (2 Samuel 23:5), and the new
covenant in Jeremiah 32:40 and Ezekiel 37:26. It begins here, attached to an arrangement with the
animals.

**And Noah, who has now been blessed, commissioned, legislated for, made a covenant with, and given a
sign, says nothing.** He has not spoken since the narrative began at 6:9. He does not answer, agree,
thank, or ask. His first recorded words in Scripture are eight verses away, on the far side of a
vineyard, and they are a curse on his grandson (9:25). See [[Noah]].

One last observation, easy to miss and load-bearing for how the chapter is read. **God is *ʾĕlōhîm*
in all seventeen verses, and *Yahweh* in none of them.** Eight verses earlier, at the altar, it was
*Yahweh* who smelled the aroma and *Yahweh* who spoke in his heart (8:20–21); here the covenant, the
blessing, the law, and the sign are all *ʾĕlōhîm*'s. The seam falls exactly where the classical
source analysis puts it, and it is worth noticing regardless of what one concludes about composition,
because the finished text uses the two names to mark two registers: the personal and interior scene
at the altar, and the universal, formal, legislative scene on the ground. What is promised in 8:21
privately is enacted in 9:11 publicly, with a sign, to everyone.

## People

- [[Noah]] — addressed by name three times (9:1, 9:8, 9:17) and silent in all seventeen verses. The
  pattern of his whole story holds to the end of it: he is the recipient of everything and the
  speaker of nothing. Worth noting what he is *not* given here. No commission, no instruction, no
  task — the ark is behind him, and this speech gives him a world rather than a job. The one thing
  asked of him is the blood prohibition of 9:4, and even that is addressed to the plural *you*, to
  the four men and, through *"your offspring after you"* (9:9), to everyone reading.
- [[God]] — *ʾĕlōhîm* throughout, and the only party in the covenant with anything to do. He blesses,
  gives, requires, establishes, sets, looks, and remembers; the verbs of 9:8–17 are almost all his
  and almost all first-person. Two features of his self-presentation here are new. He **binds his own
  future conduct in public** — 8:21 was a resolve spoken in his heart to nobody, and this is the same
  content stated to witnesses with a sign attached, which is the whole difference between a decision
  and a covenant. And he **gives himself a reminder** (9:16), which no other passage in Scripture
  does.
- [[Shem]], [[Ham]], [[Japheth]] — present as *"his sons"* (9:1, 9:8) and still unnamed in the unit,
  still without an act or a word. They are covenant parties in their own right, though: the promise
  runs *"with you, and with your offspring after you"*, which through these three is the whole human
  race of Genesis 10. Everything that individuates them begins in the very next pericope.
- **Noah's wife and his sons' wives** — unnamed as always, and here not even mentioned. The blessing
  of 9:1 (*"be fruitful, multiply"*) and the covenant of 9:9 (*"your offspring after you"*) are both
  addressed to the men and are both arithmetically impossible without the four women the boarding
  lists carefully counted. Genesis has stopped listing them at exactly the point their absence is
  most conspicuous.
- [[Cain]] and [[Abel]] — not named, and audible anyway. *"At the hand of every man's brother I will
  require the life of man"* (9:5) is the only reason the phrase *man's brother* is in the sentence,
  and 4:10's *"the voice of your brother's blood"* is the case the statute is written against.

## Places

None. The pericope names no location at all — not Ararat, where the ship came to rest, and not any
point on the ground where the altar was built. Its arena is *hāʾāreṣ*, the earth, which occurs
fourteen times in seventeen verses and is the covenant's actual counterparty in 9:13. Geography
resumes at Genesis 10.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the theme's decisive passage. *Bərît* seven times; established rather than cut;
  unilateral, unconditional, everlasting, universal in scope, and negative in content. It is the only
  covenant in Scripture that places no obligation of any kind on the human party, and the only one
  whose signatories include animals.
- [[Blessing]] — restored to humanity for the first time since 1:28, and restored with subtractions.
  The three fertility verbs return; *subdue* and *have dominion* do not.
- [[Image of God]] — the doctrine's second load-bearing text. 9:6 grounds a capital statute in the
  *tselem*, and by doing so establishes that the image survives the fall, Cain, and the flood.
- [[Vengeance]] — the arc from 4:15 to here completed: God's own sevenfold protection of a murderer,
  then Lamech's seventy-sevenfold boast, and now a requirement, capped at one life for one, executed
  by human hands.
- [[Judgment]] — formally limited for the second time and now publicly. 8:21 was the resolve; 9:11 is
  the undertaking, witnessed and signed. And the Genesis pattern holds: the sentence has been carried
  out and provision follows it — a world, a diet, a law, and a guarantee.
- [[Life and Death]] — blood as the seat of *nepeš* (9:4), and human life as the one thing whose
  taking God undertakes to investigate personally (9:5).
- [[Remembrance]] — *zākar* twice (9:15, 9:16), with God as subject and a covenant as its object, and
  a physical sign installed to occasion it. This is the verb of 8:1 given an instrument.
- [[Creation]] — Genesis 1:28–30 quoted clause by clause and altered clause by clause. The second
  world runs on the first world's charter, amended downward.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — by its absence. Meat is granted without any distinction of species, four
  chapters after 7:2 assumed one and one chapter after 8:20 used it.
- [[Curse]] — unmentioned, and the reason 8:21's promise not to *"curse the ground any more"* is not
  the same as lifting the curse of 3:17. Nothing here restores Eden; the ground is still worked in
  sweat on the far side of the rainbow.
- [[Favor and Grace]] — the terms are given to a race whose *yēṣer* God has just declared evil from
  youth (8:21), and are conditioned on nothing about it.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — the blood rule of 9:4 is the premise Leviticus 17:11 turns into the
  logic of atonement: blood is withheld from the table because it is reserved for the altar.

## Notes

- **What the alterations to Genesis 1 add up to.** Set the two charters side by side and the pattern
  is consistent enough to be an argument rather than a coincidence.

  | Genesis 1:26–30 | Genesis 9:1–7 |
  |---|---|
  | *Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth* (1:28) | *Be fruitful, multiply, replenish the earth* (9:1) — retained |
  | *Subdue it… have dominion* (1:28) | absent |
  | Creatures under human rule (1:26, 28) | Creatures in *fear and dread*, *delivered into your hand* (9:2) |
  | Plants for food, to humans and animals (1:29–30) | *Every moving thing that lives* (9:3), with blood withheld (9:4) |
  | Made *in his own image* (1:27) | Still *in his own image* (9:6) — retained, and made the ground of a statute |
  | No law | A homicide law (9:5–6) |
  | *Very good* (1:31) | *Evil from his youth* (8:21), stated as the reason for the promise |

  Everything retained is retained exactly; everything altered is altered in one direction. The new
  world gets a licence to kill animals, a prohibition it did not need before, and a criminal statute.
  A charter that has to include a murder law is a charter written for a world where murder is
  expected — and Genesis 8:21 has already said so in advance. This is the concrete answer to what the
  flood accomplished: it removed a generation and changed nothing about the material, and the terms
  of the reissue are adjusted to that fact rather than to a hope.

- **The war-bow, and how far the reading can be pressed.** *Qešet* is the weapon everywhere else in
  the Hebrew Bible, there is no other Hebrew word for a rainbow, and God is elsewhere described as
  an archer (Psalm 7:12; Habakkuk 3:9; Lamentations 2:4, *"He has bent his bow like an enemy"*). On
  that basis a long tradition reads 9:13 as a weapon laid down: the bow set in the cloud, its string
  slack and its arc turned away from the earth. Three cautions. Genesis says only *nātattî* — *I have
  set* or *given* — and adds nothing about position, tension, or retirement. The Hebrew is not
  peculiar in using one word for both senses, since a rainbow simply is bow-shaped and other
  languages do the same. And Ezekiel 1:28, the passage's only canonical parallel, uses the
  meteorological sense straightforwardly with no martial overtone at all — though Ezekiel's rainbow
  does surround the appearance of God's glory, which Revelation 4:3 then puts around the throne. The
  reading is a strong inference and a reading; the wiki records it as one. What is not in doubt is
  that the sign is meteorological, that it appears in the presence of the very clouds that would
  otherwise be frightening, and that God says its purpose is to prompt his own memory.

- **Enuma Elish, and the bow set in the sky.** In the Babylonian creation epic Marduk defeats Tiamat
  — the primeval salt water — splits her body to make sky and earth, and the assembled gods then take
  his bow and set it in the heavens as a constellation, saluting it as a memorial of the victory. The
  configuration is close enough to Genesis 9:13 to be worth stating: a bow, placed in the sky, after
  a conflict involving cosmic waters, as a permanent token. The differences are the usual ones and
  they run the same way as in the flood account itself (see [[Noah and the Flood]] on Atrahasis and
  Gilgamesh). Marduk's bow commemorates a victory over a defeated enemy; God's bow commemorates a
  promise made to the survivors of the thing he did himself. In *Gilgamesh* XI the goddess Ishtar
  lifts her lapis-lazuli necklace and swears by it that she will remember the days of the flood and
  not forget — an object raised, a memory pledged, in the same scene position as Genesis 9:12–16. The
  Genesis version differs in that the one who must remember is the one who sent the water, and in
  that nobody in Genesis suggests the flood was a mistake, which both Akkadian texts do at length.

- **The seven Noahide commandments.** Rabbinic tradition (*t. Avodah Zarah* 8:4; *b. Sanhedrin* 56a)
  derives from Genesis 2 and 9 a set of seven laws binding on all humanity, not only on Israel:
  prohibitions of idolatry, blasphemy, murder, sexual immorality, theft, and eating flesh torn from a
  living animal, plus the positive requirement to establish courts of justice. A gentile who keeps
  them is a *ḥasid ummot ha-olam*, a righteous person of the nations, with a share in the world to
  come — which is the classical Jewish answer to what is required of non-Jews, and it is derived from
  this chapter. Only two of the seven are actually explicit in Genesis 9: the blood prohibition (9:4)
  and the homicide statute (9:5–6), with the courts read out of *"by man"* in 9:6. The rest are
  inferred from the earlier chapters and from the address to all humanity. The overlap with Acts
  15:20's four abstentions — idolatry, sexual immorality, blood, and things strangled — has been
  noticed since antiquity and is one of the more plausible explanations of that otherwise puzzling
  list. See [[The Life Is in the Blood]].

- **"By man" — agency or exchange.** *Bāʾādām dāmô yiššāpēk*. The preposition *bə-* is one of the
  most elastic in Hebrew. Read as *instrumental*, the clause licenses human execution: his blood is
  shed *by* a human agent, which is how the Septuagint, the Vulgate, the Targums, and effectively
  every modern version take it, and how the Torah's later homicide legislation reads it. Read as
  *beth pretii*, the preposition of price, it means *in exchange for the man* — a life for a life,
  with the agent unspecified and possibly God himself, which fits 9:5's threefold *"I will require"*
  and would make the verse a statement of principle rather than a grant of authority. A third
  proposal takes *bāʾādām* as *"among mankind"*. The instrumental reading is much the strongest and
  is not seriously in doubt; the point of recording the alternatives is that the amount of weight
  Genesis 9:6 has borne in the capital-punishment debate is greater than a single ambiguous
  preposition can carry alone. See [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]].

- **What kind of covenant this is, and why the category matters.** Ancient Near Eastern treaty forms
  divide roughly into the *suzerain–vassal* type — a great king imposes stipulations on a lesser one,
  with blessings for compliance and curses for breach, the form Deuteronomy is built on — and the
  *royal grant* type, in which a king rewards a servant with an unconditional gift binding on
  himself and his heirs. Genesis 9 is neither, quite, but it is far closer to the second, and it is
  more unconditional than any attested grant, because a grant is at least *given for* something.
  Here nothing has been done to earn it: 8:21 rules out human merit as the ground in the same breath
  that states the promise. The absence of stipulations is not an oversight to be filled in from the
  Noahide tradition — it is the passage's most distinctive formal feature, and it is what makes this
  the one covenant in Scripture that cannot be broken from the human side, because there is nothing
  on the human side to break. Isaiah 24:5 nonetheless says the everlasting covenant *has* been
  broken, which is either a different covenant or a prophet pressing 9:4–6 into the role of
  stipulations after all; the wiki notes the tension rather than resolving it. See [[Covenant]].

- **The reception history of 9:6, in one paragraph, because it is unavoidable.** No verse in Genesis
  has done more work in public argument. It is the standard scriptural warrant for capital
  punishment, cited as such from the rabbis through Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, and the framers of
  colonial capital codes, and it is still the text at the centre of the debate — the usual argument
  being that because 9:6 predates and stands outside the Mosaic law, its abrogation cannot be argued
  from the abrogation of that law. It has been read the opposite way with equal seriousness: as
  descriptive rather than prescriptive (this is what happens to those who shed blood — compare
  Matthew 26:52, which uses the same form to *forbid* violence), or as a principle whose grounding
  clause undercuts its application, since the murderer is also made in God's image. Both readings
  have to reckon with the fact that the verse gives its reason, and the reason is about the victim.
  The wiki records the debate and takes no side in it; what belongs to the exegesis rather than to
  the ethics is that 9:6 is the first delegation of lethal judicial authority in Scripture, that it
  is framed as a limit as much as a licence, and that it is spoken to the whole human race rather
  than to a nation.
