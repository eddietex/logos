# Abram and Lot Separate

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 13:1–18

## Summary

The land cannot carry them both, so the man who holds the deed to it gives the choice away.

Genesis 13 is built out of one verb. *Nāśāʾ* means **to lift, to carry, to bear**, and it turns up
three times in eighteen verses at exactly the three hinges of the story: the land *cannot bear*
them (13:6), Lot *lifts* his eyes (13:10), and Yahweh tells Abram to *lift* his eyes (13:14). A land
that will not carry two households produces two liftings of the eyes, one self-appointed and one
commanded, and the whole rest of the Abraham cycle follows from which man did which.

**The chapter opens by undoing chapter 12's descent.** *"Abram went up out of Egypt"* (13:1) —
*wayyaʿal*, *he went up*, answering the *wayyēred*, *he went down*, of 12:10. The Exodus shape that
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]] traces closes here: famine, descent,
plagues, expulsion, and now the going up, with the plunder. *"Abram was very rich in livestock, in
silver, and in gold"* (13:2) is *kābēd meʾōd*, literally **very heavy** — the root behind *kābôd*,
glory, and the same root Exodus uses for the hardening of Pharaoh's heart and for the weight of God's
presence. Genesis says he was *heavy*, and the next four verses are about what the weight does.

**Then he retraces his own route in reverse and comes back to the altar.** *"He went on his journeys
from the South even to Bethel, to the place where his tent had been at the beginning, between Bethel
and Ai, to the place of the altar, which he had made there at the first. There Abram called on
Yahweh's name"* (13:3–4). Three times in two verses the text insists on *the place* — *hammāqôm*,
the word that will become Israel's technical term for a sanctuary — and twice on *at the beginning /
at the first*. In Egypt he built nothing and called on no one; here he goes back to the last spot
where he had, and does it again. This is the only altar in Genesis a patriarch is said to return to.
See [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].

**The wealth that came out of Egypt is what breaks the household.** *"The land was not able to bear
them, that they might live together; for their substance was great, so that they could not live
together"* (13:6). The blessing of 12:2 arrived as a bride-price in 12:16, hardened into *very heavy*
in 13:2, and now it is too much for the country to hold. The land of promise has now failed the
promise-bearer twice in four verses — it starved him out at 12:10 and it cannot carry him at 13:6 —
and Genesis draws no line between either failure and any fault of his. See [[Land and Sojourning]].

**And the quarrel happens in front of the owners.** *"The Canaanites and the Perizzites lived in the
land at that time"* (13:7). The note is dropped in without comment at the exact moment the two
Hebrew households are shouting at each other over water and grazing, and it does three things: it
concedes that the promised land is fully occupied, it makes the strife a public embarrassment in
front of the people who hold it, and — because 13:7's second name is not in the Table of Nations —
it quietly widens the list of who is there. Genesis 10:15–18 gives Canaan eleven descendants and no
Perizzites; 13:7 supplies them anyway, and 15:20 will put them in the land grant. The wiki notes
this as an unexplained addition, not a contradiction: the Table's list is genealogical and 13:7's is
residential, and nothing requires them to match.

### Abram's speech, and what he gives up in it

> **8** *"Please, let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herdsmen and my
> herdsmen; for we are relatives.*
> **9** *Isn't the whole land before you? Please separate yourself from me. If you go to the left
> hand, then I will go to the right. Or if you go to the right hand, then I will go to the left."*

Four things are happening in two verses.

**He calls his nephew his brother.** *Kî ʾănāšîm ʾaḥîm ʾănāḥnû* — literally *for we are men,
brothers*. Lot is his brother's son (11:27), and Abram uses the word Genesis has so far spent on
[[Cain]] and [[Abel]] (4:2–11, where *brother* occurs seven times and ends in a corpse), on Shem,
Ham, and Japheth (9:22–25), and on Terah's sons. This is the Bible's first quarrel between kin that
somebody deliberately stops, and the word Abram reaches for to stop it is the word that failed in
chapter 4.

**He addresses the herdsmen's fight as his own.** The strife in 13:7 is between the *herdsmen*;
Abram's first clause makes it *"between you and me"*. He does not blame the staff.

**He surrenders two rights at once.** He is the elder, and the elder divides or chooses in any
ordinary settlement; he is also the one to whom the entire land has been promised (12:7), which
makes the offer *"isn't the whole land before you?"* a giveaway of something that is, by the terms
of chapter 12, not Lot's to receive. Abram proposes the standard equitable division — one party
splits, the other picks — and then hands his nephew the splitting *and* the picking.

**And the offer is geographically empty.** *If you go to the left hand … the right hand* is the
Hebrew orientation, facing east: left is north, right is south. Abram names the two directions that
keep a man in the hill country, and Lot takes neither — he goes east, off the map Abram offered, out
of the land entirely by the reckoning of 13:12. See [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

### Two liftings of the eyes

**Lot lifts his own.** *"Lot lifted up his eyes, and saw all the plain of the Jordan, that it was
well-watered everywhere, before Yahweh destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, like the garden of Yahweh, like
the land of Egypt, as you go to Zoar"* (13:10). It is one of the densest verses in Genesis:

- **He lifted up his eyes, and saw.** The Genesis formula for a disastrous look. *"The woman saw
  that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes"* (3:6); *"God's sons saw
  that men's daughters were beautiful"* (6:2). In each case the seeing is accurate and the
  taking is the problem.
- **Two similes, and both of them are places you cannot stay.** *Like the garden of Yahweh* — Eden,
  which humanity was driven out of with a sword at the gate (3:24). *Like the land of Egypt* — which
  Abram was escorted out of eleven verses ago (12:20). Lot is looking at the best land in sight and
  the narrator describes it with the Bible's two great expulsions.
- **The narrator tells the reader the ending before Lot chooses.** *Before Yahweh destroyed Sodom and
  Gomorrah* is an aside to us, not information available to Lot, and it converts the whole verse from
  a description into an irony. Compare 13:13, which does the same thing again from the other side.
- **The last words are a road sign.** *As you go to Zoar* is the guidebook idiom of Genesis 10:19,
  and Zoar is the one city of the plain that will survive chapter 19 because Lot begs for it
  (19:20–22). The verse that begins his descent into Sodom ends with the name of the place he will
  crawl out to.

*"So Lot chose the Plain of the Jordan for himself. Lot traveled east, and they separated themselves
the one from the other"* (13:11). **East is the wrong direction in Genesis.** The cherubim are
posted *"at the east of the garden of Eden"* (3:24); Cain settles *"in the land of Nod, east of
Eden"* (4:16); the tower-builders travel *miqqedem* into Shinar (11:2). Every eastward move in the
book so far is a move away from where God is, and Genesis marks Lot's with the same word.

*"Abram lived in the land of Canaan, and Lot lived in the cities of the plain, and moved his tent as
far as Sodom"* (13:12). The sentence draws a border the offer of 13:9 never mentioned: Abram is *in
the land of Canaan* and Lot is not. And the verb of Lot's move is *wayeʾĕhal*, a denominative from
*ʾōhel*, **tent** — *he tented as far as Sodom*. He is still a tent-dweller here, aimed at a city;
by 19:1 he is *"sitting in the gate of Sodom"*, which is where a city's elders sit.

*"Now the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners against Yahweh"* (13:13). The narrator's
flattest moral verdict since the flood (6:5, 11–12), placed **after** the choice rather than before
it. Lot chose on the evidence of irrigation; the reader is handed the other half of the file one
verse too late to do anything with it.

**Then Yahweh lifts Abram's.** *"Yahweh said to Abram, after Lot was separated from him, 'Now, lift
up your eyes, and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and
westward'"* (13:14). This is the first divine speech since 12:7 — nothing was said before the
famine, in Egypt, or during the quarrel — and its timing is stated as a clause: *after Lot was
separated from him*. The text ties the enlargement of the promise to the loss of the only heir Abram
had.

The differences between the two liftings are the point of the chapter:

| | Lot, 13:10–11 | Abram, 13:14–17 |
|---|---|---|
| Who initiates | *Lot lifted up his eyes* | *Yahweh said … lift up your eyes* |
| How much he sees | one plain, *as you go to Zoar* | *northward and southward and eastward and westward* |
| The basis | *well-watered everywhere* | *I will give it to you* |
| What he does next | *chose … for himself* | *arise, walk through the land* |
| What he gets | the cities of the plain, then 14:12 and 19:1 | the land, *to your offspring forever* |
| Where he ends | a cave above Zoar with two daughters (19:30) | an altar at Mamre, and a grave at Machpelah (23:19) |

See [[Lift Up Your Eyes - Two Ways of Seeing the Land]].

### What the promise gains here

Yahweh's speech at 13:14–17 is the second land promise, and it is not a repetition. Set against
12:7 — *"I will give this land to your offspring"* — every clause has moved:

- **To him, and not only to his offspring.** *"For all the land which you see, I will give to you,
  and to your offspring"* (13:15). Chapter 12 promised the land past him to a generation he did not
  have. Hebrews 11:9 and Acts 7:5 both notice that he never possessed a foot of it, and Genesis 23
  will have him buy a burial cave at full price from a Hittite.
- **Forever.** *ʿAd-ʿôlām*, the first appearance of the word in the promises, and the term the
  covenant chapters will keep (17:8, 48:4).
- **Offspring like the dust of the earth** (13:16) — the first of the three great similes. Dust here,
  stars at 15:5, stars and sand at 22:17. Dust is what the man was made of (2:7) and what he returns
  to (3:19), and it is the material of the curse; the promise takes the word for human mortality and
  makes it a number nobody can count. See
  [[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]].
- **A command to walk it.** *"Arise, walk through the land in its length and in its width; for I will
  give it to you"* (13:17). Walking a property's boundaries is how possession is taken and claims are
  publicly asserted in the ancient Near East and in later Jewish law alike (*ḥăzāqâ*). Abram is told
  to perform the legal act of taking title to a country he will die a resident alien in.

**And he answers with a move and an altar.** *"Abram moved his tent, and came and lived by the oaks
of Mamre, which are in Hebron, and built an altar there to Yahweh"* (13:18). His third altar
([[Shechem]] 12:7, between [[Bethel]] and [[Ai]] 12:8, now [[Mamre (place)]]), and the one he stays at:
Hebron is his address for the rest of his life, the site of the three visitors (18:1), the field and
cave he buys for Sarah (23:17–20), and the grave of four of the six patriarchs and matriarchs.
Lot chose the best-watered land in sight; Abram was given all of it and pitched a tent under some
trees in the hills.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *Abram*, and this is the first pericope in which he does something admirable. He
  comes back up out of Egypt to the altar he abandoned and calls on the name again; he stops a family
  quarrel by naming himself and Lot brothers; he gives away the choice of a land that is legally his
  by promise; and when God enlarges the promise he answers by walking and building rather than
  speaking. He says nothing at all to God in this chapter — no thanks, no question, no vow — which is
  consistent with everything before it: he is still a man Genesis narrates entirely from the outside.
- [[Lot]] — who lifts his own eyes, chooses for himself, travels east, and pitches toward a city the
  narrator has just told us is doomed. Genesis does not call the choice a sin, and it is worth being
  precise about that: he was invited to choose, he chose the visibly better ground, and any herdsman
  would have. What the chapter does instead is arrange the consequences in a line — the plain, then
  the cities of the plain, then *as far as Sodom*, then the gate of Sodom (19:1) — and let the reader
  watch a tent drift toward a city over six chapters. See [[City and Civilization]].
- [[God]] — *Yahweh*, silent through the famine, Egypt, the return, and the quarrel, and speaking
  only once the household has split. His speech gives more than 12:7 did in every dimension: to Abram
  himself, forever, in four directions, with an innumerable offspring and a command to walk the
  ground. He is also the subject of the narrator's aside at 13:10 — *before Yahweh destroyed Sodom
  and Gomorrah* — which is the first time in Genesis that a judgment is announced by being remembered
  in advance.
- **The herdsmen of Abram and of Lot** — the only actual antagonists in the chapter, and they never
  speak. Their quarrel is over the two things that decide whether pastoralists can share a range:
  water and grazing. Isaac's herdsmen will have the same fight with Gerar's over wells (26:20), and
  Jacob's arrival at a well guarded against exactly this dispute (29:2–8).
- **The Canaanites and the Perizzites** — the land's actual occupants, named in one clause and given
  nothing to do. The Canaanites descend from [[Canaan (son of Ham)]] and are already in the wiki via
  [[The Table of Nations]]; the Perizzites are not in that chapter's genealogy at all, and enter
  Scripture here. Their name may not be an ethnic one — it is plausibly related to *pərāzôt*,
  *unwalled settlements* (Ezekiel 38:11; Zechariah 2:4), which would make them the country people
  rather than a tribe. Neither gets a page yet; both are due one when the conquest lists start doing
  work (15:19–21).
- **The men of Sodom** — introduced only to be condemned: *"exceedingly wicked and sinners against
  Yahweh"* (13:13). No individual among them is named until 19:1. See [[Sodom]] and [[Sin]].

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — behind him now, and the source of the wealth that causes the whole chapter. The going
  up of 13:1 completes the descent-and-return arc of 12:10–20.
- [[The Negev]] — *the South* in the WEB, crossed in both directions: the last stage of the descent
  (12:9) and the first of the return (13:1, 13:3).
- [[Bethel]] and [[Ai]] — the ridge camp of 12:8, revisited, and the site of the altar he comes back
  to. Two towns and a tent between them; the return to it is the pericope's quiet act of repentance,
  performed without a word of confession.
- [[Canaan]] — the land that cannot bear both households, and the land 13:12 uses to draw the line
  between the two men: *"Abram lived in the land of Canaan, and Lot lived in the cities of the
  plain."*
- [[The Plain of the Jordan]] — the *kikkar*, the **disk** or **round** of the Jordan: the deep rift
  valley south of the river, which Lot sees as well-watered everywhere and which the narrator sees as
  a place about to be destroyed.
- [[Sodom]] and [[Gomorrah]] — named together, as they will be for the rest of the Bible. They were
  already on the map as border markers in [[The Table of Nations]] (10:19); here they acquire a moral
  character, six chapters before anything happens to them.
- **Zoar** — *"as you go to Zoar"*, the road marker at the end of 13:10 and the smallest of the five
  cities of the plain. It has no page yet; it earns one at 19:18–23, where Lot bargains for it and it
  is spared because he asks.
- [[Hebron]] — the hill town, some nineteen miles south of Jerusalem, that becomes Abram's permanent
  address from this verse on.
- [[Mamre (place)]] — the oak grove at Hebron where he pitches and builds his third altar; the site of 18:1
  and the landmark that fixes the field of Machpelah in 23:17.

## Themes

- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the land is promised to him *and to his offspring forever* (13:15) in the
  same chapter in which it proves unable to feed two of his households, and the man it is given to
  responds by moving his tent. Genesis 13 is the theme's clearest statement: total title, zero
  possession.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the Bible's first quarrel that anybody stops, and the terms on which
  it is stopped: the senior party names the kinship, takes the dispute on himself, and gives away his
  own advantage.
- [[Blessing]] — the wealth of 12:2 has become a problem. Genesis reports the blessing as *heavy*
  (13:2), makes it the direct cause of the strife (13:6), and never suggests it should be given back.
- [[Covenant]] — the land grant expands: to Abram himself, in four directions, forever, with
  innumerable offspring and a command to take symbolic possession by walking. Everything chapter 15
  ratifies with fire and chapter 17 seals with circumcision is stated here first.
- [[Call and Election]] — enlarged, unconditionally, immediately after Abram gives away his claim.
  Nothing in 13:14–17 is offered as a reward, and Genesis never says the generosity earned it.
- [[City and Civilization]] — the chapter that sets Abram's tent against Lot's cities. Abram lives in
  a tent for the rest of Genesis; Lot moves from the plain, to the cities of the plain, to Sodom's
  gate. Hebrews 11:9–10 reads the whole contrast as deliberate.
- [[Judgment]] — announced twice by anticipation and executed nowhere: *before Yahweh destroyed Sodom
  and Gomorrah* (13:10), and *"the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked"* (13:13). This is the first
  time Genesis tells the reader what is coming instead of narrating it.
- [[Sin]] — 13:13 uses both of the Bible's blunt words at once, *rāʿîm wəḥaṭṭāʾîm*, **wicked and
  sinners**, and adds *lə-Yhwh*, *against Yahweh* — the specification that makes it more than social
  wrongdoing.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — two altars in one chapter, one revisited (13:4) and one new (13:18),
  in a period with no priesthood, no tabernacle, and no commanded sacrifice. See
  [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- [[Favor and Grace]] — the promise is enlarged after a famine, a lie in Egypt, a bride-price, and a
  family quarrel, with none of the four mentioned.

## Notes

- **The pun the chapter is built on.** *Nāśāʾ* carries and *nāśāʾ* lifts. *"The land was not able to
  **bear** them"* (13:6, *lōʾ nāśāʾ ʾōtām hāʾāreṣ*) — *"Lot **lifted up** his eyes"* (13:10,
  *wayyiśśāʾ Lôṭ ʾet-ʿênāyw*) — *"**lift up** your eyes"* (13:14, *śāʾ nāʾ ʿênêkā*). A land that
  cannot lift two men makes each of them lift his own eyes, and the chapter's whole argument is about
  the difference between the two liftings. No English version can carry all three with one word, and
  the WEB, like most, does not try.
- **Was Lot's choice wrong?** Genesis says only that he chose, and the narrator's asides (13:10b,
  13:13) are addressed to the reader rather than to him. Three readings are standard. The
  **moralizing** one, common from the church fathers onward, treats the eye-lifting of 13:10 as a
  deliberate echo of Eve's at 3:6 and reads the whole trajectory to 19:30 as the wages of choosing by
  sight. The **realist** one points out that Lot was invited to choose, that the plain really was the
  better grazing, that no character in the chapter knows what the narrator knows, and that Genesis
  never rebukes him. The **structural** one notes that the separation is required — the promise
  cannot pass to a nephew, and 13:14 begins *after Lot was separated from him* — so the chapter's
  function is to clear the field of the wrong heir, and Lot's motives are beside its point. The wiki
  records all three. What is not disputable is that the text arranges his stopping-places in a line
  that ends inside the city.
- **The New Testament calls him righteous, and Genesis does not.** 2 Peter 2:7–8 has *"righteous
  Lot"*, *"tormented in his righteous soul from day to day with seeing and hearing lawless deeds"* —
  a strikingly generous reading of a man Genesis shows choosing well-watered ground, sitting in
  Sodom's gate, offering his daughters to a mob (19:8), lingering until angels drag him out (19:16),
  and bargaining down his own rescue (19:20). This wiki flags the tension rather than resolving it:
  Genesis narrates without a verdict, 2 Peter supplies one, and both are canonical. See [[Lot]].
- **Sodom's sin, and what the Bible says it was.** Genesis 13:13 gives the charge with no content,
  Genesis 18:20 speaks of an outcry, and Genesis 19 supplies the scene the city's name became a word
  for in English. The Old Testament's own most explicit definition is Ezekiel 16:49–50 — *pride,
  fullness of bread, and prosperous ease*, and *"she didn't strengthen the hand of the poor and
  needy"*, followed by *"they were haughty and did abomination before me"* — and Isaiah 1:10–17,

  independent of the answer: the well-watered plain that Lot chooses for its abundance is destroyed,
  on Ezekiel's account, for what the abundance did to the people living on it.
- **The archaeology and geology of the plain.** The *kikkar* is part of the Jordan Rift, one of the
  lowest and most seismically active places on the land surface of the earth, sitting on the
  Dead Sea Transform with bitumen seeps (14:10 has *"tar pits"*) and, at the southern end, salt domes
  at Mount Sodom. Attempts to locate the five cities have gone two ways: a southern theory placing
  them under the shallow water south of the Lisan peninsula, and a northern one identifying
  Tall el-Hammam on the eastern plain north of the Dead Sea, whose excavators have argued for a
  violent Middle Bronze destruction. Both are contested, and the northern site's proposed cause — a
  cosmic airburst — was published in 2021 and has drawn substantial methodological criticism. The
  wiki records the debate and endorses no identification. What is uncontested is that the region is a
  place where the ground genuinely does burn, subside, and salt over.
- **Where the herdsmen's quarrel comes from.** The strife is a direct consequence of Egypt: the
  bride-price of 12:16 becomes the *very heavy* wealth of 13:2, which is what the land cannot carry
  at 13:6. Genesis never draws the line, but it lays the three verses close enough together that the
  chain is hard to miss — the household is split by the profit of the lie. It is one of the clearest
  examples in the book of consequence narrated without comment.
- **The four directions, and who else gets them.** *Northward and southward and eastward and
  westward* (13:14) recurs almost verbatim to Jacob at Bethel (28:14) and to Moses on Nebo
  (Deuteronomy 34:1–3, in the order north, south, west, east). Two of the three are told to look at a
  land they will not hold in their lifetime; the third is Jacob, on his way out of it. The formula is
  always spoken to a man who is leaving or has nothing.
- **Abram's silence continues.** He speaks once in this chapter, to Lot, and not at all to God. God
  makes him the largest promise yet and gets no reply — the same shape as 12:1–4, where the command
  gets a verb instead of an answer. His first recorded words to God are still two chapters away, at
  15:2, and when they come they are a complaint about having no heir: *"Lord Yahweh, what will you
  give me, since I go childless, and he who will inherit my estate is Eliezer of Damascus?"* — which
  is exactly what 13:11 cost him.
