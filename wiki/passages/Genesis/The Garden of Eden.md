# The Garden of Eden

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 2:4–25

## Summary

The camera drops from the cosmos to a patch of ground. Where [[The Creation of the World]] moved
across six days and the whole created order, this account holds still in one place and follows
one man, and it tells the story in a different order and a different register.

It opens on a dry world: no rain, no plant of the field, and — the reason given — *"not a man to
till the ground."* God forms the man from the dust of that ground and breathes into his nostrils,
and the man becomes a living being. Then God plants a garden eastward in Eden and puts the man in
it, grows every tree that is beautiful and edible, and sets two particular trees in the middle:
the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. A river waters the garden and
divides into four.

The man is put in the garden *"to cultivate and keep it"* and given one command in two halves —
a wide permission (*every* tree) and a single prohibition (not that one), with death attached.

Then the first *"not good"* in the Bible: *"It is not good for the man to be alone."* God's
answer comes in two stages. First he forms the animals and brings them to the man to name — an
act of dominion that is also a search, and the search fails: *"for man there was not found a
helper comparable to him."* Only then does God put the man to sleep, take from his side, and
build a woman. The man's response is the first human speech in Scripture, and it is poetry:
*"This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh."* The narrator steps forward to draw the
consequence — a man leaves his parents, joins his wife, and the two become one flesh — and the
scene closes on a line whose calm is about to break: they were naked, and not ashamed.

**How it differs from Genesis 1**, laid side by side:

| | Genesis 1:1–2:3 | Genesis 2:4–25 |
|---|---|---|
| Divine name | Elohim ("God") | Yahweh Elohim ("Yahweh God") |
| Starting state | too much water — dark, watery deep | too little — dry ground, no rain |
| Order | vegetation → animals → humanity | man → garden → animals → woman |
| Humanity | male and female together, last | man first, woman last, in two acts |
| Scale | cosmic, ordered, liturgical | local, earthy, narrative |
| God pictured as | sovereign who speaks | potter, gardener, surgeon, matchmaker |

See [[Two Creation Accounts - Genesis 1 and Genesis 2]] for how the two relate.

## Key verses

> **4** This is the history of the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were
> created, in the day that Yahweh God made the earth and the heavens.

> **7** Yahweh God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the
> breath of life; and man became a living soul.

> **9** Out of the ground Yahweh God made every tree to grow that is pleasant to the sight, and
> good for food, including the tree of life in the middle of the garden and the tree of the
> knowledge of good and evil.

> **15** Yahweh God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it.

> **16–17** Yahweh God commanded the man, saying, “You may freely eat of every tree of the
> garden; but you shall not eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; for in the day
> that you eat of it, you will surely die.”

> **18** Yahweh God said, “It is not good for the man to be alone. I will make him a helper
> comparable to him.”

> **23** The man said, “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. She will be called
> ‘woman,’ because she was taken out of Man.”

> **24** Therefore a man will leave his father and his mother, and will join with his wife, and
> they will be one flesh.

> **25** The man and his wife were both naked, and they were not ashamed.

## People

- [[God]] — here **Yahweh God** for the first time, and portrayed with his hands in the material:
  he forms, breathes, plants, takes, puts, commands, builds, and brings.
- [[Adam]] — formed from dust, animated by God's breath, given a garden, a job, a command, and
  finally a counterpart. Not yet a proper name in this passage: the Hebrew reads *hā'ādām*,
  "the man".
- [[Eve]] — built from the man's side and brought to him; unnamed here (she is *ishshah*,
  "woman"), and named Eve only after the fall, at 3:20.

## Places

- [[Eden]] — the region; the garden is planted *"eastward, in Eden"*, watered by a river that
  divides into four. Two of the four (the Hiddekel/Tigris and the Euphrates) are known rivers;
  Pishon and Gihon are not identifiable, and the lands they name — Havilah, Cush — get their
  pages when Genesis 10 arrives.

## Themes

- [[Creation]] — the second account, at ground level: creation as forming, planting, and
  building rather than speaking.
- [[Work]] — labour appears before any disobedience. The man is placed in the garden *to
  cultivate and keep it*; work is a commission, not a punishment.
- [[Marriage]] — 2:24 is the Bible's foundational statement on marriage, and the verse the New
  Testament reaches for whenever the subject comes up.
- [[Life and Death]] — the tree of life stands unguarded and unforbidden; death enters the story
  only as the stated consequence of one prohibited act.
- [[Image of God]] — the phrase is absent here, but the man's naming of the animals enacts the
  dominion Genesis 1:28 granted.

## Notes

- **The divine name changes at 2:4.** Genesis 1 uses *Elohim* forty-odd times and never *YHWH*;
  from 2:4 through the end of chapter 3 the text says *YHWH Elohim* — "Yahweh God" — some twenty
  times, the generic word for God bolted to the personal covenant name. This shift, along with
  the different order of creation, is the classic starting point of Pentateuchal source criticism
  (the "P" account in chapter 1, the "J" account here). Whatever its origin, the canonical effect
  is to identify the creator of the cosmos with the God who walks in a garden and speaks to a man.
- **"This is the history of the generations of…"** (2:4) is the first of the ten *toledot*
  formulas that segment Genesis. Every other one introduces a person's descendants; this one is
  applied to *"the heavens and the earth"*, which reads their sequel — the human story — as their
  offspring.
- **Adam and adamah.** *"Yahweh God formed man (ʾādām) from the dust of the ground (ʾădāmâ)"* —
  the man is named for the soil he comes from. English can only approximate it: *human* from
  *humus*, *earthling* from *earth*. The pun returns in 3:19 (*"to dust you shall return"*) and
  in 3:23 (sent out to till the ground he was taken from).
- **"A living soul" is not a soul added to a body.** The Hebrew *nephesh ḥayyāh* is the same
  phrase Genesis 1:20–24 uses of fish, birds, and land animals, where WEB translates it "living
  creature". The man does not *receive* a soul; he *becomes* a living being when breath enters
  dust. What distinguishes him here is the source of that breath — breathed personally, nostril
  to nostril — not a separate component.
- **Cultivate and keep** (2:15) translates *ʿābad* and *šāmar*. That exact word pair is later
  used of the Levites' service and guarding of the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7–8, 8:26, 18:5–7),
  which is one of several threads — the eastward entrance, the cherubim posted at it in 3:24, the
  gold and onyx of 2:12, the lampstand shaped like a tree — that lead many readers to see the
  garden as the first sanctuary and the man as its priest-gardener.
- **"The knowledge of good and evil"** is disputed. Candidates: moral discernment (but the man is
  already held responsible for obeying); sexual awareness (but the phrase is used of children too
  young to choose, Deuteronomy 1:39); a merism for *everything*, "good and evil" standing for the
  whole range, hence omniscience; or — the reading that fits chapter 3 best — the prerogative of
  *deciding* good and evil for oneself, moral autonomy, which is exactly what the serpent offers
  and what "you will be like God" (3:5) means.
- **"You will surely die"** is *môt tāmût*, an infinitive absolute doubling the verb for
  emphasis: "dying you shall die". They eat, and do not drop dead that day. The main readings:
  the sentence is death entering as a certainty rather than an instant (they are barred from the
  tree of life in 3:22–24 and do eventually die); the death is relational and spiritual, immediate
  in kind if not in body; or God commutes the sentence in chapter 3 — mercy, not error. The text
  itself simply lets the tension stand.
- **"It is not good"** (2:18) is the first negative verdict in the Bible, and it lands inside a
  creation that Genesis 1 has just called *very good*. Whatever else the verse does, it makes
  human aloneness a defect in an otherwise finished world.
- **"A helper comparable to him"** renders *ʿēzer kenegdô*. *ʿēzer* carries no hint of
  subordination in Hebrew — of its roughly twenty other occurrences most describe **God** as
  Israel's helper (Psalm 33:20, 121:2, Deuteronomy 33:7). *Kenegdô* is literally "like
  opposite him": a counterpart, a match, one who corresponds. "Helpmeet", the KJV's "help meet
  for him" fused into a noun, has obscured this for four centuries.
- **The rib is more likely a side.** *Ṣēlāʿ* appears about forty times elsewhere in the Hebrew
  Bible and never means "rib" — it means the side of a building, a hillside, or (most often) the
  side of the tabernacle and the ark. And the verb in 2:22 is *bānâ*, "built", used of
  construction. The picture is architectural: God takes a side of the man and builds it into a
  woman, which is precisely why the man can say *"bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh."*
- **The first human words are a poem.** 2:23 falls into Hebrew verse, with a wordplay the English
  preserves better than most: *ʾishshâ* ("woman") taken out of *ʾîsh* ("man"). Note that the man
  does not *name* her here as he named the animals — he recognises her, in the same breath he
  identifies himself. The naming comes in 3:20, after everything has changed.
- **"Therefore a man will leave his father and his mother"** (2:24) is the narrator speaking to
  the reader, not part of the scene — the first such aside in Scripture. In a patrilineal,
  patrilocal society where a wife joined her husband's household, the verse says the opposite: the
  *man* leaves. Whether this is describing a social custom, prescribing one, or making a
  theological point about the priority of the marriage bond over the parental one has been argued
  in every direction; the New Testament consistently takes it as prescriptive.
- **Naked** (2:25) is *ʿărummîm*; **crafty**, the serpent's characteristic in 3:1, is *ʿārûm*.
  The pun is the hinge between the chapters: their unashamed openness meets a shrewdness that
  will make them cover up.
