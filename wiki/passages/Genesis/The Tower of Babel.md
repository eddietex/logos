# The Tower of Babel

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 11:1–9

## Summary

Nine verses, no names, and the end of the primeval history. A migrating people find a plain, invent a
building material, and resolve to put up a city and a tower — *"and let's make a name for
ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of the whole earth."* God comes down to look,
says that with one language nothing they intend will be beyond them, confuses their speech, and
scatters them. They stop building. The city is called Babel.

**Nobody in it is named.** This is the only narrative in Genesis without a single named human being,
and it comes immediately after a chapter that is seventy names and almost nothing else. The subject
throughout is *they* — a plural verb with no antecedent — and the one label the text supplies is
*bənê hāʾādām*, **the children of men** (11:5), which is a description of the species, not of a
group. Every attempt to say who these people were, [[Nimrod]] included, is supplied from outside
the passage.

**God is not said to be angry, and no wrong is named.** There is no *evil*, no *sin*, no *wickedness*
in these nine verses — the vocabulary Genesis used freely at 6:5 and 6:11 is entirely absent. God
states a fact (*"nothing will be withheld from them"*) and acts on it. Whatever the builders did
wrong, the text does not say, which is why the passage has carried so many different readings and why
this page sets them out rather than choosing one.

**It is built as a mirror.** The nine verses fold around 11:5, and the second half undoes the first
phrase by phrase:

| | |
|---|---|
| **1** one language, one speech | **9** the language of all the earth confused |
| **2** they lived *there* | **8** scattered abroad *from there* |
| **3** *"Come, let's make bricks"* | **7** *"Come, let's go down… and confuse"* |
| **4** *"let's build ourselves a city"* | **8** *"They stopped building the city"* |
| | **5 Yahweh came down to see the city and the tower** |

Two human *"Come, let's"* speeches, one divine *"Come, let's"* answering them, and the hinge is the
sentence in which God has to descend to inspect a tower whose top was in the sky.

### One language (11:1–2)

> **1** The whole earth was of one language and of one speech.
> **2** As they traveled from the east, they found a plain in the land of Shinar, and they lived
> there.

*Śāpâ ʾeḥāt ûdəbārîm ʾăḥādîm* — literally **one lip and one set of words**. The doubling is emphatic
and the vocabulary is concrete: *śāpâ* is the lip, the organ, used for language the way English uses
*tongue*. Nothing here says the one language was good, original, or God-given; the verse is a
description of a situation, and the situation is what makes the project possible at 11:6.

**This is the flat contradiction with the chapter before**, where the nations divide *"everyone after
his language"* three times over (10:5, 20, 31). [[The Table of Nations]] sets out the three standard
accounts — achronological arrangement, source seam, or a narrower scope for *"the whole earth"* —
and the wiki does not adjudicate between them.

*Miqqedem*, **from the east**, can also be read *eastward* or *in the east*, and the ambiguity is old.
Either way the word is loaded in Genesis: the garden was planted *miqqedem* (2:8), Cain settled east
of Eden (4:16), and Lot will choose the east (13:11). Movement east in this book is movement away.

**They found a plain.** *Biqʿâ* — a broad valley, and the first thing the passage says about the site
is that it is flat. A people who wanted height picked the one landscape that has none, which is why
they have to build.

### Brick and bitumen (11:3)

> **3** They said to one another, “Come, let’s make bricks, and burn them thoroughly.” They had brick
> for stone, and they used tar for mortar.

**The narrator stops to explain the technology, and the aside gives away where he is standing.**
*"They had brick for stone, and they used tar for mortar"* is addressed to somebody who builds with
quarried stone and lime — a highlander, a Levantine — and who would not otherwise know that in the
Mesopotamian alluvium there is no stone at all. Fired mud brick set in bitumen is exactly how
Babylonian monumental architecture was built, and the detail is accurate. It is also faintly
condescending: the greatest city in the world, made of mud.

**The Hebrew puns forward.** *Nilbənâ ləbēnîm*, *let us brick bricks*, shares its consonants with
*nābəlâ*, *let us confuse*, in 11:7 — l-b-n against n-b-l. The sentence in which God undoes the
project is the builders' own opening line with its letters turned around.

*Ḥēmār*, **tar** or bitumen, occurs three times in the Torah: here, in the tar pits the kings of
Sodom and Gomorrah fall into (14:10), and on the basket that carries the infant Moses (Exodus 2:3).

### The plan (11:4)

> **4** They said, “Come, let’s build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top reaches to the sky, and
> let’s make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of the whole earth.”

Four clauses, and it is worth taking them apart, because most of what is popularly believed about
this passage is read into one of them.

**A city and a tower.** *ʿÎr ûmigdāl*. The city is named first and is what the passage keeps
returning to — 11:5 has God look at *"the city and the tower"*, but 11:8 says only *"They stopped
building the city"*, and 11:9 names the city, not the tower. Popular memory has this story as *the
tower of Babel*; Genesis has it as a city with a tower in it. See [[City and Civilization]].

**Whose top reaches to the sky.** *Wərōʾšô baššāmayim* — literally *and its head in the heavens*, with
no verb of reaching in the Hebrew at all. **This is ordinary hyperbole for great height**, and the
Bible uses it elsewhere without any suggestion of assault: the spies report Canaanite *"cities great
and fortified up to the sky"* (Deuteronomy 1:28; 9:1). The passage never says the builders meant to
climb to God, invade heaven, or escape a second flood. Those are all later readings, some of them
very early, and none is in the text.

What the phrase does evoke is the **ziggurat**. Babylon's was called *Etemenanki*, *the house of the
foundation of heaven and earth*; ziggurats were mud-brick platform temples with a shrine on top, and
their names routinely claimed to join earth to heaven. A ziggurat is not a staircase for people going
up. It is a landing platform for a god coming down — which makes 11:5 a joke told at the building's
expense. See [[Babel]].

**Let's make a name for ourselves.** *Naʿăśeh-lānû šēm.* This is the clause the passage cares about,
and it is the one the rest of Genesis answers. *Šēm* is name, fame, reputation, monument. Two
occurrences frame it:

- Behind it, 6:4 — the pre-flood *gibbōrîm* were *ʾanšê haššēm*, **men of the name**, and the next
  verse is God seeing that human wickedness was great. See
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]].
- Ahead of it, 12:2 — *"I will make of you a great nation. I will bless you and make your name
  great."* The identical noun, and the difference is entirely in who is the subject of the verb.

That is the hinge of the primeval history into the patriarchal one, and it is made of one word. See
[[A Name for Ourselves and a Great Name]].

**Lest we be scattered.** *Pen-nāpûṣ.* The motive is fear, and the thing feared is dispersal —
which is to say, the thing God commanded. *"Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the earth"* was said
to Noah's sons twice (9:1, 9:7) and reported done at 10:32. The builders are trying to stop it. The
verb is *pûṣ*, and it is the verb of 9:19, where the earth was *"populated"* out of the three
brothers, and of 11:8 and 11:9, where God does to them precisely what they built a city to prevent.
See [[Blessing]].

### Yahweh came down (11:5)

> **5** Yahweh came down to see the city and the tower, which the children of men built.

**The hinge, and the funniest verse in Genesis.** The tower's head was in the heavens; God has to
descend to get a look at it. *Wayyēred* is a real motion, not a figure — the same descent he makes to
Sodom (18:21, *"I will go down now, and see"*), to Sinai (Exodus 19:20), and to the tent (Numbers
11:25). Genesis nowhere suggests God needed the trip; it stages one anyway, and the staging is the
comment.

*Bənê hāʾādām*, **the children of men**, is the passage's only name for the builders, and it stands
against *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, the sons of God, of 6:2. The last time Genesis put those two phrases near
each other, the result was the flood.

### The deliberation (11:6–7)

> **6** Yahweh said, “Behold, they are one people, and they have all one language, and this is what
> they begin to do. Now nothing will be withheld from them, which they intend to do.
> **7** Come, let’s go down, and there confuse their language, that they may not understand one
> another’s speech.”

**God's stated reason is capability, not offence.** *Lōʾ-yibbāṣēr mēhem kōl ʾăšer yāzəmû laʿăśôt* —
*nothing that they scheme to do will be cut off from them*. The verb *bāṣar* is what you do to a
fortified city: make it inaccessible. Nothing will be walled off from them. It is a statement about
what unlimited human coordination makes possible, and it is not obviously a compliment or an
accusation.

**Genesis has said this once before, in the same grammar.** At 3:22: *"Behold, the man has become
like one of us, knowing good and evil. Now, lest he reach out his hand, and also take of the tree of
life, and eat, and live forever—"* — a divine observation about a human capacity, breaking off
mid-sentence, followed by a preventive act. Both times the sentence is not finished and the action
is. See [[The Fall]].

*"this is what they begin to do"* — *hēḥillām laʿăśôt*, from *ḥālal*, the verb Genesis uses to open
an era: men began to call on Yahweh's name (4:26), men began to multiply (6:1), Noah began to be a
man of the ground (9:20), Nimrod began to be a mighty one (10:8). Something is starting here, and God
interrupts the start rather than the finish.

**"Come, let's go down"** — *hābâ nērədâ* — takes the builders' own imperative, *hābâ*, used twice by
them at 11:3 and 11:4, and turns it back. And it is a **first-person plural in the mouth of God**, the
third in Genesis after *"Let us make man in our image"* (1:26) and *"the man has become like one of
us"* (3:22). All three are readable as a plural of deliberation, as speech to a heavenly court, or as
something more; see [[Let Us Go Down - The Divine Plural in Genesis]].

*Wənābəlâ*, **and let us confuse** — from *bālal*, to mingle or confound. It is not the destruction of
language but the mixing of it.

### Scattered, and named (11:8–9)

> **8** So Yahweh scattered them abroad from there on the surface of all the earth. They stopped
> building the city.
> **9** Therefore its name was called Babel, because there Yahweh confused the language of all the
> earth. From there, Yahweh scattered them abroad on the surface of all the earth.

**They got the name and the scattering, in the wrong order and the wrong sense.** They built to make a
*šēm* and not be *pûṣ*; they are *pûṣ*, and the *šēm* they end up with is Babel. The passage answers
its own verse 4 clause by clause, which is the point of its mirrored shape.

**No one dies.** Set beside the flood two pericopes back, the judgment here is remarkably light:
confusion, dispersal, an abandoned building site. Nothing is destroyed, nobody is cursed, and there
is no oracle. Some readers take this as the mildest divine sentence in Genesis; others take the
scattering as itself the fulfilment of 9:1, in which case it is not straightforwardly a punishment at
all. The text supplies the act and withholds the label.

**The etymology is a pun and a polemic.** *Bābel* is derived from *bālal*, **to confuse**. This is not
philology: the Akkadian *bāb-ilim* means **gate of the god**, which is what Babylon's own inhabitants
understood their city's name to say. Genesis takes the gate of the god and calls it *babble*. It is
the same kind of naming Genesis does with [[Peleg]] (10:25) and will do with Isaac, Jacob, and
Israel — an etymology that is an interpretation. See [[Babel]].

## Key verses

> **1** The whole earth was of one language and of one speech.

> **4** They said, “Come, let’s build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top reaches to the sky, and

> **5** Yahweh came down to see the city and the tower, which the children of men built.

> **6** Yahweh said, “Behold, they are one people, and they have all one language, and this is what

> **7** Come, let’s go down, and there confuse their language, that they may not understand one

> **9** Therefore its name was called Babel, because there Yahweh confused the language of all the

## People

- [[God]] — **he acts, and he deliberates aloud, and he does neither in anger.** He comes down twice
  (once narrated at 11:5, once resolved at 11:7), inspects, states a fact about human capability,
  speaks in the first-person plural, confuses, and scatters. He passes no verdict, issues no curse,
  destroys nothing, and kills nobody. This is his first appearance since 9:17 — two and a half
  pericopes of silence, the longest in Genesis — and his next word is *"Now Yahweh said to Abram"*
  at 12:1.
- **The builders** — unnamed, uncounted, and grammatically a bare *they*. Genesis calls them only
  *"the children of men"* (11:5). They speak twice and are quoted both times; they are the only
  collective protagonist in the book. **No page**: there is no person here to make one for, and that
  is a feature of the passage rather than a gap in it.
- [[Nimrod]] — **not in this passage.** Genesis 11:1–9 names nobody, and his name appears nowhere in
  it. The tradition that he built the tower is old and nearly unanimous — Josephus, *Targum
  Pseudo-Jonathan*, *Genesis Rabbah*, Jerome, Dante — and it is an inference from 10:10, where Babel
  is *"the beginning of his kingdom"*, joined to the reading of *lipnê YHWH* as defiance. The wiki
  keeps the inference labelled as one. See
  [[Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria]].
- [[Peleg]] — named *division* at 10:25 because *"in his days the earth was divided"*, five
  generations from Noah. It is the Table of Nations' one hint that the scattering has a cause, and
  this is the pericope that supplies it — though the verbs differ (*pālag* there, *pûṣ* here) and
  Genesis never joins them explicitly.
- [[Shem]], [[Ham]], [[Japheth]], [[Noah]] — absent by name, present by implication: the builders are
  their descendants, and 11:10 picks Shem's line straight back up on the far side of the scattering.

## Places

- [[Shinar]] — the whole passage happens here, and it is the second time in two chapters that
  Scripture's picture of organized human ambition is set on this plain. 10:10 put the first kingdom
  here; 11:2 puts the first collective project here; Genesis 14:1 will put a king of Shinar in a
  coalition against Abram, and Daniel 1:2 will have the temple vessels carried here.
- [[Babel]] — **named for the second time and defined for the first.** 10:10 listed it as one of four
  cities; 11:9 gives it an etymology and a meaning, and from here the name runs the length of the
  canon to Revelation 18. The city that gets built in this passage is the city that never stops being
  built in the rest of the Bible. See [[Babel and Babylon the Great]].
- **The plain** — *biqʿâ*, a broad valley; unnamed beyond *"in the land of Shinar"*, and given no page.
- **Not mentioned: Eden, Ararat, Canaan.** The primeval history ends without returning to any of its
  earlier locations. The next place Genesis names is Ur of the Chaldees (11:28), and the movement of
  the book from that verse on is out of Mesopotamia and toward [[Canaan]].

## Themes

- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — **the theme this passage creates**, and the classical reading of it.
  *"let's make a name for ourselves"* is self-exaltation stated in the first person plural, and the
  prophets will put the same sentence in the mouth of the king of Babylon (Isaiah 14:13–14) and the
  prince of Tyre (Ezekiel 28:2). What is worth noting is that Genesis does not use the word: there is
  no *gāʾôn*, no *rûm*, no accusation of any kind. The pride is in the quoted speech, not in the
  narrator's comment.
- [[Language and Speech]] — **also new here.** One lip, and then many; *śāpâ* and *dābār* as the
  medium of everything the builders manage and the thing God adjusts to stop them. The Bible's
  interest in speech as power runs from this verse through the prophets to Pentecost and James 3.
- [[The Nations]] — the pericope that turns the seventy from a fact into an event. Genesis 10 reported
  a division; Genesis 11 narrates one, and makes it something God did.
- [[City and Civilization]] — the second city in Scripture and the first collective one. Cain built
  his alone and named it for his son (4:17); this one is built by everybody and named by God.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — one people, one language, one project, one site. The passage does not use
  political vocabulary, but the thing it describes is what an empire is for, and it is set in the
  capital 10:10 has already assigned to the first kingdom.
- [[Blessing]] — *"Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the earth"* (9:1, 9:7) is the command the
  builders are explicitly acting against: *"lest we be scattered abroad on the surface of the whole
  earth."* Genesis 10:32 said the command was carried out. Genesis 11 says people tried to stop it and
  God enforced it — which makes the scattering a blessing executed as a judgment, and the wiki does
  not resolve that.
- [[Judgment]] — the fourth divine judgment of the primeval history, after Eden, Cain, and the flood,
  and by a distance the mildest. God comes down to see before acting, exactly as at 3:8–9, 4:9–10, and
  18:21; nothing is destroyed and nobody dies.
- [[Work]] — human making at its most competent. The passage is the only place in Genesis that
  describes a manufacturing process, and it describes it approvingly enough to explain the technique.
- [[Sin]] — **conspicuously unnamed.** The word does not occur, nor does *evil*, nor *wickedness*, nor
  *violence*. Whatever the builders did, Genesis leaves the reader to supply the category, and the
  history of interpretation is the history of readers supplying different ones.

## Notes

- **What was wrong with it? Genesis does not say, and the readings are worth keeping distinct.** The
  passage names no sin, and every account below is an inference. They are not all compatible, and the
  wiki holds them rather than ranking them.

  1. **Pride.** *"let's make a name for ourselves"* is self-glorification, and the tower reaching to
     the sky is presumption. This is the oldest and most widespread reading, it fits 11:4 exactly, and
     it is how the prophets talk about Babylon. Against it: the narrator supplies no word of censure,
     and *wərōʾšô baššāmayim*, *its head in the heavens*, is ordinary idiom for tall.
  2. **Refusal to fill the earth.** The builders state their motive and it is *"lest we be scattered"*
     — a refusal of 9:1. On this reading God's act is not retribution but enforcement, and the
     scattering is the blessing being carried out over the objection of its recipients. This is the
     reading with the strongest textual anchor, since it is the only motive the builders themselves
     give.
  3. **Autonomy and self-security.** The city and the tower are an attempt to secure human existence
     without reference to God — the political form of Genesis 3's reach for the fruit. God is not
     mentioned in the builders' speech at all, which on this reading is the point.
  4. **Empire.** *"they are one people, and they have all one language"* (11:6) is what an imperial
     power imposes, and the setting is Babylon. On this reading the passage is about coerced
     uniformity rather than pride, God's act is
     the liberation of the peoples from a totalizing project, and the scattering is a mercy. The
     Assyrian and Babylonian annals' boast of ruling peoples *"of one speech"* is the background
     usually adduced.
  5. **Not a judgment at all.** A minority reading takes 11:6 at face value as a statement about
     limitless human capability and God's act as a protective limit set on it — of a piece with the
     tree of life barred at 3:22 and the hundred and twenty years at 6:3. Diversity of language is
     then a good, not a penalty.

  What can be said flatly is that readings 1 and 4 pull in opposite political directions and both are
  available in the text, and that reading 2 is the only one quoting the builders' stated reason.

- **The passage has an ancient-Near-Eastern setting, and one probable literary neighbour.** The
  ziggurat is the architecture: Babylon's *Etemenanki* was a mud-brick platform temple, rebuilt by
  Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar II, and Herodotus describes it (*Histories* 1.181). *Enuma Elish*
  has the gods themselves build Babylon and its temple Esagila, moulding bricks for a year — a
  foundation story in which the city is divine work, and which Genesis 11 inverts into mud and human
  scheming. And the Sumerian *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta* contains the "spell of Nudimmud", a
  passage about the whole world speaking to Enlil in one tongue and Enki changing the speech in their
  mouths. Whether it describes a lost unity or a promised future one is disputed by Sumerologists, and
  it is not a source for Genesis; it does show that the motif was current in the region.

  What Genesis does with the material is the point. Babylon's own literature makes the city the work
  of gods and the centre of the world; Genesis makes it an unfinished building site named after a
  speech defect.

- **The tower is not the story's subject, and Genesis 11:9 proves it.** *ʿÎr* occurs four times in
  nine verses, *migdāl* twice. The tower never appears after 11:5, the abandoned works at 11:8 are
  *the city*, and the name given at 11:9 is the city's. The popular title of the passage — this page's
  included — puts the emphasis where the text does not.

- **A flat contradiction with Genesis 10, recorded rather than harmonized.** 10:5, 20, and 31 have the
  nations already divided by language; 11:1 has the whole earth speaking one. Both statements are in
  the wiki, both are attributed, and the three standard accounts are set out on
  [[The Table of Nations]]. This ingest adds nothing to that except the observation that 11:1's
  *"the whole earth"* is exactly the phrase 10:32 has just finished using of the divided nations.

- **Source-critically this is J and chapter 10 is largely P**, which is the usual account of why the
  seam is visible. The passage's vocabulary (*Yahweh* throughout, the anthropomorphic descent, the
  folk etymology, the wordplay) is the Yahwist's signature, and it reads continuously with 2:4b–4:26,
  6:1–8, and 9:18–27.

- **Quotation provenance.** All Genesis text on this page is quoted verbatim from
  `raw/bible/Genesis.md` (WEB). Quotations from Daniel, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, Philippians, and
  elsewhere outside Genesis and Job cannot be checked against `raw/`, which holds only those two
  books; they are given as WEB and believed accurate but are unverified, and the shorter or less
  certain ones have been paraphrased rather than quoted. This caveat applies to every ingest so far
  and is repeated here because this passage's reception runs heavily outside Genesis.
