# The Brothers' First Journey to Egypt

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 42:1–38

## Summary

**The dream of 37:7 comes true in verse 6, and the man it comes true for responds by putting his
brothers in prison.**

Twenty-two years after they threw him in a pit, ten of Joseph's brothers bow to the ground in front of
him to buy grain. **He knows them at once. They do not know him at all.** And instead of the
recognition scene the reader has been waiting five chapters for, **he calls them spies, jails them for
three days, keeps one of them in chains, and sends the rest home with the purchase money hidden in
their luggage.**

**Genesis does not explain why**, and the chapter is built so that it cannot be explained simply. Every
reading that has ever been offered — revenge, testing, buying time to get at [[Benjamin]], an inability
to speak — has to survive verse 24, where **the man doing it turns around and weeps.**

### The chapter is Genesis 37 replayed with the parts exchanged

**Almost every cruelty in this chapter is a cruelty from the pit, handed back.** Not as symbol — as
vocabulary. The chapter reuses Genesis 37's actual words:

| Genesis 37 | Genesis 42 |
|---|---|
| *"your sheaves … bowed down to my sheaf"* (37:7) | *"bowed themselves down to him … to the earth"* (42:6) |
| *"Examine it, now"* — ***hakker-nāʾ*** (37:32) | ***"he recognized them"*** — *wayyakkēr* (42:7, 8) |
| **they throw him into a pit** (37:24) | **he puts them into custody** — *mišmār* (42:17) |
| *"The child **is no more**"* — ***ʾênennû*** (37:30) | *"one **is no more**"* (42:13, 32); *"Joseph **is no more**, Simeon **is no more**"* (42:36) |
| *"his brothers **listened** to him"* — the sale (37:27) | *"he begged us, and we **wouldn't listen**"* (42:21) |
| *"**Shed no blood**"* — Reuben (37:22) | *"his **blood is required**"* — Reuben (42:22) |
| *"I will go down to **Sheol** to my son mourning"* (37:35) | *"bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to **Sheol**"* (42:38) |
| **a garment dipped in blood is carried to Jacob** (37:31–33) | **money hidden in sacks is carried to Jacob** (42:35) |

**The brothers' own crime is being returned to them in a language they do not recognise as their own** —
which is exactly the condition of verse 8: *"Joseph recognized his brothers, but they didn't recognize
him."*

### The turning point is a sentence nobody in the chapter is meant to hear

**Genesis 42:21 is the moral centre of the whole Joseph cycle**, and it is spoken by men who believe
they are alone:

> **21** They said to one another, "We are certainly guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the
> distress of his soul, when he begged us, and we wouldn't listen. Therefore this distress has come
> upon us." (42:21)

**This is the first time in twenty-two years that any of them says the thing out loud** — and
**it contains information Genesis 37 withheld.** Chapter 37 never records Joseph speaking in the pit.
It reports the stripping, the throwing, and then, with a coldness the reader notices on first pass,
*"they sat down to eat bread"* (37:25). **It does not say he begged.**

**The begging arrives here, two decades late, out of the mouths of the men who ignored it.** Genesis
told the story the first time the way they lived it; it tells this part the way they remember it. See
[[Truly We Are Guilty - The Brothers' Confession and the Gap in Genesis 37]].

### Everything the chapter does hangs on a language barrier

*"They didn't know that Joseph understood them; for there was an interpreter between them"* (42:23).
**The interpreter is the chapter's whole machine.** He is why the brothers speak freely in front of the
man they are confessing to; he is why Joseph, who has just heard the most important sentence of his
life, **has to keep his face still while a third party finishes translating;** and he is why the
weeping in verse 24 happens off to one side.

**He is also the sign of how far Joseph has gone.** At 41:45 he was given an Egyptian name, an Egyptian
wife, and Egyptian office. Here he needs a man to render his brothers' Hebrew — **not because he has
forgotten it, as the verse is careful to say, but because the Egyptian governor they are addressing
cannot be seen to know it.**

### The shape

| verses | what happens |
|---|---|
| **42:1–5** | Jacob sends ten sons to Egypt and keeps Benjamin home |
| **42:6–17** | They bow; Joseph knows them, calls them spies, jails them three days |
| **42:18–20** | The terms are softened — one hostage, not nine |
| **42:21–24** | The confession, the interpreter, the weeping, Simeon bound |
| **42:25–28** | The money returned; *"What is this that God has done to us?"* |
| **42:29–38** | The report to Jacob, the second discovery of the money, and his refusal |

### 42:1–5 — a father who will not say the name of the country

> **1** Now Jacob saw that there was grain in Egypt, and Jacob said to his sons, "Why do you look at one
> another?" **2** He said, "Behold, I have heard that there is grain in Egypt. Go down there, and buy
> for us from there, so that we may live, and not die." **3** Joseph's ten brothers went down to buy
> grain from Egypt. **4** But Jacob didn't send Benjamin, Joseph's brother, with his brothers; for he
> said, "Lest perhaps harm happen to him." **5** The sons of Israel came to buy among those who came,
> for the famine was in the land of Canaan.

**The first words of the chapter are an old man's irritation.** *"Why do you look at one another?"* —
ten grown men, several of them grandfathers by now, standing around in a famine. **It is the only line
of comedy in the chapter and it is over in four words.**

***Yārad***, *go down*, is the verb (42:2, 3), **and it is the verb of every descent into Egypt in the
Bible.** Abram goes down at 12:10 because of a famine; Joseph is brought down at 39:1; **Jacob will go
down at 46:3 and the nation will not come up for four hundred years** (15:13). See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]]. Jacob's last word in the chapter is
the same verb twice over — *"my son shall not **go down** with you … you will **bring down** my gray
hairs"* (42:38).

**Verse 4 names the thing the chapter is really about, forty-nine verses before anyone acts on it.**
*"Jacob didn't send Benjamin, **Joseph's brother**"* — not *his own youngest son*, not *Rachel's son*,
but **Joseph's brother**, which is the narrator telling the reader what Jacob will not: **the surviving
child of the wife he loved is the only son he has left, and the other ten know it.** The favouritism
that produced the coat has simply moved down one place.

*"Lest perhaps harm happen to him"* — ***ʾāsôn***, a rare word for fatal mishap. **Jacob uses it here
and twice more (42:38; 44:29), and outside the Joseph story it appears in the Bible only in the law of
Exodus 21:22–23**, of injury to a pregnant woman. He is saying, in a word he reserves for this, that he
expects to lose this one too.

**Verse 5 changes the name.** Jacob acts in 1–4; *"the sons of **Israel**"* travel in verse 5. The
narrator has been alternating the two names since 32:28 without explaining the rule, and here the
covenant name attaches to the ten sons at the moment they cross out of Canaan. See
[[Renamed and Still Called Jacob - The Two Namings of Israel]].

### 42:6–9 — the dream arrives, and the dreamer does not celebrate

> **6** Joseph was the governor over the land. It was he who sold to all the people of the land.
> Joseph's brothers came, and bowed themselves down to him with their faces to the earth. **7** Joseph
> saw his brothers, and he recognized them, but acted like a stranger to them, and spoke roughly with
> them. He said to them, "Where did you come from?" They said, "From the land of Canaan to buy food."
> **8** Joseph recognized his brothers, but they didn't recognize him. **9** Joseph remembered the
> dreams which he dreamed about them, and said to them, "You are spies! You have come to see the
> nakedness of the land."

**Verse 6 is the sentence the book has been walking toward since 37:7, and Genesis spends eleven words
on it.** *"Joseph's brothers came, and bowed themselves down to him with their faces to the earth."*
No comment, no *and so it was fulfilled*, **no indication that anyone but the reader notices.** See
[[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]].

**Only ten bow.** The first dream had eleven sheaves; the second had the sun, the moon and eleven stars
(37:9). **Benjamin is in Canaan and Jacob is with him** — which is, mechanically, the reason the rest of
the Joseph narrative has to happen. **The chapter's entire plot is Joseph arranging the conditions for
his own dream to finish.** Whether he knows that is the question verse 9 raises and never answers.

**Verse 7 does the whole psychology in one Hebrew root.** *"He **recognized** them"* — *wayyakkēr* —
*"but **acted like a stranger** to them"* — *wayyitnakkēr*. **Same root, ***nkr***, in two stems, in
consecutive clauses: to know a face, and to make one's own face unknown.** The root has been running
through the cycle since the brothers held up a bloodied coat and said *"**Examine** it, now"* (37:32,
*hakker-nāʾ*) **and their father *recognized* it** (37:33). See
[[Discern, Please - Hakker-na from Jacob's Coat to Judah's Pledge]].

**Verse 8 repeats the recognition in plain form so the asymmetry cannot be missed**: *"Joseph
recognized his brothers, but they didn't recognize him."* Twenty-two years, a shaved head, a foreign
name, a court wardrobe and an interpreter stand between the two halves of that sentence.

*"The **nakedness** of the land"* (42:9, 12) is ***ʿerwâ***, **the word of Noah's tent** (9:22–23) and
of the sexual prohibitions of Leviticus 18. **Applied to a country it means its undefended border** —
but the choice of word puts the accusation of espionage in the vocabulary of shameful exposure, **and
the brothers are standing in front of the one person alive who could accuse them of uncovering
something.**

*"Governor"* is ***šallîṭ*** (42:6), an unusual word in the Torah and at home in the later, Aramaic-
influenced books; it is the vocabulary of imperial administration rather than of Genesis.

### 42:10–17 — twelve brothers, one is no more

> **10** They said to him, "No, my lord, but your servants have come to buy food. **11** We are all one
> man's sons; we are honest men. Your servants are not spies." **12** He said to them, "No, but you have
> come to see the nakedness of the land!" **13** They said, "We, your servants, are twelve brothers, the
> sons of one man in the land of Canaan; and behold, the youngest is today with our father, and one is
> no more."

**The brothers volunteer the two facts Joseph most wants** — that Benjamin is alive and at home, and
that there were twelve — **and they volunteer them while protesting their honesty.** The protest is
*"we are **honest** men"* (***kēnîm***), said three times in the chapter (42:11, 19, 31) and twice more
in the report to Jacob. **It is the claim the chapter exists to test**, and the men making it are
describing themselves to the brother they sold.

***"And one is no more"*** — ***wəhāʾeḥād ʾênennû***. **This is the sentence the chapter turns on, and
it is a lie told in a form that is also true.** They cannot say *we sold him*; **they have said
*he is not* for so long that it has become how they think of it.** And the phrase is not theirs
originally: **[[Reuben]] invented it at the empty pit** — *"The child **is no more**"* (37:30) — and
**their father will use it twice in one breath at 42:36.** Three uses across two decades, **and every
one of them is a man refusing to name what happened.**

> **14** Joseph said to them, "It is like I told you, saying, 'You are spies!' **15** By this you shall
> be tested. By the life of Pharaoh, you shall not go out from here, unless your youngest brother comes
> here. **16** Send one of you, and let him get your brother, and you shall be bound, that your words
> may be tested, whether there is truth in you, or else by the life of Pharaoh surely you are spies."
> **17** He put them all together into custody for three days.

***"By the life of Pharaoh"*** (42:15, 16) is **the only oath of its kind in Scripture, and Joseph
swears it twice.** The Bible's oath formula is *as Yahweh lives*; **this is an Egyptian courtier's
oath, sworn on a king's life by a man who told that king three chapters earlier that God was doing the
talking.** It is the most thoroughly Egyptian thing he says in the chapter, and it is said to make his
Hebrew brothers believe he is not one of them. See
[[By the Life of Pharaoh - Oaths Sworn by a King]].

*"You shall be **tested**"* — ***bāḥan***, twice (42:15, 16), **a verb of assaying metal.** The chapter
is an examination and says so. But **it is not clear who is being examined**: the stated test is whether
their story about a youngest brother is true, and the actual test — the one the next three chapters
resolve — **is whether ten men who sold one favoured son will do it again to another.** See [[Testing]].

**Verse 17 is the chapter's cruellest sentence and it is four words long.** *"He put them all together
into custody for three days."* ***Mišmār*** is **the exact word for the place Joseph spent his own
years** — *"He put them in **custody** in the house of the captain of the guard … the place where
Joseph was bound"* (40:3). **He puts his brothers in the institution he came out of.** Genesis does not
say he intended the correspondence and does not need to.

### 42:18–20 — "for I fear God"

> **18** Joseph said to them the third day, "Do this, and live, for I fear God. **19** If you are honest
> men, then let one of your brothers be bound in your prison; but you go, carry grain for the famine of
> your houses. **20** Bring your youngest brother to me; so will your words be verified, and you won't
> die." They did so.

**On the third day the terms reverse: nine held and one sent becomes one held and nine sent** — and the
reason given is ***"for I fear God."***

**The sentence is doing at least three things at once.** It is a ruler reassuring frightened foreigners
that he is under restraint; **it is Joseph's only theological statement in the chapter**, in a book
where he attributes everything to God in front of Pharaoh (41:16, 25, 28, 32) and says nothing at all
in front of his brothers; **and it is a phrase with a history in Genesis.** Abraham said *"surely the
fear of God is not in this place"* (20:11) as his excuse for lying to [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]]
— **the fear of God is what Genesis calls the thing that makes a stranger safe.** Joseph is telling ten
men that they are safe with him, in the middle of a sentence that keeps one of them in chains. See
[[The Fear of God]].

**Note also what changes between verse 16 and verse 19.** The first plan was cruelty for its own sake —
nine men rotting in an Egyptian jail while one walks to Canaan and back. **The second plan sends the
grain home.** *"Carry grain for the famine of your houses"* — **the households in Canaan include his
father.** Something happened in those three days, and Genesis reports the three days and not the
something.

### 42:21–24 — the confession, and the man it was not meant for

> **21** They said to one another, "We are certainly guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the
> distress of his soul, when he begged us, and we wouldn't listen. Therefore this distress has come
> upon us." **22** Reuben answered them, saying, "Didn't I tell you, saying, 'Don't sin against the
> child,' and you wouldn't listen? Therefore also, behold, his blood is required." **23** They didn't
> know that Joseph understood them; for there was an interpreter between them. **24** He turned himself
> away from them, and wept. Then he returned to them, and spoke to them, and took Simeon from among
> them, and bound him before their eyes.

***"We are certainly guilty"*** — ***ʾăšēmîm ʾănaḥnû***, from ***ʾāšām***, **the root that will name
the guilt offering of Leviticus 5.** It is the vocabulary of incurred liability, not of regret, and
**this is its only appearance in Genesis.** They are not saying *we feel bad*; **they are saying
*we owe*.**

**The verse contains a fact the reader has never been given.** *"We saw the distress of his soul, when
he **begged** us, and we wouldn't listen."* **Genesis 37 does not record Joseph saying one word from
the moment his brothers see him coming.** He is stripped, thrown, and left; the narration moves
immediately to *"they sat down to eat bread."* **The pleading was always there and the narrator never
mentioned it.** It surfaces now because these are the men who heard it, and **twenty-two years of not
saying so is what verse 21 finally breaks.**

**And the verb they use for their own failure is the one Genesis 37 used for their success.** *"We
**wouldn't listen**"* (42:21) — ***šāmaʿ***; *"his brothers **listened** to him"* (37:27), where the
proposal they listened to was Judah's, to sell him. **They could hear a sales pitch and not a boy.**
Reuben's line in verse 22 presses the same verb a third time: *"and you **wouldn't listen**."*

***"Therefore this distress has come upon us"*** — ***ṣārâ***, **the same noun as *"the distress of his
soul"* in the first half of the verse.** They name the punishment with the word they just used for the
crime. **This is the chapter's whole theory of what is happening to them**, and it is the closest thing
Genesis offers to a verdict: see [[Retribution]].

**Reuben's contribution is self-exculpation, and it misquotes him.** *"Didn't I tell you, saying, 'Don't sin against the
child,' and you wouldn't listen?"* — **but that is not what 37:22 records him saying.** What he said was
*"Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit … lay no hand on him"*, **which is the sentence of a man
amending a murder into an abandonment**, and the narrator had to add the explanation that he meant to
come back for him. **Twenty-two years on, he is quoting himself as having made a moral objection he did
not actually make.** **The reproach is his only contribution to the chapter, and it is spoken over the head of his
imprisoned younger brother.**

***"His blood is required"*** — ***dāmô nidrāš***, the passive of ***dāraš***. **This is the Noahic
formula.** *"I will surely **require** your life's blood … at the hand of every man's brother I will
**require** the life of man"* (9:5). **Reuben applies the covenant with Noah to his own family and
identifies his brothers as the hand it will be required from** — and he says it about a man who is
standing six feet away, alive, listening. See [[Whoever Sheds Man's Blood]] and
[[His Blood Is Required - Darash and the Reckoning for Blood]].

**Verse 23 is the hinge and it is a parenthesis.** *"They didn't know that Joseph understood them; for
there was an interpreter between them."* ***Mēlîṣ*** — a rare noun; **Job 33:23 uses it of a mediating
angel and Isaiah 43:27 of an intermediary.** Here it is a functionary, and **the only character in the
chapter whose presence is a technical necessity**: without him the brothers would not speak, and
without their speaking there is no chapter. **He has no name, no speech, and no page** — see the note
below.

**Verse 24 is the first weeping of the Joseph cycle, and it is hidden.** *"He turned himself away from
them, and wept."* **He will weep six more times before the book ends** (43:30; 45:2, 14–15; 46:29;
50:1, 17), **each time less concealed than the last**, until at 45:2 *"He wept aloud. The Egyptians
heard."* Here he goes behind something. **Then he comes straight back and puts a man in chains.**

**Why [[Simeon]]?** Genesis does not say. **The second-eldest, taken because the eldest had just
identified himself as the one who objected** is the reading the sequence invites — Reuben speaks in
verse 22 and Simeon is bound in verse 24, **and Joseph has just learned, through an interpreter, which
brother tried to stop it.** Others point to Simeon's record at Shechem (34:25–31). The text supplies
the order of events and no motive. **What it does supply is *"bound him before their eyes"*** — the
binding is staged, **as the stripping of the coat was.**

### 42:25–28 — "What is this that God has done to us?"

> **25** Then Joseph gave a command to fill their bags with grain, and to restore each man's money into
> his sack, and to give them food for the way. So it was done to them. **26** They loaded their donkeys
> with their grain, and departed from there. **27** As one of them opened his sack to give his donkey
> food in the lodging place, he saw his money. Behold, it was in the mouth of his sack. **28** He said
> to his brothers, "My money is restored! Behold, it is in my sack!" Their hearts failed them, and they
> turned trembling to one another, saying, "What is this that God has done to us?"

**Joseph refuses to be paid by his family and cannot say so**, so the refusal is carried out in secret
and arrives as a terror. **The three orders in verse 25 are generosity, every one of them** — grain,
the money back, provisions for the road — **and the brothers experience them as a trap**, because they
are. Men accused of espionage who arrive home with the purchase price still in their bags are men who
can be hanged.

***"What is this that God has done to us?"*** (42:28) is **the first time any of Joseph's brothers
names God in the entire cycle.** Through the dreams, the sale, the lie to their father and twenty-two
years of living with it, **not one of them has mentioned him.** They mention him now, **and the
sentence is not a confession but a question, asked in the dark, about a kindness they have mistaken for
judgment.** It is the exact inverse of 50:20, where Joseph will tell them **God meant a cruelty for
good**; here they read a generosity as doom.

*"Their hearts failed them"* — literally *their heart went out* — *"and they turned **trembling**"* —
***ḥārad***, the verb of Isaac's violent shudder when he learned he had blessed the wrong son (27:33).
**Genesis reserves it for the moment a man discovers the ground has moved under a thing he thought
settled.**

### 42:29–38 — the report, and a father who says no

> **29** They came to Jacob their father, to the land of Canaan, and told him all that had happened to
> them, saying, … **33** The man, the lord of the land, said to us, 'By this I will know that you are
> honest
> men: leave one of your brothers with me, and take grain for the famine of your houses, and go your
> way. **34** Bring your youngest brother to me. Then I will know that you are not spies, but that you
> are honest men. So I will deliver your brother to you, and you shall trade in the land.'"

**The retelling is the chapter's second-longest speech and it is edited.** Set against 42:7–20 it drops
the three days in custody entirely, **drops the binding of Simeon in front of them**, softens *"you are
spies"* into a suspicion the man merely *"took us for"*, and **adds a promise Joseph never made** —
*"and you shall trade in the land"* (42:34). **Ten men who once brought their father a bloodied coat
and a story are standing in the same place bringing him a story again.** The narrator lets the two
accounts sit side by side and says nothing.

> **35** As they emptied their sacks, behold, each man's bundle of money was in his sack. When they and
> their father saw their bundles of money, they were afraid. **36** Jacob, their father, said to them,
> "You have bereaved me of my children! Joseph is no more, Simeon is no more, and you want to take
> Benjamin away. All these things are against me."

**The money is discovered twice**, once on the road by one man (42:27) and once at home by all of them
(42:35) — **and the doubling is the point, because the second discovery happens in front of Jacob.**
The chapter's last movement begins with a father watching silver come out of his sons' luggage.
**Twenty-two years earlier they had gone out and come back with twenty pieces of silver they never told
him about** (37:28).

**Jacob's lament is an accusation, and it is arithmetically exact.** *"**Joseph is no more**, **Simeon
is no more**"* — ***ʾênennû*** twice, **the word Reuben coined at the pit and the brothers used to
Joseph's face**, now in their father's mouth, **counting.** *"You have bereaved me"* — ***šikkaltem*** —
**he says they did it**, which is true of one and false of the other, and he has no way of knowing he
is right about the first.

***"All these things are against me"*** is the most quoted line in the chapter and one of the most
poignantly wrong sentences in Scripture. **Joseph is alive and governing Egypt; Simeon is fed and held
by his own brother; and the grain in the next room came from the son Jacob is mourning.** Every item on
the list is mercy. **Genesis lets him say it and does not correct him for five chapters.** See
[[Providence]] — and 45:5–8 and 50:20, where the correction finally comes.

> **37** Reuben spoke to his father, saying, "Kill my two sons, if I don't bring him to you. Entrust
> him to my care, and I will bring him to you again." **38** He said, "My son shall not go down with
> you; for his brother is dead, and he only is left. If harm happens to him along the way in which you
> go, then you will bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to Sheol."

**Reuben's offer is grotesque and it is the second time in the cycle that his instincts are right and
his execution is catastrophic.** He proposes that his father kill two of his own grandsons — **Jacob's
great-grandsons** — as the penalty for failure. **It is meant as the strongest surety a man can give
and it offers Jacob more bereavement as insurance against bereavement.** Jacob does not answer it.
**Compare [[Judah]] at 43:9, who offers himself** — *"I'll be collateral for him"* — **which is the
offer that works**, and which is why the next chapter belongs to Judah and not to the firstborn.

**Jacob's refusal contains the sentence that ends the chapter and closes the circle.** *"His brother is
dead, and **he only is left**"* — **said in the hearing of ten sons**, who are, by this accounting, not
his children. **The favouritism is undiminished and now it is spoken.**

***"You will bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to Sheol"*** (42:38) is **word for word the vow he
made over the bloodied coat**: *"I will go down to Sheol to my son mourning"* (37:35). **The same
destination, the same grief, and the same refusal to be comforted** — except that the tally has gone
from one son to two, and he is being asked to risk a third. See [[Sheol]].

**The chapter ends with the answer "no."** Nothing in it is resolved: Simeon is in an Egyptian jail,
the money is a standing accusation, Benjamin is at home, and **the only man who could explain any of it
is the one who arranged it all and is not speaking.** The famine will do what Joseph's plan could not,
and the next pericope opens with the grain running out.

## People

- [[Joseph]] — **the chapter he has been waiting twenty-two years for, and he spends it in disguise.**
  He is *"the governor over the land"* (*šallîṭ*), the seller of grain to all comers, and the first to
  recognise anyone; **he makes his own face strange** (*wayyitnakkēr*), **speaks roughly, accuses them
  of espionage twice, swears twice by Pharaoh's life, and jails them for three days.** Then he softens
  the terms, says *"I fear God"*, overhears the confession he could never have asked for, **turns away
  and weeps for the first time in the book**, and comes back and binds [[Simeon]] in front of them.
  **He does not tell them who he is, does not ask after his father, and does not send word to Canaan** —
  the same silence as 41:53–57, now sustained in the presence of the people it costs. **He also returns
  their money**, which is the chapter's one unambiguous kindness and is delivered so that it arrives as
  a terror
- [[Jacob]] — **frames the chapter and never leaves Canaan.** He opens it with an old man's irritation
  (*"Why do you look at one another?"*) and closes it with a refusal. **He keeps [[Benjamin]] home in
  verse 4 and the narrator calls Benjamin *"Joseph's brother"* to explain why;** he hears an edited
  report; **he says *ʾênennû* twice over two sons**, accuses the ten of bereaving him, and says *"all
  these things are against me"* while standing next to grain his living son sent. **His last speech
  repeats 37:35 exactly** — the gray hairs, the sorrow, Sheol. *"He only is left"* is said in front of
  ten sons
- [[Benjamin]] — **the chapter's subject and he is not in it.** Named three times and never present:
  withheld at 42:4, described to Joseph at 42:13 as *"the youngest … today with our father"*, demanded
  at 42:15, 20, 34, and refused at 42:38. **He is the missing eleventh sheaf of 37:7**, and the reason
  Genesis still has three chapters of plot to run
- [[Simeon]] — **bound before their eyes and left in Egypt**, the chapter's price and its hostage.
  **Genesis gives no reason for choosing him** and the sequence invites one: [[Reuben]] identifies
  himself in verse 22 as the brother who objected, and the next brother in birth order is seized two
  verses later. **He has no speech in the chapter** — he does not have one in Genesis 34 either — and
  his brothers leave without a recorded word about him. He is named again only at 42:36 and 43:23
- [[Reuben]] — **two speeches, and both of them are Reuben in miniature: the right instinct, delivered
  uselessly.** At 42:22 he answers a genuine confession with *"didn't I tell you"*, **misquoting his own
  words at 37:22** into a moral objection he did not make, and invoking the Noahic *"his blood is
  required"* over a brother who is alive in the room. At 42:37 he offers his father the lives of two
  grandsons as surety for a third son. **Jacob does not reply to him.** The firstborn's authority is
  visibly gone, and [[Judah]] takes the role in the next chapter
- [[Judah]] — **not named, and his absence is loud.** He said *"what profit is it if we kill our brother
  and conceal his blood?"* (37:26) and **his brothers listened to him** — the verb whose failure they
  confess at 42:21. **He is silent through this whole chapter and speaks first thing in the next**,
  where the surety he offers is himself
- [[Levi]], [[Dan (person)]], [[Naphtali]], [[Gad]], [[Asher]], [[Issachar]], [[Zebulun]] — **the rest
  of the ten, who act and speak only as a plural.** Genesis has now told four consecutive chapters in
  which *"his brothers"* is a single character with one voice; **the confession of 42:21 is spoken by
  the group and attributed to no one**
- [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] — **present only as the thing Joseph swears by**, twice (42:15, 16).
  He has no action in the chapter, **and the oath is the measure of how completely Joseph now wears his
  office**
- **The interpreter** — ***mēlîṣ*** (42:23), **no page**, per the standing practice on unnamed
  functionaries with no recorded speech (the keeper of the prison, Egypt's magicians). **He is
  nonetheless the mechanism of the chapter**: the brothers confess in front of Joseph only because they
  believe a translator is required, **and Joseph has to keep his face while the translation finishes.**
  The noun is rare and elsewhere carries the sense of *mediator* — Job 33:23, Isaiah 43:27 — which
  makes its one appearance in a story about a hidden brother a small irony the text does not press
- **Reuben's two sons** — **no page.** Offered at 42:37 as forfeit, unnamed here (they are Hanoch and
  Pallu at 46:9), **and they exist in this chapter only as the measure of what their father thinks a
  guarantee is worth**
- [[God]] — ***ʾĕlōhîm* twice, both inside quotation marks, and both from men who do not understand
  their situation.** Joseph's *"I fear God"* (42:18) reassures men he is currently imprisoning; the
  brothers' *"What is this that God has done to us?"* (42:28) **reads a returned purse as a
  punishment.** The narrator again says nothing — **the third consecutive chapter in which everything
  attributed to God is attributed by a character**, and the narration stays silent from 39:23 until
  46:1–2

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — **the destination of the verb *yārad* and, for the first time, a place Israelites travel
  to voluntarily and in a group.** It is where the grain is, where the governor is, where the *mišmār*
  is, and **where one of the twelve is now being held.** Genesis has been narrowing toward this: Abram
  went down alone and came back (12:10–20), Joseph was carried down as property (37:28), **and here ten
  free men go down and one of them stays**
- [[Canaan]] — **named four times as the place with no food** (42:5, 7, 13, 29, 32), and **the place the
  chapter keeps returning to and resolving nothing in.** The famine that drives the plot is stated of
  Canaan specifically at 42:5. It is also **the answer the brothers give to Joseph's first question**,
  and therefore the first word he hears from them
- [[Sheol]] — **Jacob's stated destination, for the second time in six chapters** (37:35; 42:38). The
  chapter ends on it. **Both occurrences are in his mouth, both are about a son, and both are a refusal
  to be comforted**
- **The lodging place** — ***mālôn*** (42:27), **no page**; a caravan halt, unlocated, and **the setting
  for the discovery that turns the journey home into a second crisis**

## Themes

- [[Guilt and Conscience]] — **new page, and this chapter is its foundation text.** *"We are certainly
  guilty"* — ***ʾăšēmîm***, **the only occurrence of the root in Genesis** — spoken twenty-two years
  after the fact, **unprompted, to no authority, by men who believe nobody is listening.** The chapter
  is Scripture's first sustained account of conscience surfacing on its own schedule
- [[Retribution]] — **the brothers' own explanation of everything happening to them**: *"Therefore this
  distress has come upon us"* (42:21), **using *ṣārâ* for their punishment and *ṣārâ* for Joseph's
  suffering in the same verse.** Reuben raises it to covenant law with *"his blood is required"* (9:5).
  **Genesis prints the theory without endorsing it — and the reader knows the retribution is a staged
  interrogation by the victim**
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — **the fulfilment of 37:7 happens in verse 6 and the text does not mark
  it.** *"Joseph remembered the dreams"* (42:9) is the whole commentary. **Only ten of the eleven
  sheaves are present**, which is the chapter's engine: the dream is not finished, so the story is not
  finished
- [[Testing]] — ***bāḥan*** twice (42:15, 16), a metallurgical verb, **and the stated test is not the
  real one.** Joseph says he is testing whether their story is true; **what the next three chapters test
  is whether they will sell a second favoured son.** The chapter also inverts the pattern: **in Genesis
  it is normally God who tests a man** (22:1), and here it is a man, hidden, testing his brothers
- [[Deception]] — **the cycle's third full-length deception and the first one Joseph runs.** Jacob
  deceived Isaac with a garment; the brothers deceived Jacob with a garment; **Joseph deceives the
  brothers with a costume, an accent, an oath, and a functionary.** *Wayyitnakkēr* — *he made himself
  unrecognisable* — **is the deception in one word, and it is the same root as the recognition beside
  it**
- [[Remembrance]] — **two memories drive the chapter and neither is affectionate.** *"Joseph remembered
  the dreams"* (42:9) precedes the accusation of espionage; **the brothers remember the pit** (42:21)
  and describe a scene Genesis never narrated. **After 40:23's *"didn't remember"* and 41:9's *"I
  remember my faults today"*, this is the cycle's third chapter in a row organised by the verb *zākar*
  and its failures**
- [[Language and Speech]] — **a chapter whose plot depends on a translator.** *"They didn't know that
  Joseph understood them"* (42:23) is the only verse in Genesis where **the barrier between two parties
  is linguistic and deliberately maintained by the one who could remove it.** Joseph speaks *roughly*
  (42:7, literally *hard things*), swears in Egyptian court idiom, and **withholds the one sentence in
  his own language that would end the chapter**
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — **built into the chapter's first and last acts.** Jacob *saw* there was
  grain (42:1); Joseph *saw* his brothers and made himself unseeable (42:7); **the brothers confess to
  having *seen* the distress of his soul and done nothing (42:21)**; and the spying charge is about
  *seeing* the land's nakedness (42:9, 12). **The chapter's sin is a thing that was watched**
- [[Famine and Plenty]] — **the mechanism that moves every person in the chapter.** 41:57's *"all
  countries came into Egypt"* becomes ten specific men with donkeys. **The famine is what makes the
  reunion possible and what will finally overrule Jacob's refusal** in 43:1–2
- [[Brotherhood]] — **new page.** *"His brother"* is the chapter's most-repeated relational term:
  Benjamin is *"Joseph's brother"* (42:4), the brothers are *"twelve brothers"* (42:13, 32), **the
  confession is *"guilty concerning our brother"* (42:21)**, and Jacob's last words are about *"his
  brother"* (42:38). **Genesis's first brothers ended at 4:8**; this chapter is the book's longest
  reckoning with what one owes one
- [[Suffering]] — ***ṣārat napšô***, *"the distress of his soul"* (42:21) — **the only description
  anywhere in Scripture of what the pit was like for the boy in it**, and it is given in a confession,
  not a narration
- [[Envy]] — the favouritism of 37:3 has simply relocated: **Jacob withholds one son by name and says of
  him *"he only is left"* in front of the other ten.** Nothing in the twenty-two years has changed the
  household's basic arrangement, and the chapter does not say what the ten make of it
- [[The Fear of God]] — *"Do this, and live, **for I fear God**"* (42:18), Joseph's only theological
  sentence in the chapter, **offered to frightened foreigners as a guarantee of restraint** — the same
  function the phrase has at 20:11, where Abraham says its absence is why he lied
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — ***mēlîṣ*** (42:23), a noun that elsewhere means *mediator*, **here
  reduced to a technical service**; and Reuben's failed surety at 42:37, **which offers the wrong
  collateral** and is answered by Judah's offer of himself at 43:9
- [[Providence]] — **new page, and the chapter is its hardest case.** *"All these things are against
  me"* (42:36) is said by a man whose son is alive, whose second son is safe, and whose food came from
  the first. **Genesis prints the mistaken verdict and waits five chapters to answer it** (45:5–8;
  50:20). The brothers' *"What is this that God has done to us?"* is the same error from the other
  direction
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — ***ʿerwat hāʾāreṣ***, *"the nakedness of the land"* (42:9, 12), **Noah's
  word applied to a border.** An accusation of exposure, levelled by the one man in the room with
  something to hide
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — ***"by the life of Pharaoh"*** twice (42:15, 16), **the Bible's only oath sworn
  on an Egyptian king**, and Reuben's forfeit of two sons at 42:37, **a vow Jacob declines to accept**
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — **the brothers call themselves *"your servants"* five times** (42:10, 11,
  13 twice, and in the report), **to the brother they sold into servitude.** The word is *ʿăbādeykā*,
  ordinary deference, and the chapter does not comment
- [[Life and Death]] — *"so that we may live, and not die"* (42:2) opens the chapter and *"you won't
  die"* (42:20) closes Joseph's terms; **the famine's stakes are stated plainly at both ends**, and
  Jacob's last word is his own grave
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — **the first descent of the sons of Israel into Egypt as a group.** *"You
  shall trade in the land"* (42:34) — a promise Joseph did not make, invented by the brothers in the
  retelling — **is the first hint of the settlement that 47:27 will report**

## Notes

**On the twenty-two years.** Joseph is seventeen at 37:2, thirty when he stands before Pharaoh (41:46),
and seven years of plenty pass before the famine begins (41:53). **The brothers arrive somewhere in the
famine's first years, so roughly twenty-two years have passed since the pit** — long enough that a
seventeen-year-old Hebrew shepherd and a shaved Egyptian official in his late thirties are not the same
face. **The asymmetry of verse 8 is not a miracle; it is arithmetic and a wardrobe.**

**On why he does not simply tell them.** Genesis gives no reason and the chapter forecloses the easy
ones. **Revenge** does not account for the returned money, the softened terms, or the weeping.
**A test** does not account for the three days in custody, which test nothing. **Bringing Benjamin
down** is the stated aim of the demand, but it does not require the accusation of espionage, and the
demand is made before verse 21 tells him anything about his brothers' state of mind. **What the text
supplies is a sequence, not a motive:** he sees them, he remembers the dreams, he accuses them. The
memory sits between the seeing and the accusing, and Genesis leaves it there.

**On *ʾāsôn*.** Jacob's word for what might happen to Benjamin (42:4, 38; 44:29) is **legal vocabulary
elsewhere in the Torah** — Exodus 21:22–23, where it distinguishes injury without lasting harm from
injury with it. Its concentration in the mouth of one frightened father across three chapters, and its
near-absence from the rest of the Bible, **gives it the quality of a private word.**

**On the two sacks.** The chapter uses ***śaq*** for the grain sack (42:25, 27, 35) and ***ʾamtaḥat***
for the container the money turns up in (42:27, 28). **The second noun is effectively confined to the
Joseph story** (Genesis 42–44) and does not survive it, which is worth noting because **chapter 44's
cup is planted in an *ʾamtaḥat* too.** The vocabulary of the second deception is installed here.

**On "he only is left" (42:38).** Jacob says it of Benjamin with ten sons standing in front of him.
**It is the single most revealing sentence he speaks in the cycle** and no one answers it. Judah will
quote it back to Joseph, without comment, at 44:20.

**A page deliberately not created.** The interpreter of 42:23 gets no page, consistent with the keeper
of the prison (39:21–23) and Egypt's magicians (41:8). **The line is unnamed functionaries with no
recorded speech**, and a mediator with no words of his own is exactly what this one is.

**Links left for later pericopes.** [[Judah]]'s counter-offer at 43:9, the second journey, the silver
cup, and the disclosure at 45:1–3 are all named above as the resolution of things this chapter opens;
their passage pages do not exist yet and are not linked.
