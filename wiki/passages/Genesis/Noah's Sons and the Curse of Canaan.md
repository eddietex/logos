# Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 9:18–29

## Summary

Twelve verses, and they undo the mood of the twenty-four before them. [[The Covenant with Noah]]
ended with a blessing, a law, a covenant, and a sign in the sky, all of it given to a man who never
answered. Here that man plants a vineyard, drinks, lies uncovered in his tent, and — waking — speaks
for the first time in Scripture. What he says is a curse on a child who was not in the room.

The unit has four movements and they do not blend: a superscription that names the three sons and
looks ahead to Genesis 10 (18–19); an incident of eleven Hebrew clauses (20–23); an oracle of curse
and blessing (24–27); and an obituary in the exact form of Genesis 5 (28–29). It is the last episode
of the flood cycle, the first episode of the ethnography that follows, and the hinge between them.

### The superscription (9:18–19)

> **18** The sons of Noah who went out from the ship were Shem, Ham, and Japheth. Ham is the father
> of Canaan.
> **19** These three were the sons of Noah, and from these, the whole earth was populated.

The three are named for the fourth time in eight chapters (5:32, 6:10, 7:13, 9:18) and the list has
not once varied. What is new is the gloss: **"Ham is the father of Canaan"** — nobody else's
paternity is noted here, and the narrator will say it again four verses later at 9:22, where it is
even less necessary. Twice in five verses, in a passage this spare, is the text telling the reader
what the episode is for before the episode happens.

Verse 19 is the thesis of Genesis 10: *"from these, the whole earth was populated"* — where the verb
is *nāpṣâ*, **they were scattered**, a by-form of the *pûṣ* that will describe the dispersal from
Babel at 11:4 and 11:8–9. Genesis has three chapters to get from eight people to seventy nations, and it opens the account by
saying where everyone came from and, in the next paragraph, deciding what the relations between them
will be.

### The vineyard (9:20–21)

> **20** Noah began to be a farmer, and planted a vineyard.
> **21** He drank of the wine and got drunk. He was uncovered within his tent.

The Hebrew of 20 is *wayyāḥel nōaḥ ʾîš hāʾădāmâ* — Noah began, **a man of the ground**. It is not the
generic word for farming. *ʾĂdāmâ* is the ground of 2:7 that [[Adam]] was taken from, the ground
cursed at 3:17, the ground that opened its mouth for Abel's blood at 4:11, and — the reason the
phrase lands here — the ground [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] had in mind when he named this son:
*"This one will comfort us in our work and in the toil of our hands, caused by the ground which
Yahweh has cursed"* (5:29). Nine hundred years and a flood later, the man named for relief from the
*ʾădāmâ* becomes its man. [[Cain]] was the only other person Genesis has given the soil to — *"Cain
was a tiller of the ground"* (4:2), *ʿōbēd ʾădāmâ* — and it did not end well for him either. See
[[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]].

**This is the Bible's first vineyard, its first wine, and its first drunkenness**, all in eleven
words. Genesis has been keeping a register of cultural origins since 4:20–22 — tents and livestock,
the harp and pipe, bronze and iron — and viticulture is the next entry, credited to the one man in
the book who is also called righteous. The verb *wayyāḥel*, "he began", is the same verb used at 4:26
of calling on Yahweh's name and at 6:1 of men multiplying; it marks the start of an era, not just of
an activity. That it is also a homonym of *ḥālal*, to profane, is a play the rabbis noticed and the
consonants permit, but it is not the plain sense.

The last clause is the one everything turns on: *wayyitgal bətôk ʾohŏlōh*, **and he uncovered
himself inside his tent**. The verb is reflexive (hitpael), and the narrator assigns the exposure to
Noah and nobody else. It is also private — *inside his tent* — which is the first tent in Genesis
since Jabal, *"the father of those who dwell in tents"* (4:20). There is no comment, no adverb, and
no verdict. Genesis does not say that Noah sinned, and it does not say that he did not.

### Seeing and not seeing (9:22–23)

> **22** Ham, the father of Canaan, saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brothers
> outside.
> **23** Shem and Japheth took a garment, and laid it on both their shoulders, went in backwards, and
> covered the nakedness of their father. Their faces were backwards, and they didn’t see their
> father’s nakedness.

The two verses are built as an exact contrast, and the Hebrew makes it by repetition rather than by
comment. Ham **saw** and **told**; the brothers **took**, **laid**, **went**, **covered**, and
**did not see**. Verse 23 spends four separate clauses on the not-seeing — the garment on both their
shoulders, walking in backwards, faces turned away, and then the flat statement that they did not
see — for an act that could have been reported in three words. The prose is as elaborate as their
choreography, and that is the rebuke: the narrative does not tell you what Ham did wrong so much as
show you, at length, what the alternative looked like.

**What was the offence?** Genesis says only that he saw and told. Three readings have been given, and
the wiki records all three without adjudicating.

**The plain sense** is that the seeing and the telling *are* the offence — a failure of filial piety,
made worse by being carried outside to an audience. On this reading the story is about honour and
shame in a household, the remedy is a garment because the injury was exposure, and the escalation in
9:24 (*"knew what his youngest son had done to him"*) is about what a son owes a father rather than
about an assault. It is adequate to everything the text actually says, and it explains why the
brothers' remedy fits.

**The sexual reading** appeals to Leviticus 18 and 20, where *"uncover the nakedness of"* (*gillâ
ʿerwat*) is a fixed idiom for intercourse, and to Leviticus 18:8 in particular — *"the nakedness of
your father's wife… it is your father's nakedness"* — to argue maternal incest. Two things weigh
against it in this passage. The verb here is *rāʾâ*, **saw**, not *gillâ*, uncovered; and the one who
uncovers, in 9:21, is Noah himself, in the reflexive. Noah's wife is not mentioned in the scene at
all.

**The Talmudic readings** take 9:24's *"what his youngest son had **done to him**"* (*ʿāśâ-lô*) as
requiring an act rather than a look, and *Sanhedrin* 70a preserves two: Rab says Ham castrated him,
Samuel says he sodomized him. The castration reading has the advantage of explaining the curse's
strange target — if Noah can have no fourth son, he strikes Ham's fourth son, Canaan (Genesis 10:6
lists Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan in that order). It is an inference from a difficulty, not from
the text.

### The oracle (9:24–27)

> **24** Noah awoke from his wine, and knew what his youngest son had done to him.
> **25** He said, “Canaan is cursed. He will be a servant of servants to his brothers.”

**These are the first words Noah speaks in the Bible.** He has been silent since he was named in
5:29 — through the commission, the ark, the year on the water, the birds, the altar, and the longest
divine speech in Genesis. He is spoken to constantly and answers nothing. The silence breaks here,
on a hangover, and what comes out is *ʾārûr kənāʿan*. See [[Noah]].

*"A servant of servants"* — *ʿebed ʿăbādîm* — is the Hebrew superlative, the construction behind
*holy of holies* and *song of songs* and *king of kings*. It means the lowest of slaves, not a slave
belonging to slaves.

**And it falls on the wrong person.** Ham saw; Canaan is cursed. Genesis offers no explanation, and
four have been proposed:

- **It is an etiology**, and the target is the point. The episode exists to ground Israel's relation
  to the peoples of the land, whom Genesis 10:15–19 will derive from Canaan and give a border running
  *"from Sidon—as you go toward Gerar—to Gaza"* and on toward Sodom. On this reading the narrative is
  built backwards from 9:25, and the gloss at 9:18 and 9:22 is the seam showing. See
  [[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]].
- **It is measure for measure.** Ham offended in a son's capacity and is struck in a son. The
  rabbinic form of the argument adds a constraint from the text: God blessed Ham twenty-three verses
  earlier (9:1), and a man cannot curse whom God has blessed, so the curse deflects to the next
  generation (*Genesis Rabbah* 36:7).
- **Canaan was the offender.** *Bənô haqqāṭān* at 9:24 is usually "his youngest son", but Hebrew uses
  *son* of a grandson freely, and a minority reading takes it of Canaan, with the double gloss at
  9:18 and 9:22 there to put him in the room. This makes the curse land on the guilty party, at the
  cost of a strained reading of 9:22.
- **The text has been reworked.** Some source critics propose an original story about Canaan into
  which *"Ham, the father of"* was inserted to harmonize with the three-son scheme. It resolves the
  difficulty by removing it, and there is no manuscript evidence for it.

> **26** He said, “Blessed be Yahweh, the God of Shem. Let Canaan be his servant.
> **27** May God enlarge Japheth. Let him dwell in the tents of Shem. Let Canaan be his servant.”

**Shem is not blessed. God is.** *Bārûk YHWH ʾĕlōhê šēm* — the first occurrence in Scripture of the
*"Blessed be Yahweh"* formula that will run through Genesis 14:20, 24:27, Exodus 18:10, and 1 Samuel
25:32. Whatever Shem receives comes to him obliquely, by way of a doxology; he is given no land, no
fruitfulness, and no name of his own in the blessing. What he is given is the God — and *"the God of
Shem"* is the first time in Scripture that God is identified by a human being's name. That is the
form the patriarchal formula takes from Abraham onward: *the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, the
God of Jacob*. It starts here, in a drunk man's oracle. See [[Shem]].

Note also the two divine names, which Noah does not use interchangeably. Shem gets **Yahweh**;
Japheth gets **ʾĕlōhîm**. Nothing in the verse comments on the difference, and Genesis will spend
forty chapters on the line that runs from the one named with the covenant name.

**Japheth's blessing is a pun.** *Yapt ʾĕlōhîm ləyepet* — *may God make room for Japheth*. The verb
is *pātâ* in the hiphil, to make wide or make space, and *yapt* against *yepet* is a sound-play, not
an etymology, in Genesis's standing manner (compare *nōaḥ* and *yenaḥămēnû* at 5:29). Then:
*"Let him dwell in the tents of Shem"* — and the Hebrew does not say who *him* is. Three readings, all
grammatically available:

1. **Japheth expands into Shem's territory** — the natural sense of a blessing of enlargement, and a
   slightly threatening one.
2. **Japheth is Shem's guest** — hospitality, with Shem the host and the tent his.
3. **God dwells in the tents of Shem** — the subject carried over from the preceding clause, which
   would make the verse a promise of divine presence to Shem's line rather than a promise of anything
   to Japheth.

Reading 1 or 2 with a Gentile-inclusion gloss is what dominated Christian interpretation from Justin
Martyr onward: Japheth's descendants brought into the blessing that runs through Shem's, which is
Ephesians 2 read back into Genesis 9. It is a legitimate typology and it is not what the verse says.
See [[Japheth]].

**The refrain.** *"Let Canaan be his servant"* is said twice more, once in each blessing, so that the
curse appears three times in three verses and is the only element common to all three lines. The
oracle is not a curse and two blessings; it is a single utterance about servitude with two doxologies
threaded through it.

### The obituary (9:28–29)

> **28** Noah lived three hundred fifty years after the flood.
> **29** All the days of Noah were nine hundred fifty years, and then he died.

This is Genesis 5's formula, word for word — *"All the days of X were Y years, and then he died"* —
and it closes the entry that chapter left open. [[From Adam to Noah]] gave nine men a total and a
death and stopped at the tenth with only *"Noah was five hundred years old"* (5:32). Four chapters
later the register finally finishes its sentence. The flood narrative sits inside a genealogical
entry, between a man's age at fatherhood and his age at death.

Two things follow from the numbers. **Noah is the last man in Scripture to live past nine hundred** —
from Genesis 11 the lifespans fall by half and then by half again, and 950 is the third-longest total
in the Bible after [[Methuselah]]'s 969 and Jared's 962. And **he is alive for most of Abram's life**.
Genesis 11:10–26 puts 292 years between the flood and Abram's birth (2 + 35 + 30 + 34 + 30 + 32 + 30 +
29 + 70), and 9:28 gives Noah 350 after it: on the received Hebrew numbers Abram is fifty-eight when
Noah dies. Genesis never remarks on it and no narrative ever brings them together, but the
chronology it supplies has the man who survived the flood and the man who is called out of Ur alive
at the same time.

### What the pericope does to the chapter it ends

Genesis 9 opens with God blessing and closes with a man cursing, and the two halves are joined by
nothing but sequence. Everything given in 9:1–17 is unconditional — the blessing asks nothing, the
covenant asks nothing, the sign is God's to watch — and 9:18–29 is what the recipients do with a
world in which nothing is asked of them. The realism of 8:21 (*"the imagination of man's heart is
evil from his youth"*) is confirmed by the first family on dry land, and it is confirmed against the
best of them.

The primeval history has now run the same shape three times. In [[The Fall]]: fruit, nakedness
discovered, a covering, a curse. In [[Cain and Abel]]: a gift, a resentment, a killing, a curse. Here:
fruit again, nakedness again, a covering again, a curse again — and this time the covering is done by
brothers and the curse is spoken by a man. See
[[Naked and Ashamed - Genesis 3 and Genesis 9]].

## People

- [[Noah]] — the pericope gives him his voice, his failure, and his death, in that order. He does
  five things: begins as a man of the ground, plants, drinks, gets drunk, and lies uncovered; then he
  wakes, knows, and speaks. **His first recorded words in Scripture are a curse on his grandson**,
  spoken 349 chapters' worth of silence after he was named. Nothing in the text withdraws the
  righteousness of 6:9 or the blamelessness of 7:1, and nothing in the text excuses the vineyard: the
  narrator reports both and reconciles neither. He is the last man in the Bible to live past nine
  hundred years.
- [[Ham]] — he sees and he tells, and the text will not say more. He is named with the gloss *"the
  father of Canaan"* both times he appears (9:18, 9:22), which is the narrator putting his son in the
  scene before the son is needed. He is called *"his youngest son"* at 9:24, against the listing order
  that has put him second eleven times. He is not cursed, not addressed, and never speaks.
- [[Shem]] — he and Japheth act as one grammatical subject for six verbs. He receives the oracle's
  only positive line, and it is not addressed to him: *"Blessed be Yahweh, the God of Shem."* This is
  the first time in Scripture that God is named after a person, and the last three-quarters of Genesis
  follow his line.
- [[Japheth]] — the same six verbs, and the only other thing the Bible ever records him doing. His
  blessing is a pun on his name and a sentence whose subject is ambiguous.
- [[Canaan (son of Ham)]] — cursed three times in three verses for something he is not said to have
  done, and not present in the scene. He is the only person in the primeval history who is given a
  destiny and never an action, and everything else Genesis says about him is a list of the peoples
  descended from him (10:15–19).
- [[God]] — **absent.** He does not speak, act, appear, or approve in any of the twelve verses. He is
  spoken *about*, twice, in the mouth of a man pronouncing the Bible's first human blessing and first
  human curse — and that is a genuinely new thing in Genesis, where every curse until now (3:14, 3:17,
  4:11) and every blessing until now (1:22, 1:28, 2:3, 9:1) has been God's own. Whether the curse of
  9:25 carries divine authority is a question the passage declines to answer, and the rest of Scripture
  never quotes it.
- **Noah's wife** — not mentioned, as she has not been mentioned in any speech since 6:18. The sexual
  reading of 9:22 requires her presence in the tent; the text does not supply it.

## Places

None named. The one location is *"his tent"* (9:21), the first tent in Genesis since 4:20, and the
scene has no geography at all — the vineyard is not sited, and the mountains of [[Ararat]] are four
verses and one covenant behind. That is about to change permanently: Genesis 10 is a map, and every
pericope from it onward has somewhere on it.

**Canaan** is a person here and not yet a place. The land will arrive at 10:19, and when it does it
takes the page `Canaan`; the man keeps [[Canaan (son of Ham)]]. The whole force of 9:25 depends on the
two being the same word.

## Themes

- [[Curse]] — the theme's turning point. *ʾĀrûr* has been spoken three times in Genesis (3:14, 3:17,
  4:11) and every time by God; 9:25 is **the first curse pronounced by a human being**, and it is
  pronounced on a child, for a father's act, by a man who has just been drunk. It is also the first
  curse in Scripture that is inherited by design rather than by consequence.
- [[Blessing]] — likewise. 9:26 is the first blessing spoken by a human being, and its object is God
  rather than a person: the *bārûk YHWH* formula enters Scripture here. Genesis has had God blessing
  creatures, days, and people; from now on people bless too, and the patriarchal blessings of chapters
  27, 48, and 49 are this scene's descendants.
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — the pericope's controlling image, and the reason it rhymes with Genesis
  3. Nakedness is exposed, seen, and covered by others; the Hebrew *ʿerwâ* used here is the word
  Leviticus 18 and 20 will build a body of sexual law around.
- [[Sin]] — reported without a verdict. The narrator does not call the drunkenness a sin, does not
  call Ham's seeing a sin, and gives no divine reaction to either. What it gives instead is a curse
  spoken by an offended human, which is a different kind of moral machinery from anything in Genesis
  3–4.
- [[Righteousness]] — complicated here. The man called *ṣaddîq* and *tāmîm* at 6:9, and vindicated by
  God's own voice at 7:1, is the man in the tent. Genesis makes no attempt to soften the sequence and
  no attempt to explain it; the New Testament, which praises Noah four times (Hebrews 11:7, 1 Peter
  3:20, 2 Peter 2:5), never mentions the vineyard.
- [[Work]] — the third occupation named in Genesis, after keeping sheep and tilling ground, and the
  first new one since the flood. *ʾÎš hāʾădāmâ*, man of the ground, is a job description built from the
  noun that has carried the curse since 3:17.
- [[City and Civilization]] — viticulture added to the register of cultural firsts that 4:20–22
  started. The pattern holds from Cain's line to Noah's: Genesis names the origin of a craft and
  immediately shows what it costs.
- [[Judgment]] — delegated further than [[The Covenant with Noah]] delegated it. 9:6 handed the
  requiring of blood to human hands; 9:25 has a human pronouncing a sentence on his own authority,
  with no offence stated against the person sentenced and no divine ratification anywhere in the text.
- [[Life and Death]] — 9:29 closes the antediluvian formula. *"And then he died"* has been said nine
  times in Genesis 5 and is said here for the tenth and last time; after Noah, the register in Genesis
  11 drops the death clause entirely.
- [[Covenant]] — by contrast. Twelve verses after a covenant made with *"every living creature"* on no
  conditions at all, a man divides his own three sons into a blessed line, an enlarged line, and a
  slave line. The universality of 9:8–17 and the partition of 9:25–27 sit in the same chapter.

## Notes

- **The "curse of Ham" and the defence of slavery.** For roughly fifteen centuries these verses were
  used to sanction the enslavement of Black Africans — in medieval Islamic and Jewish sources that
  attached blackness to Ham's line, through the Atlantic slave trade, into the nineteenth-century
  American proslavery apologetic, and on into the twentieth-century racial doctrines of several
  churches. It is the single most consequential misreading of a passage in the history of biblical
  interpretation, and the wiki names it as a misreading on grounds internal to the text:

  1. **Ham is not cursed.** Canaan is, three times, and Ham never.
  2. **God does not curse anyone.** Noah does, and Noah has just come off a bender. God does not speak
     in the pericope, does not ratify the oracle, and is never afterwards shown acting on it.
  3. **The servitude named is to Shem and Japheth**, whose lines Genesis 10 puts in Mesopotamia, Aram,
     Arabia, Anatolia, and the Aegean. It is a statement about Israel's neighbours, not about a
     continent.
  4. **Ham's line in Genesis 10:6 is Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan** — Nubia, Egypt, Libya, and the
     Levant. The curse falls on the one of the four who lived in Israel's own back yard, and on none of
     the African ones.
  5. **Nothing in the Bible connects the curse to skin colour.** The association is absent from the
     Hebrew, from the Septuagint, and from the New Testament; it enters through post-biblical
     tradition.
  6. **Scripture never invokes 9:25.** No conquest text, prophet, psalm, or epistle cites Noah's curse
     as a warrant for anything. When the Torah explains the dispossession of the Canaanites it appeals
     to what they did (Leviticus 18:24–30, Deuteronomy 9:4–5), and when Joshua 9 puts Canaanites into
     perpetual service it is the outcome of a broken treaty. The verse's whole career as a proof-text
     happens outside the canon.

  This note is repeated in substance on [[Ham]], because the association attaches to the name, and is
  developed against the conquest material on [[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]].

- **On slavery in the oracle itself.** Setting the racial misreading aside does not make the passage
  comfortable, and the wiki does not pretend otherwise. 9:25 is a text in which a man consigns a whole
  people to hereditary servitude, and Genesis reports it without a word of criticism. Two things can
  be said and neither is a defence. First, the narrator's silence is the same silence he keeps over
  the drunkenness, over Lamech's boast at 4:23–24, and over most of what people do in the primeval
  history; Genesis routinely reports without endorsing, and its habit of declining to editorialize is
  visible in every pericope so far. Second, the oracle is human speech, uniquely so — it is the first
  thing in Genesis that a person says which shapes the future, and the book gives it no divine
  underwriting. What it does not permit is a reading in which God authorizes the arrangement, because
  God is not in the scene.

- **Why the doubled gloss matters.** *"Ham is the father of Canaan"* at 9:18 is unnecessary — 9:22
  will say it again — and 9:18 as a whole repeats information the reader has had four times. Both
  features are the kind of seam that source criticism reads as a join, and the standard analysis has
  9:18–27 as J (Yahweh at 9:26, an etiological interest in nations, a wholly anthropomorphic scene
  with no divine speech) spliced to the P obituary at 9:28–29, whose formula is Genesis 5's. Whatever
  the compositional history, the finished text uses the repetition to a clear end: it tells you three
  times, before and during the offence, that the man who sees is the father of the man who will be
  cursed. The reader is not allowed to be surprised by 9:25.

- **What Genesis 10 does with the oracle.** The Table of Nations immediately following does not
  mention the curse. It gives Ham four sons and Canaan eleven descendants, sets Canaan's border *"from
  Sidon… toward Gerar, to Gaza; as you go toward Sodom"* (10:19), and moves on. The two chapters make
  a pair — a moral partition and then a geographical one — but Genesis does not make the second depend
  on the first, and the descendants of Canaan appear in the Table on exactly the same footing as
  everyone else's.

- **Noah's arithmetic and the overlap with Abram.** 9:28–29 gives Noah 350 years after the flood.
  Genesis 11:10–26 gives the interval from the flood to Abram's birth as 2 + 35 + 30 + 34 + 30 + 32 +
  30 + 29 + 70 = 292 years. On the Masoretic figures Noah is therefore alive for Abram's first
  fifty-eight years, and Shem — who is 100 two years after the flood and lives 500 more — outlives
  Abram altogether. The Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint carry different numbers and produce
  different overlaps, so the calculation is a feature of one textual tradition rather than of the
  narrative. Genesis never brings the men into a room together and shows no interest in the fact,
  which is worth noticing precisely because later Jewish tradition found it irresistible (the
  *Sefer haYashar* and several midrashim have Abram taught in the house of Shem and Eber).

- **The first vineyard and the ancient Near East.** The Mesopotamian flood tradition also ends with a
  drink. In *Gilgamesh* XI, Utnapishtim gives the workmen who built the boat wine *"as if it were
  river water"* before the flood, and the gods, after it, swarm to the sacrifice; in *Atrahasis* the
  survivor's offering is likewise followed by feasting. The configuration Genesis shares — flood
  survivor, altar, drink — is old, and what Genesis does with it is not: the drink comes after the
  altar rather than before, it is planted by the survivor rather than given to him, and it produces
  not a feast but a family quarrel and a curse. As with the bow of 9:13 (see
  [[The Bow in the Cloud]]), the parallels establish a shared repertoire and not a direction of
  borrowing.
