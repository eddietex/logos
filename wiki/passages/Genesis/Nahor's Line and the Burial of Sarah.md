# Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 22:20–23:20

## Summary

**God has just sworn to give Abraham offspring like the stars and the gate of his enemies. The next
two things Genesis reports are a name in somebody else's family tree and a funeral.**

That is the whole shape of this pericope, and it is not an anticlimax — it is the book showing its
working. The covenant has two contents, **offspring** and **land**, and both are stated at their
largest at the end of chapter 22: *"I will multiply your offspring greatly like the stars of the
heavens, and like the sand which is on the seashore. Your offspring will possess the gate of his
enemies"* (22:17).
Then, immediately:

- **22:20–24** — news from Mesopotamia. Abraham's brother has twelve sons. The eighth of them has a
  daughter, and her name is Rebekah. That is the offspring promise, arriving as a clause in a list.
- **23:1–20** — Sarah dies, and Abraham buys a cave to put her in. That is the land promise, arriving
  as a field with a hole in it, paid for in cash, from its Hittite owner, in public.

**Neither unit mentions the promise.** Neither mentions God — the second is one of the very few
chapters in the Abraham cycle in which God neither speaks, acts, nor is invoked by anybody, and the
first mentions him only in the Hittites' compliment. The man who has just been sworn to by Yahweh
spends this pericope receiving a piece of family gossip and haggling — or refusing to haggle — over
real estate.

### *"After these things"*, twice

***ʾAḥărê haddəbārîm hāʾēlleh*** opens 22:1 (*"After these things, God tested Abraham"*) and opens
22:20 (*"After these things, Abraham was told"*) — the same four words nineteen verses apart, the
second of them tying the genealogy to the Akedah rather than to whatever else was happening in
Haran. **Genesis is saying that this news arrived after Moriah**, and it is saying so at the moment
Isaac's marriage has become the outstanding question in the book.

### 22:20–24 — twelve sons, and the one that matters

> **20** After these things, Abraham was told, “Behold, Milcah, she also has borne children to your
> brother Nahor:
> **21** Uz his firstborn, Buz his brother, Kemuel the father of Aram,
> **22** Chesed, Hazo, Pildash, Jidlaph, and Bethuel.”
> **23** Bethuel became the father of Rebekah. These eight Milcah bore to Nahor, Abraham’s brother.
> **24** His concubine, whose name was Reumah, also bore Tebah, Gaham, Tahash, and Maacah.

**Abraham is *told*.** *Wayyuggad lə-ʾAbrāhām* — a passive with no agent. Somebody brought word, and
Genesis does not say who, or when, or how far the news had travelled. This is the **only piece of
news from Mesopotamia in the entire Abraham cycle**; the family that stayed behind at
[[Haran (city)]] has been off the page since 11:31, and it surfaces once, here, for four verses, and
then supplies the wives of the next two generations.

***"Milcah, she also has borne children"*** — *gam-hîʾ*, **she also**, a comparative with nobody
named on the other side of it. The obvious candidate is [[Sarah]], who has borne one son at ninety
and has one chapter left to live. [[Milcah]] has borne eight. Genesis puts the two women's
fruitfulness in the same breath as an unstated contrast, and the notice of Sarah's death follows in
the next verse.

**Twelve sons, eight by the wife and four by the concubine.** The arithmetic is the point, and it is
the first of three twelves in Genesis built the same way:

| father | primary wife | second woman | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nahor** | Milcah, 8 | Reumah, 4 | **12** (22:20–24) |
| **Ishmael** | — | — | **12 princes** (17:20; 25:13–16) |
| **Jacob** | Leah 6, Rachel 2 | Bilhah 2, Zilpah 2 | **12** (35:22–26) |

Ishmael's twelve are promised before any of them exists — *"He will become the father of twelve
princes"* (17:20) — and delivered as a list eleven chapters later. **Genesis gives three men twelve
sons and makes a nation out of exactly one of them.** See
[[Twelve Sons - Nahor, Ishmael, and Jacob]].

**The names are a map, not a family.** Several of them are peoples and places elsewhere in Scripture:

- **Uz** — the land [[Job (person)]] comes from (Job 1:1), placed in Edom by Lamentations 4:21 and
  in Aramean territory by Genesis 10:23; a third Uz is Horite at 36:28. **The Bible has three
  Uzzes pointing in two directions**, and this is one of them. See [[Uz]].
- **Buz** — paired with Dedan and Tema in Jeremiah 25:23, so a north Arabian locality; and the home
  of Elihu the Buzite, the fourth speaker in Job (32:2).
- **Kemuel the father of Aram** — *Aram* is the Aramean people, and this verse makes them Abraham's
  brother's grandsons. Genesis 10:22 had already made Aram a son of Shem. The book is describing
  the Aramean tribal map twice, by two different routes, and does not reconcile them.
- **Chesed** — *Keśed*, and the plural *Kaśdîm* is **Chaldeans**. Abraham came out of
  *ʾÛr Kaśdîm*, [[Ur of the Chaldees]] (11:28, 31). **A nephew of Abraham is named for the people
  whose city Abraham left**, which is either an etymology Genesis is offering for the Chaldeans or a
  coincidence it declines to notice.
- **Maacah** — later a small Aramean kingdom below Hermon, named beside Geshur (Deuteronomy 3:14;
  2 Samuel 10:6).
- **Tebah** — compare *Tebah*, a city of Hadadezer of Zobah taken by David (2 Samuel 8:8).
- **Hazo, Pildash, Jidlaph, Gaham, Tahash** — attested nowhere else in the Bible as anything.

**And then the clause the whole list exists for.** *"Bethuel became the father of Rebekah."* One
granddaughter, named out of nowhere, in a register of sons. [[Rebekah]] is the only person in these
five verses the narrative will ever need, and the only reason [[Bethuel]] — the last-listed and
least-described of the eight — is worth a syllable. **Genesis 24 opens two verses later with
Abraham sending a servant back to *"my country, and to my relatives"* for a wife, and the reader has
already been told where she is.** It is the tidiest piece of narrative preparation in the book.

**Reumah, and the Bible's first concubine.** *"His concubine, whose name was Reumah"* — *pîlegeš*,
and **this is its first occurrence in Scripture**. The word describes a secondary wife of lower legal
standing, not a mistress; her sons are counted in the family total, which is why the twelve works.
Note what the arrangement is *not* said to have cost: **Abraham's household ran the same structure —
a wife, a second woman, and a son by each — and it produced [[Hagar]]'s flight, [[Ishmael]]'s
expulsion, and two chapters of grief.** Nahor's produces four names in a list and no comment
whatever. See [[Reumah]].

### 23:1–2 — the only woman in the Bible whose age is given

> **1** Sarah lived one hundred twenty-seven years. This was the length of Sarah’s life.
> **2** Sarah died in Kiriath Arba (also called Hebron), in the land of Canaan. Abraham came to mourn
> for Sarah, and to weep for her.

**No other woman in Scripture has her lifespan recorded.** Not Eve, not Rebekah, not Rachel, not
Miriam, not Deborah, not Ruth, not Esther. Genesis has spent eleven chapters giving men's ages to the
year, and it gives one woman's, and it gives it in the formal register it uses for the patriarchs —
the Hebrew repeats the word *year* after each numeral, the same construction used of Abraham at 25:7
and Ishmael at 25:17 — and then closes with a summary clause it uses for nobody else: *"This was the
length of Sarah's life."*

**The arithmetic dates the Akedah, and that is the problem.** She was ninety at Isaac's birth
(17:17) and dies at a hundred twenty-seven, so **Isaac is thirty-seven when his mother dies**. The
rabbinic tradition that makes him thirty-seven on Moriah gets there by reading chapters 22 and 23 as
consecutive — and then, in *Genesis Rabbah* and in Rashi, by making the news of the binding the cause
of her death. **Genesis says nothing of the kind.** It gives no cause, records no reaction from her
to chapter 22, and never puts her in the same verse as the event. The tradition is old, it is
attractive, and it is an inference; the wiki records it as one. See [[Isaac]] and
[[The Binding of Isaac]].

**She dies at Kiriath Arba, and he was somewhere else.** 22:19 left Abraham at [[Beersheba]] —
*"Abraham lived at Beersheba"* — and 23:2 has Sarah dying at [[Hebron]], some twenty-five miles
north up the ridge, with Abraham *coming* to mourn: *wayyābōʾ*, **he came**. Genesis does not say
they were living apart, does not say they were not, and does not remark on the gap at all. It is the
same silence it keeps about Isaac after 22:19. **What the book actually reports about this family
after Moriah is that they are in three different places** — Abraham at Beersheba, Sarah at Hebron,
Isaac at Beer Lahai Roi when the servant brings his bride home (24:62).

***"to mourn for Sarah, and to weep for her."*** Two verbs, in that order. *Sāpad* is the public
rite — the formal lament, the beating of the breast, the thing a household does where people can see
it. *Bākâ* is weeping. Genesis puts the ceremony first and the tears second, and gives the second
one word.

**A scribal note, three thousand years old.** In the Masoretic text the *kaf* of *wəlibkōtāh*, *and
to weep for her*, is written **small** — one of a handful of miniature letters the scribes preserved
without ever explaining. The tradition has always read it as saying something about the size of the
weeping, and every explanation offered (that his grief was private, that it was brief, that he had
already accepted the loss) is a reading of a letter shape, not of a word. **It is a genuine feature
of the transmitted text and not a genuine piece of evidence about Abraham**, and the wiki records it
as the first of those and not the second.

### 23:3–20 — a legal document with a bereavement at the front

The rest of the chapter is a property conveyance, and it is told with a precision Genesis uses for
nothing else. Seven moves, three of them bows:

| verses | move |
|---|---|
| 3–4 | Abraham stands up from beside the body and asks the children of Heth for *a possession of a burying-place* |
| 5–6 | They answer: *you are a prince of God among us* — use the best of our tombs |
| 7–9 | He bows; names his target — **Ephron's cave of Machpelah** — and states his terms: *for the full price* |
| 10–11 | Ephron, who has been sitting there the whole time, offers the field **and** the cave, publicly, free |
| 12–13 | He bows again; refuses again: *I will give the price of the field* |
| 14–15 | Ephron: *what is a piece of land worth four hundred shekels of silver between me and you?* |
| 16 | Abraham weighs out four hundred shekels, merchants' standard |
| 17–20 | The deed: field, cave, and every tree in it, witnessed at the gate — and Sarah is buried |

**Every refusal in this chapter is part of the sale.** Ephron does not offer a gift and Abraham does
not decline one; this is how a price gets named in a society where naming it outright would be an
insult. *"I give you the field, and I give you the cave that is in it"* is the seller declaring the
lot he is willing to part with. *"What is a piece of land worth four hundred shekels of silver
between me and you?"* is the number, phrased so that it never has to be demanded. And Abraham's part
is to keep refusing until the number is on the table, and then **to pay it without a word of
counter-offer** — which is the one move in the whole exchange that is not standard.

### *"I am a stranger and a foreigner living with you"*

> **4** “I am a stranger and a foreigner living with you. Give me a possession of a burying-place
> with you, that I may bury my dead out of my sight.”

***Gēr wətôšāb ʾānōkî ʿimmākem.*** Both nouns at once. *Gēr* is the resident alien, the protected
outsider Israelite law will spend three books insisting on (Exodus 22:21; Leviticus 19:34);
*tôšāb* is the settler with no title to anything. **This is the flattest statement of his position
Abraham ever makes**, and he makes it in the sentence where he asks to own something — the theme's
whole tension in one verse. See [[Land and Sojourning]].

The pair goes on to have a career. Leviticus 25:23 turns it around and applies it to Israel in the
land: *"The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are strangers and
live as foreigners with me."* David prays it back at 1 Chronicles 29:15 and the psalmist at
Psalm 39:12. And Hebrews 11:13 makes it the patriarchs' own confession — *"strangers and pilgrims on
the earth"* — with 11:9–10 supplying the reason: they were *"looking for the city which has
foundations."* See [[A Stranger and a Foreigner - The Sojourner's Confession]].

***"You are a prince of God among us."*** *Nəśîʾ ʾĕlōhîm* — a prince of God, or, read as a
superlative, **a mighty prince**. Set it beside what he has just called himself. **He says
*foreigner*; they say *prince*; and neither valuation gets him a grave without four hundred
shekels.** The Hittites are perfectly courteous, perfectly generous in words, and perfectly
immovable on the one thing at issue, which is whether an outsider may hold land among them. What
they offer — *"Bury your dead in the best of our tombs"* — is hospitality. What he asks for is
title.

### Why he will not take it as a gift

Nothing in the chapter says. But the chapter is unusually clear about what he does instead, and it
says it twice:

> **17** So the field of Ephron, which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field, the cave
> which was in it, and all the trees that were in the field, that were in all of its borders, were
> deeded
> **18** to Abraham for a possession in the presence of the children of Heth, before all who went in
> at the gate of his city.

> **20** The field, and the cave that is in it, were deeded to Abraham by the children of Heth as a
> possession for a burial place.

**A gift can be taken back; a purchase witnessed at the gate cannot.** The plain reading is that
Abraham is not buying a cave, he is buying a *title* — the one thing in Canaan that will still be his
family's when the goodwill of the children of Heth has moved on, and the only ground in the promised
land any of them will hold for four hundred years. He has been promised *"all the land of Canaan,
for an everlasting possession"* (17:8) — *ʾăḥuzzat ʿôlām* — and the word he uses here for what he
wants, and the word the deed uses twice, is that same *ʾăḥuzzâ*, **holding**. **He is asking for the
smallest possible instance of the promised thing, under the promise's own legal noun, and paying
cash for it.**

**Note where the chapter ends.** Verse 19 buries Sarah. Verse 20 goes back to the deed and restates
it. **The last sentence of the chapter is about the title, not about the grave** — which is a
strange way to end a funeral and exactly the right way to end a conveyance.

### The mechanics: silver, witnesses, and a scope clause

***"Abraham weighed to Ephron the silver … four hundred shekels of silver, according to the current
merchants' standard."*** There is no coinage in this world; *wayyišqōl*, **he weighed**, is the verb,
and *ʿōbēr lassōḥēr* — silver *passing to the merchant* — certifies the weight as commercially
accepted. It is the sort of clause a scribe writes and a storyteller does not.

**Four hundred shekels is a large number, and Genesis does not say whether it is a fair one.** The
Bible supplies its own comparisons and they do not flatter the price:

| transaction | price | reference |
|---|---|---|
| a slave gored by an ox | 30 shekels | Exodus 21:32 |
| Jeremiah's field at Anathoth | 17 shekels | Jeremiah 32:9 |
| Araunah's threshing floor and oxen | 50 shekels of silver | 2 Samuel 24:24 |
| **Machpelah: field, cave, and trees** | **400 shekels** | **Genesis 23:16** |
| the hill of Samaria — a whole city site | 2 talents (6,000 shekels) | 1 Kings 16:24 |

Readers have drawn opposite conclusions from this — that Ephron gouged him, or that a large sum
publicly paid is precisely what makes a title unassailable. **The text grades neither the price nor
the seller**, which is consistent with a book that has just declined to grade five pericopes in a
row.

**The transaction is made durable by being heard.** *"in the hearing of the children of Heth, even of
all who went in at the gate of his city"* (23:10), and again at 23:18. The gate is where legal
business happens in the Hebrew Bible, and the closest parallel in Scripture is Ruth 4:1–11, which
convenes ten elders at the gate of Bethlehem and narrates a land transfer at the same deliberate
pace. **No document is signed here. The public is the archive** — the same logic as the seven ewe
lambs two chapters ago, where an object was appointed to outlive a memory. See
[[The Treaty at Beersheba]].

**And the scope clause is itemized like a deed**: *"the field, the cave which was in it, and all the
trees that were in the field, that were in all of its borders"* Land, structure, standing timber,
boundaries. Genesis does not describe property this way anywhere else.

### The children of Heth, and who they were

*Bənê-ḥēt.* **Heth is a son of Canaan in the Table of Nations** (10:15), which makes these people
Canaanites by Genesis's own reckoning. Whether they have anything to do with the Anatolian Hittite
empire, with the Neo-Hittite states of northern Syria, or with a local group Genesis simply calls by
that name, is unresolved and probably unresolvable. Esau marries two of their women in the same
country (26:34; 27:46), and Ezekiel tells Jerusalem *"your father was an Amorite, and your mother a
Hittite"* (16:3). See [[The Children of Heth]].

**A proposal worth naming and not adopting.** It has been argued — most influentially by M. R.
Lehmann in 1953 — that the chapter reflects **Hittite** land law specifically: the Hittite Laws
§46–47 attach feudal obligations to a whole holding, so a buyer would prefer part of a field and a
seller would prefer to unload all of it, which is exactly the shape of the negotiation, since
Abraham asks for the cave *"which is in the end of his field"* and Ephron insists on selling field
and cave together. The objections are serious: the Hittite empire's law did not run in Hebron at any
date proposed for Abraham, the relevant provision is disputed, and the same bargaining shape occurs
in Mesopotamian and later Near Eastern conveyances that have nothing to do with Hatti. **The wiki
records the observation as an observation about the negotiation's shape, and not as evidence that
these are Anatolian Hittites.**

### What is actually accomplished

**Abraham arrives in Canaan at 12:5 and dies at 25:8, and in between he owns one field.** He has
built altars at Shechem, between Bethel and Ai, and at Mamre; he has pitched tents; he has dug a
well and planted a tamarisk and taken a treaty for the right to stay. **The only ground he ever holds
by title is the plot he buys to bury his wife in**, and Genesis will bury him in it too (25:9–10),
and Isaac and Rebekah and Leah, and Jacob will make his sons swear to carry him back to it from
Egypt (49:29–32; 50:13). **Six graves, one deed, four hundred shekels.** See [[Machpelah]] and
[[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].

Acts 7:5 states the balance with no softening at all: *"He gave him no inheritance in it, no, not so
much as to set his foot on. He promised that he would give it to him for a possession, and to his
offspring after him, when he still had no child."* **A man with no land and no child is promised
land and children; by the end of this pericope he has a grave and a grandniece.** That is not the
promise fulfilled. It is the promise given something to stand on.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — receives the one piece of news from home in the whole cycle, buries his wife of at
  least sixty-two years, and buys the only ground he will ever own; he bows three times, refuses a
  gift twice, and pays a large price without a word of counter-offer.
- [[Sarah]] — dies at a hundred twenty-seven at Kiriath Arba, the **only woman in Scripture whose
  lifespan is recorded**, in a formal register otherwise reserved for the patriarchs; her death is
  the first thing reported after the chapter that nearly ended her son, and Genesis gives no cause.
- [[Ephron the Hittite]] — the owner of the field, sitting among his people the whole time; he offers
  everything free twice and names four hundred shekels once, in a rhetorical question, and never
  demands anything.
- [[The Children of Heth]] — the assembly at the gate: courteous, generous in words, and the reason
  the transaction is legally durable. They call Abraham *a prince of God* and sell him a cave.
- [[Milcah]] — mother of eight in the notice that reaches Abraham, and grandmother of Rebekah; the
  maternal line of Israel runs through the branch that never left Mesopotamia.
- [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]] — the brother left off the travel list at 11:31, now reported with
  twelve sons by two women; his household will supply wives for the next two generations.
- [[Bethuel]] — the eighth and last of Milcah's sons, named for one reason and given one clause:
  *"Bethuel became the father of Rebekah."*
- [[Rebekah]] — named here, one verse, decades before she appears; the only granddaughter in a
  register of sons, and the answer to a question chapter 24 has not asked yet.
- [[Reumah]] — Nahor's concubine, the **first *pîlegeš* in the Bible**, and the mother of four of his
  twelve; her sons are counted in the family total and nothing else is said about her.
- [[Isaac]] — thirty-seven when his mother dies, on the chapter's own arithmetic; absent from the
  pericope, as he has been since 22:19, and unmentioned at her funeral.
- [[Hagar]] — off-stage, and the reason Reumah's four verses are worth reading twice: the same
  household structure, in the other branch, with none of the consequences.

## Places

- [[Hebron]] — *Kiriath Arba*, where Sarah dies and where the family plot is bought; the patriarchs'
  address, their graveyard, and later David's first capital.
- [[Machpelah]] — the field and cave, *before Mamre*, deeded to Abraham for four hundred shekels;
  **the only real property any patriarch owns in Canaan**, and the grave of six of them.
- [[Mamre (place)]] — the surveyor's benchmark. The deed is fixed by it (*"which was before Mamre"*),
  and Genesis repeats the phrase at 23:19, 25:9, 49:30, and 50:13.
- [[Canaan]] — named twice in the chapter, at the death and at the burial, as though to fix the
  transaction inside the promised territory.
- [[Beersheba]] — where 22:19 left Abraham living, and where he is not when his wife dies.
- [[Uz]] — the land of Job, and here the name of Nahor's firstborn; one of the Bible's three Uzzes.
- [[Ur of the Chaldees]] — the city Abraham came out of, and the people *Chesed* stands behind.
- [[Haran (city)]] — where the news comes from, and where Abraham's servant will go for a wife.

## Themes

- [[Land and Sojourning]] — *"I am a stranger and a foreigner living with you"*, said by the man
  promised the whole country, in the sentence where he asks to buy part of it. The pericope is the
  theme's centre of gravity.
- [[Burial and the Grave]] — the first burial in Genesis given more than a clause, and the one that
  sets the pattern: a named place, a purchased title, and a family that keeps coming back to it.
- [[Life and Death]] — Sarah's death, uniquely dated, uncaused, and unmourned by anyone the text
  names but her husband.
- [[Covenant]] — offspring and land, the covenant's two contents, delivered in miniature and
  back-to-back: a granddaughter's name and a field.
- [[Marriage]] — a wife and a concubine in Nahor's house, a wife dead in Abraham's, and a bride named
  three chapters before she is fetched.
- [[The Nations]] — the negotiation is with Canaanites, conducted in their forum, under their law,
  and they behave impeccably throughout.
- [[Hospitality]] — offered generously and refused deliberately: the one thing Abraham will not
  accept from the children of Heth is their kindness.
- [[Blessing]] — *"You are a prince of God among us"*: 12:2's *great name*, read off him by the
  people whose land he is asking for.
- [[Faith]] — Hebrews 11:13's reading of this verse: they *"confessed that they were strangers and
  pilgrims on the earth"*, and bought a grave in the country they were promised.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's split stands and **no unchecked boxes were redrawn**.
  22:20 opens with *"After these things"* and 24:1 opens a new self-contained narrative with its own
  cast, so the unit is clean at both ends. The live alternative — attaching 22:20–24 to chapter 24,
  since the genealogy exists to introduce Rebekah and does nothing else — was considered and
  rejected: the notice is tied to the Akedah by its own opening formula, and both halves of this
  pericope do the same job, which is to deliver a miniature of a covenant promise immediately after
  the promise's largest statement. Keeping them together is what makes that visible. The reverse
  case — splitting 23:1–20 off as its own pericope — is defensible on length alone and would cost
  nothing but the juxtaposition.
- **What Genesis does not say about Sarah's death.** No cause. No reaction from Isaac, who is not
  mentioned. No connection to chapter 22, which the placement invites and the text never makes. No
word from her since 21:10, where the last thing she said was *"Cast out this servant and her son!"*.
  **The book gives her a lifespan and a burial and no death scene**, which is more than it
  gives any other woman in Scripture and less than the reader is by now expecting.
- **On the rabbinic reading that the Akedah killed her.** *Genesis Rabbah* 58:5 and Rashi on 23:2
  connect the two chapters causally, in a tradition where Satan or Isaac himself brings her the news;
  Josephus does not. It is a reading of the sequence, not of a statement, and the wiki names it as
  tradition. What is checkable is only the arithmetic: 17:17 and 23:1 together put Isaac at
  thirty-seven when she dies, which is where the rabbinic age for Isaac on Moriah comes from and is
  not itself a statement about Moriah.
- **On the small *kaf*.** The miniature letter in *wəlibkōtāh* (23:2) is a real and ancient feature of
  the Masoretic text, transmitted with no explanation attached. Every meaning proposed for it is a
  later reading. It is recorded here because it is part of the text's history, not because it tells
  us anything about Abraham's grief.
- **On the price.** Four hundred shekels is not commented on by Genesis and the Bible's other
  recorded prices make it look high. Both standing readings — that Ephron was a smooth operator who
  named an outrageous number in the politest possible sentence, and that a large sum weighed out in
  public is exactly what makes a title unchallengeable — are compatible with every word of the
  chapter. **The wiki records both and adopts neither.** What the text does say, twice, is that the
  transfer took effect.
- **On the Hittite-law proposal.** Lehmann's 1953 argument that the negotiation reflects Hittite
  Laws §46–47 is widely cited and widely doubted; the provision's reading is contested, the Hittite
  empire's law is not plausibly operating in Hebron, and the whole-field-versus-part-field shape is
  common to conveyance narratives across the region. Named here as a proposal about the negotiation's
  form, not as an identification of these people.
- **On Acts 7:16.** Stephen says the patriarchs were *"carried over to Shechem, and laid in the tomb
  that Abraham bought for a price in silver from the sons of Hamor of Shechem."* Genesis has **two**
  purchases: Abraham buys at Hebron from Ephron the Hittite (23), and Jacob buys at Shechem from the
  sons of Hamor (33:19); Joseph is buried at Shechem (Joshua 24:32) and Jacob at Machpelah (50:13).
  Stephen's sentence merges them. The readings on offer — a compression in a summarising speech, an
  independent tradition, or Luke reporting a speech accurately including its compression — are all
  live, and the discrepancy is **flagged rather than harmonised**, per the schema. See
  [[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].
- **On the pîlegeš.** Genesis 22:24 is the first occurrence of the noun in the Bible, and Genesis
  uses it afterwards of Abraham's own secondary wives (25:6), of Bilhah (35:22), and of Reuben's
  offence against her. That Nahor's arrangement is reported without comment, in the same book that
  spends two chapters on what Abraham's cost, is the sort of asymmetry Genesis produces constantly
  and never remarks on.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Rebekah, Bethuel, and Machpelah all get pages here on the
  strength of one verse each, because each is load-bearing for material two chapters away; their
  pages say plainly what is still ahead. The eleven other sons of Nahor — Uz, Buz, Kemuel, Chesed,
  Hazo, Pildash, Jidlaph, Tebah, Gaham, Tahash, and Maacah — **were deliberately not given pages**,
  on the precedent of [[The Table of Nations]], whose seventy names are likewise handled on the
  passage page. They are discussed above and on [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]]; if any of them recurs
  in a way that earns a page, it can be made then.
- **A collision that did not happen.** [[Uz]] is a place page (the land of Job) and 22:21 names a
  person. Under the `AGENTS.md` person/place rule both would need qualifiers — but only if both
  pages exist, and no person page was created, so the bare `Uz.md` stands. **If a later pass gives
  Nahor's firstborn a page it must be `Uz (son of Nahor)`**, and the place page should then be
  reconsidered; the patronymic is enough on its own, so no rename of `Uz.md` is required. This note
  exists so the next agent does not create a second `Uz.md` by accident.
