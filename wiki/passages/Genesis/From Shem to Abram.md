# From Shem to Abram

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 11:10–32

## Summary

The primeval history ends the way it has ended twice before — with a list. But this list has had
something taken out of it, and it stops on a sentence that no genealogy can absorb.

Genesis 11:10–32 is two *toledot* headings in twenty-three verses, the only pericope in the book
that contains two. *"This is the history of the generations of Shem"* (11:10) reruns the register
of [[From Adam to Noah]] on the far side of the flood: ten more generations, one man per
generation, an age at fatherhood and a remainder of years, ending with a man at a round age
fathering three sons. Then *"Now this is the history of the generations of Terah"* (11:27) drops
the register entirely and gives four verses of family — a death, two marriages, a barren wife, and
a journey that stops halfway. Between them, Genesis crosses from the history of everybody to the
history of one household, and it does it without a break in the paragraph.

**The formula has lost its last clause.** Genesis 5 ran its entries in three parts: *X lived n
years and fathered Y; X lived m years after that and fathered other sons and daughters; all the
days of X were n+m years, **and then he died***. Genesis 11 keeps the first two parts and deletes
the third. Nine entries, nine fatherings, nine remainders — and no totals and no deaths. Not one
man in 11:10–26 is said to die. The refrain that was the whole argument of Genesis 5 is simply
gone, and Genesis never says why.

What replaces it is arithmetic of a different kind. **The numbers fall off a cliff, twice.** Noah
lived 950 years and Shem 600; Arpachshad, Shelah, and Eber run 438, 433, and 464; and then Peleg,
Reu, Serug, and Nahor run 239, 239, 230, and 148. Two halvings in ten generations, and the ages at
fatherhood collapse with them — the men of Genesis 5 father between 65 and 187, and the men of
Genesis 11, once past Shem's hundred, father at 35, 30, 34, 30, 32, 30, 29. By the end of the
register a man's whole life is shorter than the gap Genesis 5 could put between one birth and the
next. Nobody in the chapter remarks on it. It is the sentence of 6:3 — *"his days will be one
hundred twenty years"* — being carried out on the page rather than announced, and it will keep
falling: Abraham 175, Isaac 180, Jacob 147, Joseph 110, and at last *"the days of our years are
seventy"* (Psalm 90:10).

**Then the second heading, and the machinery stops working.** Terah fathers Abram, Nahor, and
Haran; Haran fathers Lot; and then, in the middle of a genealogy, comes a verse that is not a
genealogical statement at all: *"Haran died before his father Terah in the land of his birth, in Ur
of the Chaldees"* (11:28). It is the pericope's first death, it is out of order, and it is the
first time in Scripture that a son dies before his father. The register that refused to record nine
deaths records this one, with a place attached.

Two verses later comes the sentence the whole book turns on. *"Sarai was barren. She had no child"*
(11:30) — and the Hebrew is as flat and as doubled as the English, *wattəhî śāray ʿăqārâ ʾên lāh
wālād*, the fact and then the fact again. For nine generations the text has done nothing but father
sons. Here it hits a wall, and the wall is a woman named in the same breath as the promise that has
not been made yet. Everything from Genesis 12 to Genesis 21 — the call, the covenant, the laughter,
Hagar, the three visitors, Isaac — is the working-out of this half-verse. Genesis states it and
moves on without comment, which is the most effective thing it could have done with it.

**And then the family leaves, and does not arrive.** *"Terah took Abram his son, Lot the son of
Haran, his son's son, and Sarai his daughter-in-law … They went from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into
the land of Canaan. They came to Haran and lived there"* (11:31). The destination is named — this
is the first time in the Bible that anyone sets out for [[Canaan]] — and it is not reached. They
stop at a city whose name in English is the name of the dead son, settle, and the pericope's last
verse buries Terah there: *"The days of Terah were two hundred five years. Terah died in Haran."*

So the primeval history closes on a man who started for the land of promise and died short of it,
in a household with a barren wife and a dead son, four hundred miles from where the promise will
be spoken. The next verse is *"Now Yahweh said to Abram, 'Get out of your country…'"*. Genesis has
spent twenty-three verses building a dead end so that a call can be heard as a rescue.

### What the register does, and what it will not do

Read forwards, 11:10–26 is unremarkable — a bridge. Read against Genesis 5, it is a set of
deliberate subtractions, and each one points the same direction.

- **No deaths.** Genesis 5's argument was *"then he died"*, eight times, proving the sentence of
  2:17 by attrition. That argument has been made and the flood has settled it; the second register
  is not proving mortality, it is delivering a family. See
  [[Ten Before and Ten After - The Registers of Genesis 5 and Genesis 11]].
- **No comment.** Genesis 5 broke its own form twice, for Enoch and for Lamech's speech. Genesis 11
  never breaks. No one walks with God, no one is taken, no one says anything. The only human speech
  in the whole of Genesis 11 was at Babel, and it was *"let's make a name for ourselves."*
- **No Yahweh.** God does not appear in these twenty-three verses. He does not act, is not named,
  and is not addressed. He was last seen scattering the builders at 11:9 and is next heard at 12:1.
  The genealogy is the silence between them.
- **And no total.** Nine men are given a birth, a son, and a remainder, and then left open — the
  device Genesis 5:32 used once, on Noah, to hold the reader, applied here to the entire list. The
  only total in the pericope belongs to Terah, and it is a death notice.

### The chronology, if the numbers are added up

As with Genesis 5, the text never does this arithmetic, and the totals below are not what it
asserts — but the figures are given, they interlock, and they are the framework every traditional
chronology of the patriarchal period was built on. Years are *anno mundi* on the same reckoning as
[[From Adam to Noah]], where Adam is year 0 and the flood falls in 1656.

| Generation | Age at fathering | Remaining years | Total (not stated) | Born | Died |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shem | 100 | 500 | 600 | 1558 | 2158 |
| Arpachshad | 35 | 403 | 438 | 1658 | 2096 |
| Shelah | 30 | 403 | 433 | 1693 | 2126 |
| Eber | 34 | 430 | 464 | 1723 | 2187 |
| Peleg | 30 | 209 | 239 | 1757 | 1996 |
| Reu | 32 | 207 | 239 | 1787 | 2026 |
| Serug | 30 | 200 | 230 | 1819 | 2049 |
| Nahor | 29 | 119 | 148 | 1849 | 1997 |
| Terah | 70 | — | 205 (stated) | 1878 | 2083 |

Four things fall out of it that the text does not say:

- **The flood to Abram's birth is 292 years** — 2 + 35 + 30 + 34 + 30 + 32 + 30 + 29 + 70 — if
  Abram is Terah's firstborn. Ten generations from Adam to the flood took sixteen centuries; ten
  from Shem to Abram take under three.
- **[[Shem]] outlives Abram.** He dies in 2158 and Abram, at 175, dies in 2123. On these figures the
  son of Noah is alive for the whole of Abraham's life, and [[Noah]] himself is alive for its first
  fifty-eight years. Genesis supplies the numbers, never remarks on them, and never has the two men
  meet.
- **[[Eber]] outlives everyone**, Shem included, dying in 2187 — sixty-four years after Abram. The
  man the word *Hebrew* stands next to is the last survivor of the pre-Abrahamic world.
- **[[Peleg]] and Nahor die first**, in 1996 and 1997, when Abram would be 48 and 49. The two
  shortest lives in the register belong to the man named for a division and to Abram's own
  grandfather.
- **Three of the nine are survived by their own fathers** — [[Arpachshad]] by Shem, Peleg by Eber,
  and [[Nahor (son of Serug)]] by [[Serug]]. In a register that has deliberately stopped recording
  deaths, its own numbers keep producing the thing Genesis 11:28 singles out as worth reporting.

The one place the arithmetic openly fights the text is the departure from Haran, and Genesis and
Acts pull in opposite directions on it — see the note on Terah's 205 years below, and
[[Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call]].

## People

- [[Shem]] — the register's head, and the only man in it whose age at fatherhood is given a fixed
  point in time: *"one hundred years old … two years after the flood."* That clause is the hinge
  that ties the whole post-flood chronology to the pre-flood one, and it also creates a small
  friction with 5:32, where Noah fathers his three sons at 500 — which would put Shem's hundredth
  year in the flood year itself, not two years after it.
- [[Arpachshad]] — Shem's third-listed son (10:22) and the one the line runs through, born two
  years after the flood; the first person in the Bible born into the new world. The Septuagint puts
  an extra generation, Cainan, immediately after him, and Luke 3:36 follows the Greek.
- [[Shelah]] — the third generation, and a name that is also the second element of *Methuselah*.
  Given 433 years and nothing else.
- [[Eber]] — already introduced four generations early at 10:21, as the descendant Shem's entire
  branch was named by. Here he gets his numbers: 464 years, the longest life in the register after
  Shem's, and on these figures the last of them to die.
- [[Peleg]] — the fork. His name and its explanation were given in
  [[The Table of Nations]]; here the branch that ran through his brother Joktan is not mentioned,
  and his 239 years are the first of the four short lives.
- [[Reu]] — the sixth generation; a name that is the ordinary Hebrew word for *friend*, and a name
  that turns up as a place in the Balikh valley near Haran, as three others in this list do.
- [[Serug]] — the seventh; *Sarugi* in Assyrian records, a town a day's walk from Haran.
- [[Nahor (son of Serug)]] — the eighth, and the shortest life in the Bible so far at 148 years;
  Abram's grandfather, and the namesake of his grandson.
- [[Terah]] — the man both halves of the pericope converge on. He ends the register at a round
  seventy with three sons, exactly as Noah ended the first at a round five hundred with three; then
  he takes over as the subject of his own *toledot*, buries a son, leads the family out of Ur
  toward Canaan, stops at Haran, and dies there at 205. He is the only man in the pericope given a
  total, and the only one said to die twice over — once in the summary of 11:32 and once, in
  Stephen's retelling, as the reason his son could finally leave.
- [[Abraham]] — *Abram* here, and for six more chapters. He is listed first of Terah's three sons
  and is probably not the eldest, on the same pattern as Shem; he is given a wife, no son, and no
  words. Nothing in this pericope suggests he is anyone in particular. He is the last name in the
  register and the first in the story.
- [[Sarah]] — *Sarai* here. Named, given a husband, and then given the pericope's decisive sentence:
  *"Sarai was barren. She had no child."* Her parentage is conspicuously omitted while Milcah's is
  supplied, which is the seed of an old and unresolvable question about who she was.
- [[Haran (son of Terah)]] — Terah's son, Lot's father, and probably Milcah's; he dies in Ur, before
  his father, in the first premature death in the Bible. He shares his English name with the city
  the family stops in, though not his Hebrew one.
- [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]] — the brother who stays. He marries his niece Milcah, is left behind
  in the account of the departure, and his household becomes the family Abraham and Isaac will both
  send back to for wives.
- [[Lot]] — introduced here as an orphan and attached to his grandfather's household; he is the only
  member of the third generation who travels, and the only relative Abram takes with him out of
  Haran.
- [[Milcah]] — Nahor's wife and Haran's daughter, named with her father when Sarai is not; through
  her come Bethuel, Rebekah, and Laban, which makes her the ancestress of the matriarchs of the next
  two generations.
- [[Iscah]] — named once in the Bible, in a subordinate clause, and never mentioned again. The whole
  of her afterlife is a rabbinic identification of her with Sarai.
- [[God]] — absent. He does not speak, act, or appear anywhere in these twenty-three verses; they
  are the gap between the scattering of 11:9 and the call of 12:1, and the silence is the pericope's
  most easily missed feature.
- [[Noah]] — not named, but the register's implicit starting point and, by its own numbers, still
  alive for the first fifty-eight years of Abram's life.

## Places

- [[Ur of the Chaldees]] — the family's origin, named twice (11:28, 11:31), and the first city in
  Genesis that anyone leaves rather than builds. *Kaśdîm*, "Chaldees", is an anachronism of some
  centuries whichever Ur is meant, and which Ur is meant — the great Sumerian city in the far south,
  or a smaller northern Ur near Haran — is genuinely open.
- [[Haran (city)]] — the caravan junction on the Balikh where the family stops, settles, and buries
  Terah. It is where Abram is called from, where Jacob will flee to, and where both Isaac's and
  Jacob's wives come from: the family's second home for three generations.
- [[Canaan]] — named as the destination and not reached. This is the land's first appearance as
  somewhere anyone is going; every previous mention placed it, described its borders, or cursed its
  ancestor.
- The Balikh valley more broadly — not named in the text, but Serug, Nahor, Terah, and possibly Reu
  all correspond to place names attested around Haran in Assyrian and Mari records. See the note
  below.

## Themes

- [[Life and Death]] — the theme this pericope handles by omission. The death refrain of Genesis 5
  is deleted from the register, and the two deaths that are recorded both belong to Terah's
  household: a son who dies before his father, and a father who dies short of the country he set out
  for.
- [[Barrenness]] — introduced here, in the flattest possible sentence, and immediately the engine of
  the next ten chapters. Genesis 11:30 is the first infertility in the Bible and the first obstacle
  the promise will have to overcome.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — also introduced here. *"They went from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into
  the land of Canaan. They came to Haran and lived there"* is the Bible's first journey toward the
  land, and it is a journey that stops.
- [[The Nations]] — the counterpart to Genesis 10. The Table gave seventy peoples their territories;
  this register pulls a single thread out of the map and follows it, and the seventy are not
  mentioned again until they are being blessed through it.
- [[Blessing]] — present only by absence. Nothing is blessed in these verses, and the blessing of
  9:1 has visibly stopped working: the lifespans are collapsing and the line ends in a barren woman.
  Then 12:2–3 uses the word five times in two verses.
- [[Marriage]] — *"Abram and Nahor married wives"* is the first marriage recorded since 6:2, and the
  first ever given names on both sides. Nahor marries his brother's daughter; Abram, by Genesis
  20:12, has married his half-sister.
- [[Judgment]] — not enacted, but its residue is the whole shape of the chapter. The scattering at
  Babel is why there are nations to be blessed, and the shortening lives are the sentence of 6:3
  working itself out in a table of numbers.

## Notes

- **Two *toledot* in one pericope, and no *toledot* of Abraham.** The formula *"these are the
  generations of"* segments Genesis ten times, and this is the only passage that carries two of
  them: Shem at 11:10 and Terah at 11:27. Its usual function holds in both cases — hand the
  narrative on by clearing the other branches first — but the second one is odd in a way worth
  naming. The section running from 11:27 to 25:11 is the Abraham cycle, and it is headed
  ***Terah's*** generations. Genesis gives a *toledot* to Ishmael (25:12) and to Isaac (25:19) and
  to Esau (36:1) and to Jacob (37:2), and never gives one to Abraham. On the formula's own logic
  that is consistent — a *toledot* introduces a man's descendants, and Abraham's descendants are
  the whole rest of the book — but it means the founding figure of Israel is filed under his
  father's name, and his father is a man who set out for Canaan and stopped.
- **The missing death formula.** Genesis 5 ends eight of its ten entries with *"and then he died"*;
  Genesis 11 ends none of its nine that way, and gives no totals either. Three readings, not
  exclusive. **(1)** Structural: the point has been made. Genesis 5 was demonstrating the sentence of
  2:17, and once the flood has killed everything that breathes the demonstration is complete; this
  register is doing a different job, which is delivery. **(2)** Directional: the first list was
  running down toward judgment and the second is running up toward promise, and the form registers
  the difference by dropping the funeral. **(3)** Source-critical: both registers are assigned to the
  Priestly source, which makes the divergence a real datum rather than a difference of hand, and it
  is usually explained as deliberate variation rather than as different provenance. Nothing in the
  text adjudicates. What is certain is the effect — nine men in a row are left unburied on the page,
  and the only grave in the pericope is Terah's.
- **Ten and ten, and three sons at a round age.** Adam to Noah is ten generations and Shem to Abram
  is ten, if Shem is counted as the first. Both lists close identically: *"Noah was five hundred
  years old, then Noah became the father of Shem, Ham, and Japheth"* (5:32) and *"Terah lived
  seventy years, and became the father of Abram, Nahor, and Haran"* (11:26). Same construction, same
  round number, same three sons, same refusal to close the entry. The symmetry is too exact to be
  accidental and is the clearest evidence that 11:10–26 was composed as a deliberate answering panel
  to Genesis 5. See
  [[Ten Before and Ten After - The Registers of Genesis 5 and Genesis 11]].
- **Abram is listed first and is probably not the eldest.** Terah is 70 at 11:26 and dies at 205
  (11:32); Abram is 75 when he leaves Haran (12:4). If Abram were born in Terah's seventieth year he
  would leave at 145, sixty years before his father's death — which is possible in Genesis and flatly
  contradicts Acts 7:4, where Abraham leaves Haran *"when his father was dead."* The resolution that
  has been standard since antiquity is that 11:26 gives the age at which Terah began fathering, not
  the age at which he fathered Abram: Haran, whose daughter is old enough to marry and whose son
  travels with the family, is the eldest, and Abram was born when Terah was 130 (205 − 75). Abram is
  then listed first for the same reason [[Shem]] is listed first among Noah's sons — because he is
  the one the book follows. The cost is that the neat 292-year interval from the flood to Abram's
  birth becomes 352. Both figures circulate; the wiki reports the 292 because it follows the text's
  plain sequence, and flags that it depends on an assumption the text does not make. See
  [[Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call]].
- **The Hebrew, Greek, and Samaritan numbers differ here even more than in Genesis 5.** The
  Septuagint adds a hundred years to most of the fathering ages (Arpachshad 135 rather than 35,
  Shelah 130, Eber 134, Peleg 130, Reu 132, Serug 130) and fifty to Nahor's, subtracting the same
  from the remainders; the Samaritan Pentateuch has a third set. It also inserts an entire
  generation, **Cainan**, between Arpachshad and Shelah, with 130 years of his own. The result is
  that the interval from the flood to Abram is under three centuries in the Hebrew and around a
  millennium in the Greek. Luke 3:36 has the extra Cainan, because Luke read the Septuagint. This
  matters less as a puzzle about which is right than as evidence about what these lists were for:
  three transmitting communities did not agree on the number of generations between Shem and Abram,
  and none of them appears to have regarded that as fatal.
- **Ur of the Chaldees, and which Ur.** *ʾÛr Kaśdîm.* The Chaldeans (*Kaldu*) are an Aramean people
  attested in southern Mesopotamia from the ninth century BC and dominant there only from the
  seventh — many centuries after any date proposed for Abraham. The phrase is therefore a
  later identifier attached to an older name, of the same kind as calling a Roman site "in France".
  The southern identification, with the great Sumerian city excavated at Tell el-Muqayyar, is the
  majority view and has the advantage that *Ur of the Chaldees* is exactly how a first-millennium
  writer would specify that city. Against it: every other place this family touches is in the far
  north, around Haran, six hundred miles away; Joshua 24:2 puts the fathers *"beyond the River"*,
  which reads more naturally of the upper Euphrates; and Genesis 24:4 and 10 call Haran, not
  southern Babylonia, Abraham's *"country"* and *"relatives"*. A northern Ur — Urfa, or a site named
  Ura near Haran — has been argued on those grounds and remains a minority position. See
  [[Ur of the Chaldees]].
- **Four names in the register are also towns near Haran.** *Serug* corresponds to Assyrian
  *Sarugi* (modern Suruç); *Nahor* to *Naḫur*, a city well attested in the Mari archives; *Terah*
  is often connected to *Til-ša-turaḫi* on the Balikh; *Reu* has been linked, less securely, to
  *Ra'gau*. All lie in the same small region, and the family settles in the middle of it. Two
  readings are possible and neither is provable: that the genealogy preserves a genuine memory of an
  Amorite clan whose ancestors' names became, or came from, the towns of its home district; or that
  personal names and place names in that region simply drew on the same small stock of words. Either
  way it is one of the few points where this list touches something outside the Bible.
- **Haran the man and Haran the city are different words.** The son of Terah is *Hārān* (הָרָן, with
  *he*); the city is *Ḥārān* (חָרָן, with *ḥet*), Akkadian *Ḥarrānu*, "road" or "crossroads", after
  the caravan routes that met there. English flattens the distinction and Hebrew never does, so the
  poignant coincidence of a bereaved father settling in a town named for his dead son exists only in
  translation. It is worth stating plainly, because it is a very common misreading and the wiki's
  own filenames have to disambiguate the two.
- **Sarai's parentage, and Iscah.** Verse 29 supplies Milcah's father and withholds Sarai's, in the
  same sentence — a conspicuous asymmetry. Genesis 20:12 later has Abraham say she is *"the daughter
  of my father, but not the daughter of my mother"*, which makes her Terah's daughter by another
  wife and Abram's half-sister. The old alternative, found in Josephus, in the Talmud, and in Rashi,
  identifies **Iscah** — named in 11:29 and nowhere else in Scripture — with Sarai, making her
  Haran's daughter and therefore Abram's niece, and giving both brothers nieces for wives. It
  explains why an otherwise pointless name is recorded, and it contradicts 20:12 outright unless
  *"daughter of my father"* is stretched to mean granddaughter. The wiki records both and adopts
  neither, noting that 20:12 is the only statement in Scripture that is actually about the question.
- **The marriages are close, and the Torah will later forbid them.** Nahor marries his brother's
  daughter; Abram, on 20:12, marries his half-sister — a union prohibited outright at Leviticus
  18:9 and 20:17 and cursed at Deuteronomy 27:22. Genesis narrates it without a flicker of comment,
  as it narrates Jacob's two sisters (Leviticus 18:18) and Amram's marriage to his aunt (Exodus
  6:20; Leviticus 18:12). The pattern is consistent enough to be a principle of reading: the
  patriarchal narratives are set before Sinai and are not written to be exemplary under it.
- **Barrenness as a literary device, and as more than one.** *"Sarai was barren. She had no child"*
  is the first of a series — Rebekah (25:21), Rachel (29:31), Manoah's wife (Judges 13:2), Hannah
  (1 Samuel 1:2), the Shunammite (2 Kings 4:14), Elizabeth (Luke 1:7) — in which the promised child
  arrives through a womb that cannot produce one. Placed here, at the end of a genealogy that has
  done nothing for twenty-three verses but produce children, it functions as the failure of the
  mechanism the chapter is made of. That the fault is stated of Sarai rather than of the couple is
  the convention of the period and of the text; Genesis 16 and 30 will both show what the convention
  costs the women in it. See [[Barrenness]].
- **God is not in this passage.** Twenty-three verses with no divine speech, no divine act, and no
  occurrence of *Yahweh* or *ʾĕlōhîm*. It is the longest stretch of Genesis so far without either.
  The pericope is bounded by God scattering the nations at 11:9 and God calling one man at 12:1, and
  what lies between is a family losing its lifespan, its firstborn, its fertility, and its
  destination. Nothing in the text says this is the point. The placement says it.
