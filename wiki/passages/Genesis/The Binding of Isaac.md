# The Binding of Isaac

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 22:1–19

## Summary

**God asks Abraham to kill the son he spent twenty-five chapters promising him, and Abraham gets up
early and goes.**

Nineteen verses. No inner life, no protest, no explanation, and — until the knife is already in the
air — no God. It is the most economical piece of narration in the Bible about the most extreme thing
in it, and almost everything the chapter does, it does by leaving something out.

**The reader is told in the first four words what Abraham is never told at all.**

> **1** After these things, God tested Abraham, and said to him, “Abraham!” He said, “Here I am.”

*Nissâ*, **tested**, and this is its first occurrence in Scripture. The narrator hands the reader the
frame before the scene starts, exactly as the prologue of Job hands the reader the wager before the
messengers arrive — and in both books the man inside the story is given nothing. **Abraham is the
only person in the pericope who does not know what the pericope is.** See [[Testing]] and
[[Job of Uz]].

**And the frame does not make the command less than it is.** *Test* explains why God asks; it does
not soften what he asks for, and Genesis does not have the angel say *I never meant it*. The angel
says *"now I know"*, which means the outcome was genuinely open on the text's own terms. The wiki
records the moral problem as the chapter's own and does not dissolve it. See the notes.

### The shape of it

| verses | | |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | the command | *lek-ləkā*, four appositions, an unnamed mountain |
| 3–4 | the departure | five verbs, no speech, three days |
| 5–8 | the ascent | the only conversation Abraham and Isaac ever have |
| 9–10 | the altar | six verbs, one of them a hapax, and a knife |
| 11–14 | the stop | a voice from the sky, a ram, and a name for the place |
| 15–18 | the oath | God swears by himself, and grounds the promise in obedience |
| 19 | the return | *"So Abraham returned to his young men"* — and Isaac is not mentioned |

### *"Here I am"* — three times, to three different speakers

***Hinnēnî*** is the pericope's structural refrain, and each of the three is answered by a demand:

| verse | who calls | what follows |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **God** — *"Abraham!"* | take your son and go |
| 7 | **Isaac** — *"My father?"* | where is the lamb? |
| 11 | **Yahweh's angel** — *"Abraham, Abraham!"* | don't lay your hand on the boy |

*Hinnēnî* is not *here I am* in the sense of location; it is the word for standing at someone's
disposal, the answer of a servant who has heard and is waiting to be told. **Abraham says it to God,
to his son, and to the angel, in that order, and the middle one is the one that costs him
something** — the boy asks a question that has only one true answer and Abraham gives him the same
word he gave God.

The doubled vocative of verse 11, *"Abraham, Abraham!"*, is the Bible's signal of an urgent
interruption, and it recurs at each of its hinges: *"Jacob, Jacob"* (46:2), *"Moses, Moses"*
(Exodus 3:4), *"Samuel, Samuel"* (1 Samuel 3:10), *"Saul, Saul"* (Acts 9:4). **Only here is it used
to stop something.**

### The command, and its four narrowing clauses

> **2** He said, “Now take your son, your only son, whom you love, even Isaac, and go into the land
> of Moriah. Offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains which I will tell you of.”

**Four appositions, each closing an escape route.** *Your son* — which one? *Your only son* — the one
I can spare? *Whom you love* — and then, refusing the last ambiguity, the name. It is the same
technique as *"your country, and your relatives, and your father's house"* at 12:1, and the two
sentences are the only two in the Hebrew Bible that open with *lek-ləkā*. See
[[Leave Your Country - The Two Lek-Lekha Commands]].

***Yāḥîd*, only,** is exact and it is only true from 21:14, when the other son was sent into the
desert. **The expulsion is what makes this sentence sayable**, which is the strongest evidence that
Genesis composed the two chapters as a pair. See
[[Two Sons Nearly Lost - The Sending of Ishmael and the Binding of Isaac]].

***And ʾāhab*, to love, occurs here for the first time in the Bible.** The Bible's first love is a
father's for a son, and it is named by God, in the sentence that asks for the son's death. The
observation is old — the rabbis made it, and it is not a trick of translation: no earlier verse in
Genesis uses the verb of anyone. **Scripture introduces the word by putting it where it will hurt
most.**

The Septuagint renders *yāḥîd* not as *only* but as **ἀγαπητός, *beloved*** — the word that comes
down at the Jordan on another son (Mark 1:11) — and John 3:16's *μονογενής*, *only-begotten*, is
built from the other half of the same idea. **The New Testament's vocabulary for what God gives up
is assembled out of this verse.** See [[The Binding of Isaac in the New Testament]].

***"the land of Moriah"*** is named nowhere else in the Torah, and the whole Bible uses the word
once more: *"Solomon began to build Yahweh's house at Jerusalem on Mount Moriah"* (2 Chronicles
3:1). Whether that identification is geography or theology is the oldest live question about the
chapter, and the Samaritan Pentateuch answers it differently — reading *the land of Moreh*, and
putting the mountain at Gerizim. See [[Moriah]] and
[[Yahweh Will Provide - Moriah and the Temple Mount]].

***"on one of the mountains which I will tell you of"*** withholds the destination exactly as 12:1
withheld the land. **God names the region and not the place, which is what makes the three days
necessary**: Abraham has to keep going while not yet knowing where he is going to stop.

### Five verbs and no answer

> **3** Abraham rose early in the morning, and saddled his donkey, and took two of his young men with
> him, and Isaac his son. He split the wood for the burnt offering, and rose up, and went to the
> place of which God had told him.

**The man who talked God down from fifty to ten over a city of strangers (18:23–33) does not say one
word about his son.** That silence is the chapter's loudest feature and Genesis never comments on it.
The wiki flags it as a genuine tension rather than as an unstated piety: either the narrator is
showing a different kind of obedience, or he is showing a man who has learned what arguing is for and
what it is not for, and the text does not adjudicate. See
[[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].

***"Abraham rose early in the morning"*** is verbatim the clause that opened the expulsion of Ishmael
at 21:14 — *wayyaškēm ʾAbrāhām babbōqer*. Twice in two chapters, and both times what follows is a son
being handed over.

**And the order of the verbs is not the order of a journey.** He splits the wood *before* he leaves,
for a fire he will light three days away in a country full of trees. It is the detail of a man doing
the one part of the task he can do in the dark, and it is the closest the pericope comes to telling
us what the night before was like.

***"two of his young men"*** — *nəʿārāyw* — will be left at the foot of the hill, and the same word
is used of Isaac at verse 5 and verse 12. **The boy and the servants are the same word**, and the
servants are the reason the walk up is a private scene: Genesis clears the stage of witnesses before
anything happens.

> **4** On the third day Abraham lifted up his eyes, and saw the place far off.

**Three days is not travel time; it is the length of the silence.** Beersheba to Jerusalem is roughly
fifty miles, which is about right for three days with a donkey, so the number is not implausible — but
what the number does in the narrative is give Abraham seventy-two hours in which he could turn round
and does not. Kierkegaard built a whole book out of that interval, imagining four different Abrahams
who fill it differently. Genesis fills it with nothing.

*"On the third day"* enters the Bible here, and later carries the pattern of a reversal after a
death-interval — *"On the third day he will raise us up, and we will live before him"* (Hosea 6:2),
and the New Testament's formula for the resurrection. Later readers made the connection; Genesis
supplies the interval and no comment. See [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].

### *"We will worship, and come back to you"*

> **5** Abraham said to his young men, “Stay here with the donkey. The boy and I will go yonder. We
> will worship, and come back to you.”

***Wənāšûbâ*** — **we will come back**, first person plural. Abraham has been given a command whose
completion makes that sentence false, and he says it anyway.

**Three readings, all serious.** It is a lie told to keep the servants from interfering. It is a
formula, said without thought, by a man who cannot say the true thing out loud. Or it is the sentence
Hebrews 11:19 reads it as — *"concluding that God is able to raise up even from the dead"* — the one
place in Scripture that offers an account of what Abraham was thinking, and offers it as an
inference, not as a report. **The wiki records all three and adopts none**, and notes that only the
third makes verse 5 and verse 8 consistent with each other.

***"We will worship"*** is *wəništaḥăweh*, from *šāḥâ* — to bow low, prostrate. The verb has appeared
before (Abraham bowing to the three visitors, 18:2; Lot to the two, 19:1), but **this is the first
time in the Bible it is used of what a person does toward God**, and what it names is a killing. The
word for worship enters Scripture attached to the hardest thing anyone in Scripture is asked to do.

### The only conversation

> **6** Abraham took the wood of the burnt offering and laid it on Isaac his son. He took in his hand
> the fire and the knife. They both went together.
> **7** Isaac spoke to Abraham his father, and said, “My father?” He said, “Here I am, my son.” He
> said, “Here is the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?”
> **8** Abraham said, “God will provide himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son.” So they both
> went together.

**These are the only words Abraham and Isaac ever exchange in the Bible.** They are also the last
words Abraham speaks to anyone in the chapter; after verse 8 he says *"Here I am"* to the angel and
nothing else, and he and Isaac never appear in the same scene again in Genesis.

***"They both went together"* is said twice, and it brackets the dialogue** — *wayyēləkû šənêhem
yaḥdāw*, at the end of verse 6 and again at the end of verse 8. The second time it means something
the first did not: they have now had the conversation and they are still walking. Rabbinic reading
takes the repetition as the moment Isaac understands, and the phrase as the text's way of saying they
went in agreement. That is a reading; the words are identical both times and Genesis marks no
difference.

**The father carries the fire and the knife; the son carries the wood.** Abraham keeps both
instruments of harm in his own hands, and Isaac carries what he will be laid on. The image is what
the second century made typological — Melito of Sardis and Irenaeus both read the son carrying the
wood up the hill as the figure of the cross — and it is worth saying plainly that the reading is a
reading, produced by the picture and not by anything the verse says.

***"but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?"*** **Isaac speaks once in the pericope and it is a
question about a missing animal.** He has watched a burnt offering prepared before; he knows the
inventory, and he knows one item is not on the donkey. It is the most ordinary sentence in the
chapter, and the reason it is unbearable is that the reader can answer it.

***"God will provide himself the lamb"*** — *ʾĕlōhîm yirʾeh-lô haśśeh*. The Hebrew is *God will see
for himself the lamb*; **the verb is *rāʾâ*, to see, and providing is what seeing means when God is
the subject** — the same idiom underneath the English *provide* (from *pro-videre*, to see ahead) and
underneath [[Hagar]]'s *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, the God who sees, at 16:13. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

**The sentence has two grammars.** *Yirʾeh-lô* is most naturally *will see for himself*, i.e. will
provide; it can also be construed *will see to himself as the lamb*, which is how the Christian
tradition has often taken it, and the ambiguity is real in the Hebrew rather than manufactured in
translation. **What Genesis then does with it is neither**: God provides a **ram**, and the *lamb*
Abraham names is not what arrives. *Śeh* is left unfilled here, and the Bible picks it up at Exodus
12 and at *"Behold, the Lamb of God"* (John 1:29). Whether that is a thread or a coincidence of
vocabulary is exactly the kind of thing the wiki flags rather than settles.

### The altar, and the word the whole episode is named for

> **9** They came to the place which God had told him of. Abraham built the altar there, and laid the
> wood in order, bound Isaac his son, and laid him on the altar, on the wood.
> **10** Abraham stretched out his hand, and took the knife to kill his son.

**Six verbs in one verse, each a step in a procedure, and one of them is a person.** Built, laid in
order, bound, laid — the first two are what Leviticus 1:7–8 will prescribe for an ordinary burnt
offering, and the sentence runs the son through the same grammar as the wood. Genesis narrates the
worst moment in the book as a liturgy.

***Wayyaʿăqōd*, "bound", is a hapax legomenon** — it occurs once in the Hebrew Bible, here. Nothing
else in Scripture is *ʿāqad*; the word is otherwise attested of hobbling an animal, tying the legs
together. **The episode's Jewish name, the *Akedah* — the Binding — is built from a word the Bible
uses exactly once**, and the wiki's page title follows the same choice.

**Isaac does not resist and is not said to consent.** He is, by any reckoning, old enough to carry
the wood for a whole burnt offering up a hill and strong enough to be a problem for a man of over a
hundred; Josephus makes him twenty-five, and the rabbinic reckoning that puts Moriah immediately
before Sarah's death at 23:1 makes him thirty-seven. Genesis gives no age and no reaction. **The
tradition's insistence that Isaac was a willing adult is filling a silence the text leaves open on
purpose.** See [[Isaac]].

***"took the knife"*** — *maʾăkelet*, a rare word, used four times in the Bible. Two of them are this
chapter (22:6, 10); one is Proverbs 30:14; and one is Judges 19:29, where a Levite takes *the knife*
and cuts up his concubine. **Scripture's slaughtering-knife word appears in exactly two narratives,
and this is the one where the blade is stopped.** See [[Gibeah and Sodom - Judges 19 as a Retelling]].

### The stop

> **11** Yahweh’s angel called to him out of the sky, and said, “Abraham, Abraham!” He said, “Here I
> am.”
> **12** He said, “Don’t lay your hand on the boy or do anything to him. For now I know that you fear
> God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.”

**The command came from *ʾĕlōhîm*; the reprieve comes from the angel of *YHWH*.** The divine names
divide the chapter cleanly — God tests (22:1, 3, 8, 9), Yahweh's angel stops and swears (22:11, 15,
16) — which is the classic seam for a source-critical reading, and also, read as it stands, a
distribution with a point: the God who demands is the God of the whole world and the God who delivers
is the one who has a name and a covenant. **The wiki records both readings and adopts neither**, as
it does at the two creation accounts. See [[The Names of God]] and [[The Angel of Yahweh]].

**A voice *out of the sky* — *min-haššāmayim* — occurs in Genesis three times, all of them within
thirty verses**: over Hagar's dying son (21:17), and twice here (22:11, 15). Everywhere else in the
book the messenger stands on the ground, eats, and takes people by the hand. **The two near-losses of
Abraham's sons are the only occasions when heaven has to be reached into.**

***"now I know that you fear God"*** is the sentence the whole chapter is built to produce, and it
carries the chapter's hardest theological problem in three words. *Now I know* — *ʿattâ yādaʿtî* —
says the test was for information, and it is not obvious what a God who knows the end from the
beginning wanted to find out. The standing answers are that the knowing is relational rather than
informational; that God is speaking within the covenant's own legal idiom, where a thing is *known*
when it has been demonstrated and can be pleaded; and that Genesis simply has a God who finds out,
as at 3:9, 11:5, and 18:21. **All three are live and the wiki does not choose.**

***Yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm*, "you fear God", is exactly what is said of [[Job (person)]]** at Job 1:1, 1:8, and
2:3 — the phrase the accuser attacks by asking *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* Two books ask the
same question about motive, both let the reader watch from outside, and both answer it by taking
everything away. See [[The Fear of God]].

**And it is the answer to Abraham's own worst assumption.** At 20:11 he explained the Gerar lie by
saying *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place"* — of a city that turned out to be full of it.
**The one man in Genesis who is told by God that he fears God is the man who used the phrase to
excuse himself.**

***"you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me"*** — *lōʾ ḥāśaktā*. The Septuagint's *οὐκ
ἐφείσω* is the verb Paul reaches for in Romans 8:32: *"He who didn't spare his own Son, but delivered
him up for us all."* **The single clearest verbal bridge from this chapter into the New Testament,
and Paul builds his argument on it without naming Abraham.** See
[[The Binding of Isaac in the New Testament]].

### The ram

> **13** Abraham lifted up his eyes, and looked, and saw that behind him was a ram caught in the
> thicket by his horns. Abraham went and took the ram, and offered him up for a burnt offering
> instead of his son.

***"lifted up his eyes, and looked, and saw"*** — the third of the chapter's seeings, after verse 4's
sighting of the place and verse 8's promise that God would see to it. **Nothing is created here.** The
ram was in the thicket; what changes is that Abraham turns his head. It is the same mechanism as
21:19, where *"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water"* that had been there all along. See
[[Seeing and Being Seen]].

***Behind him*** — *ʾaḥar* — is the Masoretic reading, and it is odd enough that the Samaritan
Pentateuch, the Septuagint, the Syriac, and the Targums all read *ʾeḥād*, **one ram**, a difference of
a single consonant. The wiki follows the WEB's *behind him* and records the variant; nothing
theological turns on it.

***"instead of his son"*** — *taḥat bənô*, **the preposition of substitution**, and this is the moment
the Bible's whole sacrificial logic is first narrated: one life is taken in the place of another, and
the one taken is an animal. Everything Leviticus later regulates is in that phrase. See
[[The Ram Instead of the Son - Substitution at Moriah]] and [[Sacrifice and Offering]].

**And the ram is not the lamb Abraham named.** *ʾAyil*, not *śeh*. God provides, and provides
something other than what was asked for — which is the first and gentlest instance of a pattern the
rest of Scripture runs on.

### The name of the place

> **14** Abraham called the name of that place Yahweh Will Provide. As it is said to this day, “On
> Yahweh’s mountain, it will be provided.”

***YHWH yirʾeh*** — *Yahweh sees*, and therefore *Yahweh provides*: the same verb Abraham used at
verse 8, turned into a place-name after the fact. **The chapter is one long conjugation of *rāʾâ*.**

| verse | form | what is seen |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | *wayyarʾ* | Abraham sees the place, far off |
| 8 | *yirʾeh-lô* | God will see to the lamb, for himself |
| 13 | *wayyarʾ* | Abraham sees the ram |
| 14a | *YHWH yirʾeh* | the name of the mountain |
| 14b | *yērāʾeh* | *it will be provided* — or *he will be seen* |

**The last one is the pivot.** *Yērāʾeh* is the same consonants in the passive, and it can be read
*it shall be provided*, *it shall be seen*, or — the reading the later pilgrimage law makes almost
irresistible — *he shall appear*, which is the standard word for presenting oneself before God at the
sanctuary three times a year (Exodus 23:17; Deuteronomy 16:16). **A verse about a mountain where
something was provided is written in the exact idiom of a mountain where people go to be seen by
God**, and 2 Chronicles 3:1 will put the temple on a hill of that name. Whether the wordplay was heard
that way in Genesis's own composition is not decidable; that it is in the consonants is not in doubt.
See [[Yahweh Will Provide - Moriah and the Temple Mount]].

*"As it is said to this day"* is the narrator stepping outside the story to a proverb his audience
already knows — the same move as 21:31's naming of Beersheba, and evidence that the saying is older
than its setting here.

### The oath

> **15** Yahweh’s angel called to Abraham a second time out of the sky,
> **16** and said, “I have sworn by myself, says Yahweh, because you have done this thing, and have
> not withheld your son, your only son,
> **17** that I will bless you greatly, and I will multiply your offspring greatly like the stars of
> the heavens, and like the sand which is on the seashore. Your offspring will possess the gate of his
> enemies.
> **18** All the nations of the earth will be blessed by your offspring, because you have obeyed my
> voice.”

***"I have sworn by myself"* — *bî nišbaʿtî* — and God swears by himself nowhere else in Genesis.**
The formula is what Hebrews 6:13–18 makes an entire argument out of: *"For when God made a promise to
Abraham, since he could swear by no one greater, he swore by himself"* — two immutable things, the
promise and the oath, and a God who has deliberately closed his own exits. See
[[I Have Sworn by Myself - The Oath at Moriah]] and [[Oaths and Vows]].

***"says Yahweh"*** — *nəʾum YHWH* — is the prophetic messenger formula, the standing signature of
Amos and Isaiah and Jeremiah, and **this is the only place in Genesis it appears.** The angel
switches, mid-sentence, into the register of a prophet quoting God, which is as close as the book
comes to explaining the identity problem the messenger poses. See [[The Angel of Yahweh]].

**And the ground of the promise has moved.** *"because you have done this thing"* … *"because you
have obeyed my voice"* — *ʿēqeb ʾăšer šāmaʿtā bəqōlî*. At 15:17 the promise was a self-curse God
walked through alone while Abram slept and undertook nothing; here the same promise is restated as a
consequence of an act. **This is a real tension inside the Abraham cycle and the wiki states it as
one.** The standing resolutions — that the covenant's *existence* is unconditional while its
*confirmation* is responsive; that 22:16–18 is the covenant's ratification rather than its ground;
that Genesis simply holds both and 26:5 doubles down (*"because Abraham obeyed my voice"*) — are all
in play, and Paul in Galatians 3 and James in James 2:21–23 read this exact seam in opposite
directions. See [[Covenant]], [[Faith]], and
[[Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James]].

**Two new things are said that were never said before.** *"like the sand which is on the seashore"*
joins dust (13:16) and stars (15:5) as the third image of the offspring promise — and it is the only
one that is not a metaphor for uncountability alone, since sand is also the boundary of the sea. And
*"Your offspring will possess the gate of his enemies"* is **the first military promise in the
Abraham cycle**: gates are where a city is taken and where its elders sit, and the phrase means
conquest and jurisdiction at once. See [[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]].

***"All the nations of the earth will be blessed by your offspring"*** is the fifth and final
statement of 12:3, and the last words God speaks to Abraham in the Bible. Two things have changed
since the first version: it is *gôyê hāʾāreṣ*, **the nations** rather than *the families* of the
earth, and the blessing now runs through *your offspring* rather than through Abraham himself. **The
promise has been handed to the next generation in the same breath that nearly ended it.** See
[[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]] and [[The Nations]].

### The return, and the sentence with one man in it

> **19** So Abraham returned to his young men, and they rose up and went together to Beersheba.
> Abraham lived at Beersheba.

**Isaac is not in this verse.** The verbs are Abraham's and the servants'; the boy who came up the
mountain is not said to come down it. That is almost certainly the narrator's economy — the
plural *"they rose up and went together"* naturally includes him, and 24:62 has him alive at Beer
Lahai Roi — but the omission is real, it is the only place in the chapter where Genesis drops him,
and an old strand of midrash filled the gap by sending Isaac away to study, or, in its boldest form,
by having him die on the altar and be revived. **The wiki records that the tradition exists and that
the text does not support it**; what the text does is stop mentioning him.

**Abraham and Isaac never speak to each other again in Scripture.** They share no further scene:
Sarah is buried in the next chapter with Isaac unmentioned, the wife is fetched by a servant, and the
two are next in the same verse when Isaac and Ishmael bury their father (25:9). **Whatever the
chapter cost, Genesis records it as distance and never as a word.**

***"went together"*** — *yaḥdāw* — is the chapter's third and last use of the word that carried
father and son up the hill in verses 6 and 8. **It is now Abraham and the servants.**

And the destination is [[Beersheba]]: the well he had contracted for, the tamarisk he had planted,
the place the previous pericope left him living in *many days*. **The narrative returns him precisely
to where it found him, with everything the same and nothing the same.** See
[[The Treaty at Beersheba]].

## People

- [[Abraham]] — tested without being told he is being tested; he answers *"Here I am"* three times,
  says nothing about the command, splits the wood the night before, raises the knife, and is told by
  God that he fears God — the only man in Genesis who is.
- [[Isaac]] — carries the wood he is to be burned on, asks the pericope's one question, is bound with
  a verb the Bible uses once, and is not mentioned coming down the mountain; he and his father never
  speak again in Scripture.
- [[God]] — *ʾĕlōhîm* through the command and the ascent; he asks for the son he promised, and says
  afterward *"now I know"*.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — calls twice *out of the sky*, stops the knife, and then swears in the
  first person as Yahweh with a prophetic formula found nowhere else in Genesis.
- [[Sarah]] — absent, unconsulted, and not told; she dies in the next chapter, which is why one old
  reading has the news of Moriah kill her.
- [[Ishmael]] — off-stage, and the reason *"your only son"* is now literally true.
- [[Hagar]] — off-stage; the other parent in Genesis to be answered by a voice out of the sky over a
  dying son, eighteen verses earlier.
- [[Job (person)]] — not in the chapter, and the only other man in Scripture the narrator lets us
  watch being tested while he cannot see the frame; *"you fear God"* is said of both.

## Places

- [[Moriah]] — the land Abraham is sent to, named in the Torah only here, and identified by 2
  Chronicles 3:1 with the hill Solomon builds the temple on; the mountain itself is not named, only
  called *Yahweh Will Provide*.
- [[Beersheba]] — the pericope's start-point by implication and its explicit end-point, twice in the
  last verse; the well, the treaty, and the tamarisk are what the chapter departs from and returns
  to.
- [[Canaan]] — the promised land, crossed for three days by a man carrying the fire for his son's
  pyre.
- [[Salem]] — off-stage; if Moriah is the temple mount, then this is Melchizedek's city and Abraham
  has been here before, at 14:18.

## Themes

- [[Testing]] — the Bible's first *nissâ*, declared to the reader in the first verse and withheld
  from the man for all nineteen.
- [[The Fear of God]] — the chapter exists to establish it, in the same words used of Job, and it is
  the one thing God says he now knows.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — a burnt offering prepared in full liturgical order with a person on
  the wood, and completed with an animal *instead of his son*.
- [[Faith]] — Genesis 15:6 believed a promise; Genesis 22 hands the promise back, and the New
  Testament argues about which of the two is the definition.
- [[Covenant]] — the promise restated as an oath God swears by himself, and grounded, for the first
  time, in something Abraham did.
- [[Blessing]] — *"I will bless you greatly"*, the fifth and last statement of 12:2–3, and the last
  words God speaks to Abraham.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — God's only self-sworn oath in Genesis, and the text Hebrews builds its
  doctrine of assurance on.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — five forms of *rāʾâ* in fourteen verses, ending in a name and a
  proverb.
- [[Life and Death]] — the promised life laid on an altar by the man it was promised to.
- [[Call and Election]] — the line runs through Isaac by God's word at 21:12, and God asks for Isaac
  at 22:2.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — three days' walk across a country he owns nothing of, to a mountain that
  will belong to his descendants.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — *nəʾum YHWH*, the prophets' formula, in the mouth of an angel in Genesis.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** 22:1 opens with *"After these things"* and 22:19 closes with
  Abraham settled at Beersheba; 22:20 opens with *"After these things"* again and turns to Nahor's
  genealogy, which the plan already attaches to the burial of Sarah. The unit is clean at both ends
  and **no unchecked boxes were redrawn.** Running 22:1–19 as one pericope rather than splitting the
  second angelic speech (15–18) off from the rescue was deliberate: verses 15–18 have no setting of
  their own and read as the chapter's conclusion, even though their doubled call is one of the
  standard source-critical seams.
- **The moral problem, stated without a resolution.** God commands a father to kill his son, and
  Scripture elsewhere calls that practice the thing *"which I didn't command, nor did it come into my
  mind"* (Jeremiah 7:31). Genesis neither defends the command nor apologizes for it. The main
  readings: **(1)** the chapter is a polemic — it stages the demand precisely in order to stop it and
  substitute an animal, which is what the law then codifies for every firstborn (Exodus 13:13);
  **(2)** it is a test in the strict sense, where the demand is real, the outcome is open, and the
  point is what Abraham does before he knows how it ends; **(3)** Kierkegaard's — the ethical is
  suspended for the sake of an absolute duty that cannot be justified to anyone else, which is why
  Abraham cannot explain himself to Sarah, to Isaac, or to the servants; **(4)** Kant's — that
  Abraham should have concluded a voice demanding this could not be God's. **The wiki records all
  four and adopts none**, and notes that the text itself does one thing the readings must all
  account for: it stops the knife.
- **What Genesis leaves out, and why it is a technique rather than an accident.** No emotion is
  reported for anyone. Abraham's night, Isaac's realization, Sarah's ignorance, the walk down — none
  of it is narrated. Auerbach's *Mimesis* opens by setting this chapter against Homer for exactly
  this reason: Homer's world is foregrounded and fully lit, Genesis's is *fraught with background*,
  and the unspoken is not missing but withheld. **Every reading of this chapter, ancient or modern,
  is filling one of those gaps**, and the wiki's rule here is to name the gap before naming the
  filling.
- **The source-critical seam.** The names divide (*ʾĕlōhîm* in 1–9, *YHWH* from 11), the angel calls
  twice, and 15–18 restates promises already made — so the standard analysis assigns 1–14, 19 to
  **E** and 15–18 to a later redactor, with 22:11–12's *Yahweh's angel* often read as an
  E-plus-adjustment. The counter-reading is that the doubled call is the chapter's own structure —
  the first voice stops an act, the second interprets it — and that the name distribution is
  meaningful rather than residual. **Both are recorded; neither is adopted**, per the wiki's standing
  posture on the two creation accounts, the two Hagar flights, and the Beersheba doublet.
- **Isaac's age is not in the text, and every number attached to him is inferred.** The rabbinic
  thirty-seven comes from placing Moriah immediately before Sarah's death (23:1) and subtracting;
  Josephus's twenty-five comes from nothing in particular; the popular image of a small boy comes
  from *naʿar*, which covers everything from an infant to a man of military age and is used of the
  servants in the same chapter. **What the text supplies is that he could carry the wood.**
- **On *"now I know"*.** Recorded above as an open question, and worth one further note: Genesis has
  a settled habit of narrating God as finding out — *"Where are you?"* (3:9), *"Yahweh came down to
  see"* (11:5), *"I will go down now, and see"* (18:21). Whether this is anthropomorphism, a covenant
  idiom for demonstrated proof, or the book's actual theology is a question the wiki has now met four
  times and has consistently declined to settle. See [[God]].
- **The one thing the chapter never says is that Isaac was in danger from anyone but his father.**
  There is no enemy, no famine, no king, no city. Every previous threat to the promise came from
  outside — Pharaoh, Abimelech, barrenness, war — and was survived. **This one comes from the God who
  made the promise, and the wiki notes it as the structural point of the chapter**: the last threat
  to the covenant is the covenant's author, and the covenant survives that too.
