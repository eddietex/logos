# The Stolen Blessing

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 27:1–45

## Summary

**A blind man is worked through his four remaining senses, one at a time, and the sense that tells
him the truth is the one he overrules.**

That is the chapter's method and very nearly its whole plot. Isaac cannot see. So Jacob is heard
(*"The voice is Jacob's voice"*), touched (goat skins on the hands and the neck), fed (two kids
cooked to taste like game), and smelled (his brother's clothes) — and **the one channel that reports
correctly is hearing, which Isaac notices, states aloud, and then sets aside.** He asks the question
twice, gets a flat lie twice, and blesses.

**And God is not in it.** The divine name appears in four of the forty-five verses — *Yahweh* at
27:7, 20, and 27, *ʾĕlōhîm* at 27:20 and 28 — and **every occurrence is in a human mouth**: Rebekah
quoting her husband (27:7), Jacob lying (27:20), Isaac smelling a field (27:27), Isaac blessing
(27:28). **God does not speak, does not appear, does not intervene, and is
not asked to.** Nothing is said about the oracle of 25:23 by anybody — least of all by the woman who
received it. **The chapter in which the promise changes hands is the chapter in which its author is
absent**, and the wiki records that as the passage's central fact rather than as an omission to be
explained away.

**Then it does the thing that makes it theologically load-bearing.** Isaac finds out. He *"trembled
violently"*, works out exactly what has happened, names it *deceit* to Esau's face — and then says
***"Yes, he will be blessed."*** **The defrauded party ratifies the fraud**, because in this book a
blessing is not a wish that can be corrected but a word that has been spent. **Genesis 27 is where
the Bible establishes that a blessing, once uttered, is irrevocable — and it establishes it in the
worst case available.**

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who speaks |
|---|---|---|
| **1–4** | Isaac's eyes are dim; he sends Esau for game, *"that my soul may bless you before I die"* | **Isaac**, at length |
| **5–10** | Rebekah, listening, issues counter-instructions and a menu | **Rebekah** |
| **11–13** | Jacob objects — **to the risk, not to the act** — and his mother takes the curse | **Jacob and Rebekah** |
| **14–17** | two kids, the savory food, Esau's clothes, the skins | nobody |
| **18–29** | five exchanges at the bedside; four senses tested; **the blessing** | **Isaac and Jacob** |
| **30–33** | Esau arrives one sentence too late; Isaac trembles; *"Yes, he will be blessed"* | **Esau and Isaac** |
| **34–38** | the bitter cry, the deceit named, the name re-derived, and *"Have you but one blessing?"* | **Esau and Isaac** |
| **39–40** | what is left: the sword, the service, and the yoke that comes off | **Isaac** |
| **41–45** | Esau's plan; Rebekah listening a second time; the flight to Haran | **Esau and Rebekah** |

### 27:1–4 — the first blind man in the Bible

> **1** When Isaac was old, and his eyes were dim, so that he could not see, he called Esau his elder
> son, and said to him, “My son?” He said to him, “Here I am.”
> **2** He said, “See now, I am old. I don’t know the day of my death.
> **3** Now therefore, please take your weapons, your quiver and your bow, and go out to the field,
> and take me venison.
> **4** Make me savory food, such as I love, and bring it to me, that I may eat, and that my soul may
> bless you before I die.”

***Wattikhênā ʿênāyw mērəʾōt*** — **his eyes grew too dim to see.** *Kāhâ* is the standing verb for
failing eyesight in the Hebrew Bible — Eli at 1 Samuel 3:2, and Moses at Deuteronomy 34:7,
of whom it is denied (*"his eye was not dim"*). **Isaac is the first person in Scripture said to have
lost his sight**, and the whole chapter is built on the fact.

**It is also the last of the ironies stacked on this family's eyes.** [[Hagar]] names God for seeing
her (16:13); Abraham lifts up his eyes and sees the ram (22:13); the servant *looks steadfastly* at
Rebekah (24:21); a king at a window sees Isaac with his wife (26:8) and that is the whole mechanism
of the previous chapter. **Now the man who was caught by being seen cannot see**, and the family's
next transaction runs entirely on what he cannot check. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

***"Here I am."*** ***Hinnēnî*** — and it is the word out of the binding. Isaac heard it twice on the
mountain: *"Abraham!" … "Here I am"* (22:1), and *"My father?" "Here I am, my son"* (22:7). **The one
conversation he ever had with his own father opened on this word**, and the one conversation this
chapter gives him with his elder son opens on it too. Genesis does not remark on it. See
[[The Binding of Isaac]].

***"I don't know the day of my death."*** **He is wrong about how close it is**, and Genesis says so
by arithmetic it never states: he dies at a hundred eighty (35:28), and on the book's own numbers
that is more than forty years from here. **No age is given for anyone in this chapter.** The
inference that Isaac is a hundred thirty-seven runs through Joseph — thirty before Pharaoh (41:46),
plus seven years of plenty and two of famine, so thirty-nine when Jacob comes to Egypt at a hundred
thirty (47:9); Joseph born in Jacob's ninety-first year, at the end of the fourteen years of service
(30:25); Jacob therefore about seventy-seven when he leaves, and Isaac, sixty at the twins' birth
(25:26), a hundred thirty-seven. **The wiki records that as an inference from four widely separated
verses and not as a statement of the text.** What it does yield, if it holds, is that **the son being
dressed by his mother in this chapter is a man of seventy-seven**, and that the deathbed lasts
another forty-three years.

***"Take me venison … make me savory food, such as I love."*** ***Ṣayid*** — game — is the word from
25:27–28, where Esau is *"a skillful hunter"* and *"Isaac loved Esau, because he ate his venison."*
**The one motive Genesis ever supplies for Isaac's preference is now the mechanism of the plot.**
And ***maṭʿammîm***, *savory food*, from *ṭāʿam*, **to taste**, occurs **six times in this chapter
and only twice elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible** (Proverbs 23:3, 6, both warnings about a ruler's
delicacies). It is effectively a Genesis 27 word.

***"That my soul may bless you."*** ***Təbārekəkā napšî*** — the idiom returns at 27:19, 25, and 31,
and it is the chapter's own theory of what a blessing is: **not a hope expressed but something spent
out of a man, requiring preparation, appetite, and presence.** The meal is not a courtesy before the
blessing; it is part of the apparatus. See [[Blessing]] and [[Language and Speech]].

**And note what is not in the verse.** Isaac says nothing about the covenant, the land, or Abraham.
He is an old man arranging a good dinner and a firstborn's portion, and the reader is the only party
in the room who knows that 25:23 exists.

### 27:5–10 — the second listener

> **5** Rebekah heard when Isaac spoke to Esau his son. Esau went to the field to hunt for venison,
> and to bring it.
> **6** Rebekah spoke to Jacob her son, saying, “Behold, I heard your father speak to Esau your
> brother, saying,
> **7** ‘Bring me venison, and make me savory food, that I may eat, and bless you before Yahweh
> before my death.’
> **8** Now therefore, my son, obey my voice according to that which I command you.
> **9** Go now to the flock and get me two good young goats from there. I will make them savory food
> for your father, such as he loves.
> **10** You shall bring it to your father, that he may eat, so that he may bless you before his
> death.”

***Wə-Ribqâ šōmaʿat*** — **a participle: she was listening.** Not *she happened to hear*; the form is
durative, and the sentence puts her in the house at the moment the instruction is given. She will do
it again at 27:42, when what is reported to her is Esau's plan to kill.

**Her quotation is not accurate, and the addition is the interesting part.** Isaac said *"that my
soul may bless you before I die"* (27:4). Rebekah reports *"and bless you **before Yahweh** before my
death"* (27:7). ***Lipnê YHWH*** **is not in what Isaac said.** Three readings, and the wiki adopts
none:

- **She heard more than 27:4 records**, and the narrator has summarized.
- **She is raising the stakes for Jacob**, making a domestic bequest into a transaction before God so
  that the thing worth stealing looks worth stealing.
- **She is telling him what she believes is at issue** — that this is the covenant blessing, which is
  what the oracle told her nine words of poetry ago — **without ever once citing the oracle.**

**Whatever the reason, it is the only time in the chapter anyone connects the blessing to God**, and
it is a phrase put into another person's mouth.

***"Obey my voice."*** ***Šəmaʿ bəqōlî*** — and she says it three times in this chapter: here, at
27:13 (*"Only obey my voice"*), and at 27:43, when she sends him away. **The idiom is one of the
loaded ones in Genesis.** *"Because you have listened to your wife's voice"* is the stated ground of
the curse on the ground (3:17); *"Abram listened to the voice of Sarai"* opens the Hagar crisis
(16:2); *"in all that Sarah says to you, listen to her voice"* is God's own command (21:12); and
*"because Abraham obeyed my voice"* is what the covenant was grounded on twice (22:18; 26:5).
**Genesis's most theologically weighted idiom for obedience is here spoken by a mother to a son about
a fraud**, three times, and the book does not comment. See
[[Listening to the Voice - Genesis 3 and Genesis 16]].

***"Two good young goats."*** ***Šənê gədāyê ʿizzîm ṭōbîm*** — for one blind man's dinner, which is a
great deal of meat, and the excess is the point: **the goats are needed for their skins as much as
for the pot.** The goat is about to become this family's instrument. Jacob deceives his father with a
garment and a goat; his sons will deceive him with a garment and a goat (37:31–33); Judah will owe a
kid to a veiled woman at a roadside (38:17). See
[[A Garment and a Goat - Jacob Deceives and Is Deceived]].

### 27:11–13 — the objection, and what it is not

> **11** Jacob said to Rebekah his mother, “Behold, Esau my brother is a hairy man, and I am a smooth
> man.
> **12** What if my father touches me? I will seem to him as a deceiver, and I would bring a curse on
> myself, and not a blessing.”
> **13** His mother said to him, “Let your curse be on me, my son. Only obey my voice, and go get
> them for me.”

**Jacob raises exactly one objection and it is operational.** *What if he touches me* — and the
consequence he fears is *"I would bring a curse on myself, and not a blessing."* **He is not troubled
that it is wrong; he is troubled that it might not work**, and the wiki states that as what the text
says rather than as a charge the text makes. ***Kimtaʿtēaʿ*** — *as one who mocks or trifles* — is
his own word for what he would look like, and the root is a rare one for deriding.

**And the risk he names is real.** *"Cursed be everyone who curses you"* is one line of the blessing
he is about to receive, and Genesis has already shown a father's word landing permanently on a son
who was not even the offender (9:25). **A blessing spoken over the wrong man cannot be recalled; the
same is true of a curse**, which is precisely why the scene is dangerous. See [[Curse]] and
[[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].

***"Let your curse be on me, my son."*** ***ʿĀlay qilləlātəkā bənî*** — four words, and **nobody else
in Genesis says anything like them.** She is the only person in the book who volunteers to take
another person's curse onto herself, and she does it in a sentence with the vocative *my son* in it.
The wiki notes the shape without inflating it: **she is not offering a substitution before God — God
is not in the room and is not consulted — she is telling her son that she will absorb the household
consequence.** Whether the offer is ever called in is a question Genesis declines to answer; **she
loses the son she is protecting within the chapter and the book never gives her a death scene.**

### 27:14–17 — the disguise

> **14** He went, and got them, and brought them to his mother. His mother made savory food, such as
> his father loved.
> **15** Rebekah took the good clothes of Esau, her elder son, which were with her in the house, and
> put them on Jacob, her younger son.
> **16** She put the skins of the young goats on his hands, and on the smooth of his neck.
> **17** She gave the savory food and the bread, which she had prepared, into the hand of her son
> Jacob.

**Four verbs, all hers, and Jacob does nothing but fetch and carry.** *She made, she took, she put,
she put, she gave* — the same construction the book used of another woman working an outcome through
a passive man (3:6: *she took, she gave*; 16:3: *Sarai took … and gave her to Abram*). **The most
active character in the chapter is Rebekah, and the most active she has been since she watered ten
camels.** See [[Rebekah]] and [[A Wife for Isaac]].

***"The good clothes of Esau … which were with her in the house."*** ***Habbəgādîm haḥămudōt*** —
*the desirable garments*, from *ḥāmad*, the verb of the tenth commandment and of the tree that was
*"to be desired to make one wise"* (3:6). **That a married man of forty-plus keeps his best clothes
at his mother's is a detail Genesis supplies and does not explain**; the reading that they are
priestly or festal garments is old and rests on nothing in the verse.

**Garments run through this family as the standing instrument of deceit.** The first clothes in the
Bible are God's, made to cover shame (3:21). After that: Esau's coat on Jacob here; Joseph's coat
dipped in blood and shown to Jacob (37:31–33); Tamar's widow's clothes exchanged for a veil (38:14);
Joseph's cloak in Potiphar's wife's hand (39:12). **Every one of them produces a false belief in
somebody, and three of the five are worked on Jacob or by him.** See [[Deception]] and
[[Nakedness and Covering]].

### 27:18–29 — four senses, two lies, and a blessing

> **18** He came to his father, and said, “My father?” He said, “Here I am. Who are you, my son?”
> **19** Jacob said to his father, “I am Esau your firstborn. I have done what you asked me to do.
> Please arise, sit and eat of my venison, that your soul may bless me.”

***"Who are you, my son?"*** — and the reply is ***ʾĀnōkî ʿĒśāw bəkōrekā***, **"I am Esau your
firstborn."**

**This is the flat lie**, and in Genesis that is rare enough to be the chapter's signature. The
family's method up to this point has been the true statement aimed to mislead: *"she is my sister"*
is defensible of Sarah (20:12), performed three times, and the whole force of the theme in this book
is that **almost nothing false is ever actually said.** Here five Hebrew words are simply untrue, and
they are untrue about identity — **not about a fact but about who is speaking.** See [[Deception]].

> **20** Isaac said to his son, “How is it that you have found it so quickly, my son?” He said,
> “Because Yahweh your God gave me success.”

***"Because Yahweh your God gave me success."*** ***Kî hiqrâ YHWH ʾĕlōhêkā ləpānāy*** — literally
*because Yahweh your God made it happen before me*, from *qārâ*, **to meet, to befall**, the verb of
chance encounter that [[Abraham's Servant]] used at the well when he asked God to *"send me good
speed"* (24:12).

**Two things about the sentence, and the wiki flags both.** First, it is **the only time in the
chapter that a character invokes God as an agent, and it is inside a lie** — the fastest cover story
in Genesis, produced under pressure, and it works. Second, ***your* God.** *ʾĔlōhêkā*, not *my God*
and not *our God*. **Jacob does not put himself inside the relationship he is invoking**, and he will
not do so until Bethel, where the promise is given to him directly and he answers with a conditional:
*"If God will be with me … then Yahweh will be my God"* (28:20–21). Genesis makes nothing of the
pronoun here. It is available to be noticed.

> **21** Isaac said to Jacob, “Please come near, that I may feel you, my son, whether you are really
> my son Esau or not.”
> **22** Jacob went near to Isaac his father. He felt him, and said, “The voice is Jacob’s voice, but
> the hands are the hands of Esau.”
> **23** He didn’t recognize him, because his hands were hairy, like his brother, Esau’s hands. So he
> blessed him.
> **24** He said, “Are you really my son Esau?” He said, “I am.”

***Haqqōl qôl Yaʿăqōb, wəhayyādayim yədê ʿĒśāw.*** **The chapter's most famous line, and it is a
correct forensic report.**

**Hearing works.** Isaac's ears identify his son, out loud, in a sentence he says to the man's face —
and he goes on anyway, because two of the other senses have voted the other way and the third is
about to. **This is not a man being fooled; it is a man overruling the evidence he has.** The four
channels, and what each returns:

| sense | what is offered to it | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **sight** | nothing — *"his eyes were dim, so that he could not see"* (27:1) | **unavailable** |
| **hearing** | Jacob's own voice, twice, unaltered | ***correct*** — and stated aloud, and overridden |
| **touch** | goat skins on the hands and the neck | deceived |
| **taste** | two kids cooked to taste like game | deceived |
| **smell** | Esau's clothes, off a man who has been in the field | deceived |

**And the question is asked twice.** *"Whether you are really my son Esau or not"* (27:21), and then,
after the touch has already satisfied him, *"Are you really my son Esau?"* (27:24) — ***ʾattâ zeh
bənî ʿĒśāw*** — to which the answer is one word: ***ʾānî***, *I am.* **Isaac is not careless. He runs
the test, gets a true reading, discounts it, and then asks a second time as if he knows the first
answer was not enough.** Genesis gives him no interior life at all in these verses, and every
proposal about what he suspected is a reconstruction.

> **25** He said, “Bring it near to me, and I will eat of my son’s venison, that my soul may bless
> you.” He brought it near to him, and he ate. He brought him wine, and he drank.
> **26** His father Isaac said to him, “Come near now, and kiss me, my son.”
> **27** He came near, and kissed him. He smelled the smell of his clothing, and blessed him, and
> said, “Behold, the smell of my son is as the smell of a field which Yahweh has blessed.

**The wine appears from nowhere.** Rebekah's instructions covered goats, food, and bread; the wine is
Jacob's own addition, and it is the second time in Genesis that a son gives his father drink before
taking something from him. The first was in a cave outside Zoar. **The wiki notes the pattern and
does not press it**: the daughters of Lot made their father drunk and Genesis says twice that he
*"didn't know"* (19:33, 35); here Isaac drinks one cup and the narrator draws no conclusion at all.
See [[Lot and His Daughters]] and [[The Vine and the Cup]].

***"The smell of a field which Yahweh has blessed."*** ***Śādeh*** — **the field** — is Esau's word.
*ʾÎš śādeh*, a man of the field, against *yōšēb ʾōhālîm*, dwelling in tents (25:27): the two
characterisations the whole cycle is built out of. **Isaac blesses the man of the tents for smelling
of the open country**, and it is the last sensory input before the word is spoken.

> **28** God give you of the dew of the sky, of the fatness of the earth, and plenty of grain and new
> wine.
> **29** Let peoples serve you, and nations bow down to you. Be lord over your brothers. Let your
> mother’s sons bow down to you. Cursed be everyone who curses you. Blessed be everyone who blesses
> you.”

**Twenty-eight words of Hebrew in two verses, and they fall into three parts.**

- **Fertility** (27:28) — *dew of the sky, fatness of the earth, grain and new wine.* Agricultural,
  not covenantal, and appropriate to the one patriarch who ever planted a crop (26:12). See [[Work]].
- **Dominion** (27:29a) — *peoples serve you, nations bow down, be lord over your brothers, let your
  mother's sons bow down.* **This is 25:23's *"The elder will serve the younger"* in the imperative**,
  and neither Isaac nor anyone else in the chapter says so. *"Your mother's sons"* is a curious
  phrase for a man with exactly one brother; it is the standard idiom of full siblings, and it is
  Isaac's, spoken to a son he thinks is the elder.
- **The Abrahamic clause** (27:29b) — *"Cursed be everyone who curses you. Blessed be everyone who
  blesses you."* **This is Genesis 12:3**, the promise the whole call was for, with the order
  reversed — 12:3 blesses first and curses second — and it will be quoted back in this order by
  Balaam at Numbers 24:9.

**And what is not in it is the whole of the covenant.** No land. No offspring like the stars. No
mention of Abraham, of an everlasting covenant, or of the nations being blessed *in* him. **The
stolen blessing is a firstborn's blessing — food and rule — plus one Abrahamic sentence**, and the
covenant proper is handed over three verses into the next chapter, by an Isaac who knows exactly who
he is talking to and is not being deceived by anybody: *"and give you the blessing of Abraham, to
you, and to your offspring with you, that you may inherit the land where you travel, which God gave
to Abraham"* (28:4). **The wiki keeps the two transfers apart**, because the difference is the whole
answer to whether the covenant was obtained by fraud. See [[Covenant]] and [[Call and Election]].

### 27:30–33 — one sentence too late

> **30** As soon as Isaac had finished blessing Jacob, and Jacob had just gone out from the presence
> of Isaac his father, Esau his brother came in from his hunting.
> **31** He also made savory food, and brought it to his father. He said to his father, “Let my
> father arise, and eat of his son’s venison, that your soul may bless me.”
> **32** Isaac his father said to him, “Who are you?” He said, “I am your son, your firstborn, Esau.”

**Genesis almost never times anything, and here it times everything.** *"As soon as Isaac had
finished … and Jacob had just gone out … Esau came in."* Three clauses to establish that the margin
was seconds.

***"I am your son, your firstborn, Esau."*** ***ʾĂnî binkā bəkōrəkā ʿĒśāw*** — **the true version of
the sentence that was false at 27:19**, in the same words and the same order, and it arrives after
the blessing rather than before it. **Genesis gives the liar's line to the man it belongs to, one
scene late**, and does not comment.

> **33** Isaac trembled violently, and said, “Who, then, is he who has taken venison, and brought it
> me, and I have eaten of all before you came, and have blessed him? Yes, he will be blessed.”

***Wayyeḥĕrad Yiṣḥāq ḥărādâ gədōlâ ʿad-məʾōd*** — **he trembled a very great trembling.** The
cognate accusative is the strongest intensification Hebrew narrative has, and *ḥārad* is the verb of
terror at a theophany or a battle line, not of an old man's annoyance. **It is the most violent
emotion Genesis ever attributes to Isaac**, whose recorded feelings otherwise are loving his wife
(24:67), loving venison (25:28), being afraid at Gerar (26:7), and being grieved by his
daughters-in-law (26:35).

***"Yes, he will be blessed."*** ***Gam-bārûk yihyeh*** — *indeed, blessed he shall be.*

**This is the hinge of the chapter and, arguably, of the cycle.** Isaac has just reconstructed the
whole fraud out loud, in a single interrogative sentence with four verbs in it, and his conclusion is
not *then it does not count* but **it stands.** He has no power to recall the word and does not
attempt to. **The blessing is treated as a released thing** — spoken over a body that was present,
into a situation, and now beyond the speaker.

**Genesis is not being naive about magic words; it is being precise about a legal-performative one.**
The same logic runs through the canon: Balaam cannot reverse what he has spoken (*"he has blessed,
and I can't reverse it"*, Numbers 23:20); Joshua honours an oath sworn to Gibeonites who obtained it
by fraud (Joshua 9:19); Jephthah keeps a vow he should never have made (Judges 11:35). **The word,
once out, has standing that the speaker's intention does not override.** See
[[Yes, He Will Be Blessed - The Irrevocable Word]] and [[Language and Speech]].

**And it is worth saying plainly what this does and does not settle.** It does not make the deceit
approved: Isaac names it *deceit* two verses later, Esau's grief is given eight verses, and Jacob
spends the next twenty years being defrauded in Paddan Aram. **What it settles is that the outcome
holds** — which is also what the oracle of 25:23 had said before either brother could act. **Genesis
puts the divine ranking and the human fraud on the same result and never tells the reader how to
divide the credit.** See
[[The Elder Will Serve the Younger - Genesis 25.23 in Malachi and Romans]].

### 27:34–38 — the cry

> **34** When Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with an exceeding great and bitter cry,
> and said to his father, “Bless me, even me also, my father.”
> **35** He said, “Your brother came with deceit, and has taken away your blessing.”

***Ṣəʿāqâ gədōlâ ûmārâ ʿad-məʾōd*** — **a great and bitter outcry, exceedingly.** ***Ṣəʿāqâ*** is not
an ordinary word for weeping; it is **the outcry word**, the noun Genesis uses of Sodom — *"the cry
of Sodom and Gomorrah is great"* (18:20) — and the one Exodus uses of Israel under the whip: *"I have
heard their cry because of their taskmasters"* (3:7). **In every other occurrence it is a cry that
reaches God and is acted on.** Here it reaches a blind old man and there is nothing left to give.

***Mārâ***, **bitter**, is from *mārar* — the root behind *mōrat rûaḥ*, the *bitterness of spirit*
his own wives caused his parents one chapter earlier (26:35). **The bitterness in this family goes
both directions inside two chapters.**

***"Your brother came with deceit."*** ***Bāʾ ʾāḥîkā bəmirmâ*** — and ***mirmâ*** is the noun the
theme is named for. **Isaac supplies the verdict the narrator withholds**, exactly as Pharaoh and
both Abimelechs supplied it for Abraham and for Isaac himself. **The difference is that this time the
person who names the deceit is inside the family**, and the accusation is against the son the book is
following. See [[Deception]].

> **36** He said, “Isn’t he rightly named Jacob? For he has supplanted me these two times. He took
> away my birthright. See, now he has taken away my blessing.” He said, “Haven’t you reserved a
> blessing for me?”

***Hăkî qārāʾ šəmô Yaʿăqōb, wayyaʿqəbēnî zeh paʿămāyim.*** **The second etymology of Jacob's name,
and the book gives it to the victim.**

At 25:26 the name came from ***ʿāqēb***, **heel** — a description of a hand on a foot, morally
neutral. Here it comes from ***ʿāqab***, **to supplant, to overreach, to take by the heel** — and it
is a charge. **One name, two derivations: Genesis supplies the innocent one at birth and the hostile
one from the mouth of the brother**, and Hosea 12:3 will keep the second as the prophets' summary of
the man. See [[Jacob]] and [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].

***"These two times."*** **Esau is the character who fuses the *bəkōrâ* and the *bərākâ***, and the
wiki keeps them apart because Genesis does: the birthright was sold, under oath, by a man who
*"despised"* it (25:33–34); the blessing was taken by disguise. **They are two words, two chapters,
two mechanisms — and Esau counts them as one grievance in one sentence**, which is the fairest thing
anyone says in the chapter and also a conflation. See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] and
[[Esau's Birthright in Hebrews]].

> **37** Isaac answered Esau, “Behold, I have made him your lord, and all his brothers I have given
> to him for servants. I have sustained him with grain and new wine. What then will I do for you, my
> son?”
> **38** Esau said to his father, “Have you but one blessing, my father? Bless me, even me also, my
> father.” Esau lifted up his voice, and wept.

***"Have you but one blessing, my father?"*** ***Hab-bərākâ ʾaḥat hîʾ-ləkā ʾābî*** — and the answer
the chapter gives is **yes.** **The blessing is finite.** Isaac's inventory in 27:37 is not rhetoric:
lordship given, brothers assigned, grain and wine spent — *"What then will I do for you, my son?"*
**A father in Genesis has a fixed quantity of this to distribute, and it has been distributed.**

**That is the assumption the book will later break.** Jacob, at the end of his life, blesses twelve
sons in a single chapter and two grandsons before that (48–49), and nobody's blessing exhausts the
supply. **Genesis 27 is the only place in the Bible where a blessing runs out**, and the wiki records
that as a feature of this scene rather than as the book's settled doctrine.

***"Esau lifted up his voice, and wept."*** These are the tears Hebrews 12:17 means — *"he found no
place for a change of mind though he sought it diligently with tears."* **What Hebrews adds to
Genesis is the word *rejected*; what Genesis has is a man crying in a tent.** See
[[Esau's Birthright in Hebrews]].

### 27:39–40 — what is left

> **39** Isaac his father answered him, “Behold, of the fatness of the earth will be your dwelling,
> and of the dew of the sky from above.
> **40** By your sword will you live, and you will serve your brother. It will happen, when you will
> break loose, that you shall shake his yoke from off your neck.”

**Verse 39 is the same two nouns as 27:28 in the opposite order, and the preposition is the crux.**

***Min*** in Hebrew is both **partitive** (*some of*) and **privative** (*away from*), and the two
readings of *mišmannê hāʾāreṣ … ûmiṭṭal haššāmayim* are opposite:

- ***"of the fatness of the earth … of the dew of the sky"*** — a real, lesser blessing: fertile
  land, second helping. **This is the WEB, the KJV, and the Septuagint.**
- ***"away from the fatness of the earth … away from the dew of the sky"*** — an anti-blessing: arid
  country, no dew. **This is the NRSV, the ESV, and most modern versions**, and it is supported by
  the fact that Edom's territory is genuinely dry, and by 27:37's *"What then will I do for you?"*,
  which has just said there is nothing left.

**The wiki records both and adopts neither**, and notes that the choice determines whether Esau
receives a blessing at all — which is precisely what Hebrews 12:17 says he did not. **The Hebrew is
identical in the two verses; only the order of the pair is switched**, which is either the point of
the reversal or a coincidence of style.

***"By your sword will you live, and you will serve your brother."*** *Wəʿal-ḥarbəkā tiḥyeh
wəʾet-ʾāḥîkā taʿăbōd.* **The oracle of 25:23 finally spoken out loud**, by the man who spent the
chapter trying to prevent it, to the son he was trying to give it to.

***"When you will break loose … you shall shake his yoke from off your neck."*** The verb ***tārîd***
is uncertain — from *rûd*, **to roam, to be restless**, hence *when you grow restless* or *when you
break loose* — and *pāraq* is to tear off. **It is a prophecy with a date in the historical books**:
2 Samuel 8:13–14 has David garrison Edom, and 2 Kings 8:20–22 has Edom revolt under Jehoram and set
up its own king, *"to this day."* **Both halves come true, in that order.** See [[Edom]] and
[[Prophecy and Vision]].

### 27:41–45 — the flight

> **41** Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing with which his father blessed him. Esau said in his
> heart, “The days of mourning for my father are at hand. Then I will kill my brother Jacob.”

***Wayyiśṭōm ʿĒśāw ʾet-Yaʿăqōb*** — **and the verb is *śāṭam*, to bear a grudge**, cousin to the
*śiṭnâ* that named a well one chapter earlier and to *śāṭān*, the accuser. **Genesis 26:21 put the
noun's first occurrence in the Bible on a hole in the ground; 27:41 puts the verb between two
brothers.** It will run to the end of the book: *"It may be that Joseph will hate us"* (50:15).

***"Then I will kill my brother Jacob."*** **The fourth threat or act of fratricide in Genesis**, and
the only one spoken *"in his heart"* rather than done. Cain kills Abel in a field (4:8); Lamech
boasts of killing a man (4:23); Joseph's brothers will say *"let's kill him"* (37:20) and sell him
instead. **Esau is the only one who plans it, waits, and never does it** — and at 33:4 he runs to
meet his brother and weeps on his neck. See [[Vengeance]] and [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

**And the delay is filial.** He will not do it while his father lives, which is a scruple, and he
mistimes it by more than forty years.

> **42** The words of Esau, her elder son, were told to Rebekah. She sent and called Jacob, her
> younger son, and said to him, “Behold, your brother Esau comforts himself about you by planning to
> kill you.
> **43** Now therefore, my son, obey my voice. Arise, flee to Laban, my brother, in Haran.
> **44** Stay with him a few days, until your brother’s fury turns away;
> **45** until your brother’s anger turn away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him.
> Then I will send, and get you from there. Why should I be bereaved of you both in one day?”

**She is listening again**, and this time what she hears is a plan against the son she engineered the
blessing for.

***"Obey my voice."*** **The third time**, and now it means *run.*

***"Stay with him a few days."*** ***Yāmîm ʾăḥādîm*** — the same phrase Genesis 29:20 uses of the
seven years Jacob served for Rachel, *"They seemed to him but a few days, for the love he had for
her."* **It is twenty years** (31:38, 41). **Rebekah's estimate is the most wrong sentence in the
chapter**, and it is the last thing she says to him.

***"Then I will send, and get you from there."*** **She never does.** Genesis records no message, no
reunion, and no death for her; she next appears as an occupant of a cave, named in Jacob's burial
instructions half a century later (49:31). **The mother who arranged everything in this chapter drops
out of the book at the end of it**, and the wiki notes that the text simply stops mentioning her.

***"Why should I be bereaved of you both in one day?"*** ***Lāmmâ ʾeškal gam-šənêkem yôm ʾeḥād*** —
and the sentence is the chapter's last, and its most exact. **It names blood-vengeance arithmetic:**
if Esau kills Jacob, the killer is liable, and she loses two — the murdered son and the son who must
answer for it. **She is the second parent in Genesis to reason this way**, after the woman of 4:14's
world where a killer must be protected by a mark. And **she is right on one side and wrong on the
other**: she does lose Jacob, for twenty years and, on the book's silence, forever; she does not lose
Esau.

**And the reason she gives Isaac, four verses later, is a different one entirely.** *"I am weary of
my life because of the daughters of Heth"* (27:46) — the grief of 26:35, produced as the pretext for
a journey whose real cause is a death threat she has just been told about. **She never mentions the
blessing to her husband, and Genesis never has the two of them speak about what happened.** That
verse opens the next pericope. See [[Isaac in Gerar]] and [[The Children of Heth]].

### What the pericope is doing

**It puts the covenant's transfer inside a fraud and refuses to separate them.**

Everything about the outcome had already been settled: the oracle ranked the twins before birth
(25:23), and the birthright had been sold under oath (25:33). **Nothing in this chapter was
necessary, and nobody in it mentions either fact.** Rebekah, who holds the oracle, behaves as though
the promise will fail unless she engineers it; Jacob, who holds the birthright, lies rather than
produce it; Isaac, who was told, tries to bless the elder anyway. **Four people act as if God has no
means of his own, and the result matches what God said.**

**Genesis does not resolve that and the wiki does not either.** The two standing readings — that the
deception was superfluous and God's word would have held without it, and that Genesis is showing
providence running through human wrongdoing without being cleaned by it — are both fully available in
the chapter, and the book gives no verdict. **What it gives instead is consequences**: Jacob leaves
with nothing but a blessing and does not come back for twenty years; he is deceived younger-for-elder
on his own wedding night by his mother's brother (29:23–26); his sons deceive him with a garment and
a goat (37:31–33); and his mother is never seen again. **The blessing holds and the cost is itemized
for eleven chapters.**

**And it is the last scene of the second generation.** Isaac has spoken his last recorded words to
Esau; Rebekah has spoken her last recorded words to Jacob; from 28:10 the book is following one man,
alone, on a road north. See
[[The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban]].

## People

- [[Isaac]] — **blind, and the first person in Scripture whose eyes are said to have failed.** He
  arranges a firstborn's blessing around a meal, runs a physical test on the man in front of him,
  gets a correct answer from his ears, states it aloud, and overrules it. When the fraud is exposed
  he trembles *"a very great trembling"*, reconstructs the whole thing in one interrogative sentence,
  and ratifies it: *"Yes, he will be blessed."* He then names the deceit to Esau's face, tells him
  the supply is exhausted, and gives him a sword and a yoke. **He speaks his last recorded words to
  his elder son here**, and it is the most emotion Genesis ever records in him.
- [[Rebekah]] — **the chapter is hers.** She is listening at 27:5 and listening again at 27:42; she
  quotes her husband with two words added that he did not say (*"before Yahweh"*); she issues the
  plan, cooks the food, dresses the son, and answers his one objection by taking his curse onto
  herself — **the only person in Genesis who offers to do that.** She says *"obey my voice"* three
  times, the last one meaning *run*. **She never once mentions the oracle she was given**, gives her
  husband a different reason for the flight than the true one (27:46), estimates the exile at *"a few
  days"*, and drops out of the book.
- [[Jacob]] — **his one recorded objection is that it might not work.** He tells the flat lie the
  family's whole method had avoided — *"I am Esau your firstborn"* — repeats it under direct
  questioning, and invents the fastest cover story in Genesis, in which he puts God's name and the
  pronoun ***your***. He never produces the birthright he legally holds. He leaves the chapter with
  a blessing, a death threat, and nothing else, and the next twenty years are spent on the receiving
  end of the same technique.
- [[Esau]] — **arrives seconds late and says truly the sentence his brother said falsely.** He cries
  an outcry Genesis otherwise reserves for Sodom and for Israel in Egypt; he supplies the second and
  harsher etymology of his brother's name; he fuses the birthright and the blessing into one
  grievance — *"these two times"* — and asks the question the chapter answers with a yes: *"Have you
  but one blessing, my father?"* Then he plans a murder, in his heart, for after his father's funeral,
  and never carries it out.
- [[God]] — **named in four verses and present nowhere.** Every occurrence is inside a human sentence:
  Rebekah's quotation (27:7), Jacob's lie (27:20), Isaac's simile (27:27), and Isaac's blessing
  (27:28). **He does not speak, act, appear, or get asked**, and the oracle he gave at 25:23 is never
  cited by anyone — including the woman he gave it to. **The outcome matches what he said anyway**,
  and Genesis makes no comment on the relation.
- [[Laban]] — named once (27:43), as the destination and as *"my brother"*; he has been standing in
  the text unused since 25:20, and he is about to own twenty years of Jacob's life and repay this
  chapter's substitution in kind.
- [[Abraham]] — not named, and his one sentence is in the blessing: *"Cursed be everyone who curses
  you. Blessed be everyone who blesses you"* is Genesis 12:3, reversed in order and spoken over the
  wrong son by a father who does not know which son it is.

## Places

- [[Haran (city)]] — the destination, named in the chapter's second-to-last speech; the town
  [[Terah]] died in and [[Abraham]] left, and the place the family keeps going back to for wives.
  Jacob's *"few days"* there will be twenty years.
- [[Edom]] — unnamed and forecast. 27:39–40 is the charter of Esau's country: fertile or arid
  depending on one preposition, living by the sword, serving its brother, and eventually breaking the
  yoke — which 2 Kings 8:20–22 records happening.
- [[Beersheba]] — unnamed, and almost certainly where the scene is: 26:23–33 settled Isaac there, and
  28:10 has Jacob *"went out from Beersheba"* on the journey this chapter starts.
- [[Canaan]] — the land the blessing does not mention. **No territorial grant is in 27:27–29 at
  all**; it arrives at 28:4, from an Isaac who knows who he is speaking to.

## Themes

- [[Blessing]] — the chapter that defines it: a finite substance, spent from a body, procured by
  fraud, and **irrevocable once released**. *"Yes, he will be blessed"* is said by the man who was
  defrauded, two verses after he worked out how.
- [[Deception]] — **the one flat lie in the family's whole repertoire**, told twice under direct
  questioning, with four senses managed one at a time and God's name used as cover.
- [[Curse]] — the risk Jacob names (*"I would bring a curse on myself"*), the counter-clause inside
  the blessing, and Rebekah's *"Let your curse be on me, my son"* — the only offer of its kind in
  Genesis.
- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — *bəkōrâ* and *bərākâ* fused by Esau at 27:36 and kept apart by
  everyone else; the fourth of the book's six inversions, and the only one worked by disguise.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the performative word at full strength: what has been said cannot be
  unsaid, the speaker's intention does not travel with it, and the whole chapter is people talking
  while one of them cannot see.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the theme inverted. Genesis has spent fourteen chapters on eyes that
  lift up, look steadfastly, and see God; here sight is simply gone, and the four remaining senses
  are worked against their owner.
- [[Vengeance]] — *wayyiśṭōm*, and the fourth fratricide in the book — planned, dated to a funeral,
  and abandoned.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — a household divided at 25:28 and detonated here; the brother who
  plans the killing is the one who runs to embrace him at 33:4.
- [[Call and Election]] — the ranking announced before birth (25:23) and then obtained by fraud, with
  no character in the chapter mentioning the first and every character participating in the second.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — the blessing as forecast, which Hebrews 11:20 reads as faith: *"By faith,
  Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau, even concerning things to come."*
- [[Covenant]] — conspicuously **not** transferred here. The land, the offspring, and Abraham's name
  are absent from 27:27–29 and arrive knowingly at 28:3–4.
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — clothes as the family's standing instrument, from 3:21 to 37:31.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's box read *Genesis 27:1–45* and **it stands as drawn;
  no unchecked boxes were redrawn this pass.** The opening is a fresh time marker (*"When Isaac was
  old"*) after 26:35's closing note, and the close at 27:45 is where Rebekah stops speaking to Jacob;
  **27:46 changes addressee to Isaac and changes the stated reason**, and the next box already begins
  there. The alternative — running the unit to 28:9, so that the whole blessing-and-departure
  sequence is one pericope — was considered and rejected because it would swallow Bethel's
  preliminaries and make a unit of nearly sixty verses. **The seam at 27:45/46 is the sharpest one
  available**, and it has the side effect of putting Rebekah's cover story in the same unit as the
  grief it is drawn from.
- **On the title.** The plan's *The Stolen Blessing* was kept. It is the traditional name, it is what
  the chapter is about, and no other book has a passage page by that title.
- **On 27:39, unresolved.** *Min* is both partitive and privative, and the two renderings of
  *mišmannê hāʾāreṣ* are opposites: a lesser blessing, or an anti-blessing. **The WEB, KJV, and LXX
  take the first; the NRSV, ESV, and most modern versions take the second.** The choice decides
  whether Esau is blessed at all, which is exactly what Hebrews 12:17 denies. **Both are recorded
  above and neither is adopted**, and the wiki notes that the Hebrew of 27:28 and 27:39 uses the same
  two nouns with only their order switched.
- **On Isaac's age and Jacob's, flagged as inference.** Genesis gives no age anywhere in this
  chapter. The traditional reckoning that makes Isaac a hundred thirty-seven and Jacob about
  seventy-seven runs through Genesis 41:46, 45:6, 47:9, 30:25, and 25:26, and **the wiki sets the
  chain out on the passage page and labels it an inference from four widely separated verses.** It is
  recorded because it changes how the scene reads — a mother dressing a middle-aged man — and not
  because Genesis asserts it.
- **On the absence of God, stated and not explained.** *Yahweh* (27:7, 20, 27) and *ʾĕlōhîm* (27:20,
  28) fall in four verses and **never as agent**; there is no theophany, no dream, no divine speech,
  and nobody prays. The oracle
  of 25:23 is not quoted by any character. **The wiki records this as the chapter's most conspicuous
  feature** and sets the two standing readings — the deception was superfluous, or providence runs
  through wrongdoing uncleaned — side by side without adopting either. This follows the same practice
  the wiki used for the narrator's asymmetry at 25:34.
- **On what the blessing does and does not contain.** 27:27–29 has fertility, dominion, and the
  Genesis 12:3 clause. **It has no land, no offspring promise, and no mention of Abraham or of the
  covenant**, all of which Isaac hands over knowingly at 28:3–4. **The wiki keeps the two transfers
  distinct**, because the common statement that Jacob "stole the covenant" is not what Genesis 27
  says. Recorded here so the next pericope can build on it rather than re-derive it.
- **On Rebekah's *"before Yahweh"* at 27:7.** The phrase is not in Isaac's speech at 27:4. Three
  readings are set out above — a summarized original, a deliberate escalation, and her own reading of
  what is at stake — and **none is adopted.** What is checkable is that the words are added in a
  quotation and that they are the only link anyone in the chapter makes between the blessing and God.
- **Which new pages were made and which were not.** No new people, places, or themes were needed:
  every figure in the chapter already has a page, and [[Haran (city)]], [[Edom]], and [[Beersheba]]
  all existed. **Three connection pages were created** —
  [[A Garment and a Goat - Jacob Deceives and Is Deceived]],
  [[The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban]], and
  [[Yes, He Will Be Blessed - The Irrevocable Word]]. **Rachel, Leah, Bethel, and Paddan Aram were
  deliberately left as plain text** in this page's forward references; their pericopes are the next
  two and three boxes on the queue.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Rebekah's cover story and Isaac's knowing transfer of the
  Abrahamic covenant (27:46–28:9); Bethel and Jacob's *"then Yahweh will be my God"* (28:10–22);
  Laban's substitution (29:21–27); the Jabbok, where a blessing is demanded instead of stolen (32:26);
  the reconciliation (33:1–4); and the coat and the goat (37:31–33). All are referenced and none is
  developed. **No dangling links were left.**
