# Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 27:46–28:9

## Summary

**The blessing is given a second time, and this time nobody is deceived.**

That is the whole reason this short passage matters, and it is why the wiki keeps it separate from
the chapter before it. In [[The Stolen Blessing]] a blind man is worked through four senses and
blesses the wrong son; here **Isaac calls Jacob by name, knows exactly who is standing in front of
him, and hands over the thing chapter 27 did not contain** — the land, the fruitfulness, and *"the
blessing of Abraham."* **No land is granted anywhere in 27:27–29.** It is granted at 28:4, openly, by
a father who has already worked out that he was defrauded and who does it anyway.

**And it is done under a cover story that everyone in the scene accepts.** Rebekah has just been told
that Esau intends to kill his brother (27:41–42). What she tells her husband is that she is sick of
Hittite daughters-in-law (27:46). **She never mentions the murder, and she never mentions the
blessing**, and Genesis never once has Isaac and Rebekah speak to each other about what happened in
that tent. **The flight becomes an errand**; the errand becomes the official version; and the
official version is the one [[Esau]] watches and acts on.

**Then Esau acts on it, and gets it half right.** Three times the narrator says *Esau saw* — he sees
the blessing, the sending, the command about Canaanite wives, and his brother's obedience — and he
draws the correct inference: *"the daughters of Canaan didn't please Isaac, his father."* **It is the
most sustained piece of reasoning Genesis ever gives him**, and the remedy he chooses is to marry a
daughter of [[Ishmael]]. **He corrects toward the family and lands on the branch of it that the
covenant had already passed over**, and he keeps the wives he had. Genesis reports it in one verse
and passes no verdict.

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who speaks |
|---|---|---|
| **27:46** | Rebekah gives Isaac a reason for the journey that is not the reason | **Rebekah** — her last recorded words in Scripture |
| **28:1–2** | Isaac summons Jacob, blesses him, and forbids a Canaanite wife | **Isaac** |
| **28:3–4** | *ʾĒl Šadday*, fruitfulness, a company of peoples, and **the blessing of Abraham** — the land, at last | **Isaac** |
| **28:5** | *"Isaac sent Jacob away"*, with a genealogical trailer that repeats 25:20 | nobody |
| **28:6–8** | three clauses of *Esau saw*, and one correct conclusion | nobody |
| **28:9** | Esau marries Mahalath, Ishmael's daughter, **besides the wives he had** | nobody |

### 27:46 — the cover story

> **46** Rebekah said to Isaac, “I am weary of my life because of the daughters of Heth. If Jacob
> takes a wife of the daughters of Heth, such as these, of the daughters of the land, what good will
> my life do me?”

***Qaṣtî bəḥayyay*** — *I loathe my life.* The verb ***qûṣ*** is stronger than the WEB's *weary*: it
is the verb of revulsion, used of Egypt dreading the Israelites (Exodus 1:12) and of God loathing a
generation (Leviticus 20:23). ***Lāmmâ lî ḥayyîm*** — *why should I have life?* — closes the sentence
with the same shape. **She is arguing at maximum volume for a thing she wants for an entirely
different reason.**

**And the grievance is real.** *"The daughters of Heth"* are [[Judith]] and
[[Basemath (daughter of Elon)]], the two Hittite women Esau married at forty, of whom 26:35 says
*"They grieved Isaac's and Rebekah's spirits."* See [[Isaac in Gerar]] and
[[The Children of Heth]]. **The grief is genuine, four verses old, and being spent as currency.**

**What she does not say is the whole of the chapter she has just come out of.** Four verses earlier
she told Jacob *"your brother Esau comforts himself about you by planning to kill you"* (27:42) and
sent him to Haran to save his life. **To Isaac she gives a marriage problem.** Three readings, and
the wiki adopts none:

- **She is protecting Jacob a second way.** Telling Isaac about the murder plan would force him to
  act against Esau, and she has already lost control of one son.
- **She is protecting the fiction.** Naming the threat means naming its cause, and its cause is what
  she engineered.
- **She is giving the true reason in a different register** — the marriage problem is what the
  journey is actually *for*, and the flight is what it is *because of*, and both are true at once.

**This is also the last thing Rebekah says in the Bible.** She has spoken in every scene since a well
in Aram Naharaim; she does not speak again, is not narrated again, and next appears as an occupant of
a cave in Jacob's burial instructions (49:31). **Genesis gives her no death scene and no reunion**,
and her last recorded sentence is a piece of misdirection aimed at her husband. See [[Rebekah]].

### 28:1–2 — the command, and whose it is

> **1** Isaac called Jacob, blessed him, and commanded him, “You shall not take a wife of the
> daughters of Canaan.
> **2** Arise, go to Paddan Aram, to the house of Bethuel your mother’s father. Take a wife from
> there from the daughters of Laban, your mother’s brother.

**Three verbs, and the middle one is the point: *he blessed him*.** ***Wayyəbārek ʾōtô wayṣawwēhû*** —
and Isaac is not being touched, fed, or smelled. He *calls* Jacob, which means he knows the name of
the man he is speaking to. **Everything in chapter 27 depended on Isaac not knowing; nothing here
does.**

***"You shall not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan."*** **This is Abraham's oath, restated as a
father's command**, and it is very nearly word for word: *"you shall not take a wife for my son of
the daughters of the Canaanites"* (24:3). What has changed is the machinery. **Abraham would not let
Isaac go** — *"beware that you don't bring my son there again"* (24:6), the one clause of that oath
with no release attached — and sent a servant with ten camels instead. **Isaac sends the son
himself**, on foot, alone, and Genesis does not mention a gift, an animal, or a companion. See
[[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]] and
[[Two Errands to Paddan Aram - Genesis 24 and Genesis 28]].

***"To the house of Bethuel your mother's father … the daughters of Laban, your mother's
brother."*** **The destination is specified through Rebekah, twice**, and [[Bethuel]] is named as
alive — a man who spoke one sentence in Genesis 24 and has not been mentioned since. **Genesis never
narrates his death and never mentions him again after this verse.** The household Jacob actually
arrives at is [[Laban]]'s (29:13–14), and Bethuel is nowhere in it.

**And the instruction is to marry a first cousin.** *"The daughters of Laban"* — plural, and the
plural is doing quiet work: [[Jacob]] is being sent for one wife and the sentence has already
provided for two. See [[Marriage]].

### 28:3–4 — what chapter 27 did not contain

> **3** May God Almighty bless you, and make you fruitful, and multiply you, that you may be a
> company of peoples,
> **4** and give you the blessing of Abraham, to you, and to your offspring with you, that you may
> inherit the land where you travel, which God gave to Abraham.”

***Wə-ʾĒl Šadday yəbārēk ʾōtəkā.*** **The name arrives, and in Genesis it arrives only at transfers
of the promise** — 17:1 to Abraham, here to Jacob, 35:11 to Jacob again, and three times from Jacob's
own mouth at the end of his life. **Its meaning is genuinely unknown**; *Almighty* descends from the
Septuagint's *pantokratōr* by way of a rabbinic reading, and is the least likely of the four standing
etymologies. See [[God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus]] and [[The Names of God]].

***Yaprəkā wəyarbekā*** — **be fruitful and multiply**, the creation blessing of 1:28, restated to
Noah at 9:1 and to Abraham at 17:6. **Isaac is not inventing a formula; he is reciting the oldest one
in the book** over a man who is about to be childless for seven years.

***Qəhal ʿammîm*** — **a company of peoples**, and ***qāhāl*** here is its **first occurrence in the
Hebrew Bible.** It is the assembly word — the noun the Septuagint renders *ekklēsia* and that the
Torah uses of Israel gathered at Sinai — and its first appearance in Scripture is a father's wish
over a fugitive. **Isaac asks for it; God grants it in the same words at Bethel**, where 35:11 has
*"a nation and a company of nations will be from you."* See [[The Nations]].

***Birkat ʾAbrāhām*** — **the blessing of Abraham.** **The exact phrase occurs here and nowhere else
in Genesis**, and in the New Testament only at Galatians 3:14, where Paul says it comes *"on the
Gentiles through Christ Jesus."*

**And its content is the thing the stolen blessing conspicuously lacked.** Set the two side by side:

| | **27:27–29 — taken by fraud** | **28:3–4 — given knowingly** |
|---|---|---|
| **fertility** | dew, fatness, grain, new wine | *be fruitful and multiply* |
| **rule** | peoples serve you, be lord over your brothers | — |
| **land** | **absent** | *"inherit the land where you travel, which God gave to Abraham"* |
| **offspring as a people** | — | *a company of peoples* |
| **Abraham** | one clause of 12:3, unattributed | *"the blessing of Abraham"*, by name |
| **God as agent** | invoked in the third person | *ʾĒl Šadday*, addressed |

**The covenant was not stolen.** It is handed over here, in five clauses, by a man who has already
reconstructed the fraud out loud (27:33) and named it *deceit* to Esau's face (27:35). **What Jacob
took by disguise was a firstborn's portion — food and dominion — and what he receives openly is the
promise.** The wiki stated this in advance on the previous page and records it now as delivered. See
[[Covenant]] and [[Blessing]].

***"The land where you travel."*** ***ʾEreṣ məgurêkā*** — **the land of your sojournings**, from
*gûr*, to reside as an alien. **It is the same Hebrew phrase God used at 17:8**, which the WEB
renders there as *"the land where you are traveling"*, and the paradox is left standing: the land to be inherited is described by the word for not owning it. **Jacob will be out of
it for twenty years, and the only ground his family holds when Genesis ends is a cave and a field.**
See [[Land and Sojourning]].

### 28:5 — the sending, and a trailer

> **5** Isaac sent Jacob away. He went to Paddan Aram to Laban, son of Bethuel the Syrian, Rebekah’s
> brother, Jacob’s and Esau’s mother.

**The English is tangled and the Hebrew is not.** The appositives stack onto their nearest owners:
Laban is *son of Bethuel the Aramean* and *brother of Rebekah*, and Rebekah is *mother of Jacob and
Esau*. The WEB's word order leaves *"Rebekah's brother, Jacob's and Esau's mother"* looking like two
descriptions of one person; they are not.

***Hā-ʾărammî*** — **the Aramean**, which the WEB renders *Syrian*. The gentilic is applied to Bethuel
and to Laban across the cycle, and it is the word behind Deuteronomy 26:5's creedal opening about a
wandering Aramean father. See [[Aram Naharaim]], which is the same region the book calls
**Paddan Aram** from 25:20 onward.

**The whole verse repeats 25:20, which said all of this already** — *"Rebekah, the daughter of
Bethuel the Syrian of Paddan Aram, the sister of Laban the Syrian"* — and repeating a pedigree at a
departure is a habit of the book's genealogical register rather than of its storytelling. See
[[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]].

***"Jacob's and Esau's mother."*** **The younger is named first**, in a construction where birth
order is the ordinary default. Genesis does the reverse at 35:29, where *"Esau and Jacob"* bury their
father with the elder first. **Two verses, two orders, no comment.**

### 28:6–9 — Esau reasons, and corrects in the wrong direction

> **6** Now Esau saw that Isaac had blessed Jacob and sent him away to Paddan Aram, to take him a
> wife from there, and that as he blessed him he gave him a command, saying, “You shall not take a
> wife of the daughters of Canaan,”
> **7** and that Jacob obeyed his father and his mother, and was gone to Paddan Aram.
> **8** Esau saw that the daughters of Canaan didn’t please Isaac, his father.
> **9** Esau went to Ishmael, and took, besides the wives that he had, Mahalath the daughter of
> Ishmael, Abraham’s son, the sister of Nebaioth, to be his wife.

***Wayyarʾ ʿĒśāw*** — **and Esau saw**, twice, governing four clauses. **This is the only place in
Genesis where the narrator takes up a position inside Esau's head and stays there**, and what it
finds is not resentment but inference: the blessing, the sending, the errand, the prohibition, the
obedience — and then a conclusion drawn from all five. **The man Genesis has so far characterised by
appetite and by an outcry turns out to be a careful observer of his own family.**

***"Jacob obeyed his father and his mother."*** ***Wayyišmaʿ Yaʿăqōb ʾel-ʾābîw wəʾel-ʾimmô*** — the
verb is ***šāmaʿ***, the same *listen / obey* that Rebekah demanded three times in the previous
chapter, and here the narrator applies it to **both parents at once**, in the sentence immediately
after the one where Jacob lied twice to one of them. **Genesis does not flag the irony.** It is
inside Esau's field of view: what he can see is a brother who did as he was told.

***"Esau went to Ishmael."*** **Ishmael is, on the book's own arithmetic, already dead.** Abraham was
eighty-six at Ishmael's birth (16:16) and a hundred at Isaac's (21:5), making Ishmael fourteen years
the elder; he died at a hundred thirty-seven (25:17), when Isaac was a hundred twenty-three; and the
traditional reckoning puts Isaac at a hundred thirty-seven in this scene. **The wiki records that as
an inference from four widely separated verses and not as a statement of the text** — the same
caution it applied to Isaac's age at 27:1 — and notes the standard resolution, that *"went to
Ishmael"* means went to Ishmael's household, which is how the phrase works elsewhere.

***"Besides the wives that he had."*** ***ʿAl-nāšāyw*** — **he adds, he does not replace.** The two
Hittite women stay. **Esau's correction is additive**, which is exactly what makes it not a
correction: the daughters of Heth who cost his mother her appetite for life are still in the camp.

***Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael.*** **He marries his first cousin, into the line the covenant
passed over.** Ishmael was the elder son sent away because *"your offspring will be named
through Isaac"* (21:12); Esau is the elder son who has just lost the blessing. **The two passed-over
branches marry each other**, and Genesis lets the symmetry stand without a word. **His mother's own
solution had been the same in shape and different in target** — go to the family — and the family she
meant was Bethuel's. See [[Mahalath]], [[Ishmael]], and [[Call and Election]].

***"The sister of Nebaioth."*** Nebaioth is Ishmael's firstborn (25:13). **Identifying a bride by her
brother rather than only by her father is how this book marks who negotiates the marriage** —
Rebekah is *"the sister of Laban"* and it is Laban who answers for her (24:29, 50, 55). **Nebaioth is
named here and never appears**; the prophets keep him as a nation (Isaiah 60:7). See
[[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]] and [[Abraham's Other Sons in Isaiah 60]].

**And the register at Genesis 36:3 calls this woman *Basemath*.** It gives *Basemath* to Ishmael's
daughter and *Adah* to Elon's, exactly reversing the names of 26:34 and 28:9. **The wiki flags the
disagreement and adopts no harmonization.** See
[[The Names of Esau's Wives - Genesis 26 and Genesis 36]].

### The seam this passage sits on

**Nothing in 27:46–28:9 knows that chapter 27 happened.** There is no deception, no bitter cry, no
death threat, no flight — only two Hittite daughters-in-law, a marriage errand, and a blessing given
in the open. **Read on its own, this is a complete and untroubled account of why Jacob went to Paddan
Aram**, and it is the account Esau receives.

**Source criticism reads that as a seam rather than as a subtlety**, and the markers are unusually
dense for nine verses: ***Paddan Aram*** (ten occurrences in Genesis, all in this register and none
in the narrative that uses *Aram Naharaim*), ***ʾĒl Šadday***, ***be fruitful and multiply***,
***qəhal ʿammîm***, the concern with marriage outside the line, and a chronological frame that gives
ages and pedigrees rather than scenes. **On that reading 27:46–28:9 is Priestly material, and the
Priestly account of Jacob's departure contains no stolen blessing at all.**

**The wiki records the reading and adopts no verdict**, as it did for the two creation accounts and
the two flights of Hagar. What is checkable without any source theory is the fact itself: **the book
supplies two reasons for one journey, puts them in adjacent verses, gives one to the mother and one
to the son, and never lets the two parents discuss it.** See
[[Two Creation Accounts - Genesis 1 and Genesis 2]] and [[The Two Flights of Hagar]].

### What the pericope is doing

**It launders the flight into an errand, and it settles the ownership question the previous chapter
opened.**

Jacob leaves Beersheba as a fugitive from a brother who means to kill him, and he leaves as a son
sent for a wife with the blessing of Abraham on him. **Both are true, and the book supplies them four
verses apart without reconciling them.** Everyone gets the version that suits them: Isaac gets a
marriage crisis, Esau gets a family policy, Jacob gets a death threat and a covenant, and the reader
gets all three.

**And it does the load-bearing thing quietly.** The most common statement about Genesis 27 — that
Jacob stole the covenant — is answered here, in five clauses, by an Isaac nobody is deceiving.
**What was stolen and what was given are two different blessings**, and the second one is the one the
rest of the Bible quotes. See
[[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]].

**What the passage does not do is exonerate anybody.** Rebekah's last sentence is a misdirection.
Jacob leaves with nothing and will be defrauded for twenty years by the man he is being sent to.
Esau, given a clear view of what his family wants, marries into the one branch of it that had already
been set aside. **Genesis hands out the promise and the consequences in the same nine verses.**

## People

- [[Isaac]] — **the same man, undeceived.** He calls Jacob by name, blesses him a second time, and
  gives him what the first blessing did not have: *ʾĒl Šadday*, fruitfulness, a company of peoples,
  the land of his sojournings, and *"the blessing of Abraham"* by name. **He restates his father's
  marriage oath as his own command** and reverses its one unreleased clause by sending the son
  himself. These are his last recorded words to Jacob; he will not speak again until 35:27–29, when
  he is buried.
- [[Rebekah]] — **she gives her husband a reason that is not the reason**, spends four-verse-old grief
  as currency, and never mentions either the murder plot or the blessing. *Qaṣtî bəḥayyay* — *I loathe
  my life* — is stronger than the WEB's *weary*, and it works: Isaac acts within one verse. **This is
  the last thing she says in the Bible**, and the book gives her no death, no reunion, and no further
  scene.
- [[Jacob]] — **silent throughout.** He is called, blessed, commanded, and sent, and Genesis records
  no reply. He leaves with a covenant and, so far as the text says, nothing else — no camels, no
  gifts, no servant — and 32:10 will have him say *"with just my staff I crossed over this Jordan."*
  The only thing said about him from outside is Esau's observation that he *obeyed his father and his
  mother*.
- [[Esau]] — **his longest stretch of reported reasoning in the book.** Two occurrences of *Esau saw*
  govern four clauses, and the conclusion he draws is correct: Canaanite wives displease his father.
  **The remedy is not.** He goes to Ishmael's house, marries [[Mahalath]] — his first cousin, out of
  the branch the covenant passed over — and keeps the Hittite wives he already had. Genesis passes no
  verdict, here or at 36:2–3, where the same woman is called Basemath.
- [[Mahalath]] — **Esau's third wife**, daughter of [[Ishmael]] and sister of Nebaioth, named once
  here and renamed in the register at 36:3. She says nothing, and her marriage is the joining of the
  two lines the promise had already stepped past.
- [[Ishmael]] — **named as a father-in-law, thirteen years after the book buried him**, on its own
  arithmetic; the phrase *"went to Ishmael"* is standardly read as his household. The elder son sent
  away at 21:12 receives the elder son passed over at 27:33.
- [[Laban]] — the destination for the second time in this book, and the first time for a man rather
  than a servant. *"The daughters of Laban"* is the whole marriage instruction, and it is plural.
- [[Bethuel]] — named alive, as the house Jacob is sent to and as Laban's father and Rebekah's; he
  does not appear when Jacob arrives (29:13–14) and Genesis never mentions him again.
- [[The Children of Heth]] — *the daughters of Heth*, twice in one verse, and the argument that moves
  Isaac. The same people who sold Abraham a burial cave supply the wives that make his son's wife
  loathe her life.
- [[Abraham]] — dead for years and named three times in five verses: the blessing is *his*, the land
  was given to *him*, and the marriage rule is *his oath* in his son's mouth. **He is the authority
  the whole passage runs on and the only party to it who cannot be consulted.**
- [[God]] — **invoked, not present.** *ʾĒl Šadday* is asked to bless, make fruitful, multiply, and
  give; God does not speak, appear, or act anywhere in these nine verses. **He will, four verses
  later, on a road out of Beersheba** — and what he says at 28:13–14 answers what Isaac asked for
  here.

## Places

- [[Aram Naharaim]] — under its Jacob-cycle name **Paddan Aram**, four times in nine verses; the
  region Bethuel and Laban live in, the source of every wife in the covenant line for three
  generations, and the one country Abraham forbade his own son ever to enter (24:6).
- [[Canaan]] — named as the place the wives must *not* come from, in the same passage that promises
  the land itself. **The land is to be inherited and its daughters refused**, which is the tension
  the whole marriage rule runs on.
- [[Haran (city)]] — not named in these verses; it is the town Laban is actually found in, and 28:10
  gives it as Jacob's destination.
- [[Beersheba]] — unnamed here and named at 28:10 as the place Jacob went out from; 26:23–33 settled
  the household there and the whole scene belongs to it.

## Themes

- [[Blessing]] — the second transfer, and the knowing one. Everything 27:27–29 omitted is here, and
  the man who gives it has already worked out that he was defrauded.
- [[Covenant]] — *"the blessing of Abraham"*, the land of sojournings, and offspring as a *qəhal
  ʿammîm*: the covenant proper, handed on deliberately in the generation after it was nearly lost in
  a tent.
- [[Marriage]] — endogamy as covenant policy: no daughters of Canaan, and a first cousin named in
  advance. Both sons act on the rule in this passage and only one of them does it as instructed.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — *ʾereṣ məgurêkā*, the land of your sojournings, promised as an
  inheritance to a man who is leaving it for twenty years.
- [[Call and Election]] — the passed-over branches converge: Ishmael's daughter marries Esau, and the
  promise goes with the man walking north alone.
- [[The Nations]] — *qəhal ʿammîm*, the first *qāhāl* in Scripture, asked for by a father and granted
  by God in the same words at 35:11.
- [[The Names of God]] — *ʾĒl Šadday*, whose meaning is unknown and whose distribution in Genesis is
  exact: it appears at transfers of the promise and nowhere else.
- [[Deception]] — at low intensity and in a new place: not a lie about identity but a true grievance
  offered as a whole reason. **Nothing Rebekah says at 27:46 is false.**
- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — the elder brother, having lost both the *bəkōrâ* and the
  *bərākâ*, marries the daughter of the previous elder brother to have lost them.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — Abraham's oath at 24:3 surviving as a command in the next generation, with the
  sanction dropped and the content kept.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundary, which was redrawn this pass.** The plan's box read *Jacob's Ladder at
  Bethel — Genesis 27:46–28:22*, one unit of twenty-seven verses under a title that describes only
  its second half. **It was split into two boxes** — *Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram* (27:46–28:9)
  and *Jacob's Ladder at Bethel* (28:10–22) — and the first was ingested. The seam at 28:9/10 is as
  sharp as any in the book: 28:6–9 closes on Esau, and 28:10 restarts with a fresh itinerary formula,
  *"Jacob went out from Beersheba, and went toward Haran"*, on a different subject in a different
  place with a different genre. **27:46–28:9 is itself framed by marriage** — daughters of Heth at
  27:46, daughters of Canaan at 28:8 — which is the strongest internal evidence that it is one unit
  and Bethel is another. The previous pericope's notes had already forecast this split; the plan had
  not been updated to match, and now is.
- **On the title.** *Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram* names the passage's own event rather than
  Rebekah's pretext or Esau's coda, and no other book has a passage page by that title. *The Second
  Blessing* was considered and rejected as too interpretive for a filename.
- **On the two reasons for one journey, recorded and not resolved.** 27:42–45 gives a death threat;
  27:46 gives a marriage problem; the second is what Isaac is told and the first is what the reader
  knows. **Both are in the text and neither is called false.** The source-critical reading — that
  27:46–28:9 is Priestly and knows nothing of the deception — is set out on the page as a reading
  with its markers named (*Paddan Aram*, *ʾĒl Šadday*, *be fruitful and multiply*, *qəhal ʿammîm*,
  pedigree over scene) and **is not adopted.**
- **On the chronology of 28:9, flagged as inference.** Ishmael is dead by roughly thirteen years at
  this point on the book's own numbers (16:16; 21:5; 25:17, plus the reckoning that puts Isaac at a
  hundred thirty-seven here). **Genesis states none of this in the passage**, the arithmetic runs
  through four widely separated verses, and the ordinary reading of *"went to Ishmael"* as *went to
  Ishmael's household* removes the difficulty. **Recorded as an inference, not as a contradiction**,
  on the same footing as Isaac's age at 27:1.
- **On *qāhāl* at 28:3.** Recorded as the word's first occurrence in the Hebrew Bible. The claim is
  checkable and load-bearing for the *ekklēsia* thread; it is stated as a distribution fact and
  nothing theological is built on it here.
- **On what was created and what was not.** One new person page — [[Mahalath]] — because she is
  named, married, and disputed between two registers, which is the standard the wiki applied to
  [[Judith]] and [[Basemath (daughter of Elon)]]. **Nebaioth deliberately did not get a page**, per
  the standing rule set at [[The Table of Nations]] and restated at
  [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]]: a bare genealogical name does not earn one, and
  if Isaiah 60 or 21 is ever ingested the page should be `Nebaioth (son of Ishmael)`. **Paddan Aram
  deliberately did not get a page** either — [[Aram Naharaim]] carries it in `also_known_as`, a
  decision recorded at [[A Wife for Isaac]] and repeated here so it is not undone. One new connection
  page: [[Two Errands to Paddan Aram - Genesis 24 and Genesis 28]].
- **On *the blessing of Abraham* and Galatians 3:14.** No separate connection page was made for it.
  [[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]] already carries Galatians 3
  and the promise-to-the-nations thread, and a second page would have restated it around one phrase.
  The phrase itself is recorded on this page and cross-referenced from there.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Bethel and the stairway (28:10–22), where God grants
  directly what Isaac asked for; Laban's household and the two daughters of 28:2 (29:1–30); the staff
  at the Jordan (32:10); the second *ʾĒl Šadday* speech and *"a company of nations"* (35:9–12); the
  burial where Esau is named first (35:29); and the register that renames Mahalath (36:2–3). All are
  referenced and none is developed. **No dangling links were left.**
