# The Fall

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 3:1–24

## Summary

Everything in [[The Garden of Eden]] is still here — the tree, the command, the woman, the
nakedness — and every one of them turns over.

A new character speaks. The serpent is introduced not as an intruder but as one of the creatures
God made, *"more subtle than any animal of the field which Yahweh God had made"*, and its first
move is a question with a false premise: *"Has God really said, 'You shall not eat of any tree of
the garden?'"* God had said almost the reverse — eat freely of *every* tree, except one. The woman
corrects the exaggeration but not cleanly: she drops the generosity (*"freely… every tree"*
becomes *"the trees of the garden"*), adds a prohibition God never gave (*"You shall not touch
it"*), and softens the sentence from *"you will surely die"* to *"lest you die."* Between the
serpent's inflation and her deflation, the actual word of God has already been lost.

The second move is a flat contradiction — *"You won't really die"* — followed by a motive
assigned to God: he is withholding, and what he is withholding is likeness to himself. *"Your
eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil."*

The taking is described in one long verse and three appetites: the tree is *good for food*, *a
delight to the eyes*, and *to be desired to make one wise*. She takes, eats, and gives *"to her
husband with her"* — he is present, and he has said nothing throughout. He eats.

Their eyes are opened, exactly as promised, and what they see is that they are naked. Fig leaves
follow, and then hiding: they hear God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the two
creatures made for his company put trees between themselves and him.

Then a trial. God's questions descend the chain of the story — *"Where are you?"*, then the man,
then the woman — and the answers climb back up it, each passing the charge along: the man to the
woman and past her to God himself (*"the woman whom you gave to be with me"*), the woman to the
serpent. The serpent is asked nothing.

The sentences come in reverse order, serpent first, and their scope is worth reading precisely.
The serpent is cursed, to go on its belly and eat dust, with hostility set between its offspring
and the woman's: *"He will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel."* The woman's sentence
falls on the two relations that defined her — childbearing, now in pain, and her husband, now
*"he will rule over you."* The man's sentence falls not on him but on the ground he was taken
from: it is cursed *for his sake*, and it will fight him with thorns until it takes him back.
*"For you are dust, and to dust you shall return."*

Three things happen after the sentencing, and all three complicate it. The man names his wife
**Eve**, *"because she would be the mother of all the living"* — a wager on life spoken directly
into a death sentence. God makes them coats of skins and clothes them, replacing their own
covering with one that cost something. And God says *"the man has become like one of us, knowing
good and evil"* — conceding that the serpent's claim was, in its way, true — and breaks off
mid-sentence: *"Now, lest he reach out his hand, and also take of the tree of life, and eat, and
live forever—"*. The unfinished thought is completed as an action. They are driven out eastward,
and cherubim with a flaming sword are posted to guard the way back to the tree of life.

What was gained is the knowledge. What was lost is the garden, the tree, the ease of the ground,
and the walking in the cool of the day.

> **4–5** The serpent said to the woman, “You won’t really die, for God knows that in the day you
> eat it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.”

> **6** When the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes,
> and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took some of its fruit, and ate; and

> **7** Their eyes were opened, and they both knew that they were naked. They sewed fig leaves
> together, and made coverings for themselves.

> **8** They heard Yahweh God’s voice walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the man
> and his wife hid themselves from the presence of Yahweh God among the trees of the garden.

> **9** Yahweh God called to the man, and said to him, “Where are you?”

> **12** The man said, “The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree,
> and I ate it.”

> **13** Yahweh God said to the woman, “What have you done?” The woman said, “The serpent deceived
> me, and I ate.”

> **15** I will put hostility between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her
> offspring. He will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel.

> **16** To the woman he said, “I will greatly multiply your pain in childbirth. In pain you will
> bear children. Your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you.”

> **17–19** To Adam he said, “Because you have listened to your wife’s voice, and ate from the
> tree, about which I commanded you, saying, ‘You shall not eat of it,’ the ground is cursed for
> your sake. You will eat from it with much labor all the days of your life. It will yield thorns
> and thistles to you; and you will eat the herb of the field. By the sweat of your face will you
> eat bread until you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken. For you are dust, and to
> dust you shall return.”

> **20** The man called his wife Eve because she would be the mother of all the living.

> **21** Yahweh God made coats of animal skins for Adam and for his wife, and clothed them.

> **22** Yahweh God said, “Behold, the man has become like one of us, knowing good and evil. Now,
> lest he reach out his hand, and also take of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever—”

> **24** So he drove out the man; and he placed cherubim at the east of the garden of Eden, and a
> flaming sword which turned every way, to guard the way to the tree of life.

## People

- [[The Serpent]] — introduced as a creature God made, not as a rival power; it speaks, questions,
  contradicts, and is never questioned in return. Genesis identifies it with nothing beyond
  itself; the identification with Satan is made elsewhere in Scripture, not here.
- [[Eve]] — the one addressed, the one who answers, the one who takes. She is called *the woman*
  through the whole scene and receives her name only at 3:20, after the sentence.
- [[Adam]] — present with her (*"her husband with her"*, 3:6) and silent until questioned, then
  answering with a sentence that blames his wife and, behind her, God. From 3:17 the text begins
  to use his name without the article.
- [[God]] — here as the one who walks in the garden, calls, questions, sentences, clothes, and
  exiles. The portrait is continuous with the potter and gardener of Genesis 2, and the mercy in
  it is left for the reader to notice rather than stated.

## Places

- [[Eden]] — the garden is lost in this passage, not merely departed from. Its entrance is on the
  east and it is now guarded, which is the first time anything in Scripture is closed off from
  human beings.

## Themes

- [[Sin]] — the Bible's first disobedience, and its anatomy: a question about God's word, a doubt
  about God's motive, an appetite, and a shared act. The word *sin* itself does not appear until
  4:7.
- [[Curse]] — the counter-word to [[Blessing]], first spoken here, and aimed with more precision
  than it is usually remembered: at the serpent, and at the ground.
- [[Judgment]] — the pattern that will govern the rest of the Bible's judgment scenes: God comes,
  seeks, questions, hears the answer, sentences — and then acts to preserve those he has just
  sentenced.
- [[Life and Death]] — the death promised in 2:17 arrives, and arrives strangely: not that day,
  but as a trajectory ending in dust, sealed by the loss of the tree of life.
- [[Marriage]] — the one-flesh union of 2:24 shows its first fracture in the same chapter: the man
  hands his wife over under questioning, and the sentence names desire and rule where 2:23 named
  recognition.
- [[Work]] — the commission of 2:15 is not withdrawn; what changes is the ground it works on.
  Thorns and sweat, not labour itself, are the curse.
- [[Image of God]] — humanity becomes *"like one of us, knowing good and evil"* by grasping rather
  than by gift, and the likeness it already had (1:26–27) is nowhere said to be lost.

## Notes

- **"Crafty" and "naked" are the same sound.** The serpent is *ʿārûm* (3:1); the man and woman
  were *ʿărummîm* (2:25). The pun is the hinge between the chapters, and it is not decoration:
  the quality that undoes them is a near-homonym of the openness they lose. *ʿārûm* itself is not
  a negative word — Proverbs uses it approvingly of the prudent (12:23, 14:8) — so the serpent is
  introduced as shrewd, not as evil.
- **Genesis never says who the serpent is.** It is *"an animal of the field which Yahweh God had
  made"* — a creature, on the same side of the creator/creature line as the humans. There is no
  fallen angel, no rebellion in heaven, no Satan in the chapter. That identification is made
  later (Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 in the Apocrypha, then John 8:44, Romans 16:20, and decisively
  Revelation 12:9 and 20:2), and readers differ on whether it draws out something Genesis
  implies or reads a later theology back into it. What Genesis itself insists on is that
  temptation came from inside the created order and through a creature under God's authority.
- **"The fall" is not a phrase Genesis uses**, and neither is *sin*: *ḥaṭṭāʾt* first appears in
  4:7, of Cain. The chapter narrates a disobedience and its consequences without offering a
  doctrine of them. The doctrinal weight it now carries is almost entirely Pauline in origin and
  was developed much further by Augustine; Jewish tradition, working from the same text, has
  generally read it as a story about mortality and moral maturity rather than about inherited
  guilt. Both readings are readings — the chapter supplies the events, not the theory.
- **God's word is misquoted three times before it is broken.** The serpent inflates the
  prohibition (*"not… of any tree"*, 3:1); the woman shrinks the permission (2:16's *"freely… of
  every tree"* becomes *"the trees of the garden"*, 3:2), adds a fence God never built (*"You
  shall not touch it"*, 3:3), and downgrades the certainty (*"you will surely die"* becomes *"lest
  you die"*, 3:3). Making the command harsher and making it vaguer turn out to be the same
  vulnerability.
- **"Your eyes will be opened" was true.** So was *"you will be like God, knowing good and evil"* —
  3:22 confirms it in God's own mouth. The lie is *"You won't really die"*, and even that is a lie
  about timing as much as outcome. What the serpent conceals is not the result but the cost, which
  is why it is described as *subtle* rather than as false.
- **The three appetites of 3:6** — good for food, a delight to the eyes, desirable for wisdom —
  are picked up almost verbatim in 1 John 2:16 (*"the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and
  the pride of life"*), and the three temptations of Jesus in Matthew 4:1–11 are frequently read
  against them: bread, spectacle, and power, refused with quotations of the word this scene
  mislaid.
- **"With her" (3:6).** The Hebrew *ʿimmāh* places the man beside the woman during the
  conversation, not elsewhere in the garden. He is not deceived by a report; he is a silent
  participant who eats when handed the fruit. Much of the tradition that assigns the woman
  primary blame — and 1 Timothy 2:14's *"the woman, being deceived"* — sits alongside this word,
  which the narrative goes out of its way to include.
- **Nobody curses the man or the woman.** The curse formula *"cursed are you"* lands twice: on the
  serpent (3:14) and on the ground (3:17, *"cursed for your sake"*). The woman's sentence and the
  man's are pronounced without it. This is easy to miss under the standing phrase "the curse on
  Adam and Eve", and it matters for reading 3:16 and 3:17–19 as consequences described rather
  than as a status conferred.
- **3:15 is the *protoevangelium* in Christian reading.** The Hebrew verb is *šûp* in both halves —
  the same action done to head and to heel, which WEB renders *bruise* both times — so the verse's
  asymmetry is anatomical, not verbal: a strike at the head is mortal, a strike at the heel is
  not. Read flatly it is an etiology of the enmity between people and snakes. Read messianically —
  the Septuagint's masculine pronoun *autos* for the neuter "offspring" already nudges this way,
  and Romans 16:20, Galatians 4:4, Hebrews 2:14, and Revelation 12 all draw on it — it is the
  first promise of a deliverer, spoken as part of a sentence on the tempter rather than as a
  consolation to the guilty. The Vulgate's *ipsa conteret* ("*she* shall crush") is a translation
  choice, not the Hebrew, and is the source of the Marian iconography of a woman standing on a
  serpent. See
  [[The Protoevangelium - Genesis 3.15 and the Seed of the Woman]].
- **"Your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you"** (3:16) is one of the most
  contested sentences in the book. *Tešûqâ* ("desire") occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible;
  the next is 4:7, where sin *"crouches at the door… its desire is for you, but you are to rule
  over it"* — an appetite to master, not to embrace. Reading 3:16 by 4:7 gives a sentence about
  two people each trying to control the other, which is why some render it *"your desire will be
  *contrary to* your husband"*. The other fork is whether *"he will rule over you"* describes the
  distortion or prescribes an order: the verse is a sentence pronounced on a broken world, in the
  same breath as thorns and painful labour, and few readers treat thorns as a mandate. Both
  questions have been decided in every direction; the text states the outcome and not its
  standing.
- **The man names his wife in 3:20, and only there.** In Genesis 2 he *recognised* her (*"bone of
  my bones"*); naming is what he did to the animals. That the naming comes now, after the
  sentence, is read two ways: as the dominion of 3:16 taking effect, or as the first act of faith
  in the Bible — *Ḥawwâ*, "Eve", is glossed by a word for *living*, and it is said over a woman
  who has just been told she will return to dust.
- **The coats of skins (3:21) are God's answer to the fig leaves.** The humans' covering was
  adequate to each other; it was not adequate to being seen. God's replacement is durable, and it
  is made of an animal — the text does not say sacrifice, and no altar or offering appears, but
  it does mean something died so that they could be clothed, and a long tradition reads the verse
  as the pattern for everything that follows. The plainer observation is that the last thing God
  does before exiling them is dress them.
- **"Like one of us"** (3:22) is the plural of 1:26 returning at the moment the resemblance is
  most ambiguous — see the note on [[The Creation of the World]] for the readings of that plural.
- **The sentence in 3:22 breaks off unfinished** — *"and eat, and live forever—"* — one of the
  clearest cases of aposiopesis in Hebrew narrative. What God does not say aloud he does: the
  expulsion is the end of the sentence. The reason given for it is not punishment but prevention.
  Endless life in this condition is treated as the thing to be stopped, which makes the exile at
  least partly an act of mercy; see
  [[The Tree of Life - Eden and the New Jerusalem]].
- **Cherubim** enter Scripture here, as guards. They are not the infants of later art but
  composite guardian figures of the kind that flanked ancient Near Eastern thrones and temple
  doorways, and they will next appear worked in gold over the ark and woven into the tabernacle
  curtains (Exodus 25:18–22, 26:1) — stationed, again, at the boundary of a holy place. Together
  with the eastward entrance and the *"cultivate and keep"* of 2:15, they are a main reason many
  readers take the garden to be the first sanctuary.
- **East is the direction of exile in Genesis.** They are driven out to the east of the garden;
  Cain settles east of Eden (4:16); Babel is built in a plain in the east (11:2); Lot picks the
  eastern plain and loses it (13:11). Movement back toward the promise runs the other way.
