# Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 25:19–34

## Summary

**Twenty years pass in one verse, and a bowl of lentils takes six.**

That is the pericope's whole method. Genesis opens the third patriarchal cycle with a genealogy
heading, disposes of two decades of childlessness in a sentence, hands out the oracle that governs
the next twenty-five chapters in four poetic lines, delivers twins, characterises them in
twenty-nine words — and then slows almost to a stop for a domestic transaction over a pot of stew
that nobody but the two brothers witnesses. **The verse count is the interpretation**: the thing the
book wants looked at is not the oracle but what the brothers do with it.

**And the oracle is spoken before either of them has done anything.** *"The elder will serve the
younger"* is delivered to a pregnant woman, about two unborn children, in a book that has spent
fourteen chapters refusing to explain why God chooses whom he chooses. Paul will build a doctrine on
exactly that sequence (Romans 9:10–13). Genesis states it and moves straight to lunch.

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who speaks |
|---|---|---|
| **19–20** | the *toledot* heading, and a marriage restated with four Mesopotamian identifiers | nobody |
| **21** | twenty years of barrenness, a prayer, and a conception | nobody quoted |
| **22–23** | the children struggle; Rebekah goes to inquire; the oracle | **Rebekah, then Yahweh** — and this is the whole of the divine speech in the pericope |
| **24–26** | twins; two etymologies; Isaac is sixty | the namers, unquoted |
| **27–28** | the boys grow, and the household divides | nobody |
| **29–34** | the stew, the oath, the sale, and the narrator's verdict | **Esau and Jacob**, four exchanges |

### 25:19–20 — a heading, and a sentence that says the same thing twice

> **19** This is the history of the generations of Isaac, Abraham’s son. Abraham became the father of
> Isaac.

***ʾĒlleh tôlədōt Yiṣḥāq*** — the sixth of the ten *toledot* headings that segment Genesis, and it
arrives seven verses after Ishmael's (25:12), exactly as the book's standing habit requires: **the
passed-over line is cleared off the stage first, briefly and completely, and then the book turns to
the one it is following.** See [[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]].

**The second clause is famously redundant.** *"Abraham's son. Abraham became the father of Isaac"* —
the reader has been told this at 21:2, 21:3, 21:5, 22:2, 24:36, 25:5, 25:6, 25:11, and again three
words earlier in this verse. The standard proposals for why it is here:

- **It is the formula's own shape.** A *toledot* heading names the father whose descendants follow;
  25:12 does the same thing for Ishmael in the same breath — *"Ishmael, Abraham's son, whom Hagar the
  Egyptian … bore"* — and the two headings are deliberately parallel.
- **It is a claim against a doubt.** Genesis 21:9–10 had a household argument about which son was the
  heir, and 20:2–7 had Sarah in another man's house for long enough that the question of Isaac's
  paternity is at least formally available. The verse forecloses it.
- **It is a seam.** The clause reads like the join between two blocks of material, and source
  criticism treats it as one.

**Genesis states it and does not explain it**, and the wiki records the proposals without choosing.

> **20** Isaac was forty years old when he took Rebekah, the daughter of Bethuel the Syrian of Paddan
> Aram, the sister of Laban the Syrian, to be his wife.

**Four identifiers for a woman the reader met sixty-seven verses ago.** Daughter of [[Bethuel]];
Aramean; of Paddan Aram; sister of [[Laban]]. **Nothing in this verse is new**, and Genesis 24 gave
all of it at greater length. The repetition is doing two things at once: it satisfies the *toledot*
form, which files a generation with its pedigree — and **it plants Laban**, who is named here for no
narrative reason whatever and who will own twenty years of Jacob's life from chapter 29.

***Paddan Aram*** is **the first of its ten occurrences in Genesis**, all of them in the Jacob
material. Genesis 24:10 called the same country *Aram Naharaim*, which the WEB renders
*Mesopotamia*. The wiki keeps them on one page; see [[Aram Naharaim]].

**And the number is the pericope's first piece of hidden arithmetic.** Isaac is forty here and sixty
at 25:26. **Rebekah is barren for twenty years**, and Genesis reports the whole of it in the next
verse.

### 25:21 — the only prayer in the patriarchal narratives for a wife's fertility

> **21** Isaac entreated Yahweh for his wife, because she was barren. Yahweh was entreated by him, and
> Rebekah his wife conceived.

**The verb is the same word twice, active and passive, in one sentence.** *Wayyeʿtar … wayyēʿāter lô*
— *he entreated … and he was entreated by him*. The root is *ʿātar*, a rare verb for supplication,
and **this is its first occurrence in the Bible**; the verb behind [[Abraham]]'s prayer for
Abimelech's household at 20:17 is a different one, *pālal*, which is that root's first occurrence in
Scripture. **The chiastic doubling is the verse's whole rhetoric**: the
petition and its answer are stated in the same word, and no divine speech is quoted at all. God does
not say yes; the narrator says he was entreated.

**Isaac is the only patriarch who prays for his wife to conceive.** Abraham never does. Confronted
with Sarai's barrenness at 15:2 he complains to God about an heir; at 16:2 he takes Hagar; at 17:17
he laughs and asks for the son he already has. **Twenty-five years of the Abraham cycle contain no
prayer for Sarah's womb.** Isaac's whole recorded initiative in Genesis is roughly this one verse,
and it is a prayer for his wife. See [[Prayer]] and [[Barrenness]].

***ʿĂqārâ*, barren** — the flat clinical word that broke over Sarai at 11:30 (*"Sarai was barren. She
had no child"*) and will break over Rachel at 29:31. **All three matriarchs are barren, and none of
their pregnancies is ordinary.** The pattern is not incidental to the book; it is how Genesis says
that the nation is not a natural product. See [[The Barren Matriarchs]].

**Twenty years, compressed to a subordinate clause.** Sarah's barrenness ran across ten chapters and
generated the Hagar crisis, the laughter, the covenant, and two annunciations. Rebekah's is disposed
of in fifteen English words with no complaint from her, no substitute wife, no household quarrel, and
no scene. **Genesis has told this story once already at full length and declines to tell it again.**

### 25:22 — a question the Hebrew leaves unfinished

> **22** The children struggled together within her. She said, “If it is so, why do I live?” She went
> to inquire of Yahweh.

***Wayyitrōṣăṣû*** — **they crushed one another.** The root is *rāṣaṣ*, used elsewhere of crushing a
bruised reed, oppressing the poor, breaking bones. **It is not a word for a kick**; the twins are
described in the womb with a verb of violence, and the reader is being told what the oracle is about
to say before it says it.

***"If it is so, why do I live?"*** The Hebrew is four words — ***ʾim-kēn lāmmâ zeh ʾānōkî*** — and
it stops before it finishes: *if so, why this — I?* **There is no verb.** Every English rendering
supplies one, and they do not agree:

- ***Why do I live?*** (WEB, and the majority) — supplying an existential verb: *why go on?*
- ***Why am I like this?*** — supplying a predicate about her state: *why is my pregnancy like this?*
- ***Why then am I?*** — leaving it broken, which is what the Hebrew does.
- ***If it is so, why am I thus?*** taken as a question about the point of a conception she prayed
  for and then read as a curse.

**The WEB's rendering is the strongest of them** — it makes her question suicidal in reach — and the
wiki records that the Hebrew does not settle it. What the clause unambiguously is: **the first words
Rebekah speaks since *"I will go"*** (24:58), and they are a question about whether the going was
worth it.

***"She went to inquire of Yahweh."*** ***Lidrōš ʾet-YHWH*** — **the first occurrence in the Bible of
*dāraš* with Yahweh as its object**, a phrase that becomes the standard Old Testament expression for
seeking an oracle (1 Samuel 9:9; 1 Kings 22:8; 2 Kings 22:13). **Genesis does not say where she went,
whom she asked, or what the procedure was.** No altar, no priest, no shrine, no messenger. The book
that has narrated theophanies in exhaustive detail — three men at a tent door, a smoking furnace, a
voice out of the sky — reports this one with a verb of motion and nothing else.

**And nobody sent her.** Isaac prayed for the pregnancy; the inquiry about it is hers alone, and he
is not in the verse. See [[Prophecy and Vision]] and [[Rebekah]].

### 25:23 — the oracle

> **23** Yahweh said to her, “Two nations are in your womb. Two peoples will be separated from your
> body. The one people will be stronger than the other people. The elder will serve the younger.”

**This is the only divine speech in the pericope, it is in poetry, and it is spoken to a woman.**

**Rebekah hears from God before her husband does.** Yahweh does not speak to Isaac until 26:2 — a
chapter later, and after a famine. **The second matriarch receives a direct oracle about the future
of two nations at a point where the second patriarch has never been addressed by God at all.** The
wiki notes this as a fact about the narration; Genesis makes nothing of it.

**She is the second woman in Genesis to be told what her unborn son will become, and the first was a
runaway slave.** [[Hagar]] at 16:11–12 is given the child's name, his character, and his relation to
his brothers, in poetry, by [[The Angel of Yahweh]] at a spring in the desert. Rebekah is given two
nations, a ranking, and an inversion, by Yahweh, at a place the text does not name. **The two
annunciations to women in this book are to a matriarch and to a slave**, and the slave's came first.

**Four lines, and the fourth one is the problem.**

| line | Hebrew sense | what it settles |
|---|---|---|
| *Two nations are in your womb* | *šənê gôyîm* | the struggle is not domestic — it is international, before either child is born |
| *Two peoples will be separated from your body* | *yippārēdû* | the separation is intrinsic; the same verb Abram used of himself and Lot at 13:9 |
| *The one people will be stronger than the other* | *ûləʾōm miləʾōm yeʾĕmāṣ* | a ranking, with neither one named |
| ***The elder will serve the younger*** | ***rab yaʿăbōd ṣāʿîr*** | **the inversion — and the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous** |

***Rab yaʿăbōd ṣāʿîr* is three words with no object marker**, and Hebrew word order is not rigid
enough in poetry to force the reading by itself. Grammatically the line can be construed either way:
*the elder shall serve the younger*, or *the elder — the younger shall serve*. **Every ancient
version, every later biblical use, and the whole subsequent narrative take the first**, and so does
this wiki. But the ambiguity is real, it has been noticed since antiquity, and it is worth having on
the page, because **an oracle that can be heard two ways is exactly what the rest of the cycle turns
on**: Isaac tries to bless the elder, Rebekah engineers around him, and Esau at 27:36 says the
younger *took* what was his. See
[[The Elder Will Serve the Younger - Genesis 25.23 in Malachi and Romans]].

**And it is a statement about nations, not about salvation.** The oracle names *gôyîm* and *ləʾummîm*
— peoples — and what it forecasts is a political relationship that 2 Samuel 8:13–14 records
(David garrisons Edom) and 2 Kings 8:20–22 records ending (Edom revolts under Jehoram). **Genesis
does not say either brother is loved or hated**; Malachi 1:2–3 says that, four hundred years of
national history later, and Paul quotes Malachi and not Genesis. The wiki keeps the two statements
apart. See [[Call and Election]] and [[The Nations]].

### 25:24–26 — two births, two puns, and a name with no etymology

> **24** When her days to be delivered were fulfilled, behold, there were twins in her womb.
> **25** The first came out red all over, like a hairy garment. They named him Esau.
> **26** After that, his brother came out, and his hand had hold on Esau’s heel. He was named Jacob.
> Isaac was sixty years old when she bore them.

**Genesis names both boys and only explains one of the names.**

***ʾAdmônî*** — **red** — and ***śēʿār*** — **hair**. Those are the two words in 25:25, and neither
of them is *Esau*. **They are the seeds of two other names**: *ʾădōmî* → **Edom**, which 25:30 will
supply on other grounds, and *śēʿār* → **Seir**, the mountain country Esau settles (32:3; 36:8).
**The verse gives the etymologies of the nation and of its territory, and gives none at all for the
man.** *ʿĒśāw* has no accepted derivation in Hebrew; Genesis, which supplies an etymology for almost
every significant name it introduces, declines to supply this one. See [[Esau]] and [[Edom]].

***Yaʿăqōb*** and ***ʿāqēb*** — **Jacob and heel**. This is the one etymology the pericope states,
and Genesis will state a second, harsher one for the same name at 27:36, from *ʿāqab*, **to
supplant** or **overreach**: *"Isn't he rightly named Jacob? For he has supplanted me these two
times."* **One name, two puns, and the book gives the neutral one at birth and the hostile one from
the mouth of the brother.** (As a name-type, *Yaʿaqōb-ʾēl* — *may God protect* — is attested in the
second millennium BC, so the puns are Genesis's theology rather than the name's history, exactly as
with *Yiṣḥāq*. See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].)

**The grabbing hand is the image the rest of the Bible keeps.** Hosea 12:3 — *"In the womb he took
his brother by the heel"* — is the prophets' summary of Jacob's whole character, and it comes from
this verse.

***"They named him Esau" … "He was named Jacob."*** The WEB is following the Hebrew closely and the
shift is in the original: 25:25 has a plural verb, **they called**; 25:26 has a singular verb with
**no stated subject**, which the WEB turns into a passive. Some ancient witnesses read a feminine
singular — *she called* — which would make Rebekah the namer of the son she loves. **The Masoretic
text does not say who named him**, and the wiki does not fill it in.

**Isaac is sixty**, so Abraham lived to see these two grow to fifteen (25:7 with 21:5 and 25:26) —
arithmetic the book supplies and never states, and which the narrative order conceals completely by
burying Abraham at 25:10 before opening the story of grandsons who were already alive.

### 25:27–28 — twenty-nine words, and the household splits

> **27** The boys grew. Esau was a skillful hunter, a man of the field. Jacob was a quiet man, living
> in tents.
> **28** Now Isaac loved Esau, because he ate his venison. Rebekah loved Jacob.

**This is the entire characterisation of two men the book will follow for twenty-five chapters.**

***ʾÎš yōdēaʿ ṣayid, ʾîš śādeh*** — **a man knowing hunting, a man of the field.** Esau is defined by
competence and by the outdoors, and the wiki notes that neither is a criticism. Genesis has approved
of a hunter before, after a fashion: Nimrod is *"a mighty hunter before Yahweh"* (10:9). See
[[Nimrod]].

***ʾÎš tām, yōšēb ʾōhālîm*** — **and this is the pericope's sharpest translation crux.**

*Tām* is not an ordinary word for *quiet*. **It is the word for moral completeness**, and in the two
other places this wiki has met its family it carries exactly that weight: [[Noah]] is *tāmîm* —
*"blameless among the people of his time"* (6:9) — and [[Job (person)]] is *tām wəyāšār*, *"blameless
and upright"* (Job 1:1), which is the whole premise of that book. Applied to Jacob, four verses
before he buys his brother's birthright off him at a moment of weakness, the word is a problem, and
the versions handle it by picking a different sense:

- ***quiet*** (WEB), ***mild***, ***plain*** — taking *tām* as *simple, settled, unadventurous*, and
  reading the clause as a contrast of ways of life rather than of characters.
- ***blameless***, ***a perfect man*** — keeping the moral sense, which then has to be squared with
  25:31–33 and with chapter 27. Rabbinic reading generally takes this line, glossing *tām* as
  *whole-hearted*, and reads *yōšēb ʾōhālîm* as study.
- ***civilised*** or ***domesticated*** — reading the two halves together, so that *tām* is coloured
  by *dwelling in tents*: a herdsman rather than a hunter.

**The wiki adopts none of them and flags the tension.** What is checkable is that the adjective
Genesis applies to Jacob here is the adjective it applies to Noah and Job, that the WEB and most
English versions do not render it that way in this verse, and that the narrator will shortly show
Jacob doing something the same narrator does not call wrong.

**Verse 28 is the first stated parental favouritism in Scripture, and only one side of it is given a
reason.** *"Isaac loved Esau, **because he ate his venison**"* — game in the mouth, and Genesis puts
the motive in a subordinate clause that is faintly ridiculous. *"Rebekah loved Jacob"* — full stop,
no clause, no reason. **The reader who has read four verses back can supply one and the narrator will
not.**

***ʾĀhab*, love** — the same verb whose first two occurrences in Scripture are *"your son, your only
son, whom you love, even Isaac"* (22:2) and *"He loved her"* (24:67). **Its third and fourth
occurrences split a household in two.** The pattern runs on: Jacob will love Rachel over Leah
(29:30) and Joseph over his brothers (37:3), *"and his brothers saw that their father loved him more
than all his brothers, and they hated him."* **Genesis never once approves of it, and never once
intervenes.** See [[Strife and Peacemaking]] and [[Deception]].

### 25:29–34 — the transaction

> **29** Jacob boiled stew. Esau came in from the field, and he was famished.
> **30** Esau said to Jacob, “Please feed me with that same red stew, for I am famished.” Therefore
> his name was called Edom.

**The scene is built out of the two characterisations, exactly.** The man of the field comes in from
the field; the man who sits in tents is at the pot. **Neither is doing anything out of character, and
that is what makes the exchange possible.**

***"Please feed me"*** — ***halʿîṭēnî*** — is a verb the Hebrew Bible uses **only here**, and its
cognates are used of **feeding animals**: to let something gulp, to stuff a mouthful down a throat.
***"That same red stew"*** is ***min-hā-ʾādōm hā-ʾādōm hazzeh***, literally **"some of this red red"**
— the adjective doubled, with **no noun at all**. He does not say *stew*. He does not know or care
what is in the pot. **The most famous meal in Genesis is asked for in the syntax of an animal at a
trough**, and the wiki notes that the narrator has chosen every word of it.

***"Therefore his name was called Edom."*** *ʾĔdôm*, from *ʾādōm*, **red** — and the etiology is
attached not to the man's own colouring at birth (25:25, which used the related *ʾadmônî*) but to the
soup. **Genesis gives the nation of Edom its name from the moment its ancestor sold his inheritance
for a hot meal**, and the placement is the comment. See [[Edom]].

> **31** Jacob said, “First, sell me your birthright.”
> **32** Esau said, “Behold, I am about to die. What good is the birthright to me?”
> **33** Jacob said, “Swear to me first.” He swore to him. He sold his birthright to Jacob.

***Bəkōrâ*, the birthright*** — the firstborn's portion. Genesis nowhere defines it; the law later
fixes it as a double share of the inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:15–17), and the patriarchal narratives
attach headship of the family to it. **In this family it is also the covenant line**, though nobody in
the scene says so. See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

**Jacob's offer is instant, prepared, and legally careful.** He does not haggle, does not soften it,
and — when the answer comes — **does not accept the answer**. He asks for an oath. *"Swear to me
first"* (*hiššābəʿâ lî kayyôm*) converts a hungry man's shrug into a sworn, irrevocable transfer, of
the same kind Abraham extracted from his servant at 24:3 and from Abimelech at 21:24. **This is the
third oath in four chapters, and it is the only one sworn between brothers.** See
[[Oaths and Vows]].

***"Behold, I am about to die."*** ***Hinnēh ʾānōkî hôlēk lāmût*** — *I am going to die.* **He is not.**
He is a young man who has come in hungry from a day's hunting to his father's household, and there is
bread in the house — verse 34 says so, and Jacob hands it to him. **Two readings, both of them
available in the Hebrew:**

- **Hyperbole**, the ordinary exaggeration of a famished man — which makes the trade a piece of
  short-sightedness rather than a considered choice, and makes Jacob's exploitation of it the sharper
  thing in the passage.
- **A real argument**, and the one that fits *"What good is the birthright to me?"*: a hunter's life
  is dangerous, an inheritance is worth nothing to a dead man, and the birthright is a claim on a
  future he does not expect to see. **On this reading he is not confused; he genuinely does not value
  it**, which is precisely the charge 25:34 lays.

**Genesis lets the second reading stand and then convicts him on it.**

> **34** Jacob gave Esau bread and stew of lentils. He ate and drank, rose up, and went his way. So
> Esau despised his birthright.

***"Stew of lentils."*** **The reader is told what was in the pot only after the sale is closed**, and
it was not what Esau asked for: he wanted *the red red stuff*, and lentils are brown. The detail is
the last twist of the knife — **he did not know what he was buying and he sold an inheritance for
it.** And *"bread"* is in the sentence: **the meal Jacob hands over includes the ordinary food that
was in the house anyway.**

**Five verbs, no adjectives, no reaction.** *Wayyōʾkal wayyēšt wayyāqom wayyēlak* — **he ate, and
drank, and rose, and went** — the fastest sequence of finite verbs in the chapter, and the effect is
that the transaction of his life takes him less time than the negotiation did. **Nobody says
anything. There is no scene, no regret, no second thought.**

***"So Esau despised his birthright."*** ***Wayyibez ʿĒśāw ʾet-habbəkōrâ*** — **the only evaluative
sentence in the pericope**, and this is where the wiki has to be careful.

**The narrator's verdict falls on Esau, and Genesis says nothing at all about Jacob.** The younger
brother waits at a pot for a starving man, names his price before offering food, refuses to close
without a sworn oath, and then hands over lentils — and **the book does not call it wrong here, or
anywhere in this chapter.** The condemnation of Jacob comes later and comes from characters, not from
the narrator: Esau at 27:36, Laban at 29:25 by doing the same thing back to him, and the whole of
chapter 32 by putting him alone at a river with a limp.

**That asymmetry is the passage, and later readers preserve it.** Hebrews 12:16–17 makes Esau the
warning — *"a profane person, who sold his own birthright for one meal"* — and says nothing about the
seller's counterparty. Malachi and Romans work from the oracle, not from the stew. **The Bible's own
reading of Genesis 25 is that Esau is the one who is judged**, and the wiki records that the text
supports it while noting exactly what the text declines to say about Jacob. See
[[Esau's Birthright in Hebrews]].

### What the pericope is doing

**It sets up an inheritance that will be taken four separate ways and never once simply received.**
The oracle gives it before birth. The sale transfers it by contract. Chapter 27 steals it by
disguise. Chapter 32 wrestles for it in the dark and comes away with a new name and a damaged hip.
**Genesis will spend eleven chapters on a promise that its recipient never stops grabbing at**, and
this pericope is where the grabbing starts, in the delivery room.

**And it changes the book's moral texture.** Abraham's cycle had one clear liar and a narrator who
kept flagging the lies through the mouths of foreign kings. This cycle opens with the narrator
declining to comment on the person he is following, and it will keep declining. **The reader is being
handed the job.**

## People

- [[Isaac]] — **forty, then sixty, and he prays.** He is the only patriarch in Genesis who asks God
  for his wife's conception, and the one verse in which he does it is close to the whole of his
  recorded initiative. He is absent from the inquiry at 25:22, absent from the oracle, and by 25:28
  he is a man who loves the son who brings him game.
- [[Rebekah]] — **the pericope's centre.** Barren twenty years without a word of complaint recorded,
  and then she speaks for the first time since *"I will go"* to ask a question the Hebrew leaves
  unfinished; she goes to inquire of Yahweh on her own initiative, with no one sending her; she
  receives the only divine speech in the pericope, a chapter before God speaks to her husband; and
  the last thing said about her is that she loved the younger son, with no reason given.
- [[Jacob]] — **born holding a heel and named for it.** *ʾÎš tām* — the adjective the Bible uses of
  Noah and Job — and the man who names his price to a starving brother and will not close without an
  oath. Genesis does not comment on him here or anywhere in the chapter.
- [[Esau]] — **red, hairy, a hunter, and the only person the narrator judges.** He gets the two puns
  that name a nation and a mountain and no etymology of his own; he asks for food in a verb used of
  animals and in a phrase with no noun in it; he swears away his inheritance and eats, drinks, rises,
  and goes.
- [[God]] — speaks four poetic lines to a woman and is otherwise present only as the one who *was
  entreated*. **He does not speak to Isaac in this pericope and has not spoken to him yet at all**;
  that waits until 26:2.
- [[Abraham]] — dead, and named twice in the heading; on the book's arithmetic he was alive until
  these twins were fifteen, which Genesis supplies and never says.
- [[Bethuel]] — named again as Rebekah's father, in a pedigree the reader already has.
- [[Laban]] — **planted.** Named here for no reason the pericope needs, four chapters before he takes
  over Jacob's life for twenty years and repays the younger-for-elder substitution in kind.

## Places

- [[Aram Naharaim]] — under its other name, **Paddan Aram**, in the first of the ten occurrences
  Genesis gives it; the country that supplies this family's wives and receives none of its promises.
- [[Edom]] — named at 25:30 from the red stew, and forecast at 25:23 as one of the *two nations*; the
  hairiness of 25:25 seeds the other name of its territory, Seir.
- [[Beer Lahai Roi]] — unstated here, and Isaac's address as of 25:11; Genesis gives the pericope no
  location of its own.
- [[Canaan]] — the land the whole struggle is nominally over, and not named once in these verses.

## Themes

- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — the *bəkōrâ*, sold under oath for a meal; the first of the
  book's four transfers of an inheritance away from an elder son.
- [[Call and Election]] — the choice announced before birth, before either child has acted, and with
  no reason given; the sequence Romans 9:10–13 builds on.
- [[Barrenness]] — the second matriarch, twenty years, and a pattern the book has now run twice.
- [[Prayer]] — *ʿātar* used actively and passively in one verse, and the only patriarchal prayer for

- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — *dāraš ʾet-YHWH*, the Bible's first inquiry of Yahweh, with no shrine,

- [[Blessing]] — held back here and spent in chapter 27; the birthright and the blessing are two

- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the third oath in four chapters, and the only one sworn between brothers.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — a struggle in the womb described with a verb for crushing, and a

- [[Deception]] — not yet, and set up in every particular: the favouritism, the game, the tents, and
  the birthright already gone before the blessing is asked for.
- [[The Nations]] — *"Two nations are in your womb"*; the oracle's subject is a political relation
  between peoples, and its terms are *gôyîm* and *ləʾummîm*.
- [[Covenant]] — the line, in transit, and never mentioned in the scene that transfers it.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's box read *Genesis 25:19–34* and **it stands as drawn;
  no unchecked boxes were redrawn this pass.** The opening is the book's own — 25:19 is a *toledot*
  heading — and the close at 25:34 is a narrator's verdict followed by a chapter break and a fresh
  time marker (*"There was a famine in the land"*, 26:1). The alternative split, taking 25:19–28 (the
  birth) and 25:29–34 (the sale) as two units, was considered and rejected: the sale scene is built
  out of 25:27's two characterisations word for word — the man of the field comes in from the field —
  and separating them removes the reason the transaction works.
- **On *rab yaʿăbōd ṣāʿîr*, unresolved.** The line has no object marker and Hebrew poetic word order
  does not force the reading. *The elder will serve the younger* is the rendering of the ancient
  versions, of the WEB, of every later biblical use of the family, and of the narrative itself, **and
  the wiki adopts it.** What is recorded alongside it is that the ambiguity is grammatically real and
  ancient, and that the cycle it opens is about two people hearing an inheritance differently.
- **On *tām* at 25:27, unresolved.** The adjective Genesis applies to Jacob is the one it applies to
  Noah (*tāmîm*, 6:9) and that Job 1:1 applies to Job (*tām wəyāšār*), where it means **blameless**.
  The WEB has *quiet*; other versions have *mild*, *plain*, *civilised*, *blameless*. **Both the moral
  and the sociological readings are recorded and neither is adopted.** The observation that matters is
  that the word choice sits four verses before conduct the narrator declines to call wrong, and that
  this is a feature of the Jacob material rather than an accident.
- **On *"If it is so, why do I live?"*, unresolved.** The Hebrew *ʾim-kēn lāmmâ zeh ʾānōkî* has no
  verb. Four renderings are set out above; the WEB's is the strongest available and the Hebrew does
  not settle it. **Recorded as a translation crux, not as a correction to the WEB.**
- **On the narrator's asymmetry.** Genesis condemns Esau in 25:34 and says nothing about Jacob in the
  whole chapter. **The wiki states this as a fact about the text rather than resolving it in either
  direction** — it neither exonerates Jacob (chapters 27, 29, and 32 do not read as approval) nor
  imports the later verdicts of Esau, Laban, or Hosea into a narrator who withheld them.
- **On what the birthright was, which Genesis does not say.** *Bəkōrâ* is undefined here. The double
  portion is Deuteronomy 21:17; family headship is inferred from the patriarchal narratives
  themselves; the covenant line is what the reader knows and what nobody in the scene mentions.
  **The wiki keeps the three distinct**, and keeps the birthright distinct from the **blessing**,
  which is a separate thing transferred separately in chapter 27 — Esau at 27:36 is the one who puts
  them together.
- **Why Esau's "I am about to die" is left open.** Hyperbole and a genuine hunter's calculation are
  both available in the Hebrew and lead to different readings of the trade. **Neither is adopted**;
  what is noted is that 25:34's *despised* fits the second better than the first, which is an argument
  from the narrator rather than from Esau.
- **Which new pages were made and which were not.** [[Jacob]] and [[Esau]] obviously earn theirs.
  [[Edom]] was created now rather than at Genesis 36 because 25:23 is an oracle *about* the nation and
  25:30 supplies its name, and because [[Teman]] has been referring to Edom without a page since the
  Job material. **Paddan Aram deliberately did not get a page**: [[Aram Naharaim]] already states as a
  settled convention that the wiki keeps the region's two names on one page, and that page was
  extended instead. Seir, Leah, and Rachel are named in this page's forward references and were left
  as plain text; their pericopes are ahead.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Everything from 26:1 on. In particular: God's first speech to
  Isaac (26:2–5); Esau's Hittite wives and his parents' grief (26:34–35); the stolen blessing and
  Esau's *"Isn't he rightly named Jacob?"* (27:36); Laban's substitution of Leah (29:21–30); the
  Jabbok (32); the reconciliation (33); and Esau's *toledot* and the kings of Edom (36). All are
  referenced and none is developed. **No dangling links were left.**
