# The Table of Nations

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 10:1–32

## Summary

Seventy names and one story. Genesis 10 is a map of the known world drawn as a family tree: every
people the Bible will ever mention is here assigned a father, and the father is one of three brothers
who came off a boat. Nothing in ancient literature is quite like it — the Mesopotamian and Egyptian
king lists trace dynasties, not humanities, and no other text from the ancient Near East attempts to
say where *everyone* came from and then puts the author's own people in the list on the same terms as
the rest.

The chapter divides mechanically and the divisions are marked by a refrain. A superscription (10:1);
[[Japheth]]'s fourteen (10:2–5); [[Ham]]'s thirty, with a five-verse narrative interruption for
[[Nimrod]] (10:6–20); [[Shem]]'s twenty-six (10:21–31); and a colophon (10:32). Each of the three
lists closes with the same formula — *"after their families, according to their languages, in their
lands and their nations"* — and the whole is sealed by a sentence that repeats the thesis of
[[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]]: *"The nations divided from these in the earth after the
flood."*

Two things are worth saying before the details, because they govern how the chapter reads.

**It is a segmented genealogy, not a linear one.** [[From Adam to Noah]] and the register that follows
this chapter at 11:10–26 are *linear*: one man per generation, with an age at fatherhood, a remainder,
and a death. Genesis 10 branches instead, and it drops all three numbers. Nobody in it has an age,
nobody dies, and — with the single exception of Nimrod — nobody does anything. The linear form
narrows toward a chosen line; the segmented form spreads out to account for everybody. Genesis puts
one immediately before the other, which is its way of saying that the election of one family happens
inside a world it has already accounted for in full.

**God is absent again.** He does not speak, act, appear, or approve. His name occurs exactly once in
thirty-two verses, at 10:9, and it occurs inside a proverb that other people say about a hunter. That
is two consecutive pericopes without divine speech — the longest silence in Genesis so far — and it
ends four verses into the next one, when *"Yahweh came down to see the city and the tower"* (11:5).

### The superscription (10:1)

> **1** Now this is the history of the generations of the sons of Noah and of Shem, Ham, and Japheth.
> Sons were born to them after the flood.

*ʾĒlleh tôlədōt bənê-nōaḥ* — the fourth of the ten *toledot* formulas that segment Genesis (2:4, 5:1,
6:9, 10:1, 11:10, 11:27, 25:12, 25:19, 36:1, 37:2). The formula always hands the narrative forward,
and it always names the father rather than the sons who will be listed: what follows is not the story
of Noah but what came *of* Noah.

*"After the flood"* is doing quiet work. Genesis 6–9 emptied the earth of everyone and then let eight
people out of a boat; this chapter is the accounting of how eight became seventy, and the phrase
insists that every nation on the list postdates the water. There is no surviving remnant from before.
Whatever Genesis thinks the nations are, it does not think any of them is older than the flood.

### Japheth's fourteen (10:2–5)

> **2** The sons of Japheth were: Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras.
> **3** The sons of Gomer were: Ashkenaz, Riphath, and Togarmah.
> **4** The sons of Javan were: Elishah, Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim.
> **5** Of these were the islands of the nations divided in their lands, everyone after his language,
> after their families, in their nations.

**The order of the three sons is reversed.** Shem, Ham, and Japheth have been listed in that order
eleven times, and here the chapter takes them backwards — Japheth, Ham, Shem. This is Genesis's
standing habit: the collateral lines are cleared away first so that the chosen one can be followed
without interruption. Cain's descendants are given before Seth's; Ishmael's before Isaac's (25:12–18
before 25:19); Esau's before Jacob's (all of chapter 36 before 37:2). The last name in the chapter's
last list is the one Genesis 11 will pick up.

Japheth's fourteen are the north and the west, as far as an Iron Age Levantine writer could see.
**Madai** is Media; **Javan** is Ionia, and stays the ordinary Hebrew word for Greece through Isaiah,
Ezekiel, Daniel, and Zechariah; **Tubal** and **Meshech** are the Anatolian Tabal and Mushki of
Assyrian records, and appear together in Ezekiel 38–39 as Gog's allies; **Ashkenaz** is the Scythian
*Ishkuza*; **Tarshish** is a far western port, Tartessos in Spain on the usual identification, and the
place Jonah runs to; **Kittim** is Cyprus, from the city Kition. **Magog** is the one name with no
secure referent, and Ezekiel 38:2 makes Gog its prince, which is where the whole apocalyptic career of
the pair begins.

*"The islands of the nations"* — *ʾiyyê haggôyîm* — is the chapter's only geographical label for a
whole branch. *ʾÎ* covers islands and coastlands alike; the phrase means the Mediterranean rim, and it
is the horizon of the map.

**The refrain arrives here for the first time**, and it will be repeated at 10:20 and 10:31 and
summarized at 10:32: families, languages, lands, nations. Four axes, and the chapter never says which
of them is the real one.

### Ham's thirty (10:6–20)

> **6** The sons of Ham were: Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan.
> **7** The sons of Cush were: Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, Raamah, and Sabteca. The sons of Raamah were:
> Sheba and Dedan.

Cush is Nubia, the Nile south of Egypt; **Mizraim** is Egypt, and is the standard Hebrew name for it
everywhere in the Bible; **Put** is Libya, on the usual identification; and **Canaan** is the strip
between the Jordan and the sea. The line runs along Africa and up the Levantine coast, and it is
worth saying flatly that these are the great powers and the near neighbours, not a race. The listing
is political geography.

**Sheba and Havilah appear here and again in Shem's line** (10:28–29, under Joktan). The chapter
duplicates them without comment. Both are Arabian, and the likeliest explanation is the honest one:
Arabia was populated from two directions and the table records both claims rather than choosing.

#### Nimrod (10:8–12)

> **8** Cush became the father of Nimrod. He began to be a mighty one in the earth.
> **9** He was a mighty hunter before Yahweh. Therefore it is said, “like Nimrod, a mighty hunter
> before Yahweh”.
> **10** The beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.
> **11** Out of that land he went into Assyria, and built Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah,
> **12** and Resen between Nineveh and the great city Calah.

**Five verses of narrative in the middle of a list, and they are the only ones in the chapter.**
Everyone else is a name; Nimrod begins, hunts, reigns, goes out, and builds. Genesis stops the map to
tell you about him and then resumes as though nothing had happened.

*"He began to be a mighty one"* — *hûʾ hēḥēl lihyôt gibbōr*. Both words are loaded. *Hēḥēl*, **he
began**, is the verb Genesis has used three times already to mark the start of an era: men began to
call on Yahweh's name (4:26), men began to multiply (6:1), Noah began to be a man of the ground
(9:20). And *gibbōr* is the word from 6:4 — *"the mighty men who were of old, men of renown"*, the
offspring of the union in
[[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]. The flood was supposed to have ended that. Four
verses into the new world's ethnography, the first man singled out for description is a *gibbōr*, and
the reader who has been paying attention has met the word once before, in a sentence that ends with
God resolving to blot out humanity. See [[The Nephilim]].

*"A mighty hunter before Yahweh"* — *lipnê YHWH* — is genuinely ambiguous and the ambiguity is old.
*Lipnê* can mean **in the estimation of** (so: exceptionally, by Yahweh's own reckoning — the reading
the WEB's rendering most naturally supports), or **in the presence of** (neutral, and the sense in
which Noah is *tāmîm* before God), or **in the face of**, that is, in defiance. Later Jewish and
Christian tradition took the third almost universally, and built on it the Nimrod who is a tyrant, a
rebel, and the builder of the tower — Josephus has him turn men against God so that they would credit
their prosperity to him rather than to heaven (*Antiquities* 1.113–114), and *Targum
Pseudo-Jonathan*, *Genesis Rabbah*, and Jerome all read the name from *mārad*, **to rebel**. The
grammar does not support the etymology (the form would not be *nimrōd*), and **Genesis 11 never
mentions him**. What Genesis says is that a proverb existed about him, which is the only proverb
quoted in the primeval history, and that his kingdom began at Babel.

*"The beginning of his kingdom"* — *rēʾšît mamlaktô*. **This is the first occurrence of *mamlākâ*,
kingdom, in the Bible**, and the first human political rule of any kind: Genesis has had a city (4:17)
and crafts (4:20–22) and a curse of servitude (9:25), but never a realm. It arrives with four cities
attached — **Babel** (Babylon), **Erech** (Uruk), **Accad** (Akkad, Sargon's capital and the name of
the language), and **Calneh**, which has no secure identification and which some read as *kullānâ*,
*all of them*, making the phrase *"Babel, Erech, and Accad, all of them in the land of Shinar"*. The
three that are certain are the three oldest and greatest cities of southern Mesopotamia. See
[[Shinar]], [[Babel]], and [[Kingship and Empire]].

**Verse 11's subject is disputed** and the two readings produce different maps. *Min-hāʾāreṣ hahîʾ
yāṣāʾ ʾaššûr* can be read *"out of that land he went to Asshur"* (the WEB's choice, and the ancient
versions'), making Nimrod the founder of the Assyrian cities as well as the Babylonian; or *"out of
that land went forth Asshur"* (the KJV's choice), making Asshur — Shem's son, listed at 10:22 — the
founder, and giving the verse a Semitic migration north. Nothing in the Hebrew settles it. On the
first reading Genesis has made one man the origin of both empires that will destroy Israel and Judah,
which is why Micah 5:6 can call Assyria *"the land of Nimrod"* in parallel with *"the land of
Assyria"*; on the second, Genesis is merely noting where Assyrians came from. See
[[Nimrod and the Kingdoms of Shinar and Assyria]].

**Nineveh** enters Scripture here, and it enters as something someone built. It will be the great city
of Jonah, and the one Nahum spends three chapters watching fall. **Calah** is Nimrud (the modern name
preserves Nimrod's), **Rehoboth Ir** means *broad places of a city* and may be a description rather
than a name, and **Resen** is unidentified. See [[Nineveh]] and [[Assyria]].

#### Egypt and Canaan (10:13–20)

> **13** Mizraim became the father of Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim,
> **14** Pathrusim, Casluhim (which the Philistines descended from), and Caphtorim.

Seven peoples, all in the plural — these are the only entries in the chapter that are grammatically
gentilic from the start, and **Pathrusim** is recognizable: *Pathros* is Upper Egypt, and Isaiah
11:11, Jeremiah 44:1, and Ezekiel 29:14 all use it.

**The Philistine parenthesis is a known crux.** Genesis derives them from Casluhim; Amos 9:7 and
Jeremiah 47:4 derive them from **Caphtor**, which is Crete, and Caphtorim is the very next word. The
simplest account is that the parenthesis has migrated one name to the left in transmission, and the
wiki notes the tension rather than resolving it: the Torah's own genealogy and two prophets give
different fathers to the same people, and the Deuteronomist agrees with the prophets (Deuteronomy
2:23).

> **15** Canaan became the father of Sidon (his firstborn), Heth,
> **16** the Jebusites, the Amorites, the Girgashites,
> **17** the Hivites, the Arkites, the Sinites,
> **18** the Arvadites, the Zemarites, and the Hamathites. Afterward the families of the Canaanites
> were spread abroad.
> **19** The border of the Canaanites was from Sidon—as you go toward Gerar—to Gaza—as you go toward
> Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim—to Lasha.

**Eleven descendants, and the only borders in the chapter.** Every other branch gets names; Canaan
gets a surveyed frontier, running north to south along the coast from Sidon to Gaza and then inland to
the cities of the plain. Genesis knows exactly which land this book is about, and it draws the line
around it two chapters before Abram is told to go there.

The names are the conquest lists in advance. **Heth** is the Hittites of the Hebron narratives (Genesis
23), the **Jebusites** hold Jerusalem until David, the **Amorites** and **Girgashites** and **Hivites**
recur in the seven-nation formula of Deuteronomy 7:1 and Joshua 3:10, and **Sidon** is the Phoenician
city that will supply Jezebel. **Admah** and **Zeboiim** are named nine chapters before they are
destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah (19:24–25; Deuteronomy 29:23; Hosea 11:8).

**And the curse is not mentioned.** Twenty verses earlier Noah consigned Canaan to servitude three
times over, and the Table gives Canaan eleven sons, a firstborn, and a border, on exactly the same
footing as everyone else. Genesis makes the moral partition of 9:25–27 and the geographical partition
of chapter 10 sit side by side without making the second depend on the first. See
[[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]], and, for the land as distinct from the man, [[Canaan]].

> **20** These are the sons of Ham, after their families, according to their languages, in their lands
> and their nations.

### Shem's twenty-six (10:21–31)

> **21** Children were also born to Shem, the father of all the children of Eber, the elder brother of
> Japheth.
> **22** The sons of Shem were: Elam, Asshur, Arpachshad, Lud, and Aram.
> **23** The sons of Aram were: Uz, Hul, Gether, and Mash.

**Shem is introduced by a descendant rather than by a father**, which happens to no one else in the
chapter: he is *"the father of all the children of Eber"* — *ʾăbî kol-bənê-ʿēber* — four generations
before Eber is born at 10:24. The chapter tells you who Shem matters *for* before it tells you who
came from him. *ʿĒber* is one consonant away from *ʿibrî*, **Hebrew**, the word Genesis 14:13 will
first use of Abram, and both stand near the verb *ʿābar*, **to cross over**. See [[Eber]].

*"The elder brother of Japheth"* is a phrase the Hebrew leaves open — *ʾăḥî yepet haggādôl* can be
*"the elder brother of Japheth"* or *"the brother of Japheth the elder"*. It is the third piece of
evidence in Genesis that the three brothers' birth order is unsettled, alongside the eleven listings
that put Shem first and 9:24's calling Ham *"his youngest"*. The wiki leaves it unsettled; see
[[Shem]].

**Elam and Asshur are the reason the Table is not a language chart.** Elamite is not a Semitic language
and is not related to any known family; Canaanite is Semitic and nearly identical to Hebrew. The Table
puts Elam under Shem and Canaan under Ham. Whatever organizes this list — political alignment,
geography, trade, a memory of relations — it is not linguistic descent, and the nineteenth-century
coinage of *Semitic* from Shem's name has misled readers into expecting something the chapter never
claimed. **Uz** here is Aram's son, and it is the name of the land the book of [[Job (person)]] opens
in; see [[Uz]].

> **24** Arpachshad became the father of Shelah. Shelah became the father of Eber.
> **25** To Eber were born two sons. The name of the one was Peleg, for in his days the earth was
> divided. His brother’s name was Joktan.

**Peleg is the chapter's one etymology and its one crack of narrative light.** *Peleg*, from *pālag*,
**to divide** — *"for in his days the earth was divided"*, *niplǝgâ hāʾāreṣ*. The verb is not the
*pûṣ* of 11:4 and 11:8, nor the *nāpaṣ* of 9:19, so it is not a direct back-reference; but the
"earth" that divides is its population, and the only division of the earth's population Genesis
narrates falls in the next pericope. This is the Table's single acknowledgement that the scattering has
a cause, and it is buried in a name. See [[Peleg]].

The line then forks, and the fork is the chapter's structure in miniature: **Joktan** gets thirteen
sons and a territory (10:26–30), all of them Arabian — **Sheba**, **Ophir** the gold country of
1 Kings 9:28, **Hazarmaveth** which is Hadramawt in Yemen — and then vanishes from Scripture.
**Peleg** gets nothing here at all, no sons and no land, because Genesis 11:16–19 is going to give him
one son and run the line through him to Abram. The branch with the detail is the branch that ends.

> **30** Their dwelling extended from Mesha, as you go toward Sephar, the mountain of the east.
> **31** These are the sons of Shem, by their families, according to their languages, lands, and
> nations.

### The colophon (10:32)

> **32** These are the families of the sons of Noah, by their generations, according to their nations.
> The nations divided from these in the earth after the flood.

The verse restates 9:19 with the list now filled in, and it closes the chapter on the same verb the
chapter has been circling: *niprədû*, **they were separated**. Genesis has said this four ways in
thirty-two verses — divided (10:5), spread abroad (10:18), divided (10:25), divided (10:32) — and it
has not once said why.

### Seventy

Counted the way the chapter presents them, the names below Noah come to **seventy**: fourteen for
Japheth, thirty for Ham, twenty-six for Shem. The number is almost certainly deliberate, and it is the
number Scripture uses whenever it wants to say *all of them, in an order*: the seventy of Jacob's
household that go down into Egypt (Genesis 46:27; Exodus 1:5; Deuteronomy 10:22), the seventy elders
who eat before God on the mountain (Exodus 24:1, 9) and who receive the spirit (Numbers 11:16–25), the
seventy bulls offered across the seven days of Sukkot (Numbers 29:12–34), which Jewish tradition
reads as an offering for the seventy nations, and the seventy Jesus sends out two by two in Luke 10:1.

Deuteronomy 32:8 puts the two seventies in one sentence: *"When the Most High gave to the nations
their inheritance, when he separated the children of men, he set the bounds of the peoples according
to the number of the children of Israel."* That is the Masoretic reading. The Septuagint and the Dead
Sea Scroll 4QDeut^j read *"according to the number of the sons of God"* instead, which makes the
verse about a division of the nations among heavenly beings rather than a correspondence of numbers.
The variant matters enough to have its own treatment; see
[[Seventy Nations - Genesis 10 in Deuteronomy 32 and Luke 10]].

The Septuagint's own Table has **seventy-two**, having added Elisha to Japheth's line and Cainan to
Shem's — and the manuscripts of Luke 10:1 are split between seventy and seventy-two in exactly the
same way. Whatever the original number, the two texts were being counted against each other early.

## People

- [[Noah]] — named twice, at the head and the foot of the chapter, and not otherwise present. He died
  three verses ago (9:29) and the whole map is filed under his name. It is the last time Genesis
  mentions him.
- [[Shem]] — twenty-six descendants, no borders, and an introduction that runs through a
  great-grandson: *"the father of all the children of Eber"*. His branch is given last, which in
  Genesis means it is the one being followed, and 11:10 will restart it in the linear form with ages
  and a line to Abram. **Elam and Asshur are his sons**, which is the single strongest evidence that
  the Table is not organized by language.
- [[Ham]] — thirty descendants, the largest branch, and the only one that gets a narrative and a
  border. Four sons: Cush, Mizraim, Put, Canaan — Nubia, Egypt, Libya, and the land Israel is going
  to. Nothing here mentions 9:22, and nothing here is presented as a consequence of it.
- [[Japheth]] — fourteen descendants and the Mediterranean rim, taken first because Genesis clears
  collateral lines out of the way before following the chosen one. This is everything Scripture ever
  says about his line, and Ezekiel 38–39 will draw Gog's coalition almost entirely out of it.
- [[Nimrod]] — **the only person in the chapter who does anything.** Cush's son; a *gibbōr*, the word
  Genesis last used of the pre-flood giants; a hunter whom a proverb remembers; the founder of the
  first kingdom in Scripture, at Babel in Shinar; and, on the WEB's reading of 10:11, the builder of
  Nineveh and Calah as well. Five verbs and no genealogy after him. Genesis 11 does not name him, and
  everything that makes him a tyrant in later tradition is read into *"before Yahweh"*.
- [[Eber]] — Shem's great-grandson, and the man Shem is introduced by. His name stands one consonant
  from *ʿibrî*, **Hebrew**, and the line of Abram runs through him.
- [[Peleg]] — Eber's son, named *division* because *"in his days the earth was divided"*: the
  chapter's only etymology, its only causal statement, and the hinge into Genesis 11.
- [[Canaan (son of Ham)]] — the man cursed three times twenty verses ago, here given a firstborn,
  eleven descendants, and the territory the rest of the Torah is aimed at. He is not the land; the
  land is [[Canaan]].
- **Joktan** — Peleg's brother, with thirteen sons across southern Arabia and a dwelling *"from Mesha,
  as you go toward Sephar"*. He never appears again outside 1 Chronicles 1:20–23. No page: he is a
  branch terminus, and everything the Bible says about him is in these five verses.
- **Cush, Mizraim, Put, Asshur, Aram, Elam, Javan, Magog, Tarshish, and the rest** — sixty-odd names
  that Genesis treats simultaneously as men and as peoples. The wiki does not give each of them a
  person page: in this chapter they are eponyms, with no act, no age, and no death, and several of
  them (Mizraim, Asshur, Canaan) are simply the Hebrew name of a country. They get pages when a
  passage treats them as somewhere or someone in particular — which is why [[Egypt]], [[Assyria]],
  [[Shinar]], [[Babel]], [[Nineveh]], and [[Canaan]] are created here as **places** and the rest wait.
- [[God]] — **absent for the second pericope running.** No speech, no act, no appearance. The name
  *Yahweh* occurs once in thirty-two verses, at 10:9, and it occurs inside a saying that other people
  quote about Nimrod. The nations divide, spread, and settle, and the text records no divine reaction
  of any kind — which is exactly the gap Genesis 11:5 fills.

## Places

The first pericope in Genesis that is mostly geography. [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] had no
setting at all beyond *"his tent"*; this one names some forty places, and from here on every pericope
in the book is somewhere.

- [[Canaan]] — **new**, and the land the whole Torah points at. 10:19 gives its border, the only one in
  the chapter: from Sidon toward Gerar to Gaza, and inland toward Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim.
  The man keeps [[Canaan (son of Ham)]]; the land takes the bare name, exactly as
  [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]] said it would.
- [[Shinar]] — **new**. Southern Mesopotamia, the plain of Babel, Erech, and Accad. It is where the
  first kingdom begins here, where the tower is built in the next pericope, and where Judah is carried
  in Daniel 1:2.
- [[Babel]] — **new**. Named at 10:10 as *"the beginning of his kingdom"*, twenty-four verses before it
  is named again as the city with a tower. Babylon's entire biblical career starts as one entry in a
  king's city list.
- [[Assyria]] — **new**. *ʾAššûr*, both a son of Shem at 10:22 and a country at 10:11, which is the
  ambiguity verse 11 turns on.
- [[Nineveh]] — **new**. Built in this verse; besieged in Nahum; spared in Jonah.
- [[Egypt]] — **new**. *Mizraim*, listed as Ham's second son and used as the country's name for the
  rest of the Bible.
- [[Uz]] — Aram's son at 10:23 bears the name of the land [[Job of Uz]] opens in. This is one of three
  genealogical Uzzes in Scripture and it points north-east, to Aramean Syria; Genesis 36:28 and
  1 Chronicles 1:42 give a Horite Uz in Edom, and Lamentations 4:21 — the only verse that puts Uz on
  a map — puts it in Edom too, which is why the southern location is generally preferred. Genesis
  asserts no connection to Job and neither does the wiki; see [[Uz]] for the three readings.
- **Named but not given pages here** — Sidon, Gerar, Gaza, Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim, Lasha,
  Erech, Accad, Calneh, Calah, Rehoboth Ir, Resen, Mesha, Sephar, and the peoples-as-places of the
  three lists. Each waits for a passage that does something with it; Sodom and Gomorrah are nine
  chapters out, Gerar and Gaza further still.

## Themes

- [[The Nations]] — **the theme this chapter creates.** *Gôyim* as a category enters Scripture here
  with seventy members, a common ancestor, and no ranking. Everything the Bible later says about the
  peoples — that they are given inheritances (Deuteronomy 32:8), that Abram's family exists to bless
  them (Genesis 12:3), that they will stream to Zion (Isaiah 2:2–4), that they are gathered again at
  Pentecost (Acts 2) and standing before the throne (Revelation 7:9) — assumes this list.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — **also new here.** *Mamlākâ*, kingdom, occurs for the first time at 10:10,
  and it occurs attached to Babel and, on the WEB's reading, to Nineveh: the two cities that will
  destroy Israel and Judah are the first two things in Scripture that a human being rules. Genesis
  offers no verdict on the institution, and it does not need to; it has told you where it started.
- [[City and Civilization]] — the register that began with Cain's Enoch (4:17) and ran through tents,
  music, and metalwork (4:20–22) to Noah's vineyard (9:20) now takes in eight cities at once. The
  pattern holds: Genesis names an origin and declines to praise it.
- [[Blessing]] — 9:1 told the three sons *"Be fruitful, multiply, and replenish the earth"*, and
  10:32 reports it done. This chapter is the only place in the primeval history where a
  command of God is simply carried out, without failure, delay, or comment. That it is fulfilled
  through a scattering the next chapter treats as a judgment is a tension Genesis does not smooth.
- [[Curse]] — conspicuous by absence. Canaan is cursed at 9:25 and is here given a firstborn, eleven
  descendants, and a surveyed border, with no reference to the oracle. The Table neither ratifies the
  curse nor withdraws it.
- [[Covenant]] — the covenant of 9:8–17 was made with *"every living creature"*, and this is the roster
  of the human ones. Genesis 10 is what the universality of the Noahic covenant looks like written out.
- [[Judgment]] — none in the chapter, and that is the point of its placement. Between two divine
  judgments — the flood behind, Babel ahead — Genesis inserts a peaceful, orderly, unremarked account
  of how the world filled up.

## Notes

- **Genesis 10 and Genesis 11 contradict each other on the surface, and the wiki flags it rather than
  harmonizing it.** Three times this chapter says the nations divided *"everyone after his language"*
  (10:5, 20, 31) — and then 11:1 opens: *"The whole earth was of one language and of one speech."* Read
  as a sequence, the second sentence cannot follow the first. The standard explanations, none of which
  the text states:
  1. **Achronological arrangement.** Genesis routinely gives the outcome and then backs up to narrate
     the cause — Cain's line is run out to the seventh generation (4:17–24) before Seth is born
     (4:25), and Terah's death is reported at 11:32 before Abram leaves in 12:1 although 11:26 and
     12:4 put the departure sixty years earlier. Chapter 10 is the result; chapter 11 is the
     explanation. This is the majority reading and it requires nothing but Genesis's own habits.
  2. **Source division.** Chapter 10 is largely P with a J core at 10:8–19 and 10:21, 24–30; 11:1–9 is
     wholly J. On this account the two were juxtaposed rather than sequenced, and the tension is a
     seam.
  3. **Different scopes.** 11:1–9 describes one migration to one plain and one city, not the whole
     human race, and the languages of chapter 10 could postdate it. This preserves a sequence at the
     cost of reading *"the whole earth"* in 11:1 narrowly.

  Peleg at 10:25 is the pressure point: his name means division and his generation is the fifth from
  Noah, which places Babel firmly inside the Table's own span. Genesis put the etymology there and
  said nothing more.

- **The Table is not a race chart, and reading it as one has a long and specific history.** Three
  observations from inside the text: Elam is under Shem though Elamite is not a Semitic language;
  Canaan is under Ham though Canaanite is a Semitic language nearly identical to Hebrew; and Asshur
  appears as a son of Shem at 10:22 and as a country at 10:11. Whatever principle organizes the list —
  geography, political alignment, trade relations, historical memory — it cuts across both language and
  descent as a modern reader would define them. The nineteenth-century terms *Semitic*, *Hamitic*, and
  *Japhetic* were coined for language families and then, in the same century, pressed into service for
  racial taxonomies that the chapter does not support and that its own contents contradict. The related
  misreading of 9:25 is set out at length on [[Ham]] and on
  [[Noah's Sons and the Curse of Canaan]].

- **On the count of seventy.** Fourteen, thirty, and twenty-six, not counting Noah or the three sons
  themselves. The count is not quite mechanical: Sheba and Havilah each occur twice (10:7, 28–29) and
  are counted twice; the seven plural names of 10:13–14 and the eleven of 10:16–18 are peoples rather
  than persons; and *Calneh* at 10:10 may be a common noun rather than a name. Different reckonings
  are therefore possible, and the ancient ones differ — the Septuagint's Table yields seventy-two.
  What is stable is that the number is round, that Scripture uses seventy for completeness elsewhere,
  and that no ancient reader seems to have counted a different total by accident.

- **What Genesis 10 has, that its neighbours in the ancient world do not.** Mesopotamian and Egyptian
  texts list kings, dynasties, and city foundations; the Sumerian King List runs rulers before and
  after a flood much as Genesis 5 and 11 run generations. None of them attempts an inventory of all
  peoples. The nearest analogues — the Assyrian royal annals' catalogues of subject nations, the
  Egyptian execration texts' lists of enemies — are lists of the *other*, drawn up from the centre. The
  Table is drawn from the edge, includes its author's own people as one entry among seventy without
  distinction or precedence, and gives the largest and most detailed branch to Israel's enemies. It
  makes no claim that Israel is older, first, or separately created. Whatever else it is, it is not a
  charter of superiority, and the promise that answers it two chapters later is a promise that the
  seventy will be blessed.

- **Sources for the identifications.** The equations used above — Javan/Ionia, Madai/Media,
  Tubal and Meshech/Tabal and Mushki, Ashkenaz/Ishkuza, Kittim/Kition, Mizraim/Egypt, Cush/Nubia,
  Put/Libya, Caphtor/Crete, Erech/Uruk, Accad/Akkad, Calah/Nimrud, Hazarmaveth/Hadramawt — rest on
  Assyrian, Egyptian, and Greek sources outside the Bible and are of varying confidence. Javan,
  Mizraim, Erech, Accad, and Caphtor are secure. Magog, Calneh, Riphath, Mash, Resen, Mesha, Sephar,
  and Lasha have no agreed referent at all. The chapter is a map whose legend is partly lost, and the
  gaps are worth marking rather than filling.
