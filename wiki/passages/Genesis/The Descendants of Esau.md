# The Descendants of Esau

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 36:1–43

## Summary

**Forty-three verses, roughly ninety names, and one sentence in which anybody does anything.** A man
named Anah, out feeding his father's donkeys, finds hot springs in the wilderness (36:24). That is the
entire narrative content of the chapter. Everything else is register: three wives, five sons, ten
grandsons, fourteen chiefs of Esau, seven sons of Seir, seven chiefs of the Horites, eight kings of
Edom with their cities, and eleven more chiefs listed by district.

**It is the longest genealogy in Genesis and the only one for a nation outside the covenant given in
full national form** — not twelve princes in a sentence, as [[Ishmael]] gets at 25:16, but clans,
chiefs, a king list, and a territory. **Genesis grants Edom a constitution and gives Israel none**,
because Israel does not have one yet. That is the point of 36:31, the chapter's most quoted verse:
*"These are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the children of
Israel."*

**The chapter is where Genesis says goodbye to [[Esau]].** He has been on the page since 25:21 — the
sold birthright, the bitter cry, the twenty-year silence, the running embrace at the Jabbok's far
side, and his father's grave, where the text named him first. Here he takes his household away
because the land cannot hold two brothers at once, settles in the hill country of Seir, and is
converted into a nation. **After 36:43 he is never mentioned in Genesis again.** The next verse,
37:2, is *"This is the history of the generations of Jacob"*, and the book belongs to [[Joseph]].

**The structural habit is the book's oldest one**, and this is the fourth and last time it runs: the
line that is not the subject is dealt with first, in full, and then closed. See
[[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]].

### The shape

**The chapter is built out of a single repeated formula** — ***ʾēlleh***, *these are* — which opens
a new list eleven times in forty-three verses and is what makes the thing readable at all.

| verses | the formula | what it registers |
|---|---|---|
| **1–5** | *"this is the history of the generations of Esau"* | three wives, five sons, all born in Canaan |
| **6–8** | — | **the separation**: the household moves to Seir, and *"Esau is Edom"* |
| **9–14** | *"this is the history of the generations of Esau … in the hill country of Seir"* | the same five sons, now with ten grandsons and a concubine |
| **15–19** | *"these are the chiefs of the sons of Esau"* | fourteen ***ʾallûpîm***, by mother |
| **20–30** | *"these are the sons of Seir the Horite"* | **the people who were there first**, and their seven chiefs |
| **31–39** | *"these are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom"* | eight kings, eight cities, no dynasty |
| **40–43** | *"these are the names of the chiefs who came from Esau"* | eleven chiefs *"according to their habitations"* |

**Note what the table makes obvious: the genealogy is given twice.** Esau's sons are listed at 36:4–5
and again at 36:10–14, under two separate *toledot* headings at 36:1 and 36:9. **No other figure in
Genesis gets two.** The most economical account is that the chapter is an anthology — several
independent Edomite registers set side by side without being merged — which is also the best
explanation of the chapter's internal disagreements, catalogued below.

## Key verses

### 36:1–5 — three wives, and a list that contradicts an earlier one

> **1** Now this is the history of the generations of Esau (that is, Edom).
> **2** Esau took his wives from the daughters of Canaan: Adah the daughter of Elon, the Hittite; and
> Oholibamah the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon, the Hivite;
> **3** and Basemath, Ishmael’s daughter, sister of Nebaioth.
> **4** Adah bore to Esau Eliphaz. Basemath bore Reuel.
> **5** Oholibamah bore Jeush, Jalam, and Korah. These are the sons of Esau, who were born to him in
> the land of Canaan.

**The first four words of the register are a verdict.** *"Esau took his wives from the daughters of
Canaan"* — ***mibbənôt kənāʿan*** — which is the exact thing [[Abraham]] made a servant swear at 24:3
would never happen, and the thing 27:46 and 28:1 forbid in so many words. **Genesis 26:35 delivered
the same judgment by reporting his parents' grief; this chapter delivers it as a heading.** See
[[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]].

**And the three names do not match the three Genesis gave before.** At 26:34 Esau's Hittite wives are
*Judith the daughter of Beeri* and *Basemath the daughter of Elon*; at 28:9 he adds *Mahalath the
daughter of Ishmael*. Here, Elon's daughter is **Adah**, Ishmael's daughter is **Basemath**, Judith is
gone, and **Oholibamah** — not previously mentioned — is present. **The name *Basemath* changes
owners between the two lists**, which is the single detail no harmonisation handles well. This wiki
flags the disagreement rather than settling it; the full treatment, with the three standing
explanations and why none is adopted, is at
[[The Names of Esau's Wives - Genesis 26 and Genesis 36]].

**A third ethnic label appears here too.** Oholibamah's father Anah is called *the Hivite* at 36:2 and
her grandfather Zibeon turns up at 36:20 among *the sons of Seir the Horite*. **Hittite, Hivite,
Horite** — Genesis uses all three of the same milieu within twenty verses, and the consonants of the
last two (*ḥwy* / *ḥry*) are close enough that one is very likely a copyist's version of the other.

### 36:6–8 — the second separation, and it is Abram and Lot again

> **6** Esau took his wives, his sons, his daughters, and all the members of his household, with his
> livestock, all his animals, and all his possessions, which he had gathered in the land of Canaan,
> and went into a land away from his brother Jacob.
> **7** For their substance was too great for them to dwell together, and the land of their travels
> couldn’t bear them because of their livestock.
> **8** Esau lived in the hill country of Seir. Esau is Edom.

**This is Genesis 13:6 with the names changed.**

> **6** The land was not able to bear them, that they might live together; for their substance was
> great, so that they could not live together. *(Genesis 13:6, of Abram and Lot)*

Same two clauses, same causal order, same vocabulary of *substance* and *bearing* and *dwelling
together* — and in both cases the result is that the two men part and one of them leaves the land of
promise. **The parallel is close enough to be a quotation**, and it is the fourth and last time
Genesis clears a brother off the stage by prosperity rather than by quarrel. See
[[Too Great to Dwell Together - Abram and Lot, Esau and Jacob]] and [[Abram and Lot Separate]].

**And the difference matters as much as the likeness.** At 13:9 [[Abraham]] offers Lot the choice and
Lot takes the well-watered plain; here nobody negotiates, nobody chooses, and nobody speaks at all.
**Esau simply goes.** The chapter that is about to give him kings and chiefs opens by having him
withdraw without a word — and Deuteronomy 2:5 will make the withdrawal permanent from the other side:
*"I have given Mount Seir to Esau for a possession."* **It is a land grant, in the same book that
grants Canaan.** See [[Edom]] and [[Land and Sojourning]].

**One quiet correction is worth noting.** At 32:3 and 33:14–16 Esau is already living in Seir, years
before this. **The chapter is not narrating in sequence**; it is summarising a move whose result the
story has been assuming for three chapters, in the place where the register needs it.

### 36:9–14 — the same sons again, and the birth of Israel's oldest enemy

> **9** This is the history of the generations of Esau the father of the Edomites in the hill country
> of Seir:
> **10** these are the names of Esau’s sons: Eliphaz, the son of Adah, the wife of Esau; and Reuel,
> the son of Basemath, the wife of Esau.
> **11** The sons of Eliphaz were Teman, Omar, Zepho, and Gatam, and Kenaz.
> **12** Timna was concubine to Eliphaz, Esau’s son; and she bore to Eliphaz Amalek. These are the
> sons of Adah, Esau’s wife.

***"And she bore to Eliphaz Amalek."*** **Nine words, at the bottom of a list, for the ancestor of the
nation Israel is commanded never to forget and never to spare.** [[Amalek]] attacks the Exodus
generation at Rephidim before anyone else does (Exodus 17:8–16); Deuteronomy 25:17–19 orders his
memory blotted out; Saul loses his kingdom for sparing Agag (1 Samuel 15); and Haman the Agagite is
still trying to destroy the Jews in the book of Esther. **Genesis mentions none of it.** It records a
concubine's son in a subordinate clause and moves to the next name. See
[[Amalek - From a Concubine's Son to Israel's First Enemy]].

**The mother is worth as much attention as the son.** [[Timna]] is the only woman in the chapter given
a status rather than a patronymic — ***pîlegeš***, concubine — and she reappears at 36:22 as *Lotan's
sister*, which makes her a Horite woman of the displaced ruling family, and again at 36:40 as a chief
in her own right. **Genesis 36 lists her three times in three different capacities and never says a
word about her.**

**Two names in Eliphaz's five point straight at other books.** ***Teman*** is the town proverbial for
wisdom and the home of Job's first friend (see [[Teman]] and
[[Eliphaz the Temanite]]); ***Kenaz*** is the ancestor of the Kenizzites, and Caleb — the one spy
besides Joshua who is not afraid — is *"the son of Jephunneh the Kenizzite"* (Numbers 32:12). **An
Edomite clan is absorbed into Judah and produces the man who says the land can be taken.**

### 36:15–19 — the chiefs, and a name in the wrong column

> **15** These are the chiefs of the sons of Esau: the sons of Eliphaz the firstborn of Esau: chief
> Teman, chief Omar, chief Zepho, chief Kenaz,
> **16** chief Korah, chief Gatam, chief Amalek: these are the chiefs who came of Eliphaz in the land
> of Edom; these are the sons of Adah.

***ʾAllûp̄***, the WEB's **chief**, is very nearly a technical term for Edom. It is built on
***ʾelep̄*** — *thousand*, and also *clan*, the unit a thousand notionally is — so it means something
closer to **clan-head** than to *prince*. **Of its occurrences in the Hebrew Bible the great majority
are in this chapter and in the copy of it at 1 Chronicles 1:51–54**, with Exodus 15:15's *"the chiefs
of Edom"* the most conspicuous of the rest. **Genesis is using Edom's own political vocabulary**, the
way it used *twelve princes* — *nəśîʾîm* — for Ishmael at 25:16 and reserves neither for Jacob.

**And the list has a seam in it.** *Chief Korah* appears at 36:16 among the sons of Eliphaz, that is,
among the sons of Adah — **but Korah is the son of Oholibamah** at 36:5, 36:14, and 36:18, where he is
listed a second time. The Samaritan Pentateuch and part of the Greek tradition simply do not have him
at 36:16. **Either a chief named Korah existed in both lines, or a copyist's eye slipped**; the wiki
records the doubling rather than choosing, on the same principle as the wives. (He is unrelated to the
Levite Korah who is swallowed by the ground in Numbers 16.)

### 36:20–30 — the people who were there first

> **20** These are the sons of Seir the Horite, the inhabitants of the land: Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon,
> Anah,
> **21** Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan. These are the chiefs who came of the Horites, the children of Seir
> in the land of Edom.

***"The inhabitants of the land"*** — ***yōšəbê hā-ʾāreṣ***, the standing phrase for a population that
is about to be displaced. **Genesis gives eleven verses of genealogy to the people Esau's descendants
dispossessed**, which is a thing it does for no one else. Deuteronomy 2:12 supplies what Genesis
withholds: *"The Horites also lived in Seir in the past, but the children of Esau succeeded them. They
destroyed them from before them, and lived in their place, as Israel did to the land of his
possession."* **The comparison is Deuteronomy's own, and it is not flattering to either party.**

Who the Horites were is unsettled. The name is either *ḥōr*, **hole** or **cave** — hence the old
rendering *cave-dwellers* — or the Hurrians, a non-Semitic people well attested across the second
millennium Near East. **The personal names in this list are not obviously Semitic**, which is the
strongest argument for the second reading; but the Hurrian heartland is in the far north, and nothing
else puts them in the Arabah.

**The two families merge rather than replace.** [[Oholibamah]] is a granddaughter of Zibeon the Horite
and a wife of Esau; Timna is Lotan's sister and the mother of Amalek. **The conquest Deuteronomy
describes as destruction Genesis records as two marriages**, and both accounts stand.

#### 36:24 — the one thing that happens

> **24** These are the children of Zibeon: Aiah and Anah. This is Anah who found the hot springs in
> the wilderness, as he fed the donkeys of Zibeon his father.

**A finite verb, a place, an errand, and a discovery — in a chapter that otherwise contains nothing but
names.** The clause survives because somebody thought it was worth remembering, and Genesis preserves
it without saying why.

**What he found is not certain.** The word is ***hayyēmim***, a **hapax legomenon** — it occurs
nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible — and the ancient versions guess differently: the Vulgate has *aquas
calidas*, hot springs, which the WEB follows and which suits the sulphur springs of the Arabah; the
Syriac reads *water*; the Targums and the KJV have **mules**, taking the word as *yēmîm* and imagining
the first crossbreeding of a donkey and a horse; the Septuagint transliterates and declines to
decide. **The one sentence of narrative in Genesis 36 is a sentence nobody can translate with
confidence.**

**And Anah's own place in the family is doubled, like Korah's.** Here he is a son of Zibeon; at 36:2
Oholibamah is *"the daughter of Anah, the daughter of Zibeon"*, which reads as though Anah were
Zibeon's daughter — and the Samaritan, Greek, and Syriac all read *son* there. **Hebrew *bat* can mean
granddaughter**, so 36:2 may simply be tracing Oholibamah two steps back; but the versions' agreement
against the Masoretic text suggests the line was hard to read early.

### 36:31–39 — eight kings, and the verse that dates the book

> **31** These are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the children
> of Israel.
> **32** Bela, the son of Beor, reigned in Edom. The name of his city was Dinhabah.
> **33** Bela died, and Jobab, the son of Zerah of Bozrah, reigned in his place.

**Read the list for what it does not contain: a single son succeeding a father.** Eight kings, and the
formula every time is *X died, and Y reigned in his place* — with Y's patronymic or home town given
fresh each time, and **eight different cities**. Dinhabah, Bozrah, the land of the Temanites,
Avith, Masrekah, Rehoboth by the River, and Pau, with only [[Bozrah]] securely identifiable today.
**This is not a dynasty; it is a succession of chiefs from different clans**, elected or imposed —
which is exactly what the surrounding registers of *ʾallûpîm* would predict, and a good sign that the
list is genuine Edomite material rather than an Israelite construction.

**Three of the eight carry something extra.**

- **Bela the son of Beor** (36:32) bears the patronymic of **Balaam son of Beor**, the foreign seer
  hired to curse Israel in Numbers 22–24. The names are not the same — *Belaʿ* and *Bilʿām* — but they
  are close, the fathers are identical, and the Transjordanian setting is the same.
- **Jobab the son of Zerah of Bozrah** (36:33) is the king the **Septuagint's appendix to the book of
  Job identifies with Job himself**, supplying a genealogy that makes him Esau's descendant in the
  fifth generation and placing him *"on the borders of Idumea and Arabia."* It is a harmonisation
  rather than evidence — but it is the earliest surviving attempt to locate the book of Job, and it
  is why the Edomite reading of [[Uz]] dominates later Christian tradition. See
  [[Jobab of Bozrah and the Septuagint's Job]].
- **Hadad the son of Bedad, *"who struck Midian in the field of Moab"*** (36:35) — **the only event
  in the chapter besides Anah's springs**, and the only military action. Edom defeating [[Midian]] on
  [[Moab (place)]]'s territory is a note from a real regional history the Bible never otherwise tells.

**The last king is not given a death.** *"Baal Hanan the son of Achbor died, and Hadar reigned in his
place"* (36:39) — and there the list stops, with Hadar alive, his city Pau named, and his wife
**Mehetabel** given a pedigree two generations deep on her mother's side: *"the daughter of Matred,
the daughter of Mezahab."* **No other woman in the Hebrew Bible is traced through two mothers.** See
[[Mehetabel]].

#### *"Before any king reigned over the children of Israel"*

**The chapter's most discussed clause, and it is a dating formula.** It takes for granted that a reader
knows there are kings in Israel, and it measures Edom's antiquity against them. **Written before Saul,
it is a prediction; written after, it is a retrospect** — and the plain sense of *before any king
reigned* is the second.

This is one of the verses Ibn Ezra hinted at in the twelfth century in the passage his successors
called *the secret of the twelve*, and it became one of the standard pillars of the case that the
Pentateuch reached its final form long after Moses. **The wiki does not need to settle that question
to notice the plainer thing**: Genesis 17:6 promised [[Abraham]] *"kings will come out of you"*, and
35:11 repeated it to [[Jacob]] one chapter ago — **and the chapter that immediately follows gives the
kings to Esau first.** See [[Kings Will Come Out of You]].

### 36:40–43 — chiefs who are places

> **40** These are the names of the chiefs who came from Esau, according to their families, after
> their places, and by their names: chief Timna, chief Alvah, chief Jetheth,
> …
> **43** chief Magdiel, and chief Iram. These are the chiefs of Edom, according to their habitations in
> the land of their possession. This is Esau, the father of the Edomites.

**The third chief-list, and it is organised on a different principle from the first.** 36:15–19 listed
chiefs by mother; this one lists them *"after their places"* and *"according to their habitations"*.
**Several of the eleven names are not persons in any recoverable sense** — *Timna* is a woman four
verses of genealogy ago and also a copper-mining valley in the Arabah; *Oholibamah* is Esau's wife;
*Mibzar* means **fortress**; *Magdiel* has the shape of a place name. **The list is a map written in
the grammar of a family tree**, which is how tribal genealogy normally works and which this chapter
makes unusually easy to see.

***"This is Esau, the father of the Edomites."*** **The last words about him in the book**, and they
are a definition rather than a farewell. The man who came out red, sold a meal's worth of future, and
wept on his brother's neck is closed out as the eponym of a nation — and the next sentence Genesis
writes is *"Jacob lived in the land of his father's travels, in the land of Canaan"* (37:1). **One
brother becomes a country; the other stays a sojourner in a land he does not own.**

## People

- [[Esau]] — given his *toledot*, twice, and released from the book; the chapter converts him from a
  brother into an ancestor
- [[Adah (wife of Esau)]] — Elon the Hittite's daughter in this register, called Basemath in the one
  at 26:34; mother of Eliphaz and so of the Amalekites
- [[Oholibamah]] — Esau's third wife, unmentioned before this chapter; a Horite woman by descent, and
  the only wife of a patriarch's son who is also listed as a chief
- [[Mahalath]] — Ishmael's daughter, married at 28:9; this register calls her **Basemath**
- [[Basemath (daughter of Elon)]] — the name 26:34 gives Elon's daughter and 36:3 gives Ishmael's
- [[Judith]] — Esau's first wife at 26:34, and absent from this chapter entirely
- [[Eliphaz (son of Esau)]] — the firstborn, father of Teman and of Amalek; he shares a name and a
  country with [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] of the book of Job
- [[Reuel (son of Esau)]] — the second son, by Basemath; head of four clans and named nowhere else in
  Genesis
- [[Timna]] — Eliphaz's concubine, Lotan's sister, and a chief: three appearances, no speech, and the
  mother of Amalek
- [[Amalek]] — a grandson of Esau in a subordinate clause, and the ancestor of the nation Israel is
  told to blot out
- [[Seir the Horite]] — the eponym of the country, and the father of the people Esau's line displaced
- [[Anah]] — who found the hot springs while feeding his father's donkeys; the only person in the
  chapter who does anything
- [[Bela (son of Beor)]] — the first king of Edom, whose father's name is Balaam's
- [[Jobab]] — the second king, of Bozrah, whom the Septuagint identifies with Job
- [[Hadad (son of Bedad)]] — the fourth king, who beat Midian in the field of Moab
- [[Hadar]] — the last king, left alive at the end of the list
- [[Mehetabel]] — Hadar's wife, and the only person in the Bible given a pedigree through two mothers
- [[Ishmael]] — Esau's father-in-law, and the previous holder of this chapter's structural position
- [[Jacob]] — named once, at 36:6, as the brother the household moves away from
- [[Job (person)]] — not in the chapter, and attached to it by the Septuagint at 36:33

## Places

- [[Edom]] — named or implied in almost every verse; the chapter is its founding document
- [[Teman]] — a grandson of Esau at 36:11 and a chief at 36:42, and the Edomite district proverbial
  for wisdom
- [[Bozrah]] — Jobab's city, the one royal seat in the list that can still be pointed at
- [[Rehoboth by the River]] — Shaul's town, and not Isaac's well of the same name at 26:22
- [[Midian]] — struck by Hadad in the field of Moab, and the chapter's only battlefield
- [[Moab (place)]] — the field where it happened
- [[Canaan]] — where the first five sons were born, and the land the household leaves
- [[Uz]] — a Horite name at 36:28, and one of the three genealogical Uzzes the book of Job is measured
  against

## Notes

**On reading a genealogy as a chapter.** Genesis 36 is the sort of text a reader skips, and the wiki's
judgment is that skipping it loses three things: the only account the Bible gives of how Edom was
governed, the only appearance of Amalek's origin, and the clearest single case of Genesis preserving
two registers that disagree without noticing. **The chapter is dull in the way a land deed is dull.**

**Almost all of it is copied at 1 Chronicles 1:35–54**, with small variations — *Zephi* for *Zepho*,
*Hadad* for *Hadar*, and a death notice added for the last king. The Chronicler keeps the king list
and drops the Horites, which is a fair indication of what a later Israelite reader thought the
chapter was for.

**On the chapter's silences.** It never says Esau died. It never says how the household got from the
grief of 26:35 to a nation with a capital. It never mentions God — **the only chapter in the Jacob
cycle that does not** — and no blessing, promise, altar, or appearance occurs anywhere in it.
Genesis 27:39–40 had given Esau a charter of sorts, the sword and the service and the yoke that
comes off; **this chapter reports the outcome and declines to connect it to the charter.**
