# Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 17:1–27

## Summary

The chapter that gives the covenant a name for God, a new name for the man, a new name for his wife,
a name for a son not yet conceived, and a mark on the body of every male in the household — including
the ones who were bought.

**Thirteen years pass between the last verse of chapter 16 and the first verse of this one.** Abram
was eighty-six when Ishmael was born (16:16); he is ninety-nine here (17:1). Genesis says nothing
whatever about those thirteen years — no altar, no journey, no word from God, no word from Abram.
It is the longest silence in the Abraham cycle, and it sits in exactly the place where the household
had solved the problem for itself. The chapter opens by reopening it.

**God does almost all of the talking.** Of twenty-seven verses, twenty-two are divine speech in five
blocks; Abram falls on his face twice (17:3, 17:17), laughs once, and says one sentence out loud
(17:18) which is a request that God settle for the son he already has. He does not answer the name
change, the land grant, the circumcision command, or the promise to Sarah. Then, without a recorded
word, he circumcises every male in his household and himself before the day is out.

| | 17:1–2 | 17:3b–8 | 17:9–14 | 17:15–16 | 17:19–21 | 17:23–27 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who speaks | God | God | God | God | God | nobody |
| Subject | *I am God Almighty* | **Abraham**'s new name; nations, kings, land | **the sign**: circumcision | **Sarah**'s new name; her son | **Isaac** named; **Ishmael** answered | Abraham obeys |
| Abram's response | falls on his face | — | — | falls on his face, laughs | *"Oh that Ishmael might live before you!"* | *"in the same day"* |

The sign sits in the middle, and everything on either side of it is a name.

### The word that runs thirteen times through it

***Bərît*, covenant, occurs thirteen times in twenty-seven verses** — a density Genesis reaches
nowhere else except the ten verses of 9:8–17, which is the other chapter in the book where a covenant
gets a sign. The two are built the same way and the wiki reads them together: an unconditional
undertaking, an *ʾôt*, a token, and the adjective *ʿôlām*, everlasting. See [[Covenant]] and
[[The Covenant with Noah]].

**But this is the third telling of the same covenant, not the first.** Genesis 15 cut it with
animals and fire; Genesis 17 seals it with a knife and a name. Critical scholarship has read the two
as a doublet since Wellhausen — 15 assigned mainly to J and E, 17 to P — and the evidence is
stylistic and lexical rather than contradictory: P's *ʾĕlōhîm* and *ʾĒl Šadday* against J's *Yahweh*,
P's *hēqîm bərît* (*establish*) against 15:18's *kārat bərît* (*cut*), P's dates and ages and
genealogical exactness against 15's night sky and smoking furnace. Nothing in either chapter denies
the other, and Genesis as it stands reads the second as a ratification: 15 gave the promise a border
and an oath, 17 gives it a sign, a stipulation, and the name of the son.

### *ʾĒl Šadday* — the name that arrives with the sign

***"I am God Almighty"*** (17:1). ***ʾĂnî ʾĒl Šadday*** — **the name's first occurrence in
Scripture**, and one of the two or three most obscure words in the Hebrew Bible.

- ***Šadday* from *šadû*, Akkadian for *mountain***, giving *God of the mountain* — the most widely
  held modern proposal, and it fits a divine title of the sort Genesis has been collecting since
  14:18.
- ***Šadday* from *šad*, breast**, giving *the God who suffices* or *who nourishes*. Genesis 49:25
  puts *Šadday* in the same breath as *"blessings of the breasts, and of the womb"*, which is either
  the etymology showing or a poet's pun.
- ***Šadday* from *šādad*, to deal violently, to destroy**, which is what Isaiah 13:6 and Joel 1:15
  play on: *"as destruction from the Almighty"* — *kəšōd miš-Šadday*.
- **The Septuagint's *pantokratōr* and the Vulgate's *omnipotens*** — *Almighty*, the rendering every
  English Bible including the WEB inherits — descend from a rabbinic reading that divides the word as
  *ša-day*, *the one who is enough*.

None of these is secure. What is observable is where the name is used: **six times in Genesis and
thirty-one times in Job**, and almost nowhere else in the Torah. In Genesis it appears at every point
where the promise of offspring is transferred — to Abraham here, to Jacob at 28:3 and 35:11, to the
sons going down to Egypt at 43:14, and in Jacob's blessing at 48:3 and 49:25. In [[Job (person)]]'s
mouth and his friends' it is the standard name for the God who is being argued about. See
[[The Names of God]] and
[[God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus]].

**And Exodus 6:2–3 makes this verse a hinge of the whole documentary question**: *"I appeared to
Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty; but by my name Yahweh I was not known to them."*
Genesis 17:1 has Yahweh appear and introduce himself as *ʾĒl Šadday* in the same sentence — which is
either exactly what Exodus 6:3 describes, or the seam between two sources showing.

### *"Walk before me, and be blameless"*

***Hithallēk lə-pānay we-heyēh tāmîm*** (17:1). **The first stipulation attached to any covenant in
the Bible.** Noah was told what to build; the animals of Genesis 9 were told nothing; Abram in
Genesis 15 slept through his own ceremony and undertook nothing at all. Here, before a word about
circumcision, there is an imperative.

Both halves are quotations of Noah. *"Noah was a righteous man, blameless among the people of his
time. Noah walked with God"* (6:9) — where *blameless* is *tāmîm*. But the preposition has changed,
and the change is consistent across the rest of Genesis:

| | |
|---|---|
| **walked *with* God** — *ʾet-hāʾĕlōhîm* | Enoch (5:22, 24), Noah (6:9) |
| **walk *before* me** — *lə-pānay* | Abraham (17:1; 24:40), Isaac and Abraham together (48:15) |

*Walking with* is companionship and it is said of two men who are exempted from a general death.
*Walking before* is the posture of a servant in the presence of his lord — the idiom of 1 Samuel 2:30
and 1 Kings 8:25, used of kings — and it means conducting your life in the sight of someone who is
watching. Genesis switches from the first to the second at the exact point where the relationship
acquires obligations. See [[Walking with God]].

*Tāmîm* is not *sinless*. It is the word for an unblemished animal (Leviticus 1:3) and for a whole,
undivided thing; of a person it means integrity rather than perfection, and Genesis applies it to a
man it will later show planting a vineyard and lying drunk and uncovered in his tent (9:20–21). See
[[Blameless Before Anyone Is Watching]].

### The renamings

***"Your name will no more be called Abram, but your name will be Abraham"*** (17:5). ***ʾAbrām***
means **exalted father** (*ʾāb* + *rām*), which was already an awkward name for a childless
seventy-five-year-old. ***ʾAbrāhām*** is glossed on the spot: *"for I have made you the father of a
multitude of nations"* — *ʾab-hămôn gôyim*.

**The gloss is a pun and not a derivation.** There is no *r* in *hămôn* and no way to get *hāhām*
from it; philologically *Abraham* is almost certainly a dialectal expansion of the same name *Abram*,
the sort of lengthening attested in Northwest Semitic personal names. Genesis is not doing
etymology — it is doing what it does with *Babel* (11:9), *Ishmael* (16:11), and *Isaac* (17:19):
hearing a meaning in a name at the moment the name is given. The meaning it hears is *nations*,
plural, which is new.

***"As for Sarai your wife, you shall not call her name Sarai, but her name will be Sarah"***
(17:15). **And here Genesis offers no explanation at all** — the only renaming in the book that comes
without one. Both forms mean **princess**; *Śāray* looks like an archaic form of *Śārâ* and the two
are the same word. She is renamed and nothing is said to have changed about the meaning.

What has changed is who the promise runs through. Genesis 15's *"he who will come out of your own
body"* (15:4) never mentioned her; 16:1 opened on that gap and the household filled it with a slave.
Here, for the first time in five promise scenes, God says *hers*: *"I will give you a son by her …
she will be a mother of nations. Kings of peoples will come from her"* (17:16). **Sarah is the only
woman in the Bible whom God renames**, and the only one given the *nations and kings* formula. See
[[Sarah]] and [[Barrenness]].

### The sign, and what it is not

***"Every male among you shall be circumcised … It will be a token of the covenant between me and
you"*** (17:10–11). *ʾÔt bərît* — **the same phrase as the rainbow's** at 9:12–13, 17, and the
comparison is the fastest way to see what kind of sign this is.

| | Genesis 9 | Genesis 17 |
|---|---|---|
| The sign | the bow in the cloud | circumcision |
| Who carries it | God — *"I will look at it"* (9:16) | the human party, in the flesh |
| Who can see it | everyone | almost nobody |
| Scope | *"every living creature of all flesh"* | every male of one household and its slaves |
| Condition | none | *"you will keep my covenant"* (17:9) |
| Sanction | none | *"that soul shall be cut off from his people"* (17:14) |

The rainbow is public, universal, and God's own reminder to himself. Circumcision is private,
particular, permanent, and carried on the body of the party who did not swear the covenant in chapter
15. It is not visible in the assembly, not visible to the nations, and not visible to the man himself
most of the time — which is exactly the feature Jeremiah and Paul will both work with.

**Three things in the legislation are worth pausing on.**

***"He who is eight days old"*** (17:12). Circumcision is attested widely in the ancient Near East —
Egyptian tomb reliefs at Saqqara show the operation, and Jeremiah 9:25–26 lists Egypt, Judah, Edom,
Ammon, Moab, and the desert peoples together as *circumcised in the flesh*. The Philistines are the
Bible's standing example of the uncircumcised, which is why the word works as an insult in Judges and
Samuel. **What is unusual here is not the rite but the age.** Elsewhere it is a puberty or marriage
rite performed on someone who chose it or endured it; Genesis puts it on an infant eight days old,
before any consent, any merit, or any memory. The sign of the covenant is administered to somebody
who cannot have done anything to deserve or refuse it, which is the same logic the chapter states
about the covenant itself. Leviticus 12:3 fixes the eighth day in law; Luke 1:59, Luke 2:21, and
Philippians 3:5 all take it for granted.

***"he who is born in the house, or bought with money from any foreigner"*** (17:12–13, and again in
23 and 27). **Slaves are circumcised, and the text says so four times.** A bought Egyptian or Amorite
carries the covenant sign in his body without having been promised anything by it — and, three
chapters after a household solved its problem with a woman it owned, Genesis puts the mark of the
covenant on the bodies of the people it owns. Exodus 12:44–48 will make this the mechanism by which a
foreigner can keep the Passover at all: circumcision is what opens the covenant community to
outsiders, and it opens it first to slaves who were not asked. The wiki records both edges of that
without smoothing either. See [[Slavery and Servitude]] and [[The Nations]].

***"that soul shall be cut off from his people"*** (17:14). ***Wə-nikrətâ*** — **the first *kārēt*
penalty in the Torah**, the formula Leviticus and Numbers will use some thirty times for offences
with no human court attached: eating leaven in the festival week (Exodus 12:15, 19), eating blood
(Leviticus 7:27), approaching the holy things unclean (Leviticus 7:20). What being *cut off* means is
genuinely undefined — execution, exile, childlessness, or a divine act outside the legal system are
all defended, and the Mishnah's discussions leave it unsettled. What is clear is the wordplay: the
covenant is *cut* (*kārat*), the foreskin is *cut* (*mûl*), and the man who is not cut is *cut off*.

**And note what 17:14 calls it.** Not *he has refused the sign* but *"He has broken my covenant"* —
the sign and the covenant are named as the same thing in one clause, which is the sentence every
later argument about whether circumcision saves anybody has to get past.

### Abraham laughs

***"Then Abraham fell on his face, and laughed, and said in his heart, 'Will a child be born to him
who is one hundred years old? Will Sarah, who is ninety years old, give birth?'"*** (17:17).

*Wayyiṣḥāq.* The verb is the name — ***Yiṣḥāq***, **Isaac**, *he laughs* — and Genesis will use it
four times about this family: Abraham here, Sarah behind the tent flap at 18:12, Sarah again at 21:6
(*"God has made me laugh"*), and Ishmael *məṣaḥēq* at 21:9, in the participle that gets him expelled.

**Abraham's laugh is not rebuked and Sarah's is.** He laughs, in his heart, at exactly the arithmetic
she will laugh at one chapter later — and God's next word to him is the name of the son. She laughs,
in her heart, and is asked *"Why did Sarah laugh?"* and *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"* (18:13–14).
Genesis records the difference and does not explain it, and the wiki declines to invent a distinction
between a laugh of faith and a laugh of doubt that the text does not make. Both of them are laughing
at a hundred-year-old man and a ninety-year-old woman, which is a reasonable thing to laugh at. See
[[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].

***"Oh that Ishmael might live before you!"*** (17:18). **Abraham's only spoken sentence in the
chapter**, and it asks God to be satisfied with the son the household produced for itself. It is not
a wicked request — *live before you* is the chapter's own opening verb, *hithallēk lə-pānay*, turned
into a prayer for a thirteen-year-old boy — and it is the most human line in twenty-seven verses.
God's answer refuses the substitution and grants the boy nearly everything else.

***"As for Ishmael, I have heard you"*** (17:20). ***Šəmaʿtîkā*** — the concession puns on the boy's
name one more time. *God hears* is what *Yišmāʿēl* means; it was given at 16:11 because God heard his
mother's affliction, and here it is given again because God heard his father's plea. Then: *blessed*,
*fruitful*, *multiplied exceedingly*, *twelve princes*, *a great nation* — every term of the
patriarchal blessing except the one that matters. ***"But my covenant I establish with Isaac"***
(17:21).

**This is the sharpest statement in Genesis of a pattern it uses four times.** Ishmael, Esau,
Manasseh, and the brothers of Judah are all genuinely blessed and genuinely not the line. The book
never makes the non-chosen the villain and never makes the choice a verdict on merit; it simply
distinguishes *blessed* from *covenanted* and keeps both words. See [[Call and Election]] and
[[Ishmael]].

### The named son

***"You shall call his name Isaac"*** (17:19). **The second annunciation in Genesis and the second
name given by God before a birth** — both of them in the space of two chapters, and the first went to
a slave in a desert (16:11). Isaac joins a very short list of people named by God before conception:
Ishmael, Solomon (1 Chronicles 22:9), Josiah (1 Kings 13:2), Cyrus (Isaiah 45:1), John and Jesus
(Luke 1:13, 31).

***"at this set time next year"*** (17:21). *Lammôʿēd*, **at the appointed time** — the first
appointment in the Abraham story, and the answer to twenty-four years of waiting since 12:4. It is
repeated at 18:14 and discharged at 21:2. After five promise scenes with no date in them, the promise
finally has one.

### The obedience

***"in the same day, as God had said to him"*** (17:23). No reply, no question, no delay. Genesis
gives the compliance the same grammar it gave at 12:4 (*"So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him"*) —
the command in full, the obedience in a clause — and then counts the household: Ishmael, everyone
born in the house, everyone bought with money, *"every male among the men of Abraham's house"*, and
the ninety-nine-year-old man himself.

**Ishmael is circumcised at thirteen** (17:25). He receives the sign of the covenant on the same day
he is told, through his father, that the covenant is not his — inside the mark and outside the
promise, which is the chapter's last and least resolvable fact. The Arab tradition of circumcising
boys at thirteen is standardly traced to this verse.

**And the last word of the chapter is *with him*.** *"All the men of his house, those born in the
house, and those bought with money from a foreigner, were circumcised with him"* (17:27). The
covenant that began with one man called out of a family ends its founding chapter as a household
event, with foreigners in it.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *Abram* for the last time in Scripture. He is ninety-nine, thirteen years past the
  chapter where his household solved the problem for him, and God's first word to him in all that
  time is an imperative. He receives a new name, a covenant with a stipulation in it, a sign cut into
  his body, and the news that the son will come from Sarah — and he falls on his face twice, laughs
  once at the arithmetic, asks for Ishmael instead, and then obeys completely and without comment
  the same day.
- [[Sarah]] — *Sarai* for the last time. She is not present, not addressed, and not consulted; the
  promise about her is spoken over her to her husband. But it is spoken: the omission that Genesis 15
  left and Genesis 16 exploited is closed here in one verse, and she becomes the only woman in the
  Bible renamed by God and the only one given *nations* and *kings*.
- [[Isaac]] — named, dated, and assigned the covenant a year before he is conceived. He exists in
  this chapter only as a name that means *he laughs*, given to a couple who laughed.
- [[Ishmael]] — thirteen years old, and the subject of the chapter's most exact distinction. His
  father asks for him by name; God says *"I have heard you"* — a pun on the boy's name — blesses him,
  makes him fruitful, promises twelve princes and a great nation, and gives the covenant to someone
  else. He is circumcised the same day, which places him permanently inside the sign and outside the
  thing it signs.
- [[God]] — who speaks for twenty-two of twenty-seven verses, introduces himself under a name
  Scripture has never used before, imposes the first covenant stipulation in the Bible, renames two
  people, names a third, refuses a substitution, and then *"went up from Abraham"* (17:22).
- [[Hagar]] — absent, and structurally everywhere. The chapter is the answer to the arrangement she
  was used for, and the son it names displaces hers.

## Places

- [[Canaan]] — *"all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession"* (17:8). The land promise
  reaches its strongest formulation here — *ʾăḥuzzat ʿôlām*, a permanent holding, the legal word for
  inherited property — spoken to a man described in the same verse as *"traveling"* in it, who will
  die owning one cave. See [[Land and Sojourning]].

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the theme's second decisive Genesis passage after chapter 15. *Bərît* thirteen times
  in twenty-seven verses; *bərît ʿôlām*, everlasting covenant, three times (17:7, 13, 19); the covenant
  formula *"to be a God to you and to your offspring after you"* (17:7) in its first occurrence, which
  runs from here to Leviticus 26:12, Jeremiah 31:33, and Revelation 21:3; the first sign carried by
  the human party; and the first stipulation ever attached — *"Walk before me, and be blameless."*
- [[Circumcision]] — the theme's founding text, and the whole of it in one chapter: the command, the
  age, the household scope, the word *token*, the penalty for omission, and the same-day compliance.
- [[The Names of God]] — *ʾĒl Šadday*, **God Almighty**, first occurrence in Scripture, of uncertain
  derivation, and the name Exodus 6:3 says was the patriarchs' own.
- [[Blessing]] — *"I will bless her"*, twice in one verse of a ninety-year-old woman (17:16), and the
  full blessing formula given to Ishmael in the same speech that withholds the covenant from him.
- [[The Nations]] — *gôyim* enters the Abrahamic promise here. Abram was to be *a great nation*
  (12:2); Abraham is *"the father of a multitude of nations"* (17:4–5), and Sarah *"a mother of
  nations"* (17:16). The narrowing to one household is stated, for the first time, as a plural.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — *"Kings will come out of you"* (17:6) and *"Kings of peoples will come
  from her"* (17:16). The first promise of monarchy in the Bible, made four centuries before anyone
  asks for a king and without a word of the ambivalence 1 Samuel 8 will attach to it.
- [[Barrenness]] — the obstacle finally named as the thing God will act on. Sarah is ninety and the
  chapter says so twice; the promise is given a date.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — *"the land where you are traveling"* given as *an everlasting
  possession*, in the same sentence.
- [[Call and Election]] — the chapter that makes the distinction explicit: Ishmael is blessed by name
  and the covenant is Isaac's, with no reason given for either.
- [[Walking with God]] — *hithallēk lə-pānay*, *walk before me*: the preposition that replaces Enoch's
  and Noah's *with*, and the first thing ever asked of a covenant partner.
- [[Righteousness]] — *tāmîm*, blameless, asked of Abraham here and declared of Noah at 6:9. Genesis
  15:6 credited him with righteousness for believing; 17:1 asks him to be blameless. The two verses
  are two chapters apart and the book makes no attempt to order them.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — every male *"born in the house"* and *"bought with money from any
  foreigner"* is circumcised, said four times. The covenant sign is put on people who were not party
  to the covenant and not asked.
- [[Life and Death]] — *kārēt*, *cut off from his people*: the Torah's first use of a penalty it never
  defines.
- [[Language and Speech]] — three namings and a renaming in one chapter, two of them with an
  etymology offered and one with none, and all of them performed by God rather than by a parent.
- [[Faith]] — the man credited with righteousness for believing a promise (15:6) laughs at the same
  promise when it is given a number (17:17), and is not rebuked. Genesis's account of faith has room
  for that too.
- [[Marriage]] — the promise is redirected from Abraham's body to the marriage, and the second wife
  of chapter 16 is not mentioned.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — the Bible's second annunciation, with a date attached.

## Notes

- **The chapter is P's covenant and Genesis 15 is J and E's, and the wiki records the analysis
  without needing it settled.** The markers are consistent — *ʾĕlōhîm* and *ʾĒl Šadday*, *hēqîm*
  rather than *kārat*, precise ages (99, 90, 100, 13, 8 days), the genealogical formula *"throughout
  their generations"*, and an interest in ritual institution rather than in narrative scene. Read as
  sources, 15 and 17 are two accounts of one covenant. Read as the book stands, they are two stages of
  one: the oath, and then the sign. Nothing in either contradicts the other, and the second explicitly
  repeats the first's content (land, offspring) before adding to it.
- **Whether circumcision is a condition is the question the chapter deliberately leaves live.** On one
  side: 17:9 says *"you will keep my covenant"*, 17:14 calls omission *breaking* it, and a sanction is
  attached — none of which appears anywhere in Genesis 15. On the other: the covenant is declared
  *everlasting* three times, is established with Isaac before Isaac exists, and 17:14 cuts off the
  individual rather than annulling the arrangement. The most defensible reading is that circumcision
  is how a person joins a covenant that does not depend on him, which is why the sign can be given to
  an eight-day-old and to a purchased foreigner. Every later argument in Scripture — Jeremiah's,
  Paul's, the Jerusalem council's — is a fight over that sentence.
- **On what the rite physically was, briefly.** The biblical operation (*mîlâ*) removed the end of the
  foreskin; the fuller *pəriʿâ*, the uncovering, is a later rabbinic requirement, and 1 Maccabees 1:15
  and 1 Corinthians 7:18 both attest to Hellenized Jews reversing the appearance of the first. Egypt
  practised a partial, dorsal incision, which is one reason Jeremiah 9:25–26 can group nations
  together as circumcised while the Old Testament still treats Israel's rite as distinct.
- **The chapter has a real cost for half the household and the wiki says so plainly.** The sign of
  the covenant is on male bodies only, women are not marked and not addressed, and Sarah — whose
  renaming and whose son are two of the chapter's five speeches — hears none of it directly. Genesis
  does not comment. The Old Testament itself moves the sign inward almost immediately (Deuteronomy
  10:16), and Paul's declaration at Galatians 3:28 that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek,
  slave nor free, male nor female arrives in the middle of an argument about exactly this chapter.
- **Reception.** *Bərît mîlâ* is the oldest continuously observed Jewish rite and the one most often
  chosen as the point of martyrdom under Antiochus IV (1 Maccabees 1:60–61) and under Hadrian. In
  Islam, *khitān* is not commanded in the Qurʾān but is universal practice traced to Ibrāhīm; the age
  varies, and Ishmael's thirteen at 17:25 is the usual authority for the older custom. In Christianity
  the rite was set aside for Gentiles at Acts 15 and re-argued at every subsequent reformation as the
  precedent for or against infant baptism — Colossians 2:11–12 is the verse both sides quote. The wiki
  records all of this as reception and adjudicates none of it.
