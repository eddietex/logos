# The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 6:1–8

## Summary

Eight verses, and they carry the hinge of the primeval history. Genesis 1–5 has been a story of
things multiplying — light, creatures, generations, and also sin. Genesis 6:1–8 is where the
narrator stops the register, opens the world's ledger, and reports a verdict. By verse 7 God has
decided to undo what he made, and by verse 8 one man stands outside the sentence for a reason the
text refuses to explain.

The unit falls into two panels with a single sentence between them, and the panels are doing very
different things.

**The first panel (6:1–4) is the strangest paragraph in Genesis, and possibly in the Bible.** It
opens on the blessing of 1:28 finally working at scale: *"When men began to multiply on the surface
of the ground, and daughters were born to them"*. The verb is the one God commanded — *be fruitful
and multiply* — and for one clause it looks like good news. Then the sentence turns: *"God's sons
saw that men's daughters were beautiful, and they took any that they wanted for themselves as
wives."*

Read that verse against Genesis 3:6, because it is built out of it. There, *"the woman saw that the
tree was good … and she took"*. Here, the sons of God *saw* … *good/beautiful* (Hebrew *ṭôb* in both
places) … and *took*. Saw, judged good, took — the same three-verb sequence that ruined the garden,
run again by a class of beings the text has not previously mentioned, on a scale the text does not
bound: *"any that they wanted"* is literally *"all whom they chose"*. Whatever else Genesis is
narrating here, it is narrating a boundary being crossed by the strong against the available, and it
is using the grammar of the first transgression to say so.

Who the sons of God are, whether the crossing is angelic or dynastic or genealogical, and what the
Nephilim of verse 4 have to do with any of it, are questions the passage does not answer and
readers have never stopped answering. All three major readings are laid out in the Notes; the
honest summary is that the narrator tells this with striking compression, names no one, judges no
one by name, and moves on in four verses. The reticence is not accidental. Compared with what later
literature does with this episode — *1 Enoch* spends chapters on it — Genesis is almost aggressively
uninterested in the mechanics. What it wants is the effect: the last boundary in the created order
has gone soft, and heaven and earth are no longer holding their places.

**The sentence between the panels (6:3) is the divine response, and it is not a punishment of
anyone named.** *"My Spirit will not strive with man forever, because he also is flesh; so his days
will be one hundred twenty years."* Coming three verses after a chapter of nine-hundred-year
lifespans, the number lands hard. Genesis has just spent thirty-two verses measuring life in
centuries; now a limit is set, and the word used of humanity is *flesh* — *bāśār*, the frailty word,
the word for what dies. Whether the hundred and twenty is a new cap on human life or a countdown to
the flood is genuinely disputed and both readings have real support (see Notes).

**The second panel (6:5–8) is the verdict, and it is written in the vocabulary of Genesis 1.** In
the creation account God repeatedly *saw* what he had made and it was *good*. Here: *"Yahweh saw
that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of
man's heart was continually only evil."* The same verb of divine inspection, the opposite finding.
Genesis 1:31 had *"God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good"*; Genesis 6:5
has three totalizing words piled in one clause — **every** imagination, **continually**, **only**
evil. This is the Bible's most sweeping statement about the human interior, and it is placed before
the flood, not after it, which matters because God says something almost identical *after* the
flood (8:21) as a reason for **not** doing it again.

Then the verse that has generated more theological argument than anything else in the passage:
*"Yahweh was sorry that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him in his heart."* The God who
has spoken the world into being and pronounced it good is here described as regretting it and being
pained by it. The Hebrew verb is *nāḥam* — the same root that Lamech used when he named this
chapter's one survivor, hoping his son would *comfort* them. Ten generations of hope for comfort,
answered by a God in grief. And *"it grieved him in his heart"* uses *ʿāṣab*, the root of the *pain*
God assigned to the woman and the *toil* he assigned to the man in Genesis 3:16–17. The sentence
passed in Eden has come back around onto the one who passed it.

The judgment itself (6:7) is described as **un-creation**, and the word order proves it: *"man,
along with animals, creeping things, and birds of the sky"* — the inhabitants of days five and six,
listed for erasure in something close to the reverse of the order in which they were made. God does
not merely punish the earth; he proposes to run Genesis 1 backwards. The rest of the flood account
will carry this through with the waters above and the waters below returning through the openings
that were made to separate them.

**And then verse 8, which is seven words in Hebrew and changes everything.** *"But Noah found favor
in Yahweh's eyes."* The *but* is doing enormous work. It is the first occurrence in the Bible of
*ḥēn* — favor, grace — and it arrives at the exact moment the world is condemned, attached to a man
whose name is its own consonants reversed: **נֹחַ** *nōaḥ* against **חֵן** *ḥēn*. And note the order
of the clauses in the paragraph: the verdict comes first, the sentence second, and the exception
third. Noah is not why the judgment is softened; the judgment is total, and Noah is what is carried
through it. The text also, pointedly, does not yet say Noah was righteous. That comes in the next
verse, on the far side of the *toledot* heading. Here he is simply the man who found favor — which
means the wiki's oldest question about this chapter is left open by design: is Noah spared because
he was good, or good because he was spared?

That is the shape of the unit. Humanity multiplies; a boundary fails; a limit is set; God looks at
the human heart and grieves; the world is sentenced; one man finds favor. Everything from Genesis
6:9 to Genesis 9:29 is the working out of the last four words.

## Key verses

> **1** When men began to multiply on the surface of the ground, and daughters were born to them,

> **2** God’s sons saw that men’s daughters were beautiful, and they took any that they wanted for
> themselves as wives.

> **3** Yahweh said, “My Spirit will not strive with man forever, because he also is flesh; so his
> days will be one hundred twenty years.”

> **4** The Nephilim were in the earth in those days, and also after that, when God’s sons came in
> to men’s daughters and had children with them. Those were the mighty men who were of old, men of
> renown.

> **5** Yahweh saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of
> the thoughts of man’s heart was continually only evil.

> **6** Yahweh was sorry that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him in his heart.

> **7** Yahweh said, “I will destroy man whom I have created from the surface of the ground—man,
> along with animals, creeping things, and birds of the sky—for I am sorry that I have made them.”

> **8** But Noah found favor in Yahweh’s eyes.

## People

- [[God]] — named **Yahweh** in every one of the four verses where he acts (3, 5, 6, 7, 8), and
  never *ʾĕlōhîm* except inside the phrase *"God's sons"*. This is the passage that shows him
  seeing, speaking, regretting, grieving, resolving, and favoring, all in four verses, and it is the
  only place in Genesis that reports an emotion in his *heart*.
- [[The Sons of God]] — *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, introduced with no explanation and dropped after two
  appearances. The identity question is the passage's central crux; the page collects the three
  readings and the evidence for each.
- [[The Nephilim]] — present *"in those days, and also after that"*, associated with the union of
  6:2 but not straightforwardly said to be its offspring, glossed as *"the mighty men who were of
  old, men of renown"*, and named again only at Numbers 13:33.
- [[Noah]] — named in the last four words and nowhere else in the unit. He is given no act, no
  speech, and not yet a character reference: only that he *found favor*. Everything the reader knows
  about him at this point comes from Genesis 5:29 and 5:32.
- [[Adam]] — not as an individual. Verses 1, 3, 5, 6, and 7 all use *ʾādām*, and all of them mean
  humanity collectively, which is the sense the name carried in Genesis 1:26–27 before Genesis 5:3
  narrowed it to one man. The whole species is on trial here under the first man's name, and the
  narrator does not distinguish them, which is exactly the ambiguity Romans 5:12 will build on.
- [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] — absent from the text, present in its vocabulary. His hope at 5:29
  used the verb *nāḥam*, "comfort"; 6:6 uses the same root of God, in its other sense, "regret".

## Places

None. Like Genesis 5, this passage names no location — only *"the surface of the ground"* (6:1, 7)
and *"the earth"* (6:1, 4, 5, 6). That is deliberate and it is a change of scale rather than an
omission: Genesis 3 and 4 tracked consequence by geography (out of the garden, east of Eden, the
land of Nod, a city), and from Genesis 5 onward the arena is simply *hāʾāreṣ*, the whole earth. The
first eleven chapters keep widening the frame, and this is the passage where it reaches everything
under the sky.

## Themes

- [[Sin]] — the passage supplies the diagnosis Genesis had so far only illustrated. 6:5 moves the
  problem from acts to the *yēṣer*, the imagination or inclination of the heart, and qualifies it
  three ways at once: every imagination, continually, only evil. Nothing earlier in the Bible says
  this and nothing later says it more strongly.
- [[Judgment]] — the pattern of Genesis 3 and 4 (God comes, questions, sentences, provides) runs
  here with the questioning removed. No one is interrogated, no one answers, no one protests. What
  survives of the pattern is the provision, and it arrives before the flood as a single name.
- [[Divine Grief and Relenting]] — introduced here, in the two occurrences of *nāḥam* at 6:6–7. The
  theme's whole difficulty is on this page: a God who regrets, against a Bible that elsewhere says
  he does not.
- [[Favor and Grace]] — *ḥēn*, first occurrence in Scripture, at 6:8. Placed after a total verdict
  and before any statement of Noah's character, which is where the doctrine of unmerited favor
  usually starts from.
- [[Life and Death]] — 6:3 sets the first numerical limit on human life, and 6:7 extends the
  sentence of death from individuals to *"all flesh"*, including animals that did nothing.
- [[Blessing]] — 6:1 is the blessing of 1:28 succeeding. *"Men began to multiply"* is the command
  being obeyed, and Genesis lets it be the setting for the corruption without ever suggesting the
  multiplying was the problem.
- [[Marriage]] — the taking of 6:2 is described in the vocabulary of marriage (*"took … for
  themselves as wives"*) and violates it in every particular that Genesis 2:24 established: not one
  flesh but many, not leaving and cleaving but seizing, and across a boundary rather than within a
  kind.
- [[Creation]] — 6:7 lists the creatures of days five and six for erasure. The judgment is framed
  as the undoing of Genesis 1, which makes the flood the Bible's first and largest statement that
  creation is not self-sustaining but held.

## Notes

- **Who are "God's sons"? (6:2, 6:4).** The Hebrew is *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, literally *sons of the
  God/gods*, and the WEB's *"God's sons"* is a defensible rendering of an ambiguous construct. Three
  readings, in rough order of their age:

  **(1) Divine beings — angels, or members of the divine council.** This is the oldest attested
  reading by a wide margin: it is what *1 Enoch* assumes, what Jubilees, the Dead Sea Scrolls,
  Josephus, Philo, and every surviving pre-Christian Jewish treatment assumes, and what most of the
  earliest church fathers (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement) assumed. Its strongest argument is
  lexical and is genuinely strong: everywhere else the exact phrase *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* or its close
  variants occurs in the Hebrew Bible — Job 1:6, 2:1, 38:7, Psalm 29:1, 89:6 — it means heavenly
  beings, never human beings. Its second argument is the contrast the verse itself draws: *sons of
  God* over against *daughters of men* is a pairing that reads naturally as two orders and awkwardly
  as two families. Its difficulties are also real. Nothing in the passage condemns these beings, and
  the sentence in 6:3 and the flood in 6:7 fall on *humanity*, which is odd if the offense was
  chiefly angelic. And Jesus says that in the resurrection people *"neither marry nor are given in
  marriage, but are like God's angels in heaven"* (Matthew 22:30) — usually cited as decisive
  against angelic procreation, though it strictly concerns the angels *in heaven* and these, on this
  reading, did not stay there.

  **(2) The Sethite line intermarrying with the Cainite line.** Sons of God = the godly line of
  Genesis 5, the men who called on Yahweh's name; daughters of men = the line of Genesis 4:17–26.
  This reading appears in Julius Africanus, is adopted by Chrysostom and then decisively by
  Augustine, and through Augustine became the standard Western view for over a millennium; it is
  still the majority view in conservative Protestant commentary. Its strength is contextual: the two
  genealogies have just been set against each other over two chapters, and it explains why the
  judgment falls on humanity. Its weakness is that it has to supply everything it needs from
  context — the Hebrew phrase is never used this way, Genesis has not called the Sethites *sons of
  God*, and it does not explain the Nephilim, who on this reading are the perfectly ordinary
  children of ordinary parents and inexplicably get called mighty men of renown.

  **(3) Divine or dynastic kings.** Sons of God = rulers, taking *ʾĕlōhîm* in the sense it may carry
  at Psalm 82:1, 6 and Exodus 21:6, and drawing on the well-documented ancient Near Eastern practice
  of kings styling themselves sons of a god. On this reading 6:2 describes despotic harem-building
  — royal *ius primae noctis*, tyranny presented as the last stage of Lamech's boast — and the
  Nephilim are the resulting warrior aristocracy, which fits *gibbōrîm* (*"mighty men"*) and *ʾanšê
  haššēm* (*"men of the name"*) very well indeed. Its weakness is that Genesis 6 gives no hint of
  kingship, and the earliest Israelite kings are a thousand narrative years away.

  What can be said without choosing: the phrase's usage elsewhere favors (1); the immediate
  narrative context favors (2); the description of the offspring favors (3); and the passage itself
  declines to adjudicate. See [[The Sons of God]] and
  [[The Sons of God and the Angels That Sinned]].

- **"My Spirit will not strive with man forever" (6:3).** Two of the four significant words in this
  clause are uncertain. *Rûaḥ* is *spirit*, *breath*, or *wind*; it may be God's Spirit (so the WEB,
  capitalized) or the life-breath God lent to dust at 2:7, in which case the verse says the animating
  breath will not stay in flesh indefinitely. The verb *yādôn* is a genuine crux — it occurs
  nowhere else in this form, and it has been derived from *dîn* (to judge, contend), *dûn* (to
  remain, abide, so the Septuagint's *katameinē*), and less plausibly from roots meaning *shield* or
  *be humbled*. The two main renderings — *strive with* and *abide in* — produce noticeably different
  verses. What is not uncertain is the reason given: *"because he also is flesh"*. *Bāśār* is the
  weakness word, and this is the first time in Genesis that being flesh is offered as a ground for a
  divine decision.

- **A hundred and twenty years: cap or countdown?** Both readings are old and both have force.
  **As a lifespan limit**, it reads straightforwardly as a new maximum, and Moses conveniently dies
  at exactly 120 (Deuteronomy 34:7); against it, Noah lives 950, Shem 600, Abraham 175, and Jacob
  147 — all after this verse — so if it is a cap it takes many centuries to bind, though the decline
  through Genesis 11 is real and steep. **As a countdown to the flood**, it reads as a period of
  reprieve, which fits the immediate context (the sentence in 6:7 follows directly), fits 1 Peter
  3:20's *"the patience of God waited in the days of Noah, while the ship was being built"*, and is
  the standard rabbinic reading; against it, the verse says *his days*, not *the earth's days*, and
  Genesis 5:32 puts Noah at 500 when he fathers his sons and 7:6 at 600 when the flood comes, which
  yields 100 years rather than 120 unless the announcement predates 5:32. Neither reading is
  embarrassed enough by its problems to be excluded.

- **The Nephilim (6:4).** The name is transliterated rather than translated in most modern versions
  because its meaning is not secure. The obvious derivation is from *nāpal*, *to fall* — hence *the
  fallen ones*, which suits the angelic reading almost too neatly and may be why it is suspected; an
  alternative traces it to an Aramaic word for *giant*, which is what the Septuagint assumed when it
  rendered *gigantes*. Three things in the verse deserve care. It says the Nephilim were *"in the
  earth in those days, and also after that"* — a parenthesis that seems designed to explain why the
  word turns up again in Numbers 13:33, long after a flood that should have ended them. It links
  them to the union of 6:2 by simultaneity (*"when God's sons came in to men's daughters"*) without
  quite saying they were the children of it; the *"mighty men … men of renown"* of the second
  sentence are the offspring, and whether *Nephilim* names the same group is a matter of reading.
  And it is the only evaluative phrase in the paragraph — *"men of renown"*, *ʾanšê haššēm*, *men of
  the name* — which is not obviously condemnation at all, until Genesis 11:4 has humanity say
  *"let's make ourselves a name"* and the flood sits between the two. See [[The Nephilim]].

- **The verdict of 6:5 and the word *yēṣer*.** *Yēṣer* is from *yāṣar*, the potter's verb used of
  God forming the man from dust at 2:7 — so *the thing formed*, the shaping or bent of the mind. In
  later rabbinic thought the *yēṣer hārāʿ*, the evil inclination, becomes a full anthropology, paired
  with a *yēṣer haṭṭôb* and understood as an impulse to be mastered rather than a total corruption;
  Genesis 6:5 is that tradition's foundation text and does not itself contain the pairing. In
  Christian theology the verse is a principal support for original sin and for the Reformed doctrine
  of total depravity, and it is worth being exact about what it does and does not carry. It asserts
  the universality and the interiority and the constancy of evil in that generation, in the strongest
  terms the language allows. It does not assert a mechanism of transmission — that is Romans 5:12's
  contribution — and it is a statement about the generation of the flood in its narrative context,
  which 8:21 then generalizes to humanity as such.

- **"Yahweh was sorry … and it grieved him in his heart" (6:6).** The verb *nāḥam* in this stem
  covers regret, relenting, being moved to pity, and comforting oneself; the noun phrase *ʿāṣab
  ʾel-libbô* is *pained to his heart*. The theological difficulty is direct: Numbers 23:19 and 1
  Samuel 15:29 say God does not *nāḥam* as a man does, and 1 Samuel 15 says this in the same chapter
  where it twice says he *did* regret making Saul king. Three main approaches. **Anthropomorphism** —
  the standard classical answer, from Philo through Augustine to Aquinas and Calvin: Scripture
  speaks of God in human terms because we have no others, and the verse describes a real change in
  God's relation to the world without a change in God. **Relational or covenantal contingency** — God
  genuinely responds to what people do, and the "does not repent" texts concern his character and
  his sworn promises rather than his announced intentions, which Jeremiah 18:7–10 explicitly makes
  conditional. **Open theism** — God's experience of the future is genuinely open, and the regret is
  regret in the plainest sense; a minority position, and this verse is one of its main texts. Note
  what the verse does not say on any reading: it does not say God was *wrong*. And note the pastoral
  weight of *ʿāṣab* — the pain God assigned to the man and the woman in Genesis 3 is now, in the
  same word, what he feels. See [[Divine Grief and Relenting]].

- **Un-creation (6:7).** *"Man, along with animals, creeping things, and birds of the sky"* is the
  population of days five and six, and the flood account will complete the picture by opening *"the
  fountains of the great deep"* and *"the sky's windows"* (7:11) — undoing the separation of waters
  made on day two. Genesis's judgment is not a natural disaster with theological commentary
  attached; it is the creative acts of Genesis 1 being deliberately reversed, and then, at 8:1–2,
  redone: a wind (*rûaḥ*, the word of 1:2 and of 6:3) passes over the waters, and dry land appears.

- **Noah and *ḥēn* (6:8).** *ḥēn* — favor, grace, the word behind *charis* in the Septuagint and
  behind most of what the New Testament means by grace — occurs here for the first time in the
  Bible. Two features of its placement are worth noticing and are not disputable. It comes **after**
  the verdict and the sentence, not before, so the paragraph's logic is not *the world was evil
  except Noah, therefore judgment fell on the rest*, but *the world was evil, therefore judgment
  fell, and Noah found favor*. And it comes **before** any statement of his character: 6:9 will call
  him righteous and blameless, but that belongs to the next unit, on the far side of a *toledot*
  heading. Reformed readers take the order as the point — grace precedes and produces the
  righteousness of 6:9. Others take 6:9 as simply supplying the reason 6:8 withheld, which the
  narrative sequence permits. The consonantal play (*nōaḥ* / *ḥēn*) is universally noticed and, as
  with all such plays in Genesis, is a matter of sound rather than derivation.

- **Where the source-critical seam is usually placed.** Standard documentary analysis assigns
  6:1–8 to J (the divine name *Yahweh* throughout, the anthropomorphic portrayal, the folkloric
  register of 6:1–4) and begins P at 6:9 with *"This is the history of the generations of Noah"* and
  its consistent *ʾĕlōhîm*. This is worth recording because it explains a real feature of the text
  that any reader notices — the abrupt heading at 6:9, the second introduction of Noah, the shift in
  the divine name — but the two accounts are interleaved so thoroughly through Genesis 7–8 that the
  flood narrative is either the strongest evidence for the theory or the hardest case for it,
  depending on the commentator. The wiki takes no position; the seam is noted here because 6:8 and
  6:9 are the two verses where it is most visible.
