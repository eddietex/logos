# Abraham and Abimelech

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 20:1–18

## Summary

He does it again.

**Eight chapters after Egypt, and one chapter before the son arrives, Abraham hands his wife to
another king with the same sentence.** *"Abraham said about Sarah his wife, 'She is my sister.'"*
There is no famine this time and no stated pressure; the chapter gives no reason at all until
Abimelech asks for one in verse 10, and the reason, when it comes, turns out to be a standing policy
adopted at the start of the journey (20:13). Genesis has told this story before, at 12:10–20, and
will tell it a third time about Isaac at 26:6–11. See [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].

**What is new is everything the first telling left out.** Genesis 12 gave the ruse, the taking, an
unexplained plague, three unanswered questions, and an expulsion. Genesis 20 supplies a dream, a
divine speech, a defence from the king, a denial that the marriage was consummated, an explanation
from Abraham, a settlement, a prayer, and a healing — and it does all of it in eighteen verses, of
which the foreign king speaks in six.

**And it is placed where it is for a reason the next chapter makes obvious.** *"Yahweh visited Sarah
as he had said"* (21:1) is the very next verse, and Isaac is born in the one after it. A wife taken
into another man's house immediately before the birth of the promised son is a paternity problem,
and Genesis 20:4 answers it in five words before anything else happens: ***"Now Abimelech had not
come near her."*** Chapter 12 had left the same question hanging and never went back to it. Here the
narrator will not let the reader out of the verse without it.

### The dream, and the first one in the Bible

***"But God came to Abimelech in a dream of the night"***. *Ḥălôm*, and **this is its first
occurrence in Scripture** — the word that will carry Jacob's ladder, Joseph's sheaves, Pharaoh's
cattle, and Nebuchadnezzar's statue enters the Bible in the bedroom of a Canaanite king who has
never been introduced.

Two things about that are worth holding together. **The dream is a real divine communication, not a
lesser one**: God speaks, is answered, answers back, issues a command, and attaches a sanction, and
nothing in the chapter treats the medium as second-rate. And **the recipient is an outsider.** God
has spoken to Abram at 12:1, 13:14, 15:1, 17:1 and 18:1, and to [[Hagar]] through his messenger at
16:7 — and now, without preface, to the king of Gerar. See [[Prophecy and Vision]].

***"Behold, you are a dead man"***. *Hinnəkā mēt* — *behold, you dying*, a participle, a sentence
already in force. It is the bluntest opening God gives anyone in Genesis, and it is given to the one
man in the chapter who has done nothing wrong.

### *"will you kill even a righteous nation?"*

**Abimelech answers back, and he answers back in Abraham's own words, two chapters after Abraham
used them.**

> **4** Now Abimelech had not come near her. He said, “Lord, will you kill even a righteous nation?

*Hă-gôy gam-ṣaddîq tahărōg* — *will you kill a nation, even a righteous one?* Set it beside 18:23,
where Abraham opened the Bible's first argument with God: *"Will you consume the righteous with the
wicked?"* Same verb-field, same premise, same appeal to a standard God must be held to. **The
difference is what happens next.** Abraham had to work down six rounds from fifty to ten and got a
conditional; Abimelech gets his concession in the opening clause of the reply — *"Yes, I know that in
the integrity of your heart you have done this"* (20:6). See
[[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].

**And note who is calling whom righteous.** *Gôy ṣaddîq* is Abimelech's description of his own people
— the only place in Genesis where a nation outside the covenant line is called righteous, and it is
said by the nation about itself, to Israel's God, and not contradicted. Two chapters earlier the
question had been whether ten righteous could be found inside a city; here a whole kingdom claims
the word and keeps it. See [[Righteousness]] and [[The Nations]].

He also addresses God as *ʾĂdōnāy*, **Lord** — the address of a subject to a sovereign, from a man
who has had no covenant, no call, and no prior word.

***"In the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands I have done this."*** *Bətom-ləbābî
ûbənīqyōn kappay* — the two-part formula of judicial innocence that the Psalms will make standard
(*"I will wash my hands in innocence"*, Psalm 26:6; *"He who has clean hands and a pure heart"*,
Psalm 24:4). **The first person in the Bible to plead innocence and be believed is a Canaanite
king.**

### *"I also withheld you from sinning against me"*

God's reply concedes the plea and then says something the chapter is easy to read past.

> **6** God said to him in the dream, “Yes, I know that in the integrity of your heart you have done
> this, and I also withheld you from sinning against me. Therefore I didn’t allow you to touch her.

**Three things are established in one verse.**

- **The integrity is genuine, and God says so.** The verdict on Abimelech's inner state comes from
  the one source in the story that can give it.
- **He was nonetheless in danger, and the danger was real.** Integrity did not make the act safe. He
  was going to die for something he could not have known, which is exactly the situation the later
  law will build a whole sacrificial category around: the *šəgāgâ*, the sin committed unwittingly,
  which still requires an offering (Leviticus 4:2, 13, 22, 27; Numbers 15:22–29). See
  [[Sinning Unwittingly - Integrity, Ignorance, and Guilt]].
- **And the offence would have been *against God*, not against Abraham.** *Mēḥăṭôʾ-lî* — *from
  sinning against me*. Abimelech will ask Abraham in verse 9 *"How have I sinned against you, that
  you have brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin?"*, and the answer the reader already has is
  that the injured party was never Abraham.

***"I didn't allow you"*** is the chapter's quiet centre. Whatever kept Abimelech from her — the
sickness of 20:17 is the usual inference, and the closed wombs of 20:18 are stated as a fact of the
whole household — the text credits it to God and not to the king's restraint. **Nobody in the story
protects Sarah, and she is protected.** It is the same arrangement as 12:17, where a plague did the
work, and the narrator marks it there and here with the identical clause: *"because of Sarai,
Abram's wife"* (12:17), *"because of Sarah, Abraham's wife"* (20:18) — the relation the husband
suppressed, restored by the narrator at the moment of rescue.

### *"he is a prophet"* — and the first prayer in the Bible

> **7** Now therefore, restore the man’s wife. For he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you
> will live.

**Two firsts in one sentence, and the wiki would rank them the most important thing in the chapter.**

***Nābîʾ*, prophet, occurs here for the first time in Scripture.** The word that will belong to
Moses, Samuel, Elijah, Isaiah, and Jeremiah is introduced in Genesis, applied to Abraham, and spoken
by God to a foreign king who needs to know what kind of man he is dealing with.

**And it is defined by intercession, not by prediction.** Nothing in the definition concerns telling
the future. *"For he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you will live"* — the prophet's
distinguishing function, the first time the office is named, is that his prayer works. See
[[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]].

***Hitpallēl*, to pray, also occurs here for the first time in Scripture**, and again at 20:17 when
the thing actually happens: *"Abraham prayed to God."* **The Bible's first prayer is prayed by the
man who caused the problem, on behalf of the man he wronged, for the restoration of fertility to a
household he had endangered** — and it is answered. See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

Genesis makes nothing of the irony and does not have to. The prophet in this chapter lies, is
rebuked by a pagan, offers a defence that is worse than the charge, and takes payment; and the title
is God's own, given before any of that comes out.

### The three-part defence

Abimelech's two questions (20:9–10) are the same interrogation Pharaoh conducted at 12:18–19, where
Abraham said nothing at all. **This time he answers, and the answer has three parts, and each part is
worse than the last.**

**1. A judgment about the place, and it is wrong.** *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place.
They will kill me for my wife's sake"* (20:11). The chapter has already shown God in this place, and
will show its king pleading integrity and its household *"very scared"* at a divine word (20:8).
**Abraham's premise is refuted by the story he is standing in**, and he is the only person in the
chapter who does not appear to fear God very much. See [[The Fear of God]].

**2. A technicality, and it is true.** *"Besides, she is indeed my sister, the daughter of my father,
but not the daughter of my mother; and she became my wife"* (20:12). **This is the only verse in
Scripture that answers the question Genesis 11:29 raised** by giving [[Milcah]]'s father and
withholding [[Sarah]]'s: she is [[Terah]]'s daughter by another woman. The half-truth turns out to
have had a true half all along — which is precisely the shape of deception Genesis specializes in,
where nothing false is said and a false belief is produced anyway. The marriage it describes is
forbidden at Leviticus 18:9 and 20:17 and cursed at Deuteronomy 27:22, none of which exists yet in
the narrative's world. See [[Deception]] and [[Marriage]].

**3. A policy, and it dates the whole thing to the call.** *"When God caused me to wander from my
father's house, I said to her, 'This is your kindness which you shall show to me. Everywhere that we
go, say of me, "He is my brother."'"* (20:13). **Egypt was not an improvisation.** The arrangement
was made at the outset of the journey of 12:1, which means the reader has been watching a standing
arrangement operate twice and has not been told about it until now.

**And look at what he calls the call.** *Hitʿû ʾōtî ʾĕlōhîm* — *God caused me to wander*, from *tāʿâ*,
the verb used of straying sheep (Psalm 119:176) and of people led astray (Isaiah 47:15). **It is the
least flattering description of Genesis 12:1 in the Bible, and it is on Abraham's own lips**, offered
as mitigation. He also uses a plural verb with *ʾĕlōhîm*, which is rare enough that both ancient and
modern commentators have noticed it; the likeliest explanations are that he is accommodating his
speech to a polytheist's ear or that *ʾĕlōhîm* is being construed as a plural of the divine world in
general. The wiki records the observation and does not build on it.

### The settlement, and the thousand pieces of silver

Abimelech gives back more than he took.

> **14** Abimelech took sheep and cattle, male servants and female servants, and gave them to
> Abraham, and restored Sarah, his wife, to him.

**Compare the endings.** Pharaoh returned the wife and *"sent him away"* with an escort (12:20) —
courteous, and an expulsion. Abimelech returns the wife, adds livestock and slaves, and then says
*"Behold, my land is before you. Dwell where it pleases you"* (20:15) — **the identical formula
Abram used to Lot at 13:9**, *"Isn't the whole land before you?"*, now offered to Abraham by the man
he defrauded. The patriarch leaves Gerar richer and with a residence permit. See
[[Land and Sojourning]].

***"To Sarah he said…"*** (20:16). **Abimelech is the only person in the chapter who speaks to her**,
and one of very few in Genesis who ever does. Her husband speaks *about* her in verse 2 and never to
her; God speaks about her at 20:3 and 20:7; the narrator names her five times. She says nothing in
the entire pericope.

What he says to her is the hardest verse in the chapter.

> **16** To Sarah he said, “Behold, I have given your brother a thousand pieces of silver. Behold, it
> is for you a covering of the eyes to all that are with you. In front of all you are vindicated.”

- ***"a thousand pieces of silver"*** — *ʾelep kesep*, a thousand [shekels of] silver, and an
  enormous sum. Abraham will pay four hundred shekels for the field and cave of Machpelah, a
  transaction Genesis treats as a large public purchase (23:15–16); Joseph is sold for twenty
  (37:28). The payment is not a fine anyone imposed. It is a voluntary public settlement by the
  injured party.
- ***"your brother"*** — the chapter's one flick of the whip. Abimelech uses the exact term Abraham
  insisted on, to the woman it was used to conceal, while handing over the money.
- ***"a covering of the eyes"*** — *kəsût ʿênayim*, and nobody knows for certain what it means. The
  main readings: **a vindication** — the money is public proof, so that no one who was there can look
  at her sideways; **a veil**, taken literally, either given or implied; or **compensation for the
  wrong done in the sight of witnesses**. The WEB's closing clause, *"In front of all you are
  vindicated"*, renders a form (*wənōkāḥat*) that is itself contested and that most translations turn
  into either *you are righted* or *you are reproved*. **The wiki records the verse as unresolved.**
  What is not in doubt is its direction: it is addressed to her, it is about her standing in front of
  other people, and it is spoken by the foreigner rather than by the husband.

### The closed wombs, and the healing

> **17** Abraham prayed to God. God healed Abimelech, and his wife, and his female servants, and they
> bore children.
> **18** For Yahweh had closed up tight all the wombs of the house of Abimelech, because of Sarah,
> Abraham’s wife.

**The sanction is withheld fertility, and the chapter saves the disclosure for its last verse.**
Verse 18 is a flashback: the wombs had been closed the whole time, which is very likely the physical
form of the *"I didn't allow you to touch her"* of 20:6 and the reason nothing happened to Sarah.

**And it lands in the one book where a closed womb is the whole problem.** [[Sarah]] has been barren
since 11:30; the promise has been waiting on that fact for ten chapters; and in the chapter
immediately before it is finally answered, **a household is struck barren because of her, and opened
again by her husband's prayer.** Genesis does not point at the symmetry. It puts *"God healed …
and they bore children"* eleven verses before *"Yahweh visited Sarah as he had said"* and lets them
sit next to each other. See [[Barrenness]].

*ʿĀṣōr ʿāṣar* — the WEB's *"closed up tight"* renders an infinitive-absolute doubling, the Hebrew
way of saying *shut, and shut hard*. The same verb is what [[Sarah]] herself used of her own case at
16:2: *"Yahweh has restrained me from bearing."*

### The names of God in this chapter

**Eighteen verses use *ʾĕlōhîm* throughout and *Yahweh* exactly once, in the last verse.** God is
*ʾĕlōhîm* at 20:3, 20:6, 20:11, 20:13, and twice at 20:17; Abimelech addresses him as *ʾĂdōnāy* at
20:4; and only at 20:18 does the narrator write *"For Yahweh had closed up tight all the wombs of the
house of Abimelech"*.

That distribution is the classic textbook case for the **E** source — a preference for *ʾĕlōhîm*
before Exodus 3, God appearing in dreams rather than in person, a marked interest in the prophet as
intercessor, and a moral sensitivity that supplies the exculpations chapter 12 lacked. Documentary
readings assign Genesis 20 to E and treat it as the first substantial E block in the book, with the
lone *Yahweh* of verse 18 as a redactional seam. **The wiki records the assignment as the standard
critical account and notes what it does not decide**: whether the chapter is a variant of 12:10–20 or
a second incident, and whether the differences between them are the growth of a tradition or the
design of a book that tells things twice on purpose. See [[The Names of God]].

## Key verses

> **1** Abraham traveled from there toward the land of the South, and lived between Kadesh and Shur.
> He lived as a foreigner in Gerar.

> **2** Abraham said about Sarah his wife, “She is my sister.” Abimelech king of Gerar sent, and took
> Sarah.

> **3** But God came to Abimelech in a dream of the night, and said to him, “Behold, you are a dead
> man, because of the woman whom you have taken. For she is a man’s wife.”

> **4** Now Abimelech had not come near her. He said, “Lord, will you kill even a righteous nation?

> **5** Didn’t he tell me, ‘She is my sister?’ She, even she herself, said, ‘He is my brother.’ In the
> integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands I have done this.”

> **6** God said to him in the dream, “Yes, I know that in the integrity of your heart you have done
> this, and I also withheld you from sinning against me. Therefore I didn’t allow you to touch her.

> **7** Now therefore, restore the man’s wife. For he is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you
> will live. If you don’t restore her, know for sure that you will die, you, and all who are yours.”

> **9** Then Abimelech called Abraham, and said to him, “What have you done to us? How have I sinned
> against you, that you have brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin? You have done deeds to me
> that ought not to be done!”

> **11** Abraham said, “Because I thought, ‘Surely the fear of God is not in this place. They will
> kill me for my wife’s sake.’

> **12** Besides, she is indeed my sister, the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my
> mother; and she became my wife.

> **13** When God caused me to wander from my father’s house, I said to her, ‘This is your kindness
> which you shall show to me. Everywhere that we go, say of me, “He is my brother.”’”

> **16** To Sarah he said, “Behold, I have given your brother a thousand pieces of silver. Behold, it
> is for you a covering of the eyes to all that are with you. In front of all you are vindicated.”

> **17** Abraham prayed to God. God healed Abimelech, and his wife, and his female servants, and they
> bore children.

> **18** For Yahweh had closed up tight all the wombs of the house of Abimelech, because of Sarah,
> Abraham’s wife.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — called *a prophet* by God for the first time in Scripture, in the same sentence that
  orders him to take his wife back; and the man who says *"the fear of God is not in this place"* in
  a chapter that has already shown otherwise. He prays the Bible's first prayer, and it is for the
  man he wronged.
- [[Sarah]] — named five times, spoken about by three men and a narrator, and silent throughout. She
  is taken, denied, restored, paid for, and addressed once — by the foreign king.
- [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] — king of Gerar; the first person in the Bible to receive a
  dream, the first to plead innocence and be believed, and the second foreign ruler in nine chapters
  to give a patriarch a moral lecture and then pay him.
- [[God]] — *ʾĕlōhîm* for seventeen verses and *Yahweh* in the last; he speaks only to the outsider,
  and what he says about the insider is a title.
- [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] — off-stage, and the measure of everything here. He asked the same
  questions and got no answers; this king asks them and gets three.
- [[Isaac]] — unborn and one verse away. The chapter's care over *"Abimelech had not come near her"*
  is care about him.
- [[Terah]] — named by implication in 20:12, the only verse in Scripture that identifies Sarah's
  father.

## Places

- [[Gerar]] — the Philistine-country town in the western Negev where Abraham sojourns; the setting of
  this chapter, of the treaty at 21:22–34, and of the whole of Isaac's version at 26:1–33.
- [[Kadesh]] — the great oasis at the southern limit of the land, and one of the two markers Genesis
  gives for where Abraham was living.
- [[Shur]] — the desert frontier toward Egypt, and the other marker. Between Kadesh and Shur is as
  far south as the patriarch ever settles.
- [[The Negev]] — *"the land of the South"*, the dry country he moves into after Sodom and never
  really leaves.
- [[Hebron]] — left behind, unmentioned, and the place the previous four chapters happened from.
- [[Egypt]] — off-stage, and the site of the first performance of this scene.
- [[Sodom]] — off-stage, and burnt. *"Abraham traveled from there"* is the only link the chapter makes
  to it.

## Themes

- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — the first *nābîʾ* in Scripture and the first *ḥălôm*, in the same
  pericope, and neither of them is about predicting anything.
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — the first *hitpallēl* in Scripture, twice: God prescribes the prayer,
  and eleven verses later it is prayed and answered.
- [[Deception]] — the same ruse a second time, now with the true half of the half-truth supplied, and
  revealed to have been standing policy since the call.
- [[The Fear of God]] — asserted to be absent from Gerar by the one man in Gerar who is not
  demonstrably afraid of God.
- [[Righteousness]] — a foreign king calls his own nation *ṣaddîq* to God's face and is not
  contradicted, one chapter after a city could not produce ten.
- [[Sin]] — *"a great sin"*, unwitting, prevented rather than committed, and reckoned as against God
  rather than against the injured husband.
- [[Barrenness]] — a whole household's wombs closed on account of the woman whose own womb is the
  book's standing problem, and opened again by prayer, eleven verses before hers is.
- [[Marriage]] — the relation denied in verse 2, restored by the narrator in verse 18, and defined by
  God in verse 3 as the fact that makes the taking fatal: *"she is a man's wife."*
- [[Judgment]] — a death sentence issued, argued against, and withdrawn inside four verses.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — *"He lived as a foreigner in Gerar"*, and the offer *"my land is before
  you"* from the king whose land it is.
- [[The Nations]] — the outsider is again the one with the moral vocabulary, and again the one God
  speaks to.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — male and female servants change hands as part of the settlement, and it
  is the female servants whose wombs the closing verses are counting.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** Chapter 20 is a self-contained unit: it opens with a move
  (*"Abraham traveled from there"*), closes with a resolution and an explanatory flashback, and is
  followed at 21:1 by a formula that starts something else. The wiki keeps the chapter whole. The
  argument for joining it to 21:22–34 — same king, same town, same officer named at 21:22 — is real,
  but 21:1–21 sits between them and is about something entirely different, so the plan's existing
  split stands unchanged.
- **Whether this is the same event as Genesis 12, retold.** The wiki's position is the one it has
  taken on the two creation accounts and the two genealogies from Adam: **record the readings, and
  do not pretend the text settles it.** The source-critical account (J at 12:10–20 and 26:6–11, E
  here) explains the divine names, the dream, and the exculpations elegantly, and has to treat 26:1's
  *"besides the first famine that was in the days of Abraham"* as a redactional stitch. The literary
  account replies that Genesis's normal method is repetition with variation, and that the escalation
  from unanswered accusation to full defence to near-miss is patterned rather than residual. Both
  accounts are built out of the same observations. See [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].
- **The chronological difficulty, stated plainly.** Sarah is eighty-nine or ninety here, by 17:17 and
  21:5, and she is taken into a king's household for her desirability. Genesis reports both facts
  without noticing a problem. The usual harmonizations — that the taking is dynastic rather than
  personal, that the numbers belong to a different stratum, that longevity in the patriarchal
  narratives implies a slower ageing — are all available and none is in the text. The wiki records
  the difficulty rather than resolving it, in the same way it records the difficulty of a
  hundred-year-old man running twice in the noon heat at 18:2–8.
- **Abimelech is a title, or very likely one.** *ʾĂbî-melek* means *my father is king*, and the same
  name belongs to the king of Gerar in Isaac's episode a generation later (26:1), to Gideon's son in
  Judges 9, and — in the superscription of Psalm 34 — to the man 1 Samuel 21 calls Achish. This wiki
  therefore files him as
  [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]], on the same convention that produced
  [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]], leaving room for the Abimelech of Isaac's time to take his own page
  when Genesis 26 is ingested. Whether they are one long-lived man, two men, or one office is not
  answerable from Genesis.
- **On the Philistines.** Genesis 20 does not use the word; 21:32, 21:34 and 26:1 do, of this same
  Gerar. The Philistines as an archaeologically visible people arrive on the coast around 1200 BC,
  which is centuries after any date proposed for Abraham, and the standard reading is that Genesis
  uses a later name for the region's inhabitants exactly as it uses *Dan* at 14:14 for a town not
  named Dan until Judges 18. The wiki records it as an anachronism of the same kind and does not
  treat it as a problem the text is trying to hide. See [[Gerar]].
- **What the chapter does not do.** It does not condemn Abraham; it does not withdraw or qualify the
  promise; it does not have God address him at all. He is given a title and an instruction about
  someone else's household, and he does what he is told. **The one verdict in the pericope is
  Abimelech's** — *"You have done deeds to me that ought not to be done!"* — and the narrator lets it
  stand without agreeing or disagreeing. This is the fourth consecutive pericope in which Genesis
  declines to grade the person it is following.
- **Rabbinic and patristic reception, briefly.** *Genesis Rabbah* 52 works hard on 20:16's *covering
  of the eyes*, reading the silver as compensation that shields Sarah from suspicion, and on the
  plural verb of 20:13. Rashi takes *"the fear of God is not in this place"* as Abraham's inference
  from being asked about his wife rather than offered hospitality. Among the Fathers, Augustine
  (*Contra Faustum* 22.36) defends the ruse as concealment rather than falsehood — *he said what was
  true and withheld what was true* — a reading that turns on 20:12 and is the ancestor of every
  later defence of the passage. The wiki records these as reception and does not adopt them.
