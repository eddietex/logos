# The Cupbearer and the Baker

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 40:1–23

## Summary

**This is the chapter where Joseph stops being a thing that happens to people and becomes a man who
reads.**

From 37:18 to the end of 39 he is acted on in almost every verse — stripped, pitted, priced, shipped,
bought, promoted, propositioned, accused, imprisoned. **Genesis 40 hands him a competence**, and the
book spends the next two chapters proving it works. **He interprets two dreams, and both come true
inside three days**, which is the shortest interval between prediction and fulfilment anywhere in
Genesis.

### Two words that exist nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible

***Pātar***, to interpret, and ***pitrôn***, an interpretation. **Between them they occur fourteen
times, and every single occurrence is in Genesis 40 and 41.** No prophet uses them, no psalm, no wisdom
book. **The Bible has a verb reserved for what Joseph does in prison and then never uses it again.**

| where | occurrences |
|---|---|
| **Genesis 40** | 40:5, 8 (twice), 12, 16, 18, 22 |
| **Genesis 41** | 41:8, 11, 12 (twice), 13, 15 (twice) |
| **the rest of the Hebrew Bible** | **none** |

**Genesis 42:23's *"there was an interpreter between them"* is not this word** — that is *mēlîṣ*, a
translator, and the book uses a different root the moment the work is linguistic rather than
revelatory. **The Aramaic cognate is *pəšar*, and it is the word Daniel uses in a Babylonian court
for the same job**; see
[[Do Not Interpretations Belong to God - Joseph and Daniel]].

### One idiom, three uses, and two opposite verdicts

***Nāśāʾ rōʾš***, **lift up the head**, is a live metaphor with more than one sense, and **the chapter
uses it three times in eight verses and lets the ambiguity do the work.**

| verse | the words | what it turns out to mean |
|---|---|---|
| **40:13** | *"Pharaoh will lift up your head, and restore you to your office"* | **reinstatement** |
| **40:19** | *"Pharaoh will lift up your head **from off you**"* | **decapitation and impalement** |
| **40:20** | *"he lifted up the head of the chief cup bearer and the head of the chief baker"* | **neither yet — a muster**, specified only by verses 21–22 |

**Three words in Hebrew are the whole difference between the two sentences Joseph speaks**, and the
second one is the first one with *mēʿāleykā*, *from off you*, added. **Genesis makes a pun carry a
death sentence**, and then uses the same idiom a third time in the narrator's own voice, where it is the
neutral bureaucratic sense — *taking the count* of the palace staff — **so that the verdict arrives
through the reader's uncertainty about which meaning is running.** See
[[Lift Up Your Head - One Idiom and Two Verdicts]].

### The last verse is the point of the chapter

> **23** Yet the chief cup bearer didn’t remember Joseph, but forgot him.

**Genesis says it twice, positively and negatively, in seven Hebrew words**: *lōʾ-zākar … wayyiškāḥēhû*.
**And *zākar* is the covenant verb.** It is what God does at 8:1 when the flood turns, at 19:29 when Lot
is pulled out, and at 30:22 when Rachel conceives — **four times in Genesis, always with God as subject,
always followed by an act in the same verse.** Here the verb is given to a human being, in the negative,
as the last word of a chapter. See [[Remembrance]] and
[[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]].

**And the chapter that begins with two men whose dreams come true in three days ends with a man waiting
two more years** (41:1). Psalm 105:19 is the only text in Scripture that says what those years were
for — *"until the time that his word happened, and Yahweh's word proved him true"* — **and Genesis
itself offers no comment at all.**

### God is named once, and Joseph is the one who names him

**Consistent with the count established at [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]]**: the eight *Yahweh*s of
chapter 39 stop dead at the chapter break, and **Genesis 40 contains one divine name, *ʾĕlōhîm*, and it
is in Joseph's mouth** (40:8). **The narrator of this chapter never mentions God.** Nothing is said to
come from God, no dream is attributed, no outcome is credited. **The only theological claim in the
chapter is made by a prisoner, as a question, to two Egyptians** — and the chapter then simply reports
that he was right.

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who acts |
|---|---|---|
| **1–4** | Two officers offend Pharaoh; the royal prison is in the captain of the guard's house; Joseph is assigned to wait on them | Pharaoh, the captain of the guard |
| **5** | Both dream, in one night, *each man according to the interpretation of his dream* | the two officers |
| **6–8** | Joseph sees their faces, asks why, and answers his own question: *"Don't interpretations belong to God?"* | Joseph |
| **9–11** | The vine, three branches, and Pharaoh's cup in his hand | the chief cupbearer |
| **12–13** | Three days; *lift up your head, and restore you to your office* | Joseph |
| **14–15** | *Remember me* — the only thing Joseph asks anyone for in the book | Joseph |
| **16–17** | Three baskets, and the birds eating out of the top one | the chief baker |
| **18–19** | Three days; *lift up your head **from off you*** | Joseph |
| **20–22** | Pharaoh's birthday; the two verdicts, *as Joseph had interpreted* | Pharaoh |
| **23** | *Didn't remember … but forgot* | the chief cupbearer |

**It is a diptych with a hinge.** Two dreams, two interpretations, two outcomes, told in matching
paragraphs — and **the hinge is verses 14–15**, the only two verses in the chapter that are about
Joseph rather than about the officers. **What he asks for there is what verse 23 withholds.**

### 40:1–4 — a prison inside a house, and the slave who waits on the king's servants

> **1** After these things, the butler of the king of Egypt and his baker offended their lord, the king
> of Egypt.
> **2** Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief cup bearer and the chief baker.
> **3** He put them in custody in the house of the captain of the guard, into the prison, the place
> where Joseph was bound.
> **4** The captain of the guard assigned them to Joseph, and he took care of them. They stayed in
> prison many days.

***"After these things."*** ***ʾAḥar haddəbārîm hāʾēlleh*** — **the same four words that open 39:7**,
and Genesis uses them the way a film uses a cut: **a new cast, no stated interval, and no causal claim.**
The formula also opens [[The Binding of Isaac]] (22:1) and the vision of
[[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] (15:1), **which is a reminder that in this book it usually precedes
something decisive.**

***"Offended their lord."*** ***Ḥāṭəʾû*** — **the ordinary word for *sinned***, the one Joseph used at
39:9 of what he would not do. **Genesis does not say what they did.** Later tradition supplies offences
(a fly in the cup, a stone in the bread, in Genesis Rabbah); **the text supplies nothing**, and the
absence matters, because **the chapter is about two men who receive opposite sentences and gives the
reader no basis for thinking either deserved his.**

***"The chief cup bearer and the chief baker."*** ***Śar hammašqîm*** and ***śar hāʾôpîm*** — **titles,
not names, and both are real Egyptian court offices.** The cupbearer (*wdpw*, and in the New Kingdom
*wbꜣ nsw*, *royal butler*) is the more significant of the two by a wide margin: **he handles what the
king drinks, which makes him the last line of defence against poison and therefore one of the most
trusted men in the palace.** Foreigners are attested in the post. **The office recurs across the ancient
Near East as a position of unusual access** — Nehemiah 1:11 is a cupbearer to a Persian king who is able
to raise a provincial matter at a private moment, and the Assyrian title *rab šāqê*, *chief cupbearer*,
reaches the Bible as the man the WEB calls **Rabshakeh** (2 Kings 18:17).

***"Into the prison, the place where Joseph was bound."*** ***Bêt hassōhar*** — **a phrase found only in
the Joseph narrative and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible**, and probably not native Hebrew at all;
*sōhar* is usually taken as an Egyptian loanword. **Genesis has a special word for this building and uses
it eight times in two chapters.**

**And it is inside Potiphar's house.** *"In the house of the captain of the guard"* — **which is the
detail 39:20 implied and this verse states**, and it puts Joseph, a slave convicted of assaulting his
owner's wife, **in the same facility as two of Pharaoh's senior household officers.** See [[Potiphar]],
whose title *śar haṭṭabbāḥîm*, *chief of the slaughterers*, is the third *śar* in three verses.

***"The captain of the guard assigned them to Joseph, and he took care of them."*** ***Wayyipqōd***,
from ***pāqad*** — **the sister verb of *zākar***, the one that enters Scripture at 21:1 when Yahweh
*visited* Sarah, and which here carries its administrative sense: *appointed, mustered, put in charge
of*. See [[Yahweh Visited Sarah - Paqad and the Visitations of God]]. **And *"took care of them"* is
*wayšāret*, minister — the exact verb of 39:4**, where Joseph *"ministered to"* Potiphar.
**He is doing the same job in the prison that he did in the house, for the third time in two chapters**,
and this time the men he waits on are two of the king's servants, one of whom waits on the king.

***"They stayed in prison many days."*** ***Yāmîm*** — an indefinite stretch, and **the chapter's first
signal that its subject is duration.** It ends on two more years.

### 40:5–8 — two dreams in one night, and the question that reframes the chapter

> **5** They both dreamed a dream, each man his dream, in one night, each man according to the
> interpretation of his dream, the cup bearer and the baker of the king of Egypt, who were bound in the
> prison.
> **6** Joseph came in to them in the morning, and saw them, and saw that they were sad.
> **7** He asked Pharaoh’s officers who were with him in custody in his master’s house, saying, “Why do
> you look so sad today?”
> **8** They said to him, “We have dreamed a dream, and there is no one who can interpret it.” Joseph
> said to them, “Don’t interpretations belong to God? Please tell it to me.”

***"They both dreamed a dream … in one night."*** **Dreams come in pairs in this cycle and Genesis says
so explicitly at the third pair**: *"the dream was doubled to Pharaoh, because the thing is established
by God, and God will shortly bring it to pass"* (41:32). **Joseph had two (37:5–11), these men have two,
Pharaoh will have two** — and the pairing here is not one man's dream twice but **two men's dreams
matched against each other**, which is a different and sharper device: **the two interpretations are
identical in form and opposite in content**, so the chapter can put restoration and execution in the
same grammar. See [[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]].

***"Each man according to the interpretation of his dream."*** **A strange clause, and it is doing real
work.** It says the dreams already had interpretations *before anyone interpreted them* — **the meaning
is a property of the dream and not of the reader.** That is the premise the whole Joseph cycle runs on
and the premise Egypt's professionals will fail on at 41:8. **Genesis is not describing a skill at
reading symbols; it is describing a message with an addressee.**

***"Saw that they were sad."*** ***Zōʿăpîm*** — a rare word for *out of sorts, haggard, stormy-faced*;
it describes Ahab going home sullen at 1 Kings 20:43 and, in its noun form, the *rage* of a king at
Proverbs 19:12. **The detail is that Joseph notices.** He is a prisoner attending two men who outrank
everyone he has met since [[Dothan]], **and the chapter opens his part with him reading faces before he
reads dreams.** See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

***"Why do you look so sad today?"*** Hebrew: ***maddûaʿ pənêkem rāʿîm hayyôm*** — literally
***"why are your faces evil today?"*** **It is the only question anyone asks in the chapter**, and it is
asked by the person with the least standing to ask it.

***"There is no one who can interpret it."*** **In a prison, this is a statement about access.** Egypt
had a professional apparatus for dream interpretation — **the Chester Beatty III dream papyrus is
essentially a two-column lookup table**, *if a man sees himself doing X, good/bad: it means Y* — and
these two men, who are courtiers, would normally have had it. **What the prison takes away is not the
dream but the expert.**

***"Don't interpretations belong to God?"*** ***Hălôʾ lēʾlōhîm pitrōnîm*** — **the theological centre of
the chapter, and it is a rhetorical question put to two Egyptians by a Hebrew slave.**

Three things are worth holding apart.

1. **He says *ʾĕlōhîm*, not *Yahweh*** — **exactly as he did to Potiphar's wife at 39:9**, and exactly
   as he will to Pharaoh at 41:16. **Joseph never speaks the covenant name to an Egyptian in the whole
   of Genesis**, and the narrator, who said *Yahweh* eight times in the previous chapter, does not say it
   here either.
2. **He denies that the skill is his and then exercises it in the next breath.** *"Please tell it to
   me"* — ***sapərû-nāʾ lî*** — follows the disclaimer immediately. **The disclaimer is not modesty and
   it is not a refusal**; it is a claim about where the answer comes from, made by the man about to give
   it. He repeats it verbatim in a throne room: *"It is not in me: God will give Pharaoh an answer of
   peace"* (41:16).
3. **It is the first thing anyone in Genesis says about how dreams work.** [[Abraham and Abimelech]]
   had the Bible's first dream at 20:3 and nobody in it needed an interpreter; the dreams of
   [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]] and [[Jacob's Flight from Laban]] are spoken instructions that mean what
   they say. **Symbolic dreams that require a reader begin here**, and so does the doctrine that the
   reading is God's to give. See [[Prophecy and Vision]].

### 40:9–15 — the vine, the cup, and the only thing Joseph ever asks for

> **9** The chief cup bearer told his dream to Joseph, and said to him, “In my dream, behold, a vine was
> in front of me,
> **10** and in the vine were three branches. It was as though it budded, it blossomed, and its clusters
> produced ripe grapes.
> **11** Pharaoh’s cup was in my hand; and I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh’s cup, and I
> gave the cup into Pharaoh’s hand.”
> **12** Joseph said to him, “This is its interpretation: the three branches are three days.
> **13** Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head, and restore you to your office. You will
> give Pharaoh’s cup into his hand, the way you did when you were his cup bearer.
> **14** But remember me when it will be well with you, and please show kindness to me, and make mention
> of me to Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house.
> **15** For indeed, I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews, and here also I have done nothing
> that they should put me into the dungeon.”

***"It was as though it budded, it blossomed, and its clusters produced ripe grapes."*** **Three stages
of a growing season compressed into one clause** — *pōraḥat*, *ʿālətâ niṣṣāh*, *hibšîlû* — **which is
how the dream encodes time**, and Joseph reads the three branches as three days rather than three years
because the vine has already told him the interval is short. **Compare 41:26, where seven cows are seven
years.** The unit is supplied by the dream, not by a rule.

***"I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh's cup."*** **Not how anybody makes wine**, then or
now: Egyptian wine came from a treading-floor and a fermentation jar, and **squeezing fresh grapes
straight into a cup is dream logic** — the job done in one motion, from vine to the king's hand.
**Some readers take it as evidence of a fresh-juice drink; the simpler account is that dreams
telescope.** What the detail does narratively is put **the cup in the king's hand as the dream's final
image**, which is the office itself. See [[The Vine and the Cup]] for what Scripture does with the
figure elsewhere — **this is the one cup in the Bible that is neither wrath nor covenant, just a job.**

***"Pharaoh will lift up your head, and restore you to your office."*** ***Yiśśāʾ parʿōh ʾet-rōʾšekā***
— **the sense here is a man brought out of disgrace and counted again among the staff**, which is
precisely the sense of the phrase in **the last two verses of 2 Kings**, where Evil-Merodach *"lifted up
the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah out of prison"* (2 Kings 25:27; Jeremiah 52:31). **Both texts are
about a prisoner restored to a foreign king's table**, and the Kings verse is the note the history of
Judah ends on. See [[Lift Up Your Head - One Idiom and Two Verdicts]].

***"Remember me when it will be well with you."*** ***Zəkartanî*** — **and then, three words later,
*wəhizkartanî*, the causative: *cause Pharaoh to remember me.* The verb twice in one verse, in two
stems.** Joseph asks for **three things in ascending order**: be remembered, be treated with ***ḥesed***,
be got out. ***Ḥesed*** is **covenant loyalty, the word the Old Testament reserves for what God is toward
Israel and what family members owe each other** — and **Joseph asks an Egyptian official for it.** See
[[Favor and Grace]].

**This is the only request Joseph makes of anyone in the book.** He does not ask his brothers for
anything at the cistern (the plea is reported only at 42:21, by them), he does not answer Potiphar's
wife's charge, and he asks Pharaoh for nothing at all. **Fourteen verses out of a fourteen-chapter cycle
have him trying to get himself out, and they fail.**

**Whether that failure is a rebuke is the oldest disputed question about this chapter.**

- **The devotional reading, which is old and widespread**: Joseph trusted an Egyptian rather than God,
  and the two extra years are the correction. **Genesis Rabbah reads the two years of 41:1 as two years
  added for the two forms of the verb in 40:14.**
- **The reading the text supports**: **the chapter passes no verdict whatever.** No narrator's comment,
  no divine speech, no consequence named as a consequence. **A wrongly imprisoned man asks a man with
  access to the king to mention his case**, which is what any competent person would do, and
  **Genesis 40:23 blames the cupbearer, not Joseph** — the verse's subject is the one who forgot.

**The wiki sets out both and adopts neither**, which is the same posture it took on
*"did Potiphar believe his wife?"* in the previous chapter.

***"I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews."*** **Two remarkable things in nine words.**

**First: *gunnōb gunnabtî*, an emphatic passive — *I was indeed stolen*.** ***Gānab*** is **the verb of
Exodus 21:16, which makes kidnapping a capital crime**, and Joseph uses it about his own brothers —
**without naming them, without saying who did it, and in a grammatical form that removes the agent
entirely.** **He tells the truth and withholds the family.** Nobody in Egypt learns he has brothers until
he tells them himself at 45:4.

**Second: *ʾereṣ hāʿibrîm*, *the land of the Hebrews*, occurs here and nowhere else in the Bible.**
**It is also Joseph's own answer to 39:14**, where Potiphar's wife spat *"a Hebrew"* at him as an insult.
**He takes the word she used as a slur and makes it the name of a country** — and there is no such
country: [[Canaan]] is not called this anywhere else, by anyone. See
[[Abram the Hebrew - The First Use of the Name]].

***"They should put me into the dungeon."*** ***Habbôr*** — **and this is the word the English hides.**
*Bôr* is **the cistern his brothers threw him into at 37:24**, *"the pit was empty, there was no water in
it"*, and it is what Joseph calls the Egyptian prison. **The WEB says *pit* there and *dungeon* here;
the Hebrew says the same word both times**, and 41:14 will use it a third time when he is brought out of
it. **Joseph's own name for thirteen years of his life is *the pit*.**

**And it is the only time in Genesis that Joseph protests his innocence.** He said nothing after the
accusation in chapter 39. **He says it here, to a cupbearer, and the phrasing is careful:** *"here also
I have done nothing"* — ***gam-pōh*** — **which quietly claims he had done nothing in the other place
either, without relitigating it.**

### 40:16–19 — the same sentence, with three words added

> **16** When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was good, he said to Joseph, “I also was in my
> dream, and behold, three baskets of white bread were on my head.
> **17** In the uppermost basket there were all kinds of baked food for Pharaoh, and the birds ate them
> out of the basket on my head.”
> **18** Joseph answered, “This is its interpretation. The three baskets are three days.
> **19** Within three more days, Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you, and will hang you on a
> tree; and the birds will eat your flesh from off you.”

***"When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was good."*** **The most human sentence in the
chapter, and the cruellest.** He volunteers *because the first answer was favourable* — **and Genesis
tells the reader his motive before it tells him his sentence.** *"I also"* — ***ʾap-ʾănî*** — is the
sound of a man joining in.

***"Three baskets of white bread."*** ***Sallê ḥōrî***, uncertain: *white bread* (from *ḥāwar*, to be
white), or *wicker/openwork baskets*. **Egyptian tomb reliefs show servants carrying stacked baskets on
the head**, exactly as described, and the detail is one of several in the Joseph cycle that reads as
though the writer had seen an Egyptian kitchen.

***"The birds ate them out of the basket on my head."*** **The one element in either dream that is not
simply the job being done.** The cupbearer's dream is his duty performed; **the baker's is his duty
interrupted by scavengers**, and it is already the interpretation before Joseph gives it. **Birds eating
a body is Scripture's standard image of a death without burial** — the threat of Deuteronomy 28:26,
Goliath's boast at 1 Samuel 17:44, Jeremiah 7:33, and the vulture-summons of Revelation 19:17–18. See
[[Burial and the Grave]].

***"Lift up your head from off you."*** ***Yiśśāʾ parʿōh ʾet-rōʾšəkā mēʿāleykā*** — **verse 13 with two
Hebrew words added, and the addition turns a promotion into a decapitation.** Joseph gives the two men
**the same opening formula, the same three-day count, and the same royal subject**, and lets the
difference fall on a preposition. **He does not soften it, delay it, or decline to give it.**

***"And will hang you on a tree."*** ***Tālâ ʿal-ʿēṣ*** — **probably impalement or the public exposure
of an already-executed body rather than hanging by the neck**, which is how the practice appears
throughout the Old Testament. **The phrase is the seed of a substantial biblical thread**:
Deuteronomy 21:22–23 requires such a body to be taken down before nightfall *"for he who is hanged is
accursed of God"*, **and Paul quotes that verse of the crucifixion at Galatians 3:13**. See
[[Hanged on a Tree - Genesis 40, Deuteronomy 21, and Galatians 3]].

**The baker says nothing after this.** **Genesis gives him no reply, no protest, and no death scene** —
he is disposed of in half a verse at 40:22.

### 40:20–22 — a birthday, and the idiom used a third time

> **20** On the third day, which was Pharaoh’s birthday, he made a feast for all his servants, and he
> lifted up the head of the chief cup bearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants.
> **21** He restored the chief cup bearer to his position again, and he gave the cup into Pharaoh’s hand;
> **22** but he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them.

***"On the third day."*** **The interval Joseph named, met exactly**, and **the first of the Bible's
third days to carry a verdict on it.** Genesis has used the phrase before — Abraham *"saw the place
afar off"* on the third day at 22:4 — and it runs forward to Exodus 19:11, Hosea 6:2's *"on the third
day he will raise us up"*, Jonah 1:17, and 1 Corinthians 15:4. **Here it is simply a deadline that
holds**, and what it authenticates is the interpreter.

***"Which was Pharaoh's birthday."*** ***Yôm hulledet ʾet-parʿōh*** — **the only explicit birthday
celebration in the Hebrew Bible.** (Job 1:4's feasts are held *"on his day"*, and whether that means a
birthday is a reading rather than a translation; see [[Job of Uz]].) **The Bible's other birthday party
is Herod's**, and a head comes off at that one too — **which is the sort of coincidence that is worth
recording precisely because neither text makes anything of it.** See
[[The Two Birthdays in the Bible - Pharaoh's Feast and Herod's]].

***"He lifted up the head of the chief cup bearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants."***
**The third use, and here the idiom means neither of the things Joseph made it mean.** ***Nāśāʾ rōʾš***
with a population and the preposition *bətôk*, *among*, **is the census idiom** — *take the sum of the
congregation* (Exodus 30:12; Numbers 1:2). **Pharaoh reviews his staff at his birthday feast and both
men are counted back in**, and only then does the verse split them. **Genesis holds the ambiguity for
one more clause than it needs to.**

***"He gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand."*** **Quoted from the dream** (40:11) **and from the
interpretation** (40:13) — **three times in eleven verses, in the same words.** The fulfilment is
reported in the dreamer's own vocabulary.

***"As Joseph had interpreted to them."*** **The chapter's verdict on Joseph, and it is one clause
long.** No praise, no acknowledgement, and nobody in Egypt is recorded as noticing. **The cupbearer will
eventually repeat this sentence almost exactly** — *"as he interpreted to us, so it was"* (41:13) — **two
years late.**

### 40:23 — the last verse

> **23** Yet the chief cup bearer didn’t remember Joseph, but forgot him.

***"Didn't remember … but forgot."*** ***Wəlōʾ-zākar … wayyiškāḥēhû.*** **A negative and its positive
twin, in a language that does not usually bother**, and the doubling is the point: **this is not an
oversight the narrator is excusing, it is a failure stated twice.**

**Against the verb's career in this book**: *"God remembered Noah"* (8:1) and the waters subside;
*"God remembered Abraham"* (19:29) and Lot is pulled out; *"God remembered Rachel"* (30:22) and her womb
opens. **Genesis has established that remembering is an act with a consequence in the next clause.**
**Here a man is asked to perform that act, agrees to nothing, and does not.** See [[Remembrance]].

**It is also, in the Septuagint, the first *"remember me"* in Scripture** — ***mnēsthēti mou*** — and it
is the same phrase the dying man beside Jesus uses at Luke 23:42. **Two condemned men, two verdicts, one
restored and one not, and a request to be remembered by the one who lives.** See
[[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]].

**And Genesis passes no judgment on the cupbearer either.** 41:9 will let him call it *"my faults"* —
***ḥăṭāʾay***, **the same root as the officers' offence in 40:1** — **so the chapter that opens with two
men sinning against a king closes with a man sinning against a slave, and the book waits two years and
two verses to say so.**

## People

- [[Joseph]] — **the chapter where he acquires the thing the rest of his life runs on.** He waits on two
  officers, notices their faces, asks the only question in the chapter, denies that interpretation is
  his and does it anyway, and **gives a death sentence without softening it.** He makes **the only
  request he makes of anyone in Genesis** (40:14), **protests his innocence for the only time in the
  book** (40:15), **calls his prison *the pit*** — the word for his brothers' cistern — **and names his
  country *the land of the Hebrews*, a phrase found nowhere else in Scripture.** He is right about
  everything and it buys him two more years
- [[The Chief Cupbearer]] — **new page.** *Śar hammašqîm*, one of the most trusted offices in an
  Egyptian palace. **He dreams his own job, is restored on the third day exactly as told, and forgets
  the man who told him** — and then, two years later, is **the only person in Genesis who gets Joseph
  in front of Pharaoh** (41:9–13). **Genesis gives him no name and no motive**, and lets him call his
  silence *"my faults"* himself
- [[The Chief Baker]] — **new page.** *Śar hāʾôpîm*. **He volunteers his dream because the first answer
  was good**, hears the same sentence with two words added, and **is never given another line.** He is
  hanged in half a verse. **The chapter's whole demonstration that the interpretations are true depends
  on him**, and Scripture never mentions him again
- [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] — **still an institution rather than a person, and for the first time
  he acts.** He is angry, he imprisons, he holds a birthday feast, and he issues two opposite verdicts in
  one sentence. **He does not appear, speak, or know that anyone in his prison predicted them.** The
  cupbearer he restores is the man who will say Joseph's name to him
- [[Potiphar]] — **present only as a title.** *"The captain of the guard"* (40:3, 4) is the office
  39:1 gave him, and **40:3 states outright what 39:20 implied: the royal prison is inside his house.**
  Whether the man who assigns Joseph to wait on the king's officers is the same man who put him there
  for assaulting his wife — and what that would mean — **is the ambiguity of 39:19 seen from the other
  side.** Genesis does not use his name here
- **The keeper of the prison** — **no page**, per the standing practice, and note that **he is absent
  from this chapter**: 39:22–23 put the prison in Joseph's hand, and 40:3–4 have *the captain of the
  guard* assign the prisoners. **The two chapters describe the same arrangement through two different
  officials**
- [[God]] — **named once, as *ʾĕlōhîm*, in a rhetorical question asked by a prisoner** (40:8). **He does
  not speak, appear, or act in the chapter**, and the narrator never mentions him. **Everything that
  happens is nonetheless claimed for him by the one line anyone says about him**

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — **the chapter is the closest look Scripture ever takes at the inside of an Egyptian
  court.** Two named household offices, a royal detention facility inside a minister's residence, a
  birthday feast for the whole staff, a muster of servants, an execution by exposure, and a dream culture
  with professionals in it. **The word *Egypt* itself occurs three times, always as *the king of Egypt***
  (40:1 twice, 40:5)
- [[Canaan]] — **called *"the land of the Hebrews"* (40:15), which is the only occurrence of that phrase
  in the Bible.** It is Joseph's own name for home, made out of the word an Egyptian woman used against
  him as a slur one chapter earlier

## Themes

- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — **the Bible's first symbolic dreams, and the first statement of how they
  work.** *"Don't interpretations belong to God?"* (40:8) is **the doctrine, and the verbs *pātar* and
  *pitrôn* occur only in Genesis 40–41 in the whole Hebrew Bible.** Dreams up to now have been speech
  (20:3; 28:13; 31:24) or self-evident (37:5–11); **these require a reader, and the reading is God's to
  give**
- [[Remembrance]] — **the theme's negative, and its first human petition.** *Zākar* twice in 40:14 and
  once, negated and then doubled by *šākaḥ*, in 40:23. **In Genesis the verb has had God as its subject
  four times and an act in the following clause every time**; here it is handed to a human being who
  does nothing with it
- [[Wisdom]] — **competence as a gift that is disclaimed in the act of using it.** Joseph is the first
  of the Bible's foreign-court wise men and the pattern-setter for Daniel: the outsider who reads what
  the professionals cannot, **and who says the reading is not his**
- [[Hope]] — **a man asks to be got out, is right about everything, and waits two more years.**
  Psalm 105:19 is the only text that says what the interval was for, and **Genesis 40 says nothing**
- [[Suffering]] — **innocence stated once, to an audience of one, and never cleared.** *"Here also I
  have done nothing"* (40:15) is Joseph's only protest in the book
- [[Judgment]] — **two sentences delivered in identical grammar, and the offence that earned either is
  never stated.** A king's anger, no hearing, and a verdict announced at a party
- [[Life and Death]] — **the chapter's two dreams are a job and a corpse**, given on the same night in
  the same room, and distinguished by three Hebrew words
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — **the first interior view of a royal household in Scripture**: officers with
  titles, a court that imprisons at the king's displeasure, and a birthday muster at which men are
  restored and killed by the same idiom
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — **Joseph waits on the men who wait on Pharaoh.** *Wayšāret*, the verb of
  39:4, used a second time of a man with no standing, and **the chapter's plot turns on whether a
  restored courtier will mention a slave's name**
- [[Favor and Grace]] — ***ḥesed*** asked of an Egyptian official (40:14) — **covenant loyalty, requested
  horizontally, and not given**
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — **Joseph asks for an advocate and does not get one**; 41:9–13 is the
  advocacy arriving two years late, and from a man admitting a fault
- [[Language and Speech]] — **a chapter built on a pun that decides who lives.** *Nāśāʾ rōʾš* three
  times in eight verses, in three senses
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — **Joseph reads two faces before he reads two dreams** (40:6), and the man
  he reads correctly does not see him at all
- [[Sin]] — ***ḥāṭāʾ*** at 40:1 of two officers against a king, and at 41:9 of the cupbearer against
  Joseph — **the same root at both ends of the episode**
- [[Work]] — **both dreams are about doing the job**, and the cupbearer's restoration is described
  entirely as the resumption of a task
- [[The Nations]] — **Egyptians receive true revelation in their sleep**, as
  [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] did at 20:3 and [[Laban]] did at 31:24, **and need an Israelite to
  read it**

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *The Cupbearer and the Baker —
  Genesis 40:1–23*, and **the chapter is a self-contained unit at both ends**: 40:1 opens *"after these
  things"* with an entirely new cast, and **41:1's *"after two full years"* is an explicit time-skip.**
  A merger with chapter 41 was considered and rejected — the two chapters are one plot and **the natural
  join is tempting**, because 40:23 is a cliffhanger the next chapter resolves. **But 40 is a complete
  demonstration in itself** (prediction, three-day interval, fulfilment, verdict), it is 23 verses
  against 41's 57, and **the two-year gap is the one boundary marker Genesis states in words.**
- **On the title, kept from the plan.** *The Cupbearer and the Baker* names the two characters whose
  fates are the chapter's content and is where a reader would look. **Alternatives considered and
  rejected**: *Joseph Interprets Two Dreams* (puts the interpreter at the centre of a chapter whose last
  verse is about him being forgotten); *Two Dreams in Prison* (accurate and colourless); *Don't
  Interpretations Belong to God* (a phrase-title, which this wiki reserves for connection pages).
  **No other book has a passage page by this title**, so no `(Book)` qualifier is needed.
- **On what was created, and the judgment calls in it.**
  - **[[The Chief Cupbearer]] and [[The Chief Baker]] both got pages**, against the wiki's standing
    practice of **not** creating pages for unnamed single-scene functionaries (the keeper of the prison,
    the midwife of 35:17, the man in the field of 37:15). **The practice does not fit these two.** Both
    have **recorded direct speech**, both have **dreams reported at length in the first person**, the
    chapter's entire content is **what happens to them**, and **the cupbearer recurs at 41:9–13 as the
    one person who moves Joseph from the prison to the throne room.** The baker's page is short and
    exists because **the chapter's demonstration needs both halves**: an interpretation that comes true
    only when it is favourable proves nothing.
  - **Titled by office, with no period qualifier.** *Pharaoh* and *Abimelech* take *(of X's time)*
    because Genesis narrates several of each. **These two offices are held once in Scripture by an
    unnamed man**, and the one other biblical *chief cupbearer* — the Assyrian *rab šāqê* of
    2 Kings 18:17 — **reaches the English Bible as a proper name, *Rabshakeh***, so no filename
    collision is foreseeable. **If one ever arises, the rule in `AGENTS.md` applies and both pages get
    qualified in the pass that creates the second.**
  - **Five connection pages, which is more than a usual pass.** Each is **a distinct verbal thread that
    starts in this chapter**, and four of them are word-level rather than thematic:
    [[Do Not Interpretations Belong to God - Joseph and Daniel]] (*pātar*/*pəšar*),
    [[Lift Up Your Head - One Idiom and Two Verdicts]] (*nāśāʾ rōʾš*),
    [[Hanged on a Tree - Genesis 40, Deuteronomy 21, and Galatians 3]] (*tālâ ʿal-ʿēṣ*), and
    [[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]] (*zākar* / *mnēsthēti mou*).
    **The fifth**, [[The Two Birthdays in the Bible - Pharaoh's Feast and Herod's]], **is a coincidence
    rather than a thread and the page says so in its first line** — it exists because the observation
    is genuinely striking and because a reader who notices it should find the wiki has already checked
    it rather than assuming it is a claim.
- **On what was deliberately not created.**
  - **No `Dreams` theme page.** [[Prophecy and Vision]] already tracks every dream in Genesis by verse
    and defines itself as *"God putting words in a human mouth, and showing a human being something"*.
    **Splitting dreams out would divide one body of material across two pages** at exactly the point
    where the cycle makes dreams the dominant mode. The theme page took a long keyed entry instead.
  - **No page for the keeper of the prison**, who does not appear in this chapter at all — see the
    People section, where the 39:22–23 / 40:3–4 discrepancy is set out.
  - **No connection page for the Egyptian dream manuals** (Chester Beatty III and its kin). **The
    parallel is real and is noted on this page**, but `AGENTS.md` defines a connection page as a link
    *within* Scripture, and **a shared cultural practice is not a dependence claim in either
    direction** — the same reasoning that kept the *Tale of Two Brothers* off the wiki in the previous
    pericope.
- **An ambiguity flagged and not resolved: is 40:14 a failure of faith?** The devotional tradition
  reads Joseph's request as self-help that God punished with two extra years, and Genesis Rabbah ties
  the two years of 41:1 to the two forms of *zākar* in 40:14. **The text says none of this.** There is
  no narratorial comment, no divine speech, and **40:23's subject is the cupbearer, not Joseph.**
  **Both readings are set out above and neither is adopted.**
- **A second ambiguity: who is in charge of the prison?** 39:22–23 gives the whole facility into
  Joseph's hand and says *the keeper of the prison* stopped looking at anything. **40:3–4 has *the
  captain of the guard* place the officers and assign Joseph to them**, with no keeper mentioned.
  **The two accounts are compatible** — a keeper running the building under the minister who owns it —
  **and they are also the sort of seam that could mark two hands.** The wiki records the observation
  and argues nothing from it, as it did with the Ishmaelites and Midianites at 37:28 and 39:1.
