# Jacob, Leah, and Rachel

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 29:1–30

## Summary

**God is not in this passage.**

Not once in thirty verses. **No speech, no dream, no altar, no vow, no prayer, and not one occurrence
of *Yahweh* or *ʾĕlōhîm*** — in the chapter immediately after the night God promised *"I am with you,
and will keep you, wherever you go"* (28:15). The next verse, 29:31, opens ***"Yahweh saw"***, and the
whole birth sequence runs on it. **Genesis puts the silence exactly here**, over the fourteen years
the promise costs, and says nothing about it.

**What happens instead is that the technique of chapter 27 is run back on the man who used it.** An
elder sibling is passed off as a younger one, in the dark, to a man who cannot see who he is dealing
with, and the deceived party wakes up, discovers it, protests, and is told the outcome stands. **Jacob
asked for *"your younger daughter"* and was handed the firstborn**, and the sentence that explains it
to him — ***"It is not done so in our place, to give the younger before the firstborn"*** — contains
the two nouns of the oracle spoken over him before he was born. **Laban is not being ironic. He is
explaining a house rule.** See [[The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban]].

**And the passage is built on a symmetry the wiki can state exactly.** Two arrivals at the same well,
in the same family, twenty years apart:

- [[A Wife for Isaac]] — a servant arrives with **ten camels of gifts**, prays, is answered before he
  has finished asking, watches a woman do the heavy work, and leaves with a bride the next morning.
- Here — a fugitive arrives with **nothing the text names**, does not pray, does the heavy work
  himself, and pays for the bride with **seven years, and then seven more.**

**Jacob has no *mōhar*, so his body is the *mōhar*.** That is the plot, and it is why the chapter is
about labour: *serve* — ***ʿābad*** — occurs **seven times** in verses 15–30, and the man doing it is
the one told at 27:29 that nations would serve him.

### 29:1–3 — a well he does not have to find

> **1** Then Jacob went on his journey, and came to the land of the children of the east.
> **2** He looked, and behold, a well in the field, and, behold, three flocks of sheep lying there by
> it. For out of that well they watered the flocks. The stone on the well’s mouth was large.
> **3** There all the flocks were gathered. They rolled the stone from the well’s mouth, and watered
> the sheep, and put the stone again on the well’s mouth in its place.

***"Then Jacob went on his journey."*** Literally *he lifted his feet* — ***wayyiśśāʾ yaʿăqōb
raglāyw***, an idiom that occurs here and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. **The last time Genesis
put a verb of lifting next to Jacob it was *wayyipgaʿ*, he ran into the place** (28:11): stumbled onto
a sanctuary in the dark. **Here he lifts his own feet and walks**, and the difference in register is
the whole distance between the two chapters.

***"The land of the children of the east."*** ***ʾEreṣ bənê-qedem*** — the phrase occurs **once in
Genesis, here**, and the wiki's other instance of it is [[Job (person)]], *"the greatest of all the
children of the east"* (Job 1:3). It is a wide, vague designation for the country beyond the Jordan
and up toward the Euphrates, and **Genesis is content to leave it wide**: 28:5 sent him to *Paddan
Aram*, 28:10 had him walking toward *Haran*, and 29:1 uses neither. **Three registers for one
destination in nine verses.** See [[Aram Naharaim]] and [[Haran (city)]].

***"Behold … and behold."*** ***Wayyarʾ wəhinnēh … wəhinnēh.*** The narrator hands us Jacob's eyes:
**a well, then the flocks, then — as the last and heaviest item — the stone.** The verse is built so
that the obstacle arrives at the end of it.

***"The stone on the well's mouth was large."*** **This is the one piece of stage furniture the
passage explains**, and verse 3 explains it twice: the flocks gather, *they* roll it, *they* water,
*they* put it back. **The plural is the point.** It is a communal fixture requiring a shift of men,
and it is put back each time — a well with a lid on it, in a country where a covered well means
ownership and rationing. **Genesis has been telling well stories for three chapters** — the servant at
the spring in 24, the six wells of [[Isaac in Gerar]], the quarrels at Esek and Sitnah — and this is
the first one nobody fights over. See [[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].

**And the stone is also the third stone in nine verses.** A stone under his head at 28:11, a stone
standing up as God's house at 28:18, and a stone on a well's mouth at 29:2. **Genesis does not connect
them and the wiki does not either**; it notes only that the man who anointed one rock at dawn moves
another one by himself before the day is out.

### 29:4–8 — the shepherds who will not

> **4** Jacob said to them, “My relatives, where are you from?” They said, “We are from Haran.”
> **5** He said to them, “Do you know Laban, the son of Nahor?” They said, “We know him.”
> **6** He said to them, “Is it well with him?” They said, “It is well. See, Rachel, his daughter, is
> coming with the sheep.”
> **7** He said, “Behold, it is still the middle of the day, not time to gather the livestock
> together. Water the sheep, and go and feed them.”
> **8** They said, “We can’t, until all the flocks are gathered together, and they roll the stone from
> the well’s mouth. Then we water the sheep.”

**Four questions and four answers, and the third answer stops being an answer halfway through.** He
asks whether Laban is well; they begin *"It is well"* and then break off to point: ***"See, Rachel,
his daughter, is coming with the sheep."*** **The type-scene delivers the woman inside a sentence
about something else**, exactly as Genesis 24:15 delivered Rebekah *"before he had finished speaking"*.
See [[The Betrothal at the Well]].

***"Laban, the son of Nahor."*** **This is not what the book has said elsewhere and the wiki flags it
rather than smoothing it.** Genesis 22:22–23 makes [[Bethuel]] the son of [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]]
and Rebekah the daughter of Bethuel; 24:24 has Rebekah introduce herself as *"the daughter of Bethuel
the son of Milcah, whom she bore to Nahor"*; 24:29 makes Laban her brother; and 28:2 sends Jacob to
*"Bethuel your mother's father."* **On the book's own genealogy Laban is Nahor's great-grandson, not
his son.**

The ordinary resolution is that ***bēn*** covers descendant as well as son, which the Bible does
constantly — *Jehu son of Nimshi* is Nimshi's grandson (2 Kings 9:2, 9:20), and 28:13 has just called
Abraham Jacob's ***ʾāb*** when he is his grandfather. **The other reading is that the household is
being identified by its head of line rather than by its living father**, which is how a stranger
asking directions would be answered anyway. **Neither requires an error and the wiki adopts neither**;
it records that Genesis uses two different filiations for the same man five chapters apart and offers
no gloss.

***"My relatives."*** ***ʾAḥay*** — *my brothers* — used of total strangers, which is ordinary
politeness. **It is also the word the whole chapter turns on.** Laban will use it twice: *"Surely you
are my bone and my flesh"* (29:14) and *"Because you are my **brother**, should you therefore serve me
for nothing?"* (29:15). **Kinship is asserted four times in this passage and is invoiced once.**

***"It is still the middle of the day."*** **His first act in Mesopotamia is to tell other men their
business.** The shepherds are lying down at noon with the flocks unwatered, and Jacob — who has been in
the country for one scene — corrects their work practice. **They answer with the rule, twice, patiently
and without taking offence**: *"We can't, until all the flocks are gathered."* And then he does exactly
what they have just told him cannot be done. **Genesis records the exchange and passes no comment**;
whether he is capable, high-handed, or simply in a hurry is left standing.

### 29:9–12 — the stone, the kiss, and the weeping

> **9** While he was yet speaking with them, Rachel came with her father’s sheep, for she kept them.
> **10** When Jacob saw Rachel the daughter of Laban, his mother’s brother, and the sheep of Laban,
> his mother’s brother, Jacob went near, and rolled the stone from the well’s mouth, and watered the
> flock of Laban his mother’s brother.
> **11** Jacob kissed Rachel, and lifted up his voice, and wept.
> **12** Jacob told Rachel that he was her father’s brother, and that he was Rebekah’s son. She ran
> and told her father.

***"For she kept them."*** ***Kî rōʿâ hîʾ*** — **she is a shepherd, and the participle is feminine.**
Genesis says it in three words and never mentions it again, and it is the reason she is at the well
at all. **Her name is the ordinary Hebrew for a ewe** — ***rāḥēl***, the word of Isaiah 53:7 — so the
book has introduced a woman called *ewe* driving sheep. See [[Rachel]].

***"His mother's brother", three times in one verse.*** ***ʾĂḥî ʾimmô***, at the daughter, at the
sheep, and at the flock. **Nothing else in the verse is repeated and the repetition is not required by
the grammar.** The narrator hammers the kinship at the exact moment Jacob performs the feat — **this
is family property, and he is working for it before anyone has asked him to.** The whole of the next
twenty years is in the sentence.

***"Rolled the stone from the well's mouth."*** ***Wayyāgel*** — the verb of verse 3, where it took
all the flocks and all the shepherds. **He does alone what the passage has twice established requires
a crowd.** Genesis does not call it a miracle, does not say he was strong, and gives no reaction from
the men standing there. **It is the only feat of strength attributed to any patriarch**, and its
counterpart in the type-scene is Moses driving off the shepherds at the well in Midian (Exodus
2:17) — where the woman is likewise served rather than serving. **Genesis 24 is the outlier**: there,
[[Rebekah]] watered ten camels and the man watched. See [[The Betrothal at the Well]].

***"And watered the flock."*** **He waters before he speaks.** The introduction at verse 12 comes
after the labour, which means Rachel has a stranger kiss her at verse 11 before she is told who he is.
**Genesis reports it in that order and does not soften it.**

***"Jacob kissed Rachel, and lifted up his voice, and wept."*** Two things, both checkable against
the book.

- ***Wayyiššaq*** — **this is the second kiss in Genesis, and Jacob gave the first one too.** At 27:27
  he came near and kissed his father while wearing his brother's clothes; the kiss was part of the
  fraud, and it is what let Isaac smell the field on the borrowed coat. **His first kiss was
  evidence; his second is relief.** Laban kisses him two verses later, and thereafter the book fills
  with kisses — Laban's grandchildren at 31:55, Esau's neck at 33:4, Joseph and his brothers at
  45:15, Jacob's blind embrace of Ephraim and Manasseh at 48:10.
- ***Wayyiśśāʾ ʾet-qōlô wayyēbk*** — *lifted up his voice and wept* — **the third and last occurrence
  of the formula in Genesis, and the first that is not grief.** [[Hagar]] used it in the desert with a
  dying child (21:16); [[Esau]] used it at his father's bedside after this man took his blessing
  (27:38). **Jacob uses it at a well, having found the house he was sent to.** The book gives the
  identical sentence to the two people he has most cost and then to him.

***"That he was her father's brother, and that he was Rebekah's son."*** ***ʾĂḥî ʾābîhā*** again —
*brother* for *nephew*, the loose usage of verse 5 applied to himself. **And the credential he leads
with is his mother.** [[Rebekah]] left this house at 24:58 with a one-word answer — *"I will go"* —
and has not been seen in it for a generation; **the son she saved by sending him away introduces
himself by her name**, in the last appearance her name makes in the Jacob narrative. **She dies
off-stage.** Genesis never records her death, never brings Jacob home to her, and does not mention her
again except as an interred body at 49:31.

***"She ran and told her father."*** In Genesis 24:28 Rebekah *"ran, and told her mother's house."*
**Same verb, same errand, different parent** — and the difference is a household in which [[Bethuel]]
is no longer mentioned at all.

### 29:13–14 — the run, and the month

> **13** When Laban heard the news of Jacob, his sister’s son, he ran to meet Jacob, and embraced him,
> and kissed him, and brought him to his house. Jacob told Laban all these things.
> **14** Laban said to him, “Surely you are my bone and my flesh.” He stayed with him for a month.

**Laban runs, exactly as he ran at 24:29 — and this time the narrator does not explain why.** Genesis
24 gave the causal clause immediately and put it in the order that matters: *"when he saw the ring,
and the bracelets on his sister's hands, and when he heard the words."* **Here there is no ring.**
There is a report and a penniless nephew, and Laban runs anyway, embraces, kisses, and takes him home.
**The wiki notes that the withheld motive is withheld**, and that the equivalent sentence arrives two
verses later in the form of a question about wages. See [[Laban]] and [[Hospitality]].

***"Jacob told Laban all these things."*** ***Kol-haddəbārîm hāʾēlleh.*** **Genesis does not say what
they were.** Whether he told him about the birthright, the blessing, the death threat, or the
stairway — or how much of it — is exactly the information the reader wants and does not get. **The
book withholds Jacob's own account of himself here and again at 31:31**, where he finally gives one
under pressure.

***"Surely you are my bone and my flesh."*** ***ʾAk ʿaṣmî ûbśārî ʾāttâ.*** **This is the second
occurrence of the idiom in the Bible.** The first is Adam's, at 2:23, of the woman built from his
side: *"This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh."* **The formula of the first marriage is
spoken here by a man about his nephew**, and it is the last warm thing he says before the accounting
starts. It becomes Israel's standard clause of kinship-obligation — the tribes to David at 2 Samuel
5:1, Abimelech's relatives at Judges 9:2 — a claim on someone rather than an affection for them. See
[[Marriage]] and [[One Flesh - Genesis 2 and the New Testament on Marriage]].

***"He stayed with him for a month."*** ***Ḥōdeš yāmîm*** — *a month of days.* **The one interval in
the passage that costs nothing**, and Genesis measures it precisely so that verse 15 can end it.

### 29:15–20 — the price, named by the buyer

> **15** Laban said to Jacob, “Because you are my brother, should you therefore serve me for nothing?
> Tell me, what will your wages be?”
> **16** Laban had two daughters. The name of the elder was Leah, and the name of the younger was
> Rachel.
> **17** Leah’s eyes were weak, but Rachel was beautiful in form and attractive.
> **18** Jacob loved Rachel. He said, “I will serve you seven years for Rachel, your younger
> daughter.”
> **19** Laban said, “It is better that I give her to you, than that I should give her to another man.
> Stay with me.”
> **20** Jacob served seven years for Rachel. They seemed to him but a few days, for the love he had
> for her.

***"Because you are my brother, should you therefore serve me for nothing?"*** **It is generosity on
its face and a contract underneath.** Laban has just had a month of unpaid labour out of a relative;
the sentence that appears to renounce it is the sentence that converts the relative into an employee.
**And it is Jacob who has to name the figure** — *"Tell me, what will your wages be?"* **The man who
made his brother name a price at 25:31, and then required an oath on it, is asked to name his own.**

**Then the narrator stops the scene to supply two facts, in the order a bargainer would want them.**
Verse 16 gives the daughters and their order; verse 17 gives their looks. **The information is
positioned between Laban's question and Jacob's answer**, so that the reader knows the shape of the
trade before the buyer speaks.

***"Leah's eyes were weak."*** ***Wəʿênê lēʾâ rakkôt.*** ***Rak*** is *soft, tender, delicate* — the
word for tender meat at 18:7 and for a delicate child at Deuteronomy 28:56. **It is not obviously a
defect**, and the readings divide:

- **A weakness or dullness of sight.** The Septuagint has ***ἀσθενεῖς***, *weak*, and the WEB follows
  it. On this reading the verse says one sister was hard to marry off and the other was not, which is
  what the plot needs.
- **A compliment, offered and then outbid.** *Soft eyes* against *beautiful in form and appearance* —
  the one attractive thing about Leah, set beside everything about Rachel. Several Jewish commentators
  read it this way, and the Hebrew adjective supports it.

**The wiki records both and adopts neither.** What is not in doubt is the structure of the sentence:
**Leah gets a clause about one feature, Rachel gets a clause about all of her**, and the conjunction
between them is adversative. See [[Leah]].

***"Jacob loved Rachel."*** ***Wayyeʾĕhab yaʿăqōb ʾet-rāḥēl.*** **This is the first time in the Bible
a man is said to love a woman before marrying her.** The verb's first occurrence in Scripture is
22:2 — *"your son, your only son, whom you love"* — and its only previous use of a wife is 24:67,
where *"Isaac … took Rebekah, and she became his wife. He loved her"*, **in that order**. Here the
order reverses, and it costs fourteen years. See [[Love]].

***"I will serve you seven years for Rachel."*** **He names the price and nobody haggles.** The
institution behind it is the ***mōhar***, the bride-payment a groom's household makes to the bride's
(Exodus 22:16–17; Deuteronomy 22:29 sets it at fifty shekels for one case), and **Jacob has nothing.**
His father's bride was fetched with ten camels of goods, silver, gold, and clothing (24:53); he
himself was sent out of Beersheba with, so far as the text says, nothing at all. **So he pays in
years.** Seven of them, offered unprompted, for a woman he met at a well four verses ago.

***"Your younger daughter."*** **Watch the vocabulary, because it changes at verse 26 and the change
is the whole point.** The narrator's pair at 29:16 is ***haggədōlâ / haqqəṭannâ***, the big one and the
little one, and Jacob uses the narrator's word here: ***bitəkā haqqəṭannâ***. **At 29:26 Laban answers
with a different pair — *haṣṣəʿîrâ* before *habbəkîrâ*, the younger before the firstborn — which is
not the narrator's vocabulary at all. It is the oracle's** (25:23) **and the birthright's.** Jacob
asked in the neutral register and is refused in the register of his own life. See
[[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

***"It is better that I give her to you, than that I should give her to another man."*** **Laban does
not say yes.** He says the arrangement is preferable to an alternative, adds *"Stay with me"*, and
**never names Rachel** — *her*, twice, with the noun supplied by Jacob's sentence and not by his own.
**Reread after verse 25, the sentence is perfectly true and commits him to nothing**, which is the
same construction as *"we can't speak to you bad or good"* (24:50). The wiki notes the wording; it
does not claim Laban planned the substitution from this verse, because Genesis does not say when he
decided.

***"They seemed to him but a few days, for the love he had for her."*** ***Kəyāmîm ʾăḥādîm.***
**Genesis has used *a few days* twice before, and the second one is his mother's.** *"Stay with him a
few days, until your brother's fury turns away"* (27:44) — ***yāmîm ʾăḥādîm***, the same two words —
**and those few days become twenty years.** (24:55's *a few days* in the WEB is a different
construction, *yāmîm ʾô ʿāśôr*, *days, or ten*, and it is also this household's, asking to keep
Rebekah.) **The idiom that sent him becomes the idiom that measures what it cost**, and the only
person who ever gets what the phrase promised is Jacob, for seven years, because he was in love.

**And this is the passage's one sentence about his interior life.** Genesis has told us nothing about
what Jacob felt at the birthright, the blessing, the flight, or the stairway. **Here it tells us that
seven years went quickly.**

### 29:21–27 — the evening, the morning, and the rule

> **21** Jacob said to Laban, “Give me my wife, for my days are fulfilled, that I may go in to her.”
> **22** Laban gathered together all the men of the place, and made a feast.
> **23** In the evening, he took Leah his daughter, and brought her to him. He went in to her.
> **24** Laban gave Zilpah his servant to his daughter Leah for a servant.
> **25** In the morning, behold, it was Leah! He said to Laban, “What is this you have done to me?
> Didn’t I serve with you for Rachel? Why then have you deceived me?”
> **26** Laban said, “It is not done so in our place, to give the younger before the firstborn.
> **27** Fulfill the week of this one, and we will give you the other also for the service which you
> will serve with me yet seven other years.”

***"Give me my wife, for my days are fulfilled."*** **The bluntest sentence Jacob speaks in the
chapter**, and it is a demand for delivery on a completed contract — *my days are fulfilled*, the
language of a term served. **Genesis gives him no wedding speech and no address to Rachel**, and the
clause it does give him — *"that I may go in to her"* — is the one that will be true of the wrong
woman in two verses.

***"Made a feast."*** ***Mišteh*** — from ***šātâ***, **to drink.** The noun is a drinking-party, and
Genesis has used it twice before: Abraham's *great feast* at Isaac's weaning (21:8), and **Lot's, in
the cave** — *"they made their father drink wine"*, on two nights, and *"he didn't know when she lay
down, nor when she arose"* (19:33, 35). **The book does not say Jacob was drunk.** What it says is that
there was a *mišteh*, then evening, then a woman he did not identify until morning. **The wiki records
the vocabulary and the sequence and draws no conclusion**, noting only that this is the second time
Genesis puts a *mišteh*, a night, and an unrecognised woman in the same paragraph. See
[[The Drunkenness of Noah and the Drunkenness of Lot]].

***"Laban gathered together all the men of the place."*** **The whole town is a witness to a wedding
whose bride is not named in front of them**, and none of them says anything. The men who told Jacob
the rule about the stone do not tell him the rule about the daughters.

***"In the evening … in the morning, behold, it was Leah!"*** ***Wayhî babbōqer wəhinnēh-hîʾ lēʾâ.***
**Genesis never explains the mechanism.** There is no veil in the text — the veil is imported from
24:65, where Rebekah covers herself before meeting Isaac — and no drug, no darkness explicitly
described, no silence enjoined. **The book gives evening, a drinking feast, and a morning**, and lets
the reader assemble the rest. **The *wəhinnēh* is the reader's surprise and Jacob's at once**: the
same construction that opened 29:2 with the well now opens the disaster.

**And the sentence is a mirror of 27:33.** Isaac *"trembled violently"* when he discovered the
substitution; Jacob gets an exclamation and then a legal question. **In both scenes the discovery
comes too late for the transaction to be reversed**, and in both the deceiver's answer is that the
thing is now simply the case. See
[[Yes, He Will Be Blessed - The Irrevocable Word]].

***"What is this you have done to me? … Why then have you deceived me?"*** **Two questions, and
Genesis has heard both before, from foreigners, about this family.** *"What is this that you have done
to me?"* is Pharaoh to Abram (12:18); *"What have you done to us?"* is Abimelech to Abraham (20:9) and
to Isaac (26:10). **At 29:25 a member of the family uses the formula for the first time, and he is the
injured party.** And ***rimmîtānî***, *you have deceived me*, is from the root of the noun Isaac used
of him at 27:35 — *"your brother came with **deceit**"*, ***bəmirmâ***. **The word that convicted him
is the word he now presses.** See [[Deception]].

**Note what he does not ask.** He does not ask where Rachel is, and he does not ask what happens to
Leah. **His three clauses are all about the contract**: what was done to him, what he served for, and
why he was cheated.

***"It is not done so in our place."*** ***Lōʾ-yēʿāśeh kēn bimqômēnû.*** **A custom, cited without
apology, by a man who could have cited it seven years earlier.** ***Māqôm*** is the word that ran six
times through the Bethel passage as *the place*, a sanctuary site; **here it means *around here*, and
it is what a local says to a foreigner who has misunderstood the rules.** The rule itself — elder
daughter first — is attested in the ancient Near East and is entirely ordinary. **What is not ordinary
is withholding it until the morning after.**

***"Fulfill the week of this one."*** ***Šābuaʿ*** — **the bridal week**, seven days of feasting,
which is what Samson's wedding runs on at Judges 14:12 and what Tobit assumes. **The offer is
therefore not seven more years before Rachel; it is seven more days.** Jacob gets Rachel at the end of
the week and pays for her over the following seven years — which 29:28–30 confirms — and **the
distinction matters, because the popular version of this story has him waiting fourteen years for
Rachel and Genesis does not.** He waits seven years and one week.

**And the sevens stack up.** Seven years, a seven-day week, seven more years, in a chapter whose
opening scene is a well — and the family's other named well, [[Beersheba]], means *well of seven* and
*well of the oath* at once. **Genesis makes nothing of it here**, and neither does this page beyond
noting that the number is doing the counting in both places.

### 29:28–30 — the second contract

> **28** Jacob did so, and fulfilled her week. He gave him Rachel his daughter as wife.
> **29** Laban gave to Rachel his daughter Bilhah, his servant, to be her servant.
> **30** He went in also to Rachel, and he loved also Rachel more than Leah, and served with him yet
> seven other years.

***"Jacob did so."*** ***Wayyaʿaś yaʿăqōb kēn.*** **Three words, no negotiation, no counter-offer, and
no further complaint** — from the man who required an oath before handing over a bowl of stew.
**Genesis gives his acceptance the shortest sentence in the passage.**

***"He loved also Rachel more than Leah."*** **The comparative is the trap the rest of the cycle
springs.** Genesis has run this construction once already, in the previous generation and in one
verse: *"Isaac loved Esau … Rebekah loved Jacob"* (25:28) — **a divided house with one parent
each.** Here both loves are in one man, ranked, **and the ranking is stated by the narrator rather
than by a character.** It will be stated again about his son: *"Israel loved Joseph more than all his
children … his brothers saw that their father loved him more"* (37:3–4), **and that sentence is
followed immediately by the word *hated*.** So is this one — 29:31 opens *"Yahweh saw that Leah was
hated."* See [[Love]] and [[Envy]].

***"Loved … more than"*** is also, in Hebrew idiom, the phrase the law will legislate. **Deuteronomy
21:15–17: *"If a man has two wives, the one beloved, and the other hated,"* he may not prefer the
loved wife's son over the hated wife's firstborn.** That is precisely this household — **and precisely
what happens in it**, since Leah's firstborn Reuben loses the double portion (49:3–4; 1 Chronicles
5:1–2). See [[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]].

***"Two sisters."*** **Genesis reports without comment a marriage Leviticus 18:18 forbids by name:
*"You shall not take a wife in addition to her sister, to be a rival, to uncover her nakedness, while
her sister is yet alive."*** The prohibition is unique in the incest code for giving a reason — *to be
a rival*, ***liṣrōr*** — and the reason is the plot of Genesis 29–30. See
[[Two Sisters - The Marriage Genesis Records and Leviticus Forbids]].

***"Zilpah … Bilhah."*** **Two women are handed over inside this passage as property**, one to each
bride, in verses that do not pause for them. They are named, and **that is the only thing the passage
gives them**; within one chapter both will be given to Jacob as wives on their mistresses' initiative
(30:3–4, 30:9), and four of the twelve tribes descend from them. See [[Zilpah]], [[Bilhah]], and
[[Slavery and Servitude]].

### What the pericope is doing

**It bills him.**

Genesis 27 gave Jacob a blessing he took by fraud and Genesis 28 gave him a promise with no condition
attached. **Neither chapter attached a price, and this one is nothing but prices**: a month free, then
seven years, then a week, then seven years more, then — at 31:41 — his own summary of the whole
period, *"twenty years in your house."* **The blessing holds. The wiki has said so at every step. What
this pericope adds is the invoice.**

**And it does it without a single word from God.** No theophany, no rebuke, no vindication, no
narrator's verdict. **The one voice that comments on Jacob's conduct in these thirty verses is
Laban's**, and Laban is not commenting on Jacob's conduct — he is explaining a wedding. **The verdict
is in the plot**: the man who put a younger sibling where an elder should be wakes up with an elder
sibling where the younger should be, and the sentence that tells him so contains the two words of the
oracle he was born under.

**What it also does is build Israel out of a grudge.** Twelve tribes are about to come out of a
household containing two sisters, two slaves, one man, and a ranked affection stated in the last verse
before God speaks. **Genesis does not present this as a good marriage or a bad one. It presents it as
the one Israel comes from.**

## People

- [[Jacob]] — **on the receiving end of his own technique, and paying for it in years.** He arrives
  with nothing the text names, corrects a group of shepherds about their work, rolls off a stone the
  chapter has twice said takes a crowd, and kisses a woman before telling her who he is. **His
  credential is his mother's name.** He names his own bride-price without haggling — seven years, for
  a woman he has known one scene — and the narrator gives him the passage's only sentence of feeling:
  *they seemed to him but a few days.* On the morning after the wedding he asks the question three
  foreign kings asked his grandfather and father, **and he is the injured party for the first time in
  the family's history.** Then he accepts the revised terms in three words. **He says nothing to God
  in this passage and God says nothing to him.**
- [[Laban]] — **runs to meet a nephew with no gold on him, and puts him on wages inside a month.** He
  pronounces the formula of the first marriage — *"Surely you are my bone and my flesh"* — over Jacob
  at 29:14, and at 29:15 turns *brother* into the premise of an employment question. **His refusal at
  29:26 is not an apology but a house rule**, delivered without embarrassment, in the vocabulary of
  the oracle Jacob was born under; and his counter-offer is generous on its face and doubles his
  labour supply. **Genesis withholds the causal clause it supplied for him at 24:30**, and the reader
  who has read that verse supplies it anyway.
- [[Rachel]] — **introduced mid-sentence, keeping her father's sheep**, and the object of the first
  love in the Bible that precedes a marriage. She is kissed by a stranger, told afterwards who he is,
  and runs to tell her father. **She has no line in this passage**, is not named by her own father in
  29:19, and is handed over a week late with a slave attached.
- [[Leah]] — **named second, described in half a clause, and married under someone else's name.**
  Genesis gives her no word, no consent, no reaction, and no reported feeling in the entire pericope;
  she is *taken*, *brought*, and *given*. **The book that recorded Rebekah's *"I will go"* records
  nothing at all here** — and then, at 29:31, makes her the first person God is said to see in the
  Jacob cycle.
- [[Zilpah]] — given to Leah at the wedding, in a verse of its own; named, and nothing else.
- [[Bilhah]] — given to Rachel a week later, in the matching verse; named, and nothing else.
- [[Rebekah]] — **named once, at 29:12, and this is her last appearance in the narrative of her son's
  life.** Jacob introduces himself as *Rebekah's son* in the house she left at 24:58, and Genesis
  never brings them back together, never records her death, and mentions her again only as a body at
  Machpelah (49:31). **Her *"a few days"* of 27:44 is the phrase 29:20 measures seven years with.**
- [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]] — **called Laban's father at 29:5**, where 22:22–23, 24:24 and 28:2
  make him his great-grandfather; the book's loose *bēn*, or a household named for its head of line.
- [[Bethuel]] — **absent.** Alive, present, and named in the negotiation of Genesis 24; not mentioned
  once in this chapter, where Laban runs the house alone and Rachel runs to *her father*.
- [[God]] — **not named, not addressed, and not acting anywhere in these thirty verses**, four verses
  after promising *"I am with you, and will keep you, wherever you go."* The next verse of the book
  opens *"Yahweh saw."*

## Places

- [[Aram Naharaim]] — the region, here under a third name: **the land of the children of the east**,
  after *Paddan Aram* at 28:5 and *Haran* at 28:10. The country that supplies every wife in the
  covenant line for three generations, and that receives no altar, no promise, and — in this
  pericope — no divine word at all.
- [[Haran (city)]] — named by the shepherds at 29:4 as where they are from, which is how the reader
  learns Jacob has arrived. Laban's town, and the last address of Terah's household.
**Not named in the passage**, and so not listed here, though the chapter invites it: [[Beersheba]],
*well of seven*, is the place Jacob left, in a pericope organised by a well and by the number seven.

## Themes

- [[Deception]] — **the family's method turned on its practitioner**, with the root of 27:35's
  *mirmâ* in Jacob's own mouth at 29:25, and the trick performed in the dark by substitution, exactly
  as he performed it.
- [[Marriage]] — a bride-price paid in labour; two sisters married to one man in a union Leviticus
  18:18 names and forbids; and the *bone and flesh* of 2:23 spoken over a nephew rather than a wife.
- [[Love]] — ***ʾāhab*** three times in thirteen verses, and **the first love in Scripture that
  precedes a marriage rather than following it** (against 24:67); then ranked, at 29:30, in the
  construction the law will legislate at Deuteronomy 21:15–17.
- [[Work]] — ***ʿābad*** seven times in verses 15–30. Fourteen years of contracted labour, and the
  only feat of physical strength any patriarch performs.
- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — *"It is not done so in our place, to give the younger before the
  firstborn"*: the ordinary rule stated out loud, in the oracle's own vocabulary, to the standing
  exception to it.
- [[Retribution]] — measure for measure, and **Genesis never calls it that.** No narrator's aside, no
  divine speech, and no remark from Jacob linking his wedding night to his father's bedside; the only
  link the text supplies is Laban's choice of nouns.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — two women given away in two verses as wedding furniture, and a free man
  who sells fourteen years of his own labour because he has nothing else to pay with.
- [[Hospitality]] — the run, the embrace, the kiss, and the house — followed by a month, and then a
  wages question. **The welcome is real and it is also the opening of an account.**
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *behold* three times (29:2 twice, 29:25), the last of them on a woman
  in the morning light; a man who could not see who he had married, in a book that has just spent a
  chapter on a father who could not see which son he was blessing.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the first well in Genesis nobody quarrels over, in the chapter that
  starts a rivalry lasting two generations.

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *Jacob, Leah, and Rachel — Genesis
  29:1–30*, and it needed no revision. **29:1 opens with a fresh itinerary formula** — *"Then Jacob
  went on his journey"* — and **29:31 restarts the narrative with God as its subject** for the first
  time in the chapter: *"Yahweh saw that Leah was hated, and he opened her womb."* Everything from
  29:31 is the births, which is the next box. **The alternative split — 29:1–14 arrival, 29:15–30
  marriages — was considered and rejected**: the well scene is not a separate episode but the first
  half of one negotiation, and the *"my bone and my flesh"* of 29:14 is what makes the wages question
  of 29:15 possible.
- **On the title.** *Jacob, Leah, and Rachel* was kept from the plan. **The order is the passage's own
  and not chronology's** — Rachel is seen first and married second — and the alternative titles
  considered (*Jacob Serves for Rachel*, *Leah for Rachel*) each name only one of the two women the
  rest of the book runs on. **No other book has a passage page by this title**, and no other Genesis
  passage does.
- **On the absence of God, which is the page's organising claim and is checkable.** *Yahweh* and
  *ʾĕlōhîm* do not occur in 29:1–30; the first divine name in the chapter is at 29:31. **This was
  verified against `raw/bible/Genesis.md` rather than asserted.** Genesis has done it before — the
  wiki records the same absence at [[The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek]] and
  [[Lot and His Daughters]] — but **this is the first time it does it immediately after a theophany**,
  which is why the page builds on it.
- **On *"Laban, the son of Nahor"* (29:5).** Recorded as a **contradiction between passages, not
  resolved**: 22:22–23, 24:24, and 28:2 make Laban the son of Bethuel and the great-grandson of
  Nahor. Both standing explanations — *bēn* in the wide sense, and identification by head of line —
  are given on the page and on [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]], and neither is adopted. **28:13's *"the
  God of Abraham your father"*, said to Abraham's grandson, is the same usage two chapters earlier.**
- **On Leah's eyes.** ***Rakkôt*** is *soft, tender, delicate*, and **both readings are on the page** —
  the Septuagint's *weak* (which the WEB follows) and the reading that takes it as her one
  compliment, outbid in the same sentence. **Neither is adopted.** The structure of the verse — one
  feature against a whole person, joined adversatively — is stated as the part that is not in doubt.
- **On the bridal week, where the popular version of the story is wrong.** 29:27–28 gives Rachel to
  Jacob at the end of Leah's ***šābuaʿ***, seven days, with the second seven years served
  **afterwards**. **He waits seven years and one week for Rachel, not fourteen years**, and the page
  says so explicitly because the fourteen-year version is the one most readers arrive with.
- **On the *mišteh*.** The noun is a drinking-feast, and Genesis's other night-and-unrecognised-woman
  scene (19:30–38) uses it. **The page records the shared vocabulary and the shared sequence and
  states that Genesis does not say Jacob was drunk**, because it does not. No new connection page was
  made for it; a sentence was added to [[The Drunkenness of Noah and the Drunkenness of Lot]] instead.
- **On the vocabulary switch at 29:26.** The narrator's pair at 29:16 (*gədōlâ / qəṭannâ*, big and
  little) is not Laban's pair at 29:26 (*bəkîrâ / ṣəʿîrâ*, firstborn and younger). **Jacob asks in the
  narrator's register at 29:18 and is refused in the oracle's**, and the observation is new on this
  page; [[The Younger Before the Firstborn - Jacob and Laban]] already carried the 29:26 pair and now
  carries the switch.
- **On *a few days*.** The WEB has the phrase three times in Genesis (24:55; 27:44; 29:20). **Two of
  them are the same Hebrew idiom, *yāmîm ʾăḥādîm* — Rebekah's instruction and Jacob's seven years** —
  and 24:55's is a different construction. The distinction is stated on the page rather than collapsed
  into a three-way pun.
- **On what was created.** **Four person pages** — [[Rachel]], [[Leah]], [[Zilpah]], [[Bilhah]] — the
  last two on the wiki's standing practice of giving a page to anyone Genesis names, and because both
  become mothers of tribes within one chapter. **One theme page**, [[Love]]: ***ʾāhab*** has been in
  the book since 22:2 and had no page, and this is the passage that forces one. **Three connection
  pages**: [[Two Sisters - The Marriage Genesis Records and Leviticus Forbids]],
  [[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]], and
  [[Like Rachel and Like Leah - The Builders of the House of Israel]].
- **On what was deliberately not created.** **No page for *the land of the children of the east***,
  which is a loose regional designation rather than a place with a location; it is carried in
  [[Aram Naharaim]]'s `also_known_as` and overview, **on the same decision the wiki recorded for
  *Paddan Aram* at [[A Wife for Isaac]] and for *Luz* at [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]].** **No page
  for the shepherds of 29:4–8**, who are a chorus. **No connection page for the *mišteh* parallel**,
  for the reason above.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** The births and Leah's *"Yahweh has looked at my affliction"*
  (29:31–30:24); the wage-changing and the flocks (30:25–43); Jacob's twenty-year summary (31:38–42);
  Rachel's death and her pillar (35:16–20); and the two burials, Leah at Machpelah and Rachel on the
  road (49:31). **All are referenced as scripture citations rather than wikilinks, so none is a
  dangling link.**
