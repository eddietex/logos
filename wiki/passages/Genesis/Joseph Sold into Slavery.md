# Joseph Sold into Slavery

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 37:1–36

## Summary

**God is not in this chapter.** Not a word spoken, not an act performed, not a name used — no *Yahweh*,
no *ʾĕlōhîm*, in thirty-six verses in which a boy is stripped, thrown in a cistern, sold, and reported
dead. **Only one other chapter in the patriarchal narratives does this**, and it is
[[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]], two chapters back. **The Joseph cycle's opening is the longest
stretch of Genesis in which heaven says nothing** — and it is followed immediately by *"Yahweh was with
Joseph"* (39:2), the sentence chapter 37 withholds.

**What the chapter has instead of God is dreams, and nobody in it treats them as revelation.** The
brothers hear a claim to rule and answer it (37:8); the father hears a claim on himself and rebukes it
(37:10); **the narrator never says the dreams came from God**, and never says they did not. It is the
one thing in the chapter that turns out to be true, and it is the one thing everybody in the chapter
argues with.

**The tenth and last *toledot* of Genesis opens here, and it is a header over the wrong man.** *"This
is the history of the generations of Jacob"* (37:2) — and what follows is not Jacob's genealogy but
Joseph's biography. **Every previous *toledot* introduced a list; this one introduces a novel**, and
runs from 37:2 to the end of the book. See
[[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]].

**The plot is set in motion by a father's gift and closed by a father's grief, and between them the
text is almost perfectly economical about blame.** Jacob makes a coat; the brothers hate; Joseph
dreams and tells; Jacob sends him alone to the town his sons sacked; ten of them decide on murder and
settle for twenty shekels. **Nobody is excused and nobody is condemned in the narration.** The one
verdict in the chapter is Jacob's, and it is wrong: *"An evil animal has devoured him."*

**And it is a sentence his sons wrote for him and never had to say.** They compose it at 37:20 —
*"we will say, 'An evil animal has devoured him'"* — and then hand him a bloody coat and ask a
question instead. **He supplies the line himself, word for word** (37:33). It is the most efficient
lie in Genesis: **the brothers never actually tell it.**

**Joseph speaks four times in the chapter and not once after verse 17.** His last recorded words are
*"I am looking for my brothers"* (37:16). He is silent in the pit, silent in the caravan, silent into
Egypt — **and five chapters later the brothers let slip what the narration left out**: *"we saw the
distress of his soul, when he begged us, and we wouldn't listen"* (42:21). **Genesis withholds his
pleading for five chapters and puts it in the mouths of the men who ignored it.**

### 37:1–4 — a header, a coat, and the verb that follows love

> **1** Jacob lived in the land of his father’s travels, in the land of Canaan.
> **2** This is the history of the generations of Jacob. Joseph, being seventeen years old, was feeding
> the flock with his brothers. He was a boy with the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, his father’s wives.
> Joseph brought an evil report of them to their father.
> **3** Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age, and
> he made him a coat of many colors.
> **4** His brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, and they hated him,
> and couldn’t speak peaceably to him.

***"This is the history of the generations of Jacob."*** ***ʾĒlleh tōlədôt yaʿăqōb*** — **the last of
the formulas that segment Genesis, and the only one that does not deliver what it promises.** The other
nine are followed by genealogy, sometimes for chapters at a time; **this one is followed by fourteen
chapters of narrative in which Jacob is mostly offstage.** The heading names the father and the book
tells the son's story, which is a fair description of what happens inside the chapter too.

***"Being seventeen years old."*** **The first age Genesis gives for Joseph, and the chapter's only
number besides the twenty shekels.** He is thirty when Pharaoh promotes him (41:46) and thirty-nine
when the brothers arrive; **the pit costs him twenty-two years of his father's life.**

***"He was a boy with the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, his father's wives."*** ***Naʿar*** — *boy*,
*lad*, and also *servant*; **the Hebrew of the clause is genuinely awkward** and the versions differ on
whether he is *with* them, *serving* them, or merely *young alongside* them. **What is not awkward is
the phrase *his father's wives*.** [[Bilhah]] is a *concubine* at 35:22, a *servant* at 35:25, and a
*wife* here — **three designations in three chapters, and Genesis reconciles none of them.** The
contradiction was flagged when it was half-visible at
[[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]]; this verse supplies the third term and still does
not settle it. See [[Bilhah]] and [[Zilpah]].

**And the clause quietly identifies who he was informing on.** Not the sons of Leah — **the sons of the
slave women**, the four with the least standing in the household, reported on by the son of the loved
wife. Whether that is the whole of it the verse does not say; the brothers who act at 37:18 are *his
brothers* without distinction.

***"Joseph brought an evil report of them to their father."*** ***Dibbâ rāʿâ*** — and ***dibbâ*** is
**not a neutral word for a report.** It is *whispering*, *defamation*: the thing the spies bring back
about the land at Numbers 14:36–37, for which they die of plague, and the thing Proverbs warns of at
10:18 and 25:10. **But the Hebrew leaves the crucial thing open.** *Their evil report* can mean the
report was slanderous, or that it was a true report of evil things; and Genesis, which is about to spend
thirteen chapters establishing that this young man tells the truth about dreams, **declines to say
whether the first thing it records him saying was true.** The wiki records the ambiguity and adopts
neither reading. See [[Language and Speech]].

***"Israel loved Joseph more than all his children … and they hated him."*** **The exact pair the
narrator used of this man's own marriage.** *"He loved also Rachel more than Leah"* (29:30), and Leah
named a child *"Yahweh has heard that I am hated"* (29:33). **Genesis uses ʾāhab and śānēʾ of the
father in chapter 29 and of his sons in chapter 37**, and the configuration is the one
Deuteronomy 21:15–17 legislates against by name: *the loved wife and the hated wife*, and the firstborn
who must not lose his portion to the loved wife's son. **Which is precisely what happens** (1 Chronicles
5:1–2). See [[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]] and
[[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

***"Because he was the son of his old age."*** ***Ben-zəqūnîm*** — **and it is not true in the plain
sense.** [[Benjamin]] is younger, and his birth killed the same mother. **The chapter that opens the
Joseph cycle gives a reason for the favouritism that the previous chapter has already disqualified**,
and Genesis neither corrects it nor explains it. The wiki records it as the narrator reporting Jacob's
own account of himself.

***"A coat of many colors."*** ***Kətōnet passîm*** — **two words, and both are more interesting than
the tradition they produced.**

- ***Kətōnet*** is the ordinary tunic, and **its first appearance in Scripture is God's**: *"Yahweh God
  made coats of animal skins for Adam and for his wife"* (3:21). It is also **the priest's garment**
  (Exodus 28:4, 39–40). **The word that clothes the first couple and the sons of Aaron is the word for
  the thing the brothers strip off Joseph.** See [[Nakedness and Covering]].
- ***Passîm*** **occurs in this sense nowhere else in the Bible except one place**, and that place
  matters: **2 Samuel 13:18–19**, where Tamar wears a *kətōnet passîm*, *"for with such robes were the
  king's virgin daughters dressed"*, and tears it after Amnon rapes her. **The garment that marks
  Joseph out is the garment that marks a royal daughter**, and both are destroyed by the wearer's own
  family. See [[Dinah and Tamar - Two Rapes and Two Avenging Brothers]].
- **Nobody knows what *passîm* means.** *Pas* is *palm* or *sole*, which yields *a robe reaching to the
  hands and feet* — long-sleeved, ankle-length, the garment of someone who does not work. The
  Septuagint's *χιτῶνα ποικίλον*, *many-coloured*, and the Vulgate's *tunicam polymitam* are where the
  English comes from, and the WEB follows them. **Either way the point is the same and the text makes
  it without saying it**: it is a garment you cannot herd sheep in, given to one of eleven shepherds.

***"Couldn't speak peaceably to him."*** ***Lōʾ yākəlû dabbərô lə-šālōm*** — **and this is the first
half of the chapter's cruellest arrangement.** At 37:14 Jacob will send Joseph to *"see whether it is
well with your brothers"* — ***šəlôm ʾaḥeykā***, *the šālôm of your brothers*. **The father sends the
boy sixty miles to enquire after the peace of men the narrator has just told us cannot speak peace to
him**, and the narrator does not point it out. See [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

### 37:5–11 — two dreams, and the only true thing anybody disputes

> **5** Joseph dreamed a dream, and he told it to his brothers, and they hated him all the more.
> **6** He said to them, “Please hear this dream which I have dreamed:
> **7** for behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and behold, my sheaf arose and also stood
> upright; and behold, your sheaves came around, and bowed down to my sheaf.”
> **8** His brothers said to him, “Will you indeed reign over us? Or will you indeed have dominion over
> us?” They hated him all the more for his dreams and for his words.
> **9** He dreamed yet another dream, and told it to his brothers, and said, “Behold, I have dreamed yet
> another dream: and behold, the sun and the moon and eleven stars bowed down to me.”
> **10** He told it to his father and to his brothers. His father rebuked him, and said to him, “What is
> this dream that you have dreamed? Will I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow
> ourselves down to you to the earth?”
> **11** His brothers envied him, but his father kept this saying in mind.

**The two dreams escalate from the field to the sky, and from ten brothers to the whole household.**
Sheaves bow to a sheaf; then the sun, the moon, and eleven stars bow to the dreamer himself. **The
first dream keeps him inside the image; the second puts him outside it and makes the cosmos the
supporting cast.**

***"He told it to his brothers."*** **Genesis does not say why, and the chapter's whole moral weather
depends on the answer it withholds.** He is not asked; he volunteers, twice, and the second time he
volunteers to the brothers *and* the father. **The wiki notes that the text supplies no motive — not
arrogance, not innocence, not obedience to a revelation** — and that the absence is of a piece with a
chapter that assigns no blame anywhere.

***"Will you indeed reign over us? Or will you indeed have dominion over us?"*** ***Hămālōk timlōk …
ʾim-māšôl timšōl*** — **two infinitive-absolute constructions, the Hebrew of scorn**, and the verbs are
*mālak* and *māšal*. **This is the first time in Genesis that *mālak*, to reign as king, is used of a
descendant of Abraham.** Kings have been promised three times — to Abraham (17:6), to Sarah (17:16),
to Jacob nine verses before this chapter's cycle opens (35:11) — **and the first Israelite the word is
applied to is a seventeen-year-old, sarcastically, by the men who sell him.** See
[[Kings Will Come Out of You]] and [[Kingship and Empire]].

**And *māšal* is the word from Eden.** *"He will rule over you"* (3:16), *"you will rule over"* sin
(4:7) — the vocabulary of contested mastery within a family, now spoken by brothers about a brother.
See [[Desire and Rule - Genesis 3.16 and 4.7]].

***"The sun and the moon and eleven stars."*** **The count is exact and the census is a problem.**
Eleven stars are the eleven brothers, which leaves the sun and the moon for the parents. **His mother
is dead.** [[Rachel]] died at 35:19, two chapters and perhaps a year or two earlier, and yet Jacob's
rebuke takes the figure at face value: *"Will I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow
ourselves down to you?"*

**Four readings, and the wiki adopts none.**

1. **The rebuke is rhetorical and the arithmetic is not the point.** Jacob is reciting the dream's own
   logic back at it to show how monstrous it is; *sun, moon, and stars* means *the whole household*, and
   a dead wife inside a stock figure is not an assertion that she is alive.
2. **[[Leah]] is the mother meant.** She is the surviving senior wife and, on the plain reading of
   37:35, the mother of the daughters who come to comfort him. **Genesis never calls her Joseph's
   mother**, and the reading has to supply that.
3. **Composite sources.** The dream belongs to a strand that did not contain Rachel's death, and the
   two were joined without reconciliation — the same account offered for
   *"born to him in Paddan Aram"* at 35:26 with Benjamin born in Canaan seven verses earlier.
4. **The dream is a promise about a house, not a guest list**, and its fulfilment is measured against
   what actually happens: **the brothers bow (42:6; 43:26; 44:14; 50:18), and the father does not.**
   Jacob's one recorded bow is at 47:31, *"Israel bowed himself on the bed's head"*, and it is toward
   God, not toward his son.

**On the dream's afterlife**: *"a woman clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and on her
head a crown of twelve stars"* (Revelation 12:1) is this verse's imagery with the count moved up by
one. See [[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]].

***"His brothers envied him."*** ***Wayqannəʾû-bô*** — from ***qānāʾ***, **the root behind both *envy*
and *zeal***, and the word used of God himself as *jealous* (Exodus 20:5). **Genesis has used the verb
once before, of the Philistines about Isaac's wells** (26:14). **The escalation across these seven
verses is deliberate and lexical**: they *hated* him at 37:4, hated him *all the more* at 37:5, hated
him *all the more* again at 37:8, and now they *envy* him. **Four steps, and the text records no word
spoken to him by any of them after 37:8.** See [[Envy]].

***"But his father kept this saying in mind."*** ***Šāmar ʾet-haddābār*** — *guarded the word*. **The
same gesture the narrator gave him at 35:22**, where *"Israel heard of it"* and nothing follows.
**Jacob's characteristic response to information about his sons is to keep it**, and the one time he
acts on this word he acts against it, by sending the dreamer to the dreamers' brothers alone. Luke uses
the idiom twice of Mary — *"Mary kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart"* (2:19, 51) —
which is the closest the New Testament comes to quoting this clause.

### 37:12–17 — the errand, and the stranger who makes it possible

> **12** His brothers went to feed their father’s flock in Shechem.
> **13** Israel said to Joseph, “Aren’t your brothers feeding the flock in Shechem? Come, and I will
> send you to them.” He said to him, “Here I am.”
> **14** He said to him, “Go now, see whether it is well with your brothers, and well with the flock;
> and bring me word again.” So he sent him out of the valley of Hebron, and he came to Shechem.
> **15** A certain man found him, and behold, he was wandering in the field. The man asked him, “What
> are you looking for?”
> **16** He said, “I am looking for my brothers. Tell me, please, where they are feeding the flock.”
> **17** The man said, “They have left here, for I heard them say, ‘Let us go to Dothan.’” Joseph went
> after his brothers, and found them in Dothan.

***"In Shechem."*** **Named twice in two verses, and Genesis says nothing about what happened there.**
[[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] is three chapters back: [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]] killed every male
in the town, the household looted it, and Jacob's own verdict was *"you have troubled me … I am few in
number. They will gather themselves together against me and strike me"* (34:30). **His sons are now
pasturing sheep on that ground, and he sends his seventeen-year-old there alone.** The wiki notes the
juxtaposition and observes that no character in the chapter mentions it.

***"Here I am."*** ***Hinnēnî*** — **and it is the word of the binding.** *"Abraham!" … "Here I am"*
(22:1), and again to his son at 22:7. **The obedient monosyllable of Genesis 22 is Joseph's last word
before the errand that ends in the pit**, and it is the whole of his reply. See
[[The Binding of Isaac]].

***"See whether it is well with your brothers."*** ***Rəʾēh ʾet-šəlôm ʾaḥeykā*** — **the errand is,
literally, to inspect their peace**, from a father whose narrator has told us they cannot speak peace.
**The word occurs twice in the verse**, once of the brothers and once of the flock, **and both enquiries
come back false**: the flock is fine, the brothers are not at peace, and the word Joseph brings back is
brought by somebody else and is a lie. See [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

***"Out of the valley of Hebron."*** ***Mē-ʿēmeq ḥebrôn*** — **a phrase found nowhere else in the
Bible**, and an odd one: [[Hebron]] sits about three thousand feet up in the hill country, and Genesis
has called its surroundings *Mamre* and *Kiriath Arba* and never a valley. **It is also the place his
grandfather is buried and his father has just buried him** (35:27–29). The journey it begins is
**roughly fifty miles to Shechem and fifteen more to Dothan**, north through the whole length of the
central highlands, **on foot, alone, in a coat you cannot work in.** See [[Hebron]] and [[Dothan]].

***"A certain man found him."*** ***ʾÎš*** — **unnamed, unplaced, and the reason the rest of the Bible
happens.** Joseph is *wandering* (*tōʿeh*, the verb used of lost sheep at Psalm 119:176), the brothers
have moved on, and **without this man's overheard scrap — *"I heard them say, 'Let us go to Dothan'"* —
he turns round and goes home.** Rabbinic tradition makes him an angel; Genesis makes him a man in a
field who answers a question. **The wiki reports the hinge and declines the identification**, and he
gets no page, on the same footing as the midwife at 35:17.

**It is the third time in the patriarchal narratives that a stranger's directions decide a generation**
— the messengers who find Abraham at Mamre, the man who wrestles Jacob and will not be named, and this
one. **Only this one is unambiguously a human being**, and he is the only one of the three who is
helpful and ordinary.

### 37:18–24 — a murder planned, amended, and executed by half

> **18** They saw him afar off, and before he came near to them, they conspired against him to kill him.
> **19** They said to one another, “Behold, this dreamer comes.
> **20** Come now therefore, and let’s kill him, and cast him into one of the pits, and we will say, ‘An
> evil animal has devoured him.’ We will see what will become of his dreams.”
> **21** Reuben heard it, and delivered him out of their hand, and said, “Let’s not take his life.”
> **22** Reuben said to them, “Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit that is in the wilderness, but lay
> no hand on him”—that he might deliver him out of their hand, to restore him to his father.
> **23** When Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped Joseph of his coat, the coat of many colors
> that was on him;
> **24** and they took him, and threw him into the pit. The pit was empty. There was no water in it.

***"This dreamer."*** ***Baʿal ha-ḥălōmôt*** — **literally *the master of the dreams*, or *the dream
owner*.** It is the only place in the Bible the phrase occurs. **They do not use his name**, and from
37:19 to the end of the chapter **nobody in the family says the word *Joseph* except the narrator.**

***"We will see what will become of his dreams."*** **The chapter's one flash of dramatic irony, and
Genesis lets them say it and says nothing back.** Twenty-two years later the brothers bow to a man they
do not recognise in a country they came to for food, and **the narrator's only comment is
*"Joseph remembered the dreams which he dreamed about them"*** (42:9). See
[[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]].

***"Let's not take his life … Shed no blood … but lay no hand on him."*** **[[Reuben]] argues three
times and wins on the letter.** He does not say *don't do it*; he says *not blood*, and proposes the
cistern the others had already mentioned as the disposal method (37:20). **The narrator has to supply
the intention the speech does not contain**: *"that he might deliver him out of their hand, to restore
him to his father."* **It is the only motive Genesis states for anyone in the chapter**, and it belongs
to the plan that fails.

***"Lay no hand on him."*** ***ʾAl-tišləḥû-bô yād*** — **the angel's words at Moriah**, where the voice
from heaven stops Abraham with *"don't lay your hand on the boy"*, *ʾal-tišlaḥ yādəkā ʾel-hannaʿar*
(22:12), **and the boy there and the boy here are both a *naʿar*.** At Moriah a divine voice arrests the
knife; **here the same clause is spoken by the eldest brother, in a chapter where no divine voice
speaks at all**, and what it buys is not a rescue but a slave sale. See
[[Two Sons Nearly Lost - The Sending of Ishmael and the Binding of Isaac]] and
[[Mediation and Advocacy]].

***"They stripped Joseph of his coat."*** ***Wayyapšîṭû*** — **the first thing they do, before the pit,
and the chapter is careful to name the garment twice in one verse.** The coat is the cause at 37:3–4 and
the instrument at 37:31–33; **it is removed from the son in verse 23 and delivered to the father in
verse 32**, and the boy travels to Egypt without it.

***"The pit was empty. There was no water in it."*** **A redundancy, and it is doing two things.** It
tells the reader **he did not drown**, which is the only reassurance the verse offers; and it puts a dry
cistern into the Bible's vocabulary of the lowest place a living man can be. **Jeremiah is lowered into
one and sinks in the mud** (Jeremiah 38:6); Zechariah's prisoners are freed *"out of the pit in which
is no water"* (Zechariah 9:11); and the Psalms' *bôr* is a synonym for [[Sheol]]. **Genesis puts a
seventeen-year-old in a hole with no water and then has his brothers sit down to a meal beside it.**

### 37:25–28 — a caravan, a calculation, and twenty pieces of silver

> **25** They sat down to eat bread, and they lifted up their eyes and looked, and saw a caravan of
> Ishmaelites was coming from Gilead, with their camels bearing spices and balm and myrrh, going to
> carry it down to Egypt.
> **26** Judah said to his brothers, “What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood?
> **27** Come, and let’s sell him to the Ishmaelites, and not let our hand be on him; for he is our
> brother, our flesh.” His brothers listened to him.
> **28** Midianites who were merchants passed by, and they drew and lifted up Joseph out of the pit, and
> sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver. They brought Joseph into Egypt.

***"They sat down to eat bread."*** **Four words, and they are the most damning in the chapter.**
Genesis does not editorialise; it reports a meal. **Five chapters later the brothers name what the
narration left out of it**: *"we saw the distress of his soul, when he begged us, and we wouldn't
listen"* (42:21). **The begging is in the picnic, and the picnic is all Genesis shows us.**

***"A caravan of Ishmaelites … from Gilead, with their camels bearing spices and balm and myrrh."***
***Nəkōʾt ûṣərî wālōṭ*** — gum, balm, and ladanum or myrrh, **the resin trade of the eastern highlands
heading for the Egyptian embalming and cosmetic market.** ***Ṣŏrî*** **is the balm of Jeremiah's
question** — *"Is there no balm in Gilead?"* (Jeremiah 8:22) — and **the same three commodities are what
Jacob sends back down that road as a gift** when he finally has to deal with Egypt (43:11). See
[[Gilead]] and [[Egypt]].

**And they are Abraham's descendants.** [[Ishmael]] is Abraham's son by Hagar (16:15); [[Midian]] is his
son by Keturah (25:2). **The men who carry Joseph out of the land of promise are his cousins**, from the
two lines Genesis has already written out of the inheritance. See
[[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]].

***"What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood?"*** ***Mah-beṣaʿ*** — ***beṣaʿ*** is
**gain, and specifically unjust gain**: it is the word in *"hating unjust gain"* (Exodus 18:21) and in
the prophets' complaints about profiteering. **[[Judah]]'s objection to murdering his brother is that it
pays nothing.** He then adds the one appeal to kinship anybody makes in the chapter — *"he is our
brother, our flesh"*, *ʾāḥînû bəśārēnû*, the idiom Laban used of Jacob at 29:14 — **and uses it to
argue for a sale rather than against one.**

***"Conceal his blood."*** ***Wəkissînû ʾet-dāmô*** — **and covering blood is the thing Genesis has
already said cannot be done.** Abel's blood *"cries to me from the ground"* (4:10), and Job will demand
*"earth, don't cover my blood"* (16:18). **Judah proposes exactly the operation Scripture says the
ground refuses to perform.** See [[Do Not Cover My Blood]] and [[Blood]].

***"Twenty pieces of silver."*** ***ʿEśrîm kāsep*** — **and the price is the law's, to the shekel.**
Leviticus 27:5 fixes the valuation of a male between five and twenty years old at **twenty shekels**,
and Joseph is seventeen. **Genesis names no other slave price**, and the figure is the one the
sanctuary tariff will later assign to a boy of exactly his age. The parallel with the thirty pieces of
Zechariah 11:12 and Matthew 26:15 is much older than either. See
[[Twenty Pieces of Silver - The Price of a Brother]] and [[Slavery and Servitude]].

***"Midianites who were merchants passed by … and sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites."*** **The verse has a
seam in it, and the wiki does not sew it shut.** The caravan is *Ishmaelite* at 37:25, 27, and 28b;
*Midianite* at 37:28a and 37:36; and when Genesis next mentions the sale it is *Ishmaelite* again
(39:1). **Read strictly, Midianites pull him out of the pit and sell him to Ishmaelites**, which leaves
the brothers as bystanders to the transaction they proposed. **Read as synonyms — which Judges 8:24 does
outright, calling Midianites Ishmaelites in a single clause — the two names are one caravan.** The three
readings are set out on
[[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]].

**And the chapter never says the brothers took the money.** Genesis gives the price and not the payee.
It is the sort of gap the book leaves often, and this one has been filled by every retelling since —
including Psalm 105:17, *"Joseph was sold for a servant"*, and Acts 7:9, *"the patriarchs, moved with
jealousy against Joseph, sold him into Egypt."* See
[[Sold for a Servant - Joseph's Sale in Psalm 105 and Acts 7]].

### 37:29–35 — two men tear their clothes, and one of them knows why

> **29** Reuben returned to the pit; and saw that Joseph wasn’t in the pit; and he tore his clothes.
> **30** He returned to his brothers, and said, “The child is no more; and I, where will I go?”
> **31** They took Joseph’s coat, and killed a male goat, and dipped the coat in the blood.
> **32** They took the coat of many colors, and they brought it to their father, and said, “We have found
> this. Examine it, now, whether it is your son’s coat or not.”
> **33** He recognized it, and said, “It is my son’s coat. An evil animal has devoured him. Joseph is
> without doubt torn in pieces.”
> **34** Jacob tore his clothes, and put sackcloth on his waist, and mourned for his son many days.
> **35** All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted. He
> said, “For I will go down to Sheol to my son mourning.” His father wept for him.

***"Reuben returned to the pit."*** **He was not there for the sale**, which is the only thing the
chapter does to soften him, and **it is also what makes his silence afterwards total.** He tears his
clothes at 37:29; his father tears his clothes at 37:34; **the same gesture, five verses apart, for the
same boy — and the firstborn who knows the boy is alive stands in the room while the father buries
him.** Genesis gives him no further word in the chapter.

***"The child is no more; and I, where will I go?"*** ***Hayyeled ʾên wa-ʾănî ʾānâ ʾănî-bāʾ*** — **and
the grammar of the grief is about himself.** Two clauses: the boy is gone, and *where do I go?* **It is
the eldest son's exposure he names, not the boy's fate**, and [[Jacob]] will use the first clause back
at him six chapters later: *"Joseph is no more"* (42:36). **Reuben answers it then with the least
persuasive offer in Genesis** — *"Kill my two sons, if I don't bring him to you"* (42:37) — and is
refused.

***"They took Joseph's coat, and killed a male goat, and dipped the coat in the blood."*** ***Śəʿîr
ʿizzîm*** — a male goat, **and the props are the ones this family uses.** Two kids' skins and a
borrowed garment took the blessing out of a blind father (27:9, 15–16); **one goat's blood and a
stripped garment take a son out of a sighted one.** The full account is on
[[A Garment and a Goat - Jacob Deceives and Is Deceived]]; see also [[Deception]] and
[[The Stolen Blessing]].

***"Examine it, now, whether it is your son's coat or not."*** ***Hakker-nāʾ*** — **and the brothers
tell no lie.** They say *we have found this*, which is false only by implication, and then **hand him a
question**. The sentence they drafted at 37:20 — *an evil animal has devoured him* — **comes out of the
father's own mouth at 37:33**, unprompted, verbatim. **It is the most efficient deception in Genesis
because the deceived party performs it.**

**Note also what they do not say: *your son*, not *our brother*.** And **the phrase does not name
Joseph either.** From 37:19 the brothers refer to him as *this dreamer*, *our brother*, *the child*, and
*your son*, and never once by name.

***Hakker-nāʾ*** **is also the phrase that comes back for [[Judah]] personally, in the next chapter.**
Tamar sends his own pledge to him with the words *"Discern, please, whose are these"* — *hakker-nāʾ* —
and he answers *"She is more righteous than I"* (38:25–26). **The man who composed the sale and the man
who is handed the *hakker-nāʾ* are the same man, one chapter apart**, and the placement of Genesis 38
is probably the reason. That page is not yet written; the pairing is left for it.

***"It is my son's coat."*** **True. Everything he infers from it is false.** Jacob reasons correctly
from real evidence to the wrong conclusion, and **Genesis gives him no scene in which he learns
otherwise until 45:26.** ***Nākar***, *recognise*, **is the verb that will govern the reunion**:
*"Joseph saw his brothers, and he recognised them, but made himself strange to them" … "they didn't
recognise him"* (42:7–8). **The recognition motif of the Joseph cycle is planted in a lie about a
coat.**

***"Jacob tore his clothes, and put sackcloth on his waist."*** ***Śaq*** — **the first sackcloth in the
Bible.** It becomes the standard dress of mourning and of repentance for the rest of the canon, from
David over Abner to the king of Nineveh to the prophets, **and it enters Scripture on a man mourning a
son who is not dead.** See [[Suffering]] and [[Burial and the Grave]].

***"All his sons and all his daughters."*** **Daughters, plural, and Genesis has named one.** [[Dinah]]
is the only daughter of Jacob the book records; 46:7 also speaks of *"his daughters, and his sons'
daughters"*. **The roster of 35:23–26 counted twelve sons and no daughters at all**, and here the
household that assembles has more women in it than any list in Genesis admits. See
[[The Twelve Sons and the Lists That Reorder Them]].

**And the comforters include the murderers.** *All his sons* rose up to comfort him — **ten of whom know
exactly where the boy is**, and one of whom knows and did not sell him. **Genesis reports the scene
without a syllable of comment**, and it is the last thing the chapter does before Egypt.

***"I will go down to Sheol to my son mourning."*** ***ʾĒrēd … šəʾōlâ*** — **and this is the first
occurrence of *šəʾôl* in the Bible.** The Hebrew Bible's realm of the dead is named for the first time
by a father who thinks his son is in it, and **Jacob will say the sentence twice more, both times about
[[Benjamin]]**: *"you will bring my gray hairs down to Sheol with sorrow"* (42:38), and again at
44:29. **Judah then quotes it back to Joseph's face as the argument that breaks him** (44:31, 34). **The
refrain that starts here is what ends the cycle**, and the wiki leaves the full account of it for the
pericopes that complete it. See [[Sheol]] and [[Life and Death]].

***"He refused to be comforted."*** ***Wayməʾēn ləhitnaḥēm*** — **and the verb is *nāḥam*, the one
Genesis uses of God at 6:6.** Jacob mourns *many days* and will still be mourning at 42:38 and 45:28,
**twenty-two years later**. See [[Divine Grief and Relenting]].

### 37:36 — an owner, a title, and the last word of the chapter

> **36** The Midianites sold him into Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh’s, the captain of the
> guard.

***"Potiphar."*** **The first Egyptian personal name in the Bible.** ***Pôṭîpar*** is a short form of
Egyptian *pꜣ-dj-pꜣ-Rꜥ*, **"he whom Ra has given"** — the fuller form of which Genesis also supplies at
41:45 as *Potiphera*, Joseph's father-in-law, **a different man with effectively the same name.** See
[[Potiphar]].

***"An officer of Pharaoh's."*** ***Sārîs*** — **a court official**, and in later Hebrew and in most of
its biblical occurrences **a eunuch**. Whether the word carries that sense here is disputed, and
Genesis 39, which turns on the man's wife, is usually read as evidence that it does not. **The wiki
notes the question and does not settle it.** See [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]].

***"The captain of the guard."*** ***Śar haṭṭabbāḥîm*** — **literally *chief of the slaughterers*.**
*Ṭabbāḥ* is a butcher or a cook, and the title is usually taken as *chief executioner* or *captain of
the bodyguard*; **Genesis 40:3 puts the royal prison in his house**, which fits either reading. **The
chapter that opens with a father's coat closes with a slaughterer's title**, and the boy who was thrown
in a pit is now inside an institution that has one.

***"Sold him into Egypt."*** **[[Egypt]] is where the family goes when the land fails** — Abram at 12:10,
and the whole household at 46:6 — **and this is the only time somebody is sent there against his will.**
The descent that will become the Exodus starts as a freight consignment. See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]] and
[[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].

## People

- [[Joseph]] — **seventeen, favoured, informing, dreaming, and silent from verse 18 on.** He brings an
  *evil report* the text will not characterise, receives a coat he cannot work in, volunteers two dreams
  nobody asked for, answers his father's errand with *"here I am"*, is stripped, pitted, and sold for
  twenty shekels. **He says nothing after 37:16**, and what he said in the pit is supplied five chapters
  later by the men who ignored it (42:21)
- [[Jacob]] — **makes the coat, keeps the dream, sends the boy to Shechem, and authors the lie himself.**
  He loves one son in the construction the narrator used of his own marriage, hears a dream and *guards
  the word*, dispatches the dreamer sixty miles alone to ask after the *šālôm* of men who cannot speak it,
  and on being handed a coat says the sentence his sons had drafted. **The first sackcloth in Scripture
  is his, and the first *Sheol***
- [[Reuben]] — **wins the argument and loses the boy.** He amends murder to a cistern, and the narrator
  has to state the rescue the speech does not contain. **He is absent for the sale**, tears his clothes
  at the empty pit, asks *"where will I go?"*, and then stands among *all his sons* comforting a father
  he could undeceive in one sentence
- [[Judah]] — **proposes the sale, and argues it on profit.** *"What profit is it if we kill our brother
  and conceal his blood?"* — *mah-beṣaʿ*, unjust gain — followed by the chapter's only appeal to kinship,
  *"he is our brother, our flesh"*, used to justify a slave sale. **His brothers listened to him**, which
  the chapter never says of Reuben
- [[Potiphar]] — **new page.** Named in the last clause: *sārîs* of Pharaoh and *śar haṭṭabbāḥîm*,
  chief of the slaughterers. **The first Egyptian personal name in the Bible**, and Joseph's owner for
  the whole of Genesis 39
- [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] — **new page.** Present only as a possessive — *"an officer of
  Pharaoh's"* — and unnamed, as every Pharaoh in Genesis and Exodus is. The page is opened here because
  this is where he first appears; chapters 39–47 are his
- [[Bilhah]], [[Zilpah]] — **called *his father's wives* at 37:2**, which is the third designation
  Genesis has used for them in three chapters: *concubine* (35:22), *servant* (35:25), *wife* (30:4;
  37:2). **The contradiction is flagged, not resolved.** Neither speaks
- [[Dan (person)]], [[Naphtali]], [[Gad]], [[Asher]] — **the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah**, named in
  37:2 only by their mothers, and the ones the *evil report* was about. **None of the four is named
  individually anywhere in the chapter**
- [[Simeon]], [[Levi]] — not named, and their chapter is why Shechem is a strange place to send a boy
  alone. See [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]]
- [[Benjamin]] — **absent, and the reason 37:3's *son of his old age* does not add up.** He is younger
  than Joseph, and the phrase is Jacob's account of his own favouritism rather than the narrator's
  arithmetic
- [[Rachel]] — **dead since 35:19, and spoken of as living at 37:10.** *"Will I and your mother … bow
  ourselves down to you?"* The four readings of the discrepancy are set out in the 37:5–11 section above
  and the wiki adopts none
- [[Leah]] — not named; **the second reading of 37:10 makes her the mother meant**, and Genesis never
  calls her Joseph's mother
- [[Dinah]] — not named; the *"all his daughters"* of 37:35 is the only plural the book gives, and she
  is the only one of them it has ever named
- [[Ishmael]] — not present, but **his descendants carry Joseph to Egypt**, and the caravan's other name
  belongs to [[Keturah]]'s son Midian. **The men who take him out of the land are Abraham's other
  grandchildren**
- **The man in the field (37:15–17)** — unnamed, two lines, and **the hinge of the chapter**: without his
  overheard *"let us go to Dothan"* Joseph goes home. **No page**, per the wiki's practice for anonymous
  single-line figures

## Places

- [[Dothan]] — **new page.** *Dōtān*, fifteen miles north of Shechem on the trunk road to the coast and
  Egypt; **where the brothers are found, the pit is, and the sale happens.** The only other narrative set
  there is Elisha and the Aramean army at 2 Kings 6:13–23, **which is also a story about a trap and men
  who cannot see what is in front of them**
- [[Shechem (city)]] — the errand's stated destination, and the town [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]] emptied
  three chapters ago. **The brothers are pasturing on it; the boy is sent to it alone; Genesis does not
  remark on either**
- [[Hebron]] — *"the valley of Hebron"*, ***ʿēmeq ḥebrôn***, **a phrase found nowhere else in the
  Bible**, for a town three thousand feet up. It is where Jacob has just buried Isaac (35:27–29) and
  where the coat is brought back to him
- [[Canaan]] — 37:1, *"the land of his father's travels"*; the land the chapter's protagonist is carried
  out of
- [[Gilead]] — where the caravan is coming from, with the balm the trade route ran on and the prophets
  remembered (Jeremiah 8:22)
- [[Egypt]] — named three times (37:25, 28, 36) and always as a **destination for goods**. Joseph goes
  down with the spices; **the only involuntary descent in Genesis**
- [[Midian]] — the *Midianites who were merchants* of 37:28 and 37:36; a people named for [[Keturah]]'s
  son, whose designation the chapter uses interchangeably with *Ishmaelite*
- [[Sheol]] — **named for the first time in the Bible**, at 37:35, by a man who expects to arrive there
  mourning. Jacob says it twice more (42:38; 44:29) and [[Judah]] quotes it back at 44:31

## Themes

- [[Envy]] — the chapter's engine, and its vocabulary escalates in four steps: *hated* (37:4), *hated all
  the more* (37:5, 8), *envied* — ***qānāʾ*** (37:11), the root behind God's own *jealous*
- [[Love]] — *"Israel loved Joseph more than all his children"*, the same comparative the narrator used
  of Rachel over Leah (29:30), producing the same second verb
- [[Deception]] — a coat, a goat, and a question; **the brothers tell no outright lie and the father
  supplies the sentence they wrote for him**
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — two dreams, uninterpreted, unattributed to God, disputed by everybody, and
  correct. **Genesis never says who sent them**
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — twenty shekels, the sanctuary tariff's exact valuation for a male of
  seventeen (Leviticus 27:5), paid to nobody the text names
- [[Blood]] — *"conceal his blood"* (37:26), the operation Genesis 4:10 says the ground refuses; and the
  goat's blood that does the concealing instead
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — the *kətōnet* given, stripped, dipped, and delivered. **The word is God's
  garment for Adam and the priest's tunic**
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — *šālôm* twice in one verse, in an errand to men who *"couldn't speak
  peaceably to him"*
- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — [[Reuben]] acts like the eldest and is not obeyed; [[Judah]] speaks
  and *"his brothers listened to him"*. **The chapter shows the succession moving without announcing it**
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — Reuben's three-clause intervention, the only one anybody attempts, which
  succeeds at preventing murder and fails at everything else
- [[Suffering]] — the first sackcloth in Scripture, and a mourning that refuses comfort for twenty-two
  years
- [[Burial and the Grave]] — a body that does not exist, mourned in full form; and the Bible's first
  *Sheol*
- [[Life and Death]] — *"the child is no more"*, said of a living boy, twice, by two different men
- [[Sin]] — attempted fratricide, a sale, and a sustained lie, and **the narration passes no verdict on
  any of it**
- [[Language and Speech]] — *dibbâ*, the ambiguous *evil report*; *baʿal ha-ḥălōmôt*, the name they use
  instead of his; and *hakker-nāʾ*, a question that does the work of a lie
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — *"will you indeed reign over us?"*, **the first time *mālak* is used of an
  Israelite**, spoken in scorn nine verses after Genesis promised Jacob kings
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — *mah-beṣaʿ*, *what profit*, as the stated reason not to commit a murder

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *Joseph Sold into Slavery — Genesis
  37:1–36*, and nothing in the text argued for changing it. **37:1–2 open the last *toledot* of the
  book** and 37:36 closes on Potiphar; **38:1 turns to Judah and Tamar and does not come back for a
  chapter.** A split at 37:11 (the dreams) and 37:12 (the errand) was considered and rejected: the
  dreams are the stated reason for the conspiracy — *"we will see what will become of his dreams"* —
  and the two halves lose that link if they are read apart. **The chapter is one of the cleanest units
  in Genesis**, which the rest of this cycle mostly is not.
- **On the title, kept from the plan.** *Joseph Sold into Slavery* names the event a reader will search
  for. **Alternatives considered and rejected**: *The Coat and the Pit* (memorable, and it buries the
  sale); *This Dreamer Comes* (now used for the connection page instead, where a phrase-title belongs);
  *Joseph and His Brothers* (names the whole cycle, not this pericope). **No other book has a passage
  page by this title**, so no `(Book)` qualifier is needed.
- **On what was created, and the judgment calls in it.**
  - **[[Potiphar]]** — unavoidable. He is named, titled twice, and owns the protagonist for the whole of
    Genesis 39.
  - **[[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]]** — created on a possessive, which is a judgment call. `AGENTS.md`
    requires the period qualifier from the first appearance rather than retrospectively, and **this is
    the first appearance**: the figure whose court Joseph enters is introduced here as Potiphar's
    master. The alternative — waiting until 39:1 or 41:1 — would have meant either a dangling link or a
    bare mention with nowhere to hang, and **the page follows the precedent of
    [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]]** exactly.
  - **[[Dothan]]** — a place page for a site that is named twice, is where the pericope's central act
    happens, and carries one substantial later narrative (2 Kings 6). **Thinner sites than this have
    pages** — [[Esek]], [[Sitnah]], [[The Tower of Eder]].
  - **Four connection pages**: [[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]],
    [[Twenty Pieces of Silver - The Price of a Brother]],
    [[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]], and
    [[Sold for a Servant - Joseph's Sale in Psalm 105 and Acts 7]].
- **On what was deliberately not created.**
  - **The man in the field (37:15–17) gets no page.** He is unnamed, speaks two lines, and is the hinge
    of the chapter — and the wiki's standing practice for anonymous single-line figures, set at the
    midwife of 35:17, is to keep them on the passage page. **The rabbinic identification of him as an
    angel is recorded and not adopted**, on the same footing as the Septuagint readings this wiki
    reports elsewhere.
  - **No page for the Ishmaelite or Midianite traders as a group.** [[Ishmael]] and [[Midian]] and
    [[Keturah]] already hold that material, and the textual problem has its own connection page.
  - **No `Sheol` page was created** — it exists already, filed under places, and was written for Job;
    this pericope adds the Genesis material and the note that 37:35 is the word's first occurrence.
  - **No connection page yet for Jacob's Sheol refrain.** It starts here and is completed at 42:38,
    44:29, and 44:31, where [[Judah]] turns it into an argument. **A page written now would be three
    quarters forward reference**, so it is left for the pericope that closes it, and the refrain is
    tracked on [[Sheol]] and [[Jacob]] meanwhile.
  - **No page for the *hakker-nāʾ* pairing with Genesis 38:25.** Same reason: it belongs to the
    Judah-and-Tamar pericope, which is next in the plan, and the passage page above states the link so
    it is not lost.
- **A contradiction flagged and not resolved: Joseph's mother at 37:10.** [[Rachel]] died at 35:19, and
  Jacob's rebuke speaks of *"your mother"* as a party to a future bowing. **Four readings are set out in
  the 37:5–11 section — rhetorical recitation, [[Leah]] as the mother meant, composite sources, and a
  promise about a household rather than a guest list — and the wiki adopts none.** It is worth noting
  that on the fourth reading the text is vindicated by its own sequel: the brothers bow four times and
  the father never does.
- **A contradiction flagged and not resolved: who bought Joseph.** *Ishmaelites* at 37:25, 27, 28b, and
  39:1; *Midianites* at 37:28a and 37:36. **Read strictly the two are different parties and the brothers
  are not the sellers**; read as overlapping designations, which Judges 8:24 does explicitly, it is one
  caravan. The three readings are on
  [[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]] **and the wiki adopts none.**
- **A contradiction carried forward, not newly created: Bilhah and Zilpah's status.** *Wives* here at
  37:2; *concubine* at 35:22; *servant* at 35:25; *wife* at 30:4. **This verse is the one
  [[Return to Bethel; the Deaths of Rachel and Isaac]] pointed forward to**, and it supplies the third
  term without reconciling the three. Flagged on [[Bilhah]] and [[Zilpah]].
- **A tension recorded rather than flagged as a contradiction: *"the son of his old age"*.** [[Benjamin]]
  is younger. The wiki treats 37:3 as **the narrator reporting Jacob's own reason**, not as an
  arithmetical claim, and notes that Jacob calls Benjamin *"the child of his old age"* in the same words
  at 44:20.
- **On the chapter's silence about God, recorded as a fact about the text and not as a theological
  claim.** Neither divine name occurs in Genesis 37. The wiki notes it, notes that
  [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] is the only comparable chapter in the cycle, and notes that 39:2
  breaks the silence in the second sentence of the next Joseph chapter. **It does not argue from the
  silence** — the dreams are not attributed to God either, and Genesis's own retrospect on the whole
  affair (45:5–8; 50:20) is put in Joseph's mouth, not the narrator's.
