# The Reconciliation with Esau

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 33:1–20

## Summary

**The meeting the whole previous chapter dreaded takes eleven verses, resolves without a word of
the fear that produced it, and is followed by nine more verses in which the two brothers walk in
different directions anyway.**

Three movements: the meeting itself (1–11), a negotiation about traveling together that ends with
Esau leaving and Jacob not following him (12–17), and Jacob's arrival, purchase, and altar at
Shechem (18–20). **The staged gift of five hundred eighty animals, the five scripted messengers,
the two companies built for a strike that never comes — all of it turns out to answer a threat
Esau never makes.** He runs, embraces, falls on Jacob's neck, kisses him, and weeps, before a
single word of diplomacy has been spoken. Everything Jacob built overnight at the Jabbok was aimed
at a man who did not need managing.

**The chapter completes the word the last one ended on.** Peniel was named for a face — *"I have
seen God face to face, and my life is preserved"* (32:30) — and here Jacob says of his brother,
*"I have seen your face, as one sees the face of God"* (33:10). The noun that governed every
calculation about Esau in chapter 32 is now spoken directly to him, and the comparison runs the
opposite direction from what the staged gift assumed: not *cover my face so he cannot see it*, but
*your face is like the face I have already survived seeing.* See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

**And the reconciliation is not total agreement.** Esau offers to travel together, twice, and is
declined twice — first the offer to go ahead of him, then the offer to leave an escort. Jacob tells
him, *"I will lead on gently… until I come to my lord to Seir"* (33:14); Esau leaves for Seir, and
Jacob goes to Succoth and then Shechem instead. **Genesis records no later journey to Seir in this
pericope or ever**, and does not say whether the itinerary was a plan later abandoned, a courtesy
neither man expected to be kept, or something closer to what the family has done with words
before. The wiki holds the gap open rather than deciding it; see the Notes below and
[[Deception]].

### 33:1–3 — the approach, and seven bows

> **1** Jacob lifted up his eyes, and looked, and, behold, Esau was coming, and with him four
> hundred men. He divided the children between Leah, Rachel, and the two servants.
> **2** He put the servants and their children in front, Leah and her children after, and Rachel
> and Joseph at the rear.
> **3** He himself passed over in front of them, and bowed himself to the ground seven times, until
> he came near to his brother.

**The four hundred men arrive exactly as reported at 32:6, and do nothing.** Chapter 32 built an
entire night of prayer and diplomacy on a number that turns out to describe an escort rather than
an army. Genesis does not say Jacob's fear was foolish; it simply lets the reader watch the danger
fail to materialize without comment.

**The order of the household is a ranking, stated twice** (here and again at 33:6–7): servants and
their children first, Leah and hers next, Rachel and Joseph last — **the position of greatest
exposure given to those Jacob values least, and the position of greatest safety to the wife and
son he loves most.** The passage does not soften this; it is the same ranking implied by *"he loved
also Rachel more than Leah"* at 29:30, now expressed in logistics rather than a sentence.

**Bowing seven times before a superior is attested Ancient Near Eastern court protocol** — the
Amarna letters have Canaanite vassals prostrating themselves *"seven times and seven times"* before
Pharaoh — and Jacob, who has just been told at the Jabbok that he has *prevailed* with God and men,
performs the vassal's gesture in front of the brother he told his servants to call *my lord* four
times over (32:4, 5, 18). **He goes first, alone, walking toward the largest possible danger ahead
of everyone he has just arranged by rank to survive it** — the one moment in the chapter where the
staged diplomacy of chapter 32 and an act of real personal risk are the same gesture.

### 33:4 — the embrace

> **4** Esau ran to meet him, embraced him, fell on his neck, kissed him, and they wept.

**Five finite verbs, and Esau performs all of them before Jacob's third bow could have finished.**
Nothing in the text explains the change from the man who planned murder for after his father's
funeral (27:41) to the man who runs. Genesis gives no intervening scene, no report of a change of
heart, and no reason — the same silence it kept about what happened to Esau in the twenty years
between 28:9 and this verse.

**The verbs recur, almost verbatim, at two later reunions this family has not reached yet**: Joseph
*"fell on his brother Benjamin's neck, and wept… and kissed all his brothers, and wept on them"*
(45:14–15), and Jacob himself, on the other end of a reunion, when Joseph *"fell on his neck, and
wept on his neck a good while"* (46:29) — where Jacob's own reply, *"since I have seen your face,
that you are still alive"* (46:30), answers this chapter's *"I have seen your face"* one generation
later. See the new connection page
[[Fell on His Neck and Wept - The Running Embrace in Genesis and Luke]], which also follows the
formula into Luke 15:20's prodigal son, where the Greek of the parable and the Septuagint's Greek
of this verse are close enough to be the same sentence.

### 33:5–7 — the household, presented in order

> **5** He lifted up his eyes, and saw the women and the children; and said, “Who are these with
> you?” He said, “The children whom God has graciously given your servant.”
> **6** Then the servants came near with their children, and they bowed themselves.
> **7** Leah also and her children came near, and bowed themselves. After them, Joseph came near
> with Rachel, and they bowed themselves.

**Every group bows**, exactly as Jacob did — the gesture of vassalage extended down through the
whole household, none of them told to and none of them declining.

***"The children whom God has graciously given your servant"*** — ***ḥānan***, the root behind
***ḥēn***, favor, the word this chapter is about to run three more times (33:8, 10, 15). **Jacob
credits the children to God in the same breath he calls himself Esau's servant**, crediting one
relationship while performing deference in the other. See [[Favor and Grace]].

### 33:8–11 — the gift, refused, and the word *blessing*

> **8** Esau said, “What do you mean by all this company which I met?” Jacob said, “To find favor
> in the sight of my lord.”
> **9** Esau said, “I have enough, my brother; let that which you have be yours.”
> **10** Jacob said, “Please, no, if I have now found favor in your sight, then receive my present
> at my hand, because I have seen your face, as one sees the face of God, and you were pleased with
> me.
> **11** Please take the gift that I brought to you, because God has dealt graciously with me, and
> because I have enough.” He urged him, and he took it.

**Esau's first line is a question, not an accusation** — he does not yet know what the staggered
herds of chapter 32 were for, and Jacob's answer, *"to find favor in the sight of my lord"* (33:8),
is the first of three occurrences of the theme's formula in this chapter (see also 33:10, 15) — the
same words Abraham used of the three visitors (18:3) and Lot of the angels (19:19), a petitioner's
idiom, now aimed at a brother rather than at God or his messengers.

***"I have enough"*** (***yeš-lî rāb***) answers ***"I have enough"*** (***yeš-lî kōl***) in Jacob's
own mouth two verses later — **the two brothers each declare sufficiency using slightly different
words for it**, Esau's the more modest *much*, Jacob's the more absolute *everything* — and neither
claim is contested by the narrator.

**The word Jacob uses for the gift in verse 11 is not the word he used in verse 10.** Verse 10 calls
it ***minḥātî***, *my present* — the ordinary word for a gift or tribute, the same one used through
chapter 32's staged messages. Verse 11 calls it ***birkātî***, *my blessing* — the WEB, like most
versions, renders both as *gift* and *present*, flattening a distinction the Hebrew keeps. **Jacob
offers Esau, in these words, the thing this book's title for it always means elsewhere**: what he
took by disguise in chapter 27 is what he now, unprompted, calls the herds he is pressing on the man
he took it from. Genesis does not have either brother remark on the word. See [[Blessing]] and
[[The Stolen Blessing]].

**And *"I have seen your face, as one sees the face of God"*** answers the previous chapter directly
— see the Summary above. **The favor language, the blessing language, and the face language all
converge in these four verses**, on the one person in Genesis whom Jacob has most wronged and has
just spent a whole night preparing to appease.

### 33:12–17 — two declined offers, and a divergence

> **12** Esau said, “Let us take our journey, and let us go, and I will go before you.”
> **13** Jacob said to him, “My lord knows that the children are tender, and that the flocks and
> herds with me have their young, and if they overdrive them one day, all the flocks will die.
> **14** Please let my lord pass over before his servant, and I will lead on gently, according to
> the pace of the livestock that are before me and according to the pace of the children, until I
> come to my lord to Seir.”
> **15** Esau said, “Let me now leave with you some of the folk who are with me.” He said, “Why?
> Let me find favor in the sight of my lord.”
> **16** So Esau returned that day on his way to Seir.
> **17** Jacob traveled to Succoth, built himself a house, and made shelters for his livestock.
> Therefore the name of the place is called Succoth.

**Esau makes two offers of continued company and Jacob declines both**, the first with a stated,
plausible reason (nursing flocks and small children cannot keep an army's pace), the second with the
chapter's third *"find favor"* — declining an escort by saying the favor already shown is enough.
**Neither refusal is unreasonable on its own terms.**

***"Until I come to my lord to Seir"*** (33:14) **is the one concrete plan Jacob states in the whole
chapter, and Genesis never shows him carrying it out.** Esau departs for Seir the same verse; the
very next verse has Jacob traveling to Succoth, well short of Edom, and the chapter ends with him
settled at Shechem, further still. No later chapter in Genesis records Jacob visiting Seir. **The
wiki does not adopt a verdict here.** It is not the family's usual method — no disguise, no false
statement of fact, nothing Esau could catch him in — but it is a stated itinerary that the text does
not show fulfilled, in a family whose last four pericopes have each turned on someone's word not
meaning quite what it said. See [[Deception]] and the Notes below.

**Succoth is named for what has just happened**, in the naming pattern this wiki has tracked since
Beersheba and Peniel: *booths*, ***sukkōt***, because Jacob built shelters there — **the same
register as Peniel and the opposite of Mahanaim**, which was named for a division that had not yet
occurred (32:2). See [[Names and Naming]] and [[Succoth]].

### 33:18–20 — Shechem, the purchase, and the altar

> **18** Jacob came in peace to the city of Shechem, which is in the land of Canaan, when he came
> from Paddan Aram; and encamped before the city.
> **19** He bought the parcel of ground where he had spread his tent, at the hand of the children of
> Hamor, Shechem’s father, for one hundred pieces of money.
> **20** He erected an altar there, and called it El Elohe Israel.

***"Came in peace"*** — ***šālēm***, from the same root as ***šālôm*** — answers, with quiet
precision, the vow he made at this same spot in the theology if not the geography twenty years
earlier: *"so that I come again to my father's house in peace"* (28:21). **He has not yet reached
his father's house**, but the word he asked for at Bethel is the word Genesis uses of his arrival
here.

**The second land purchase any patriarch makes in Canaan**, after Abraham's field and cave at
Machpelah (23:16–18) — **and the only one bought by a living man for himself to live on rather than
to bury someone in.** A hundred pieces of silver, paid to *"the children of Hamor"*, is a private
transaction with no witnesses named and no negotiation reported, in sharp contrast to the public,
theatrical bargaining Abraham conducts at the city gate. See [[Machpelah]] and
[[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].

**The altar's name embeds the name Jacob was given two chapters ago.** ***ʾĒl ʾĕlōhê Yiśrāʾēl*** —
*God, the God of Israel* — is the first time *Yiśrāʾēl* appears in the text as anything other than
the man's own name, and Jacob is the one who attaches it to God rather than waiting for God to use
it of him again (that does not happen until 35:10). **He names an altar the way the chapter has
already named a place**, for what has just happened to him, except that what has just happened here
is two chapters old. See [[The Names of God]] and [[Names and Naming]].

**Hamor is named for the first time, and does nothing.** He is introduced only as *"Shechem's
father"*, identifying whose land this is; neither he nor his sons speaks or acts in this pericope.
Genesis 34 will make him a central figure. See [[Hamor]].

## People

- [[Jacob]] — **crosses the whole chapter from vassal to landowner.** He bows seven times, ranks his
  own household by how expendable they are, offers Esau the word *blessing* for a gift of animals,
  states a plan to follow his brother to Seir that the text never shows him keeping, and ends the
  chapter buying land and naming an altar after his own new name.
- [[Esau]] — **runs, embraces, weeps, and is generous with no explanation given for any of it.** He
  asks a plain question about the staged gift he does not yet understand the reason for, refuses it
  once, accepts it when pressed, offers his company twice, and is declined twice without apparent
  offense. He returns to Seir and exits the pericope.
- [[Leah]], [[Rachel]], [[Joseph]] — presented to Esau in the order Jacob arranged them, the same
  ranking of exposure the chapter opens on; none of them speaks.
- [[Hamor]] — named once, as Shechem's father and the seller of the field; new page, thin until
  Genesis 34.
- [[God]] — invoked twice by Jacob, both times as the reason for what he already has: *"the children
  whom God has graciously given"* (33:5) and *"God has dealt graciously with me"* (33:11). Not a
  speaking or acting character in this pericope.

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *The Reconciliation with Esau —
  Genesis 33:1–20*, the whole chapter, and it needed no revision. **33:1 opens on Esau's arrival**,
  the thing the previous chapter spent thirty-two verses dreading, and **33:20 closes the chapter on
  an altar**, with 34:1 opening on a different character (Dinah) and a different location's trouble.
  A split at 33:17 — separating the meeting from the Shechem purchase — was considered and rejected:
  the purchase and the altar name (built on *Yiśrāʾēl*) only make sense as the far side of the
  reconciliation the chapter is named for, and the chapter is short enough that dividing it would
  produce two passage pages smaller than most single scenes elsewhere in the cycle.
- **On the title.** *The Reconciliation with Esau*, kept from the plan. **Alternatives considered
  and rejected**: *The Face of God and the Face of Esau* (accurate to the wiki's own reading, but
  imports an interpretive claim into a title where the plan's plainer wording already worked), and
  *El Elohe Israel* (names only the last three verses).
- **On the unfulfilled promise to follow to Seir, which the page declines to adjudicate.** Three
  readings are available and the wiki adopts none: (1) a plan genuinely intended and later
  abandoned for reasons Genesis does not report; (2) a courtesy neither brother expected literally —
  compare Esau's own two declined offers, where politeness is extended and not pressed; (3) the
  family's pattern, continued — a technically true statement (Jacob does eventually settle in
  Canaan, in the general direction stated) used to end an encounter on the asker's terms. Unlike
  27:19 or 27:24, nothing here is a false statement of present fact, so the wiki does not classify it
  with the flat lies [[Deception]] tracks elsewhere in this family; it is recorded as an open
  question instead.
- **On Hamor, created now rather than at Genesis 34.** He is named at 33:19 only as *"Shechem's
  father"*, with no action of his own — the same situation [[Dinah]] was in when she was named at her
  birth, eleven chapters before her own chapter. The page is created thin now, on that precedent, and
  will be filled out when Genesis 34 is ingested.
- **On what was created.** **One place page**, [[Succoth]], flagged in advance for a future
  collision with the Exodus 12:37 site of the same name. **One person page**, [[Hamor]], deliberately
  thin. **One connection page**,
  [[Fell on His Neck and Wept - The Running Embrace in Genesis and Luke]], tracing 33:4's five verbs
  through Joseph's two reunions (45:14–15; 46:29–30, not yet ingested) into Luke 15:20, where the
  Septuagint's Greek and the parable's Greek are close to identical.
- **On what was updated rather than created.** [[Shechem]], whose page already anticipated this
  purchase from [[The Call of Abram]]'s ingest; and the connection page
  [[The Altar at Shechem and the Covenant at Shechem]], whose *(not yet ingested)* marker on
  33:18–20 is now cleared.
- **On stale forward-reference markers cleared elsewhere in the wiki.** *(not yet ingested)* markers
  pointing at some or all of Genesis 33 were cleared on [[Jacob]], [[Esau]], [[Leah]],
  [[Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright]], [[The Stolen Blessing]], and
  [[Wrestling at the Jabbok]].
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Dinah and the sons of Hamor by name (34:1–31); Jacob's
  return to Bethel (35:1–7); Esau's *toledot* and settlement in Seir (36:1–43); Joseph's two
  reunions (45:14–15; 46:29–30). All are given as plain scripture references rather than wikilinks,
  so none is a dangling link.
