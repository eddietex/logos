# From Adam to Noah

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 5:1–32

## Summary

Ten generations, nine deaths, and one man who does not die.

Genesis 5 is the first place in the Bible where the text becomes a *register*. After four chapters
of scenes — a garden, a conversation with a serpent, a murder in a field, a song about killing — the
narrative stops narrating and starts filing. Each entry takes the same form, and it takes it nine
times: *X lived n years, and became the father of Y. X lived after he became the father of Y m
years, and became the father of other sons and daughters. All the days of X were n+m years, then he
died.* Nothing else. No place, no act, no word, no evaluation. The form is so rigid that the two
places it breaks are the whole point of the chapter.

It opens by starting over. *"This is the book of the generations of Adam"* — the second of the ten
*toledot* formulas that segment Genesis, and the only one called a **book** (*sēper*), the first
book named inside the Bible. And rather than picking up from Genesis 4:26 where the last pericope
left off, it reaches back past everything: *"In the day that God created man, he made him in God's
likeness. He created them male and female, and blessed them. On the day they were created, he named
them 'Adam'."* Those two verses are Genesis 1:27–28 compressed, and they do two things at once.
They put the whole genealogy under the heading of the image and the blessing — whatever else is
about to be recorded, it happens to creatures God made like himself and told to multiply. And they
quietly delete Cain. The line of [[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]] is not refuted or
condemned here; it is simply not the book of the generations of Adam. Adam's third son is treated
as his first.

Then the register's most theologically loaded sentence, and it is the third verse: *"Adam lived one
hundred thirty years, and became the father of a son in his own likeness, after his image, and
named him Seth."* The two words of 1:26 — image and likeness — are picked up and applied, for the
only time in Scripture, to a human father and a human son. Whatever the image of God is, it is
heritable; it survives Genesis 3 and it travels down the list. Genesis does not say whether
anything *else* travels down the list with it, and readers have been filling that silence with
doctrine ever since (see Notes).

After that the machine runs. Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared — five entries, thirteen verses,
identical to each other but for the numbers, and each one ending in the same three words. **Then he
died.** The refrain lands eight times in the chapter, and its cumulative effect is the argument:
the sentence of 2:17 and 3:19 is not a threat any more, it is a fact of record, and the way the
text conveys it is by making the reader feel the repetition. The longevity is the counterpoint —
930, 912, 905, 910, 895, 962 — and the longevity loses every time. Methuselah lives 969 years,
longer than anyone in the Bible, and the entry ends the way all of them end.

Then the form breaks. The seventh entry begins normally, and at the second sentence it swerves:
*"After Methuselah's birth, Enoch walked with God for three hundred years."* No one has walked with
God before. And where every other entry closes with a total and a death, this one closes with a
total and a disappearance: *"All the days of Enoch were three hundred sixty-five years. Enoch
walked with God, and he was not found, for God took him."* His years are the shortest in the
chapter by more than five centuries — 365, the number of days in a solar year, which has never
looked accidental to anyone — and the verb that should be *he died* is *God took him*. Against
eight recorded deaths, one man is subtracted from the list rather than removed from it. See
[[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]].

Methuselah follows, with the longest life and the ordinary ending, and then the form breaks a
second time — this time for a speech. Lamech fathers a son and, alone among the ten, says something
about him: *"This one will comfort us in our work and in the toil of our hands, caused by the
ground which Yahweh has cursed."* It is the only sentence anyone speaks in Genesis 5, the only
mention of the curse, and the only time the divine name **Yahweh** appears in a chapter that
otherwise says *God*. And it points backwards, precisely, at 3:17 — *"cursed is the ground for your
sake; in toil you will eat of it"* — which means that ten generations in, the sentence passed in
Eden is still what the ground feels like, and someone is still waiting for it to lift. He names the
boy **Noah**, *rest*. See
[[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]].

The chapter's last verse does not close him out. Nine men get a death; Noah gets an age and three
sons — *"Noah was five hundred years old, then Noah became the father of Shem, Ham, and Japheth"* —
and the register stops mid-entry, holding the total open. The genealogy has run out of past and is
now pointing at something. What it is pointing at arrives four verses later, when the earth is
found to be corrupt.

So the chapter that looks like the most skippable in Genesis is doing three things at once. It is
carrying the image of God from the first man to the man who will survive the flood, across a
distance the reader can feel because it is measured in years. It is proving the sentence of Genesis
3 by attrition rather than by argument. And it is placing two exceptions in the machinery — a man
who is taken and a man who is named for relief — so that the reader arrives at Genesis 6 already
knowing that this is a world where death is the rule and where the rule has twice been interrupted.

### The chronology, if the numbers are added up

Genesis never does this arithmetic itself, and the results are not part of what the text asserts —
but the numbers are given, they are internally consistent in the Hebrew text, and adding them up
produces the framework nearly every traditional chronology of the world was built on. Dating from
the creation of Adam as year 0:

| Generation | Age at fathering | Remaining years | Total | Born | Died |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adam | 130 | 800 | 930 | 0 | 930 |
| Seth | 105 | 807 | 912 | 130 | 1042 |
| Enosh | 90 | 815 | 905 | 235 | 1140 |
| Kenan | 70 | 840 | 910 | 325 | 1235 |
| Mahalalel | 65 | 830 | 895 | 395 | 1290 |
| Jared | 162 | 800 | 962 | 460 | 1422 |
| Enoch | 65 | 300 | 365 | 622 | *taken* 987 |
| Methuselah | 187 | 782 | 969 | 687 | 1656 |
| Lamech | 182 | 595 | 777 | 874 | 1651 |
| Noah | 500 | — | — | 1056 | — |

Three things fall out of the table that the text never mentions:

- **Methuselah dies in the year of the flood.** Noah is born in 1656 − 600 = 1056, and the flood
  comes in his six hundredth year (7:11) — year 1656, which is the year Methuselah's 969 run out.
  The longest life in Scripture ends exactly at the waters. Genesis does not say he drowned and
  does not say he was spared; it says nothing at all.
- **Lamech dies five years before the flood**, at 777 — see the note on his namesake's
  seventy-seven below.
- **Every generation from Adam to Lamech is alive at the same time.** Lamech is born in 874 and
  Adam dies in 930, so for fifty-six years the first man and the ninth generation of his line are
  contemporaries. Noah, born in 1056, is the first man in the chapter who never met Adam; his
  father did.

The overlaps are also how the chapter answers a question it never raises: how anything from before
the flood could be known afterwards. In this chronology, nobody in the line ever needs more than
one intermediary.

## People

- [[Adam]] — the register's title and its first entry. Genesis 5:1–5 completes his passage from
  *humanity* to *this man*: 5:2 uses *ʾādām* of the male-and-female pair God named, and 5:3 uses it
  of one man who lived 930 years. Both senses in three verses, which is the whole ambiguity of his
  name held on one page.
- [[Seth]] — fathered *"in his own likeness, after his image"*, the one verse in Scripture that
  applies the language of 1:26 to human descent. He is Adam's third son and this chapter's first,
  because the book of Adam's generations does not run through Cain.
- [[Enosh]] — the third generation; formally entered here (born to Seth at 105, dead at 905) after
  Genesis 4:26 gave him nothing but the note that in his time people began to call on Yahweh's
  name.
- [[Kenan]] — the fourth generation, and one of the six names in this list with a near-twin in
  Cain's: *Qênān* against *Qayin*, the same consonantal root as the name of the first murderer.
- [[Mahalalel]] — the fifth generation, and the only name in the line that is a sentence about God:
  *"praise of God"*, or *"God shines"*.
- [[Jared]] — the sixth generation, whose 962 years are the second longest in the Bible, and whose
  name (*"descent"*) later apocalyptic literature read as a coded reference to the descent of the
  watchers.
- [[Enoch (son of Jared)]] — the seventh from Adam; the man who *"walked with God"* and was taken.
  The first exception the register makes, and the only person in the Bible before Elijah who does
  not die.
- [[Methuselah]] — the eighth generation, the longest-lived human being in Scripture at 969, and by
  the chapter's own arithmetic a man whose life ends in the year the flood comes.
- [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] — the ninth generation, and the only person in the chapter who
  speaks. His one sentence names his son for the relief of a curse imposed in Genesis 3 and still
  in force.
- [[Noah]] — the tenth generation and the chapter's open end: given an age and three sons, but no
  total and no death, because his story has not happened yet.
- [[God]] — named *ʾĕlōhîm* throughout, and never quoted. He creates, blesses, and names in the
  first two verses, and after that he does exactly one thing in the chapter: he takes Enoch. The
  divine name *Yahweh* appears once, and only inside Lamech's speech.
- [[Eve]] — unnamed and present only as the plural of 5:2, *"he created them male and female"*. The
  chapter's ten entries name ten fathers and no mothers, which is a feature of the form and worth
  noticing rather than reading past.
- [[Cain]] — conspicuously absent. Genesis 5 runs the line of Adam and does not mention that Adam
  had older sons, or that one killed the other. The omission is the loudest thing in the chapter
  after the deaths.
- Shem, Ham, and Japheth — named in the last verse and given no page yet; the flood narrative and
  Genesis 10 are where they become people rather than a list.

## Places

No place is named anywhere in Genesis 5. It is the first passage in the Bible with no geography at
all — no Eden, no east, no Nod, no city. The chapter measures in years instead of in distance from
somewhere, which is a genuine shift: for four chapters, location has been how Genesis tracked the
consequences of the fall, and here time takes over the job.

## Themes

- [[Life and Death]] — the chapter's argument, delivered as a refrain. *"Then he died"* eight
  times, against lifespans of nine centuries, is Genesis 2:17 being proved by attrition. The two
  entries that omit the refrain are the two that matter.
- [[Image of God]] — 5:1 restates it and 5:3 transmits it. This is the single verse in Scripture
  that says the image passes from parent to child, and it is placed after the fall, not before.
- [[Walking with God]] — introduced here, in a phrase with no antecedent, and immediately given to
  the one man in the register who is not buried by it. Noah will be described the same way at 6:9.
- [[Blessing]] — 5:2 puts the blessing of 1:28 at the head of the list, and the list is that
  blessing being carried out: *"became the father of other sons and daughters"*, nine times, is
  what *be fruitful and multiply* looks like from inside.
- [[Creation]] — the chapter opens by summarizing Genesis 1 rather than continuing Genesis 4, which
  is how it signals that this is a fresh start on the whole story rather than the next scene.
- [[Curse]] — named once, by Lamech, ten generations after it was pronounced. 5:29 is the only
  verse between Genesis 3 and the flood that treats the curse on the ground as an ongoing condition
  someone is waiting to be released from.
- [[Sin]] — never mentioned. Genesis 5 records the wages without naming the cause, and then Genesis
  6:5 supplies the cause in a single verse. The silence is doing work.

## Notes

- **The *toledot* formula, and what it does here.** *"This is the book of the generations of…"*
  (*sēper tôledōt*) is the second of the ten headings that segment Genesis, after 2:4. It is the
  only one called a *book*, which has led some to propose that 5:1–32 is a self-contained document
  the compiler quoted; that is a reasonable inference from the wording and it is not provable. The
  formula's consistent function elsewhere in Genesis is to hand the narrative from a family to the
  one branch of it the story will follow, usually by disposing of the other branch first — Ishmael
  before Isaac, Esau before Jacob. Genesis 4 and 5 are the pattern's first run.
- **Genesis 5 has no Cain in it.** The chapter says Adam fathered Seth at 130 and then *"other sons
  and daughters"*, and never mentions the two sons the reader has just spent a chapter with. Three
  readings, and they are not exclusive: that the register is a *line of descent* and simply records
  the branch that continues, as all such registers do; that the silence is deliberate erasure, the
  book of Adam's generations declining to enter a murderer; or that Genesis 4 and Genesis 5 come
  from different sources with different interests, and the seam is visible here. What is not in
  doubt is the effect — after 4:24, Cain's line is never traced again in Scripture.
- **"In his own likeness, after his image" (5:3).** The two nouns of 1:26 (*ṣelem*, image;
  *demût*, likeness) return in reversed order and with the referent changed: there, God makes man
  in his image; here, man fathers a son in his. Nothing else in the Bible says this. Two large
  conclusions have been drawn from it, and the verse supports the first much more clearly than the
  second. **(1)** The image survives the fall and is transmitted — which Genesis 9:6 confirms
  independently, grounding the prohibition of murder in the image *after* the flood, and which
  James 3:9 assumes. **(2)** That the verse also means fallen*ness* is transmitted — that Seth
  inherits Adam's corrupted state, not merely his status. This is the standard Augustinian and
  Reformed reading, and Romans 5:12 is the load-bearing text for it, not this one. Genesis 5:3 says
  only that a son resembled his father in the terms Genesis 1 used of God and man; it does not say
  what changed between 1:26 and 5:3, and the chapter's placement — the sentence of death now
  running through every entry — is what invites the darker reading rather than the wording itself.
- **The numbers, and what to do with them.** The lifespans have been read at least four ways.
  *Literally*, as reported ages, with the decline after the flood (Genesis 11's halving, and Psalm
  90's seventy years) taken as a real physiological change. *Symbolically or numerologically* — the
  figures are strikingly non-random, resolving into round numbers plus multiples of five and seven,
  and Mesopotamian scribes are known to have used schematic numbers for prestige. *As a different
  unit*, the oldest of the ad-hoc harmonizations (months, or seasons), which fails immediately:
  Enoch fathering Methuselah at 65 months would make him five. Or *as honorific*, with the length
  of a life functioning the way a long reign does in a king list. The comparison usually made is the
  **Sumerian King List**, which assigns its eight antediluvian kings reigns of 18,600 to 43,200
  years, also drops sharply after its own flood, and shares the pattern of a shortening lifespan
  across a flood boundary. Genesis's numbers are an order of magnitude smaller and its people are
  fathers rather than kings, which is either evidence of a shared and demythologized tradition or
  evidence of a sober record beside an inflated one, depending on where the reader starts.
- **The Hebrew, Greek, and Samaritan numbers do not agree.** This chapter is one of the largest
  numerical divergences between the textual traditions. The Septuagint generally adds a hundred
  years to the fathering ages of most of the patriarchs while subtracting the same from the
  remainder, keeping the totals close but pushing the flood roughly six centuries later; the
  Samaritan Pentateuch differs from both. The chronology above, and every chronology derived from
  it — including Ussher's 4004 BC creation and 2348 BC flood — rests on the Masoretic figures. This
  also means the striking coincidences below are features of one textual tradition, not of "the
  Bible's numbers" in general.
- **Methuselah's name, and the year he dies.** The arithmetic putting his death in the flood year
  is exact in the Masoretic text and is very old — it appears in rabbinic tradition, which reads
  the flood's seven-day delay in 7:4 as the week of mourning for him. The etymology often attached
  to it is shakier. *Metûšelaḥ* is usually parsed as *"man of the dart/weapon"* (*šelaḥ*, a missile
  or javelin), or *"man of Shelah"* if the second element is a divine or personal name. The popular
  gloss *"when he dies, it shall be sent"* — reading *mût* + *šelaḥ* and taking "it" as the flood —
  is a homiletic reconstruction, not a philological one: it requires a verb form the name does not
  have. The coincidence of dates is real and worth keeping; the name is not evidence for it.
- **Enoch's 365 years.** The shortest life in the chapter, by 530 years, and the number of days in
  a solar year. Enoch is also the seventh generation, and seven is the chapter's other structural
  number. Attempts to read him as a solar or calendrical figure — his Mesopotamian counterpart in
  some reconstructions is Enmeduranki, the seventh antediluvian king, associated with the sun-god
  and with divination — go back a long way and are speculative in proportion to their detail. The
  minimum honest statement is that the number is conspicuous, that the text does not comment on it,
  and that *1 Enoch*, which is largely a calendrical work, clearly thought it meant something.
- **"He was not found, for God took him."** The Hebrew is *wəʾênennû kî lāqaḥ ʾōtô ʾĕlōhîm* —
  literally *"and he was not, for God took him"*. The verb *lāqaḥ* is the same one used of Elijah
  in 2 Kings 2, and of God's rescue of the psalmist from Sheol in Psalm 49:15 and 73:24. The
  Septuagint renders it *metethēken*, "translated/transferred", which is the word Hebrews 11:5
  picks up. A minority reading takes the verse as an unusually gentle euphemism for an early death,
  which is grammatically available; it has almost no support in the reception history, where the
  verse is uniformly read as a departure without dying. See
  [[The Taking of Enoch - Genesis 5.24 in Hebrews and Jude]].
- **"Walked with God" is a strange phrase and a new one.** The verb is the *hithpael* of *hālak* —
  a reflexive or iterative form, *walked back and forth*, *walked habitually* — with the
  preposition *ʾet*, "with". It appears here (twice), of Noah at 6:9, and then never again of a
  human being in exactly this form; God himself *"walks"* in the garden in the same stem at 3:8.
  Abraham is told to *"walk before"* God (17:1), which is not the same preposition and arguably not
  the same relation. What the phrase means is not defined anywhere. It is plainly commendatory and
  plainly intimate, and the Septuagint's paraphrase — *euērestēsen*, "he pleased God" — is an
  interpretation, and the one Hebrews 11:5 builds its argument on. See [[Walking with God]].
- **Lamech's sentence, and its wordplay.** *"He named him Noah (nōaḥ), saying, 'This one will
  comfort us (yenaḥămēnû)…'"* — the name is from *nûaḥ*, to rest, and the verb is from *nāḥam*, to
  comfort. Different roots that sound alike, which is exactly what Genesis does with names (Cain,
  Seth, Eve, Babel all work this way): the gloss is a play on the sound, not an etymology. What
  makes 5:29 remarkable is not the pun but the theology in it — this is the only place between the
  garden and the flood where anyone is shown reckoning with the curse of 3:17 as a present burden,
  and the only hope expressed by any human being in these chapters. Whether it is answered is a
  real question: God does promise never again to curse the ground (8:21), and Noah is the first
  vinedresser (9:20), and wine is the standard biblical figure for the relief of toil (Psalm
  104:15) — and the same chapter ends with him drunk and his family cursed. See
  [[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]].
- **777, and the man who claimed seventy-seven.** Lamech son of Methuselah lives 777 years (5:31).
  Lamech son of Methushael, seven generations back in the other line, declared himself avenged
  *"seventy-seven times"* (4:24). Two men with the same name, one number, and opposite uses of it —
  one a boast about killing, one a lifespan ending five years short of the flood. Genesis does not
  connect them. Given how much else in these two chapters is deliberately paired, most readers take
  it as one more piece of the design rather than as chance; it cannot be demonstrated either way.
- **Ten generations, and the shape of the list.** Adam to Noah is ten; Shem to Abram (11:10–26) is
  also ten. Ten-generation spans are a known organizing device, and Matthew 1 will do something
  comparable with three sets of fourteen — including, in Matthew 1:17, a count that requires
  omitting kings the Old Testament names, which is the clearest biblical evidence that ancient
  genealogies could be selective by design without being thought inaccurate. That bears directly on
  whether Genesis 5 is meant to be a complete father-to-son chain with no gaps, which is the hinge
  of every argument about dating the world from it. The Hebrew *yālad* in the *hiphil* — "became
  the father of" — is used elsewhere of more distant descent, so gaps are linguistically possible;
  the tight interlocking of the ages here is the strongest argument that this particular list does
  not intend them.
- **Nine hundred years, and then a hundred and twenty.** The chapter ends at 5:32, and three verses
  later Yahweh says *"his days will be one hundred twenty years"* (6:3). Whether that is a new
  lifespan cap or a countdown to the flood is disputed and belongs to the next pericope, but the
  juxtaposition is deliberate: Genesis puts its longest lives immediately before the sentence that
  ends them.
