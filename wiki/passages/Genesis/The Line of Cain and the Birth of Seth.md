# The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 4:17–26

## Summary

The murderer's story does not end when he leaves. It becomes a civilization.

[[Cain and Abel]] closed with Cain walking out of Yahweh's presence into a land whose name is the
sentence he was given — *wandering*. The very next sentence has him father a son and build a city.
Those two facts are not reconciled and are not even acknowledged as a problem: *"Cain knew his
wife. She conceived, and gave birth to Enoch. He built a city, and called the name of the city,
after the name of his son, Enoch."* The first city in the Bible is founded by a man under sentence
to have no fixed place, and named after a child rather than after himself — the one soft detail in
the whole line.

Then the text does something it has not done before: it goes flat. Five names in a chain, no
scenes, no speech, no comment — *"To Enoch was born Irad. Irad became the father of Mehujael.
Mehujael became the father of Methushael. Methushael became the father of Lamech."* This is the
Bible's first genealogy, and it functions the way genealogies function all through Genesis: as a
fast-forward. Generations pass in a verse so that the narrative can stop where it means to stop.

It stops at **Lamech**, the seventh generation from Adam, and everything the list was building
arrives at once. He takes *two* wives, Adah and Zillah — the first departure from the one flesh of
2:24 anywhere in Scripture, reported without a word of judgment. His three sons are given not
births but **vocations**, each one the head of a whole human practice: Jabal *"the father of those
who dwell in tents and have livestock"*, Jubal *"the father of all who handle the harp and pipe"*,
Tubal Cain *"the forger of every cutting instrument of brass and iron."* Herding, music,
metalwork. In three verses the Bible accounts for the origins of nomadic economy, of art, and of
technology, and it places all three in the line of the first murderer.

Then Lamech speaks, and it is a poem — the first since the man's two lines in 2:23, and the first
in the Bible spoken by anyone other than Adam:

*"Adah and Zillah, hear my voice. You wives of Lamech, listen to my speech, for I have slain a man
for wounding me, a young man for bruising me. If Cain will be avenged seven times, truly Lamech
seventy-seven times."*

The form is careful — matched line pairs, his name in the middle of it — and what it is careful
about is a killing. He has killed a man for a wound; where Cain's murder was answered by a
sentence and then covered by a divine promise of sevenfold vengeance on anyone who touched him
(4:15), Lamech takes that promise, appropriates it, and multiplies it by eleven. Cain's protection
was something granted to him; Lamech's is something he declares for himself. The trajectory of the
chapter is exact: a man kills and hides, then his descendant kills and sings about it.

That is where Cain's line stops. It produces the city, the tent, the harp, the forge, and a song
in praise of disproportionate revenge, and the narrative never returns to it — the flood will end
it without comment.

And then, without transition, Genesis rewinds four chapters' worth of ground and starts again from
the two people it left in Eden's shadow: *"Adam knew his wife again."* The verb and the phrasing
deliberately repeat 4:1, so the reader is put back at the beginning of the family. Eve bears a
third son and names him **Seth**, and her sentence is the passage's second speech and the answer
to its first: *"for God has given me another child instead of Abel, for Cain killed him."* The
Hebrew behind *another child* is *zeraʿ ʾaḥēr* — literally *another seed*, the word of 3:15. She
has now lost both of the sons she had, one to death and one to exile, and she names the third by
what he replaces.

The last verse is eleven words long in English and carries more weight than the eleven that
precede it. Seth has a son, Enosh — a name that is simply another Hebrew word for *human* — and
then: *"At that time men began to call on Yahweh's name."* Cain's line ends in a boast made to two
wives. Seth's line begins in a sentence about people addressing God. Genesis 4 sets those two
endings side by side and declines to editorialize, which is how it has handled everything since
the garden gate closed.

## Key verses

> **17** Cain knew his wife. She conceived, and gave birth to Enoch. He built a city, and called
> the name of the city, after the name of his son, Enoch.

> **18** To Enoch was born Irad. Irad became the father of Mehujael. Mehujael became the father of
> Methushael. Methushael became the father of Lamech.

> **19** Lamech took two wives: the name of the first one was Adah, and the name of the second one
> was Zillah.

> **20** Adah gave birth to Jabal, who was the father of those who dwell in tents and have
> livestock.

> **21** His brother’s name was Jubal, who was the father of all who handle the harp and pipe.

> **22** Zillah also gave birth to Tubal Cain, the forger of every cutting instrument of brass and
> iron. Tubal Cain’s sister was Naamah.

> **23** Lamech said to his wives, “Adah and Zillah, hear my voice. You wives of Lamech, listen to
> my speech, for I have slain a man for wounding me, a young man for bruising me.

> **24** If Cain will be avenged seven times, truly Lamech seventy-seven times.”

> **25** Adam knew his wife again. She gave birth to a son, and named him Seth, saying, “for God
> has given me another child instead of Abel, for Cain killed him.”

> **26** A son was also born to Seth, and he named him Enosh. At that time men began to call on
> Yahweh’s name.

## People

- [[Cain]] — leaves the passage as he entered it, but the passage is his: he fathers a son, builds
  the first city, and heads the line that produces both civilization and Lamech. This is the last
  Genesis says of him.
- [[Enoch (son of Cain)]] — Cain's firstborn, who does nothing in the narrative and has the first
  city named after him. His name is the one the Sethite line will reuse for a very different man.
- [[Lamech (son of Methushael)]] — the seventh from Adam, the Bible's first polygamist, and the
  author of its first recorded poem after Adam's. What he uses it for is a boast about killing.
- [[Jabal]] — *"the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock"*: the origin of nomadic
  herding, and the ancestral shape of the patriarchs' own way of life.
- [[Jubal]] — *"the father of all who handle the harp and pipe"*: the origin of music, string and
  wind, given a single verse and no evaluation.
- [[Tubal Cain]] — *"the forger of every cutting instrument of brass and iron"*: the origin of
  metallurgy, and of the edge that can be a plough or a blade.
- [[Seth]] — the third son, named for what he replaces, and the head of the line the rest of the
  Bible actually follows: Noah, Abraham, David, and in Luke's genealogy, Jesus.
- [[Enosh]] — Seth's son, whose name means *man*, and in whose days *"men began to call on
  Yahweh's name"* — the Bible's first note of anything like public worship.
- [[Eve]] — speaks for the second and last time in Scripture, naming Seth and crediting God, as she
  did at 4:1. Both of her recorded sentences are about a son given.
- [[Adam]] — named as *Adam* rather than *"the man"* for the first time in a narrative sentence
  (4:25), which is where the word finishes becoming a name.
- Adah and Zillah — Lamech's two wives, the first women named in the Bible after Eve, and the
  addressees of its first poem. Genesis gives them no words and no story; what they carry is the
  fact of the marriage itself.
- Irad, Mehujael, Methushael — links in the chain, with no act, word, or gloss attached. Their
  function is to make the distance from Cain to Lamech felt.
- Naamah — Tubal Cain's sister, named and then dropped. Genealogies of this period name women only
  when something hangs on them, which is why her bare mention has attracted so much speculation
  (see Notes).
- [[God]] — does not speak or act anywhere in 4:17–24. He returns in the last two verses only as
  the one Eve credits and the one whose name people begin to call on.

## Places

- [[Enoch (city)]] — the first city in the Bible, built by Cain and named for his son. Its location
  is never given and it is never mentioned again.
- [[Nod]] — the land Cain settled in at 4:16, and by the sequence of the text the ground the city
  is built on, though 4:17 does not say so.

[[Eden]] is not named. It is the first passage since Genesis 2 in which it is neither a place nor a
bearing — the story has finally travelled far enough east that it has stopped measuring.

## Themes

- [[City and Civilization]] — herding, music, and metalwork all enter Scripture here, in seven
  verses, in the line of the man who was cursed. Genesis reports the goods and their parentage
  together and refuses to separate them.
- [[Vengeance]] — the sevenfold guarantee God gave Cain becomes Lamech's seventy-sevenfold threat.
  The Bible's first two data points on retribution are a protection and its perversion.
- [[Sin]] — no act here is called sin and no one is warned, but the thing warned about in 4:7 is
  visibly running: it is now hereditary in fact if not in doctrine, and it has learned to sing.
- [[Marriage]] — 4:19 is the first marriage in Scripture that is not the one flesh of 2:24, and the
  narrative neither defends nor condemns it.
- [[Work]] — the vocations of 4:20–22 are the first specialized trades after the two of 4:2, and
  the first that are *inherited* rather than chosen. Cain's ground refused him; his descendants
  find other ground to stand on.
- [[Life and Death]] — a genealogy is a way of narrating death, and this one has a killing at both
  ends of it: Abel at the start, Lamech's unnamed man at the finish.
- [[Judgment]] — conspicuously absent. God pronounces nothing on the city, the bigamy, or the
  boast. The chapter that gave judgment its fullest form so far ends with none.

## Notes

- **A wanderer builds a city.** The sentence of 4:12 was *"you will be a fugitive and a wanderer in
  the earth"*, and 4:17 is the second verse after Cain settles in a land named Wandering. Genesis
  states the tension and leaves it. The readings on offer: that the city is Cain's defiance of the
  sentence, an attempt to manufacture the security God took away; that it is what a sentence looks
  like once it is lived in, since a man still has to eat and his children still have to sleep
  somewhere; or that the narrator simply had two traditions and set them next to each other. The
  first reading is the most common in Christian commentary — Augustine's *City of God* is built on
  Cain's city as the archetype of the earthly city, founded by a fratricide, as Rome was — but it
  is inference, not text. Genesis says only that the man who lost the ground built walls.
- **Whose city?** The Hebrew of 4:17 is *wayehî bōneh ʿîr* — *"and he was building a city"* — with
  a pronoun that most naturally refers to Cain, the subject of the sentence before. A minority
  reading takes Enoch as the builder (the city is named for him, after all, which is a slightly odd
  thing for a father to do), and one text-critical proposal reads the name of Irad in 4:18 as
  connected to *ʿîr*, "city". WEB and nearly all translations take Cain as the builder. Nothing
  much turns on it except the irony, which is sharpest if it is Cain.
- **The three culture-founders, and what Genesis does not say about them.** *"Father of"* here is
  not biological — Jabal is *"the father of those who dwell in tents"*, an idiom for the founder of
  a guild or a way of life. Each is given a domain: livelihood (Jabal), art (Jubal), technology
  (Tubal Cain). The striking thing is the total absence of evaluation. These are unambiguously good
  things — the patriarchs will live in tents with livestock, the temple will run on harps, and the
  cutting instruments of brass and iron are the tools of every trade in the ancient world — and
  Genesis places their origin in the cursed line without either condemning them or excusing the
  line. Two over-readings are worth naming as over-readings: that the passage means to taint
  culture with Cain's guilt, and that it means to celebrate human progress. It does neither. What
  it does is refuse to let the reader sort the world into a clean half and a dirty one.
- **Jubal's instruments.** *Kinnôr* is the lyre or harp — David's instrument (1 Samuel 16:23) and
  the one hung on the willows in Psalm 137. *ʿÛgāb* is a pipe or flute, and appears only four times
  in the Bible. So the pair is strings and winds: music is introduced as a category, not as a
  single instrument. That Jubal and Jabal are near-homophones of each other and of *yôbēl*, the
  ram's-horn "jubilee" trumpet, is almost certainly deliberate wordplay.
- **Tubal Cain and the metal.** *"Brass and iron"* is WEB's rendering; the metals are bronze
  (*neḥōšet*, copper or its alloy) and iron (*barzel*). Historically these belong to two separate
  ages a millennium apart, which is one of the places where Genesis 4's picture of the earliest
  world does not line up with an archaeological chronology; readers who take these chapters as a
  differently-shaped kind of history and readers who take them strictly have both had to say
  something about it. The name itself contains *Cain*, and *qayin* in cognate Semitic languages
  means *smith* — which suggests the whole family is being told as the story of the metalworkers,
  and puts a small tremor under the name of the murderer himself.
- **Lamech's song is the Bible's first poem after Adam's** (2:23), and the two are worth reading
  together: the first is a man recognizing a person, the second is a man announcing that he has
  destroyed one. It has the shape of Hebrew verse — the second line of each pair restating the
  first (*"hear my voice" / "listen to my speech"*, *"a man for wounding me" / "a young man for
  bruising me"*) — which means the killing was put into a memorable form on purpose. The Hebrew
  tense of *"I have slain"* is ambiguous between a completed act and a threat of one (*"I would
  kill"*), so the poem is either a confession sung as a trophy or a warning issued in advance.
  Either way the moral content is the same, and it is the reason the song is called the Song of the
  Sword.
- **Seventy-seven, or seventy times seven.** The Hebrew *šibʿîm wešibʿâ* is "seventy and seven". In
  the Septuagint it is *hebdomēkontakis hepta*, and the same Greek phrase appears in Matthew 18:22
  where Jesus answers Peter's *"until seven times?"* with it — which can be rendered "seventy-seven
  times" or "seventy times seven". The verbal link is not a coincidence of translation: Jesus is
  quoting the number of the Bible's worst man and reassigning it from vengeance to forgiveness. See
  [[Seventy-Sevenfold - Lamech's Boast and Jesus on Forgiveness]].
- **The first polygamy, reported without comment.** 4:19 is one chapter and sixteen verses after
  *"they will become one flesh"* (2:24), and Genesis says nothing about the discrepancy — no
  sentence, no narrator's aside. What it does instead is put the practice's first instance in the
  mouth of its worst man, and then keep doing this: every polygamous marriage in Genesis (Abraham,
  Jacob, and their households) is narrated as a source of grief. The evaluation is structural
  rather than stated, which is how Genesis usually evaluates. See [[Marriage]].
- **Naamah.** Genealogies of this type name sons; a daughter named with no husband, no child, and
  no act attached is unusual enough that readers have always assumed something is missing.
  *Naʿămâ* means "pleasant". Genesis Rabbah 23:3 identifies her as Noah's wife, and the
  pseudepigraphal *Book of Jubilees* gives Noah's wife a different name (Emzara), so the tradition
  is not unanimous; other rabbinic voices read her as a singer, hearing *nʿm* as a root for sweet
  sound
  and pairing her with her brother Jubal. She is not the Naamah of 1 Kings 14:21, an Ammonite and
  the mother of Rehoboam, though the identical name has caused confusion. The honest summary is
  that the text names her and stops.
- **"Adam knew his wife again" (4:25) restarts the chapter.** The verb *yādaʿ* and the whole
  construction repeat 4:1 exactly, which puts the reader back at the family's beginning and marks
  everything between as a detour. This is also the first place the Hebrew uses *ʾādām* as a plain
  personal name in a narrative sentence, without the definite article — the word finishing its
  passage from *humanity* to *this man*, a transition Genesis 5:1–5 will complete.
- **Seth's name, and the word underneath it.** *Šēt* is glossed by Eve's own verb: *šāt lî ʾĕlōhîm*,
  *"God has set/appointed for me"*. As with Cain at 4:1, the gloss is a sound-play rather than an
  etymology, which is Genesis's consistent habit. Note also that she says *ʾĕlōhîm*, "God", where at
  4:1 she said *Yahweh* — a small shift that source critics read as a seam and others as her
  speaking after a loss rather than after a gift.
- **"Another seed instead of Abel."** WEB's *"another child"* renders *zeraʿ ʾaḥēr* — *seed*, the
  same word as the *"offspring of the woman"* in 3:15. If 4:1 was the first hope that the promise
  had arrived (and Eve's naming there reads that way), then 4:25 is that hope re-lodged after it
  failed twice over: the first candidate became a murderer and the second was murdered. The line
  the rest of the Bible follows starts here, and Luke 3:38 runs Jesus's descent back through Seth
  by name. See [[The Protoevangelium - Genesis 3.15 and the Seed of the Woman]].
- **Enosh means "man".** *ʾĕnôš* is one of the Hebrew Bible's several words for a human being, used
  most often in poetry and often with a note of frailty in it — *"what is man, that you think of
  him?"* (Psalm 8:4) uses it. So the Sethite line opens with a name meaning *appointed* followed by
  a name meaning *mortal*, and the whole family reads Adam, Appointed, Mortal.
- **"Then men began to call on Yahweh's name" is a genuinely contested half-verse.** Three
  problems sit in it. **(1)** The verb *huḥal* is from *ḥālal*, "to begin" — but an identically
  spelled root means "to profane", and a strand of rabbinic reading (Genesis Rabbah 23:7, followed
  by Rashi) takes it that way: *then men began to profane the name of Yahweh*, making 4:26 the
  origin of idolatry rather than of worship. Almost all modern translations take "began". **(2)**
  *Qārāʾ bešēm YHWH* — "call on the name" — covers proclamation as much as petition; it is what
  Abram does at his altars (12:8) and what the phrase means when Joel and Paul use it of salvation.
  **(3)** It sits awkwardly against Exodus 6:2–3, where God tells Moses that to Abraham, Isaac, and
  Jacob *"I was not known to them by my name Yahweh"*. Genesis 4:26 has the name in public use ten
  generations before Abraham. This tension is one of the classic pillars of the documentary
  hypothesis — a source (J) that uses the name from the beginning against one (P) that has it
  revealed at the burning bush — and it is also answerable within the text, by reading Exodus 6:3 as
  a statement about knowing God's character rather than about hearing the syllables. Both readings
  are long-standing and neither is a fringe position. See [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].
- **The two endings are the point.** Nothing in 4:17–26 is judged, and nothing needs to be, because
  the chapter's architecture does the work. Cain's line runs seven generations to a man who makes a
  song out of killing. Seth's line runs two generations to a sentence about people calling on God.
  Genesis 5 will then take the seventh generation of *Seth's* line — a second Enoch — and say that
  he *"walked with God, and he was not found, for God took him"* (5:24). Seventh from Adam in one line
  boasts; seventh from Adam in the other is taken. That symmetry is almost certainly deliberate,
  and it is the reason the two genealogies are set out the way they are. See
  [[Two Lines from Adam - Cain's Genealogy and Seth's]].
