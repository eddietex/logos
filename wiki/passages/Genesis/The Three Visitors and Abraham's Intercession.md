# The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 18:1–33

## Summary

A meal under a tree at the hottest hour of the day, and then the first argument anyone in the Bible
has with God about justice.

**The chapter is one continuous scene with three movements and no break between them.** Yahweh
appears at the grove (18:1); three men are fed (18:2–8); the promise of a son is given and Sarah
laughs behind the tent flap (18:9–15); the party walks out toward Sodom and Yahweh deliberates aloud
about whether to tell Abraham what he is going to do (18:16–21); and Abraham, left standing on the
road, asks whether the Judge of all the earth can be counted on to do right (18:22–33). The frame is
exact: *"Yahweh appeared to him"* (18:1) and *"Yahweh went his way"* (18:33), with a man who does
not know at the start who has come, and who by the end is negotiating with him.

**Chapter 17 was twenty-two verses of divine speech and one human sentence. This chapter reverses
the ratio.** Abraham speaks in almost every scene, and in the last one he speaks six times and God
answers six times and concedes six times without a single objection.

| | 18:1–8 | 18:9–15 | 18:16–21 | 18:22–33 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Setting | the tent door, noon | the tent door, from inside | the road out, looking down | *"before Yahweh"* |
| Who speaks | Abraham, then *they* | *they*, then **Yahweh**, then Sarah | **Yahweh**, to nobody | Abraham and **Yahweh**, alternating |
| Subject | water, bread, a calf | *"Sarah your wife will have a son"* | *"Will I hide from Abraham what I do?"* | fifty, forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten |
| What is not said | who the visitors are | why one laugh is questioned | what the verdict is | whether ten were found |

### The numbers problem, stated from the text

The chapter never resolves how many people are talking, and the alternation is not a slip — it is
the same seam that runs through [[The Angel of Yahweh]], set here in its most sustained form.

| Verse | The text says |
|---|---|
| 18:1 | *"**Yahweh** appeared to him"* — singular, and told to the reader, not to Abraham |
| 18:2 | *"**three men** stood opposite him"* |
| 18:3 | *"**My lord**, if now I have found favor in **your** sight … from **your** servant"* — singular |
| 18:4–5 | *"wash **your** feet, and rest **yourselves** … **They** said"* — plural |
| 18:9 | *"**They** asked him"* |
| 18:10 | *"**He** said, 'I will certainly return … Sarah your wife will have a son'"* — singular, and only God can say it |
| 18:13 | *"**Yahweh** said to Abraham"* |
| 18:16, 22 | *"**The men** rose up"*; *"**The men** turned from there, and went toward Sodom"* |
| 18:22 | *"but Abraham stood yet before **Yahweh**"* |
| 19:1 | *"**The two angels** came to Sodom"* |

Read as the book stands, the arithmetic works: three come, one of them is Yahweh, two go on ahead and
arrive at Sodom the same evening as *malʾākîm*. What the chapter will not do is say so. Abraham
addresses one and feeds three; the narrator names Yahweh and the characters see men.

***ʾĂdōnāy* or *ʾădōnî*** (18:3). The consonants are the same. The Masoretes pointed the word as the
divine title rather than as an ordinary *my lord, sir* — a reading decision, not a translation, and
the WEB's lower-case *"My lord"* takes it the other way. The whole question of what Abraham knew and
when hangs on vowel points added some fifteen centuries after the consonants.

### The hospitality

***"as he sat in the tent door in the heat of the day"*** (18:1). *Kəḥōm hayyôm* — the siesta hour,
when nobody travels and nobody calls. The visitors arrive at the least plausible moment, and the
verse that has him sitting is the last thing in the chapter he does slowly.

**Genesis reports four bursts of hurry in seven verses.** He *ran* to meet them (18:2); he *hurried*
into the tent and told Sarah *"Quickly"* (18:6); he *ran* to the herd (18:7); the servant *hurried*
to dress the calf (18:7). He is ninety-nine or a hundred years old, three days at most past
[[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]] on the traditional reckoning, and he runs twice in the
noon heat.

**And what he offers is not what he serves.**

| He offers (18:4–5) | He produces (18:6–8) |
|---|---|
| *"a little water"* | — |
| *"a morsel of bread"* — *pat-leḥem* | *"three seahs of fine meal"* — some twenty litres of flour, bread for dozens |
| *"rest yourselves under the tree"* | *"a tender and good calf"*, killed and dressed for three men at noon |
| — | *"butter, milk"* — *ḥemʾâ* and *ḥālāb*, curds and milk |

The gap between the offer and the table is the chapter's entire characterization of him, and it is
done without one word of praise. He also does not sit down: *"He stood by them under the tree, and
they ate"* (18:8) — the host on his feet, serving, while his guests eat.

***"and they ate."*** Genesis says it flatly and the rest of Scripture is more careful. The angel of
Judges 13:15–16 refuses the meal and tells Manoah to offer it to Yahweh instead; Tobit 12:19 has
Raphael say that his eating was only an appearance. Genesis reports a meal and does not explain it,
and the meal is curds and milk served with veal — a combination later halakhah forbids, which
Genesis Rabbah 48 notices and works to account for.

**Sarah is inside the tent for the whole of it.** She makes the bread and does not carry it out; she
is asked after by name (18:9) and answered for by her husband; she hears the promise through a wall
of cloth with her back to the speaker. The chapter's central announcement is delivered about her, in
her hearing, without her being addressed — which is exactly what happened at 17:15–16, and this time
she is close enough to react. See [[Sarah]].

### *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"*

***"Now Abraham and Sarah were old, well advanced in age. Sarah had passed the age of childbearing"***
(18:11). The Hebrew is *ḥādal lihyôt lə-Śārâ ʾōraḥ kannāšîm* — *it had ceased to be with Sarah the
way of women*. **The Bible states menopause once, and this is the verse.** It is the narrator's
comment, not anyone's excuse, and it is placed between the promise and the laugh so that the reader
grants Sarah her arithmetic before she does it.

***"Sarah laughed within herself"*** (18:12). *Wattiṣḥaq*, the verb that is the boy's name. Her
reason is not the same as her husband's at 17:17: he did the years, she does the body — *"After I
have grown old will I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"* *ʿEdnâ*, **pleasure**, occurs nowhere
else in the Hebrew Bible; it shares its consonants with *ʿēden*, and whatever else it carries it is
not a word about fertility statistics. Genesis's two laughs are at two different things, and only
one of them is asked about. See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].

***"Why did Sarah laugh, saying, 'Will I really bear a child, yet I am old?'"*** (18:13). **The
quotation is not what she said.** She said *"my lord being old also"*; Yahweh reports her as saying
only *"yet I am old"*, and drops the clause about her husband's age. The Talmud takes this as the
Torah's own licence to alter a report for the sake of peace in a household (*b. Yebamot* 65b, *Bava
Metzia* 87a), and it is one of the very few places where a rabbinic reading turns on a discrepancy
that is unmistakably there in the text.

***"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"*** (18:14). *Hă-yippālēʾ mē-YHWH dābār.* The root is **p-l-ʾ**,
the word for what is beyond reach — *wonder*, *extraordinary*, the noun behind the *wonders* of the
exodus. The question is closer to *is any thing too wonderful for Yahweh* than to *too difficult*,
and it is the first time in Scripture that the promise is defended by an appeal to God's nature
rather than restated. Jeremiah asks it back twice from a besieged city (32:17, 27), Zechariah asks
it of a remnant (8:6), and Gabriel answers Mary with the Septuagint's wording of this verse (Luke
1:37). See [[Is Anything Too Hard for Yahweh]].

***"I didn't laugh," for she was afraid. He said, "No, but you did laugh."*** (18:15). **This is the
only verse in the Bible in which God speaks directly to Sarah**, and it is a contradiction. It is
also the second time Genesis has a divine question met with a denial — *"Where is Abel your
brother?" … "I don't know"* (4:9) — and the second time the denial is simply overruled. Nothing
follows. She is not punished, the promise is not withdrawn, and three chapters later she names the
laugh herself: *"God has made me laugh"* (21:6).

### *"Will I hide from Abraham what I do?"*

***"The men rose up from there, and looked toward Sodom"*** (18:16). The pivot of the chapter is a
change of view: from a tent door at Mamre, in the hills, to the plain below. Abraham walks out with
them, which is ordinary courtesy for a departing guest and is what puts him on the road when the
next thing is said.

**And the next thing is said to nobody.** *"Yahweh said, 'Will I hide from Abraham what I do?'"*
(18:17). It is a divine soliloquy overheard by the reader — the fourth in Genesis after *"Let us make
man"* (1:26), *"the man has become like one of us"* (3:22), and *"Come, let's go down"* (11:7) — and
the only one whose subject is whether to take a human being into confidence. See
[[Let Us Go Down - The Divine Plural in Genesis]].

***"For I have known him"*** (18:19). ***Kî yədaʿtîw.*** *Yādaʿ* here is not information; it is the
verb of acknowledgment and choice that Amos 3:2 uses — *"You only have I known of all the families
of the earth"* — and this is its first use in that sense. **And it is the only place in Genesis where
election is given a purpose the elect has to carry out**: *"to the end that he may command his
children and his household after him, that they may keep the way of Yahweh, to do righteousness and
justice."*

***Ṣədāqâ ûmišpāṭ*, righteousness and justice — the pair's first occurrence in Scripture.** It
becomes the prophets' standing formula for what God actually requires (Amos 5:24; Isaiah 1:27; 5:7;
Jeremiah 22:3, 15; Psalm 33:5; 89:14), and it arrives here, in a verse about raising children, four
sentences before Abraham uses it on God. The chapter teaches the standard and then has the man apply
it to the one who set it. See [[Righteousness]] and [[Call and Election]].

***"Because the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great"*** (18:20). ***Zaʿăqat Sədōm.*** **A *cry* in
this sense always has a victim behind it.** It is the noun of Abel's blood *"crying to me from the
ground"* (4:10), of Israel's groaning under Egypt (Exodus 2:23; 3:7), and of the widow and orphan in
the law that warns *"if they cry at all to me, I will surely hear their cry"* (Exodus 22:23). Genesis
13:13 called the men of Sodom *"exceedingly wicked and sinners against Yahweh"* and gave the charge
no content; 18:20 supplies the first content it has, and what it says is that somebody down there is
being wronged. See [[Sodom]].

***"I will go down now, and see"*** (18:21). *ʾĒrədâ-nnāʾ wə-ʾerʾeh* — the same descent to inspect
as [[The Tower of Babel]], where *"Yahweh came down to see the city and the tower"* (11:5). **The
anthropomorphism is doing work**: it means the sentence has not been passed. God has a report and
has not yet acted on it, and the verse ends on a bare, unfinished *"If not, I will know"* — which is
precisely the opening Abraham walks into. The rabbinic reading takes it as instruction for human
judges: no verdict on a report, however loud.

### The intercession

***"Abraham came near"*** (18:23). *Wayyiggaš* — the verb for closing the distance, used of armies
joining battle, of Judah stepping up to Joseph (44:18), and later of a priest approaching the altar.
He has been walking beside them; now he stops and closes.

***"Will you consume the righteous with the wicked?"*** **This is the first time in Scripture that
anybody holds God to a standard**, and the ground he does it on is the one God has just named:
*mišpāṭ*, justice, given to Abraham as the thing his household is to be taught, and turned around
inside four verses.

Two things about the request are easy to misread.

**He does not ask for the righteous to be rescued. He asks for the city to be spared *for their
sake*.** *"Will you consume and not spare the place for the fifty righteous who are in it?"* (18:24)
— *wə-lōʾ-tiśśāʾ lammāqôm*, *lift the burden from the place*. The flood saved eight out of a
condemned world; Abraham proposes the opposite arrangement, in which a few righteous hold up the
guilty around them. Nothing before this in the Bible works that way.

**And he never mentions Lot.** His nephew lives in the city he is arguing for, and in a chapter of
fifteen exchanges his name does not come up once. The wiki notes that this is either the argument's
great dignity — a case made on justice and not on family — or the reason it is made at all, and that
Genesis, which was perfectly willing to tell us at 14:14 that Abraham armed his household *"when
Abram heard that his relative was taken captive"*, declines to tell us here. See [[Lot]].

***"Shouldn't the Judge of all the earth do right?"*** (18:25). ***Hă-šōpēṭ kol-hāʾāreṣ lōʾ yaʿăśeh
mišpāṭ.*** **God has not been called a judge before this verse.** He judged at Eden and at the flood
and at Babel; the title is Abraham's, coined in a rhetorical question, and it presumes something the
book has not stated: that God's dealings are answerable to a standard that can be named in advance
and that a human being can see he is meeting. Job will spend thirty chapters on the same premise
without getting an answer (Job 9:24; 34:12–17), and Paul will use the sentence as an axiom — *"For
then how will God judge the world?"* (Romans 3:6). See
[[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].

**The descent is not even.**

| Ask | 50 | 45 | 40 | 30 | 20 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Step down | — | 5 | 5 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| Abraham's framing | *"Will you consume the righteous with the wicked?"* | *"See now, I have taken it on myself … although I am dust and ashes"* | *"He spoke to him yet again"* | *"Oh don't let the Lord be angry"* | *"See now, I have taken it on myself to speak to the Lord"* | *"Oh don't let the Lord be angry, and I will speak just once more"* |
| Yahweh's answer | *"I will spare the whole place for their sake"* | *"I will not destroy it"* | *"I will not do it"* | *"I will not do it"* | *"I will not destroy it"* | *"I will not destroy it for the ten's sake"* |

He tests the first cut small — five — and when it costs nothing he goes down by tens. The apologies
escalate as the numbers fall, and they are apologies for an anger that never appears: **God does not
resist once.** Six requests, six concessions, no counter-argument, no floor, no warning. Whatever
this passage is, it is not a man wearing God down.

***"I am dust and ashes"*** (18:27). *ʿĀpār wāʾēper* — a rhyming pair, and a bold thing to say while
proposing terms. It is Job's phrase too, in his complaint (30:19) and in his last words (42:6), and
in both books it is said by the one person willing to argue.

**Abraham stops at ten, and Genesis does not say why.** The obvious candidates: ten as the smallest
number that constitutes a community rather than a family — the reckoning behind the later *minyan* —
or the point at which the next question would have to be *one*, which is a question the prophets do
ask and get a different answer to (Jeremiah 5:1; Ezekiel 22:30). What the text records is that the
human party ended the conversation. *"Yahweh went his way, as soon as he had finished communing with
Abraham"* (18:33) — as soon as *Abraham* had finished, in the Hebrew's own ambiguity, and both of
them go home.

**And the chapter ends with no verdict.** Nobody says whether there are ten. The answer arrives in
the next pericope and it is not the one that was negotiated: the city is destroyed, so there were
not ten, and *"God remembered Abraham, and sent Lot out of the middle of the overthrow"* (19:29).
The intercession is answered — but by the rescue of the relative Abraham never mentioned, not by
the sparing of the place he argued for.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — who runs twice in the noon heat, promises a morsel and serves a banquet, stands
  while his guests eat, and then, on the road down, becomes the first person in Scripture to argue
  with God about justice. He does it on the standard God has just handed him four verses earlier,
  he does it without once naming the nephew who lives in the city, and he is the one who stops.
- [[Sarah]] — inside the tent for the whole visit. She makes the bread and does not carry it out,
  is asked after by name and answered for by her husband, hears the promise with her back to the
  speaker, laughs at her own body rather than at the arithmetic, denies it, and is contradicted. It
  is the only verse in the Bible where God speaks to her.
- [[God]] — who appears as one and is fed as three, quotes Sarah inaccurately, deliberates aloud
  about whether to take a human being into his confidence, refuses to act on a report without
  going down to look, and then concedes six times in a row without once pushing back.
- [[Isaac]] — unnamed here and dated twice. The promise of 17:21 is repeated in the same formula
  (*"at the set time"*) with a year attached, and this time his mother hears it.
- [[Lot]] — never mentioned, and the reason the chapter matters to him. He is inside the city whose
  survival is being negotiated, and 19:29 will say that what saved him was not the bargain but that
  *"God remembered Abraham"*.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — not the phrase this chapter uses, and the same problem in a larger
  form. Three *men* are fed; one of them speaks and is named *Yahweh*; two walk on and are
  *"the two angels"* by 19:1.

## Places

- [[Mamre (place)]] — the grove at Hebron where he has lived since 13:18, and where the most
  domestic scene in the Abraham cycle happens: a tent door, a tree, the heat of the day.
- [[Sodom]] — looked at from above (18:16), charged with a *cry* for the first time (18:20), and
  argued over for eleven verses without a verdict being given.
- [[Gomorrah]] — named with Sodom in the charge (18:20) and then not again; the whole negotiation
  is about one city.
- [[Hebron]] — the town the grove belongs to, and Abraham's fixed address for the rest of his life.

## Themes

- [[Hospitality]] — the Bible's founding text for it. The offer is a little water and a morsel of
  bread; the table is twenty litres of flour, a calf, and curds. The theme's whole later shape —
  that a guest may be more than a guest, and that a city is judged by how it treats one — is set
  here and paid off in the next chapter.
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — intercession's first appearance in Scripture, and the boldest form
  it ever takes: not a plea for mercy but an argument from justice, made by someone with no office
  and no standing, on behalf of a city he has no tie to.
- [[Judgment]] — the chapter that says judgment is investigated before it is executed (*"I will go
  down now, and see"*), that it can be suspended for the sake of a minority, and that the standard
  it answers to can be stated by a human being in advance.
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣaddîq* seven times in eleven verses, and *ṣədāqâ ûmišpāṭ* in its first
  occurrence in Scripture, in a verse about how Abraham is to raise his household.
- [[Barrenness]] — the promise given a second date and, for the first time, given in Sarah's
  hearing. The narrator states her menopause in the verse before she laughs.
- [[Faith]] — and its limits, on both sides of the tent wall: the man who was credited with
  righteousness for believing (15:6) is not the one who doubts here, and the woman who does is not
  rebuked for it beyond a question.
- [[Call and Election]] — *"For I have known him"* (18:19): the first time being chosen is given a
  content, and the content is teaching a household to do justice.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"He lifted up his eyes and looked"* (18:2); *"looked toward Sodom"*
  (18:16); *"I will go down now, and see"* (18:21). Three sightings, and the decisive one is God's.
- [[The Nations]] — *"all the nations of the earth will be blessed in him"* (18:18), the promise of
  12:3 restated with *gôyim* in place of *families* — and restated in an argument about whether a
  Canaanite city can be spared.
- [[Blessing]] — the promise of 12:2–3 quoted back by God as the reason for taking Abraham into his
  confidence; the blessing is what qualifies him to be told.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — a divine intention disclosed to a human being before it is carried out,
  which is what a prophet is for. Genesis 20:7 will use the word.
- [[Life and Death]] — *"to kill the righteous with the wicked, so that the righteous should be
  like the wicked"* (18:25): the problem of collective destruction, stated before Ezekiel 18 and
  Deuteronomy 24:16 legislate around it.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the man who ended a family quarrel by conceding the land (13:8–9)
  now stands between a city and its sentence.

## Notes

- **On where the pericope ends.** The chapter divides naturally at 18:15 and again at 18:21, and a
  plan could reasonably cut it into two or three. The wiki keeps 18:1–33 whole because the scene
  never breaks: the same three visitors carry the annunciation and the sentence, the walk out to
  the ridge at 18:16 is the hinge that joins them, and the two halves are held together by a
  contrast the chapter is clearly making — the God who is fed under a tree is the God who is
  argued with on the road. Splitting it would put *"Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"* and
  *"Shouldn't the Judge of all the earth do right?"* on different pages, and they are the same
  chapter's two questions.
- **What the visitors are is not answered, and three answers have been given.** *(i)* Yahweh and
  two attendants — the plain reading of the final form, and what 19:1 requires. *(ii)* Three angels,
  with *Yahweh appeared* in 18:1 as the narrator's summary of an appearance made through
  messengers; the Targums lean this way, and Jewish tradition assigns each a job — one to announce
  the birth, one to destroy Sodom, one to rescue Lot (*b. Bava Metzia* 86b). *(iii)* The Trinity —
  Christian from at least Ambrose's *tres vidit, unum adoravit*, "he saw three and worshipped one",
  argued at length by Augustine in *De Trinitate* II, and given its permanent visual form in
  Rublev's icon of about 1425, whose proper title is *The Hospitality of Abraham*. Because Orthodox
  practice does not depict the Father directly, this scene became **the** icon of the Trinity. The
  wiki records reading (iii) as reception rather than as exegesis: Genesis's own interest is in a
  man who did not know who had come, and the New Testament, which quotes this chapter four times,
  never once reads the three that way.
- **The tiqqun sopherim at 18:22.** *"The men turned from there, and went toward Sodom, but Abraham
  stood yet before Yahweh."* The rabbinic lists of *tiqqunê sōpərîm* — the scribes' corrections —
  put this verse near the top and say the original read *"but Yahweh stood yet before Abraham"*,
  altered because *ʿāmad lipnê*, to stand before, is what a servant does in the presence of a
  master. Whether the tradition preserves a real textual change or is a way of pointing at what the
  scene actually looks like is disputed and probably unresolvable; every manuscript witness has the
  received reading. It is worth recording either way, because it is the earliest evidence that
  readers found this chapter's posture startling.
- **Source criticism, briefly.** Genesis 18–19 is assigned to J by near-consensus, and reads as the
  companion piece to Genesis 17 (P): the same promise, once as legal institution and once as a
  meal. Within the chapter, 18:17–19 is widely taken as a later insertion, on the strength of its
  Deuteronomic vocabulary (*the way of Yahweh*, *righteousness and justice*, commanding one's
  children), and 18:22b–33 is sometimes read as a post-exilic addition wrestling with why Jerusalem
  fell. The wiki records the analysis and does not need it: as the book stands, 18:19 is what makes
  18:25 possible, because it is where Abraham is told what the standard is.
- **What the passage does not concede.** It is often summarized as Abraham talking God down, and
  the text will not support that. God offers the first concession before it is asked for in full
  (18:26), never argues, never sets a limit, and never shows the anger Abraham twice apologizes for
  in advance. The one who runs out of nerve is the man. And the chapter's own resolution, one
  pericope later, is more generous than the bargain: the ten are not found, and Lot is brought out
  anyway.
- **The measure of hospitality cuts both ways in the next chapter.** Abraham runs to meet three
  strangers at noon; Lot, sitting in the gate at evening, also runs to meet two and presses them
  hard to come in (19:1–3), and the town they are in demands them at the door. Genesis puts the
  two receptions three verses of narrative apart, and Luke 10:10–12 takes the whole comparison as
  the standard by which a town is judged. See [[Hospitality]].
