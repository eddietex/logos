# Lot and His Daughters

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 19:30–38

## Summary

Nine verses, and they undo everything the previous twenty-nine were spent obtaining.

**Lot's last scene ends where the angel told him to go in the first place.** *"Escape to the
mountains, lest you be consumed!"* (19:17) was the instruction; *"I can't escape to the mountain,
lest evil overtake me, and I die"* (19:19) was the refusal; a whole city was spared to accommodate
it (19:21). And then: *"Lot went up out of Zoar, and lived in the mountain"*. **He arrives at the
mountain anyway, having cost the narrative a concession to avoid it, and Genesis states the reason
in five words without explaining any of it — *"for he was afraid to live in Zoar."*** What he was
afraid of is not said. That the fire would come for the fifth city after all, that a town of
survivors had no use for the refugee whose arrival coincided with the end of their neighbours,
that the plain was uninhabitable — the text supplies none of it, exactly as it supplied no motive
for his wife's turning her head.

***"He lived in a cave with his two daughters."*** ***Məʿārâ*, and it is the first cave in
Scripture.** The word's next appearance in Genesis is Machpelah, which Abraham buys at full price
in front of witnesses as a burial plot and a foothold in the land (23:9, 11, 17, 19–20). **The two
men who separated at Genesis 13 both end in caves, and the difference between them is the whole
point of the intervening chapters**: one is bought, deeded, and used to bury the woman he was
promised a nation through; the other is where the other man hides.

### The descent, finished

The wiki has tracked this line across six chapters. It closes here.

| | |
|---|---|
| 13:11 | *"So Lot chose the Plain of the Jordan for himself"* — the well-watered country |
| 13:12 | *"moved his tent as far as Sodom"* — outside the walls |
| 14:12 | *"who lived in Sodom"* — inside them |
| 19:1 | *"Lot sat in the gate of Sodom"* — the civic centre |
| 19:30 | *"He lived in a cave"* — no town, no tent, no goods, no wife |

**He lifted up his eyes at 13:10 and took the best land in the story. He ends in a hole above the
ash of it.** Genesis does not say this is a judgment on the choice, and it does not have to: it
simply sets the two verses in the same book and leaves them there. See
[[Lift Up Your Eyes - Two Ways of Seeing the Land]].

### *"there is not a man in the earth"*

***"Our father is old, and there is not a man in the earth to come in to us in the way of all the
earth"*** (19:31). **The sentence is the pericope's one interpretive crux, and it turns on a word
Hebrew does not disambiguate.** *ʾÎš ʾên bā-ʾāreṣ* — *ʾereṣ* is **earth** and it is equally
**land, country, region**, and Genesis uses it both ways within single chapters (compare 11:1,
*"the whole earth"*, with 13:6, *"the land was not able to bear them"*). Two readings follow, and
the wiki records both because the text will not adjudicate between them.

- **The global reading.** They believe the human race is finished — that what came down on the
  plain came down everywhere, and that they and their father are what is left. On this reading the
  scene is a deliberate second flood, and the daughters are doing consciously what the text says
  Noah's household was preserved to do.
- **The local reading.** *In this land* — they are stateless refugees in a cave, their betrothed
  died in Sodom (19:14), their mother is a pillar on the road behind them, and their father has
  just moved them away from the only surviving town. There is no man **for them**, and the
  observation is about their circumstances rather than about the world.

**The verb they use is the flood's verb, and this is the strongest evidence in the passage.**
*"that we may preserve our father's family line"* (19:32, and again verbatim at 19:34) renders
*ûnəḥayyeh mē-ʾābînû zāraʿ*, literally *and we will keep seed alive from our father* — the piel of
*ḥāyâ*, **to keep alive**, governing *zeraʿ*, **seed**. That exact pairing occurs once before in
Scripture, in the ark's loading manifest: *"Also of the birds of the sky, seven and seven, male
and female, **to keep seed alive** on the surface of all the earth"* (7:3). Whatever the daughters
can see from the cave mouth, **the words Genesis puts in their mouths are the words it used for
the survival of life on earth**, and they are used here of one family, by two women, about their
father. See [[Noah and the Flood]] and
[[The Drunkenness of Noah and the Drunkenness of Lot]].

Against the global reading stands one hard fact of the plot: **they have just come from Zoar**,
which was full of people and which they left on foot. The strongest form of the reading has to
treat 19:31 as a fear rather than an inference — the last two women of a burned country, in a cave,
reasoning from what they watched happen. Genesis reports the reasoning and never says whether it
was right.

### The wine, and the verb the chapter is built on

***"Come, let's make our father drink wine"***. Where wine comes from in a cave above a scorched
plain the narrative does not say, and the question is old; it is also the kind of question Genesis
never answers, of a piece with where Cain's wife came from. What the text is interested in is what
the wine does.

**It produces the only unambiguous statement in the passage: twice, in identical words, *he didn't
know*.**

> **33** … He didn’t know when she lay down, nor when she arose.

> **35** … He didn’t know when she lay down, nor when she got up.

***Yādaʿ*, and the chapter has been running on this verb since verse 5.** Genesis 19 uses it at
every hinge:

| | |
|---|---|
| 19:5 | the town at the door: *"Bring them out to us, that we may have sex with them"* — *wənēdʿâ ʾōtām*, *that we may **know** them* |
| 19:8 | Lot's offer: *"two virgin daughters"* — *ʾăšer lōʾ-yādəʿû ʾîš*, *who have not **known** a man* |
| 19:33, 35 | Lot in the cave: *"He didn't know"* — *wə-lōʾ yādaʿ* |

**A town demands to know two strangers; two daughters have known no one; and the man who offered
them does not know his own bed.** The wiki records the chain as a fact of the Hebrew and notes
that Genesis does not comment on it — as it has not commented on anything in the chapter.

**And it is the second half of the reversal that began at 19:8.** *"you may do to them what seems
good to you"* was said of these two women by this man, eleven verses before *"we will lie with
him"*. Whether the passage intends the symmetry is not something Genesis says out loud; that it is
there, at that distance, in one chapter, is not in dispute. See [[Lot's Daughters]].

### The two nations

***"The firstborn bore a son, and named him Moab. He is the father of the Moabites to this
day."***

***"The younger also bore a son, and called his name Ben Ammi. He is the father of the children of
Ammon to this day."***

**Two peoples, two etymologies, and the Hebrew supplies only one of them.** *Ben ʿAmmî* means **son
of my kinsman** — transparent Hebrew, needing no gloss, and the reason 19:38 gives no explanatory
clause. **Moab** is not glossed at all in the Masoretic text; the traditional reading takes it as
*mē-ʾāb*, **from father**, and that reading is at least as old as the Septuagint, which supplies
the missing clause outright — the elder daughter names him *saying, "From my father"*, and the
younger names hers *saying, "Son of my people"*. So the Greek makes explicit what the Hebrew leaves
as a pun a reader either hears or does not.

***"to this day"***, twice. **The narrator is writing in a world where both nations exist**, are
known by name, and are somebody's neighbours. That is the point of the pericope's placement, and it
is the reason it is here rather than anywhere else: Genesis is explaining who the people east of
the Dead Sea are, and it explains them by way of Abraham's nephew.

**What the wiki will not do is treat that as settling the passage's purpose.** The reading that
Genesis 19:30–38 is anti-Moabite and anti-Ammonite polemic — a birth-slur attached to two rival
neighbours — is the standard critical account, and the text supports it as far as it goes. What it
does not account for is what the rest of the canon then does with these two nations, which is not
what a polemic would do with them:

- **Deuteronomy 2:9** — *"Don't bother Moab, neither contend with them in battle; for I will not
  give you his land for a possession, because I have given Ar to the children of Lot for a
  possession."* And 2:19, the same for Ammon. **In the book that grants Canaan, God grants Moab and
  Ammon their territory by name, and identifies them as *the children of Lot*.** The cave's
  offspring hold their land on the same footing Israel holds its own: by divine assignment.
- **Ruth** — a Moabite woman is the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:17–22), and Matthew 1:5 puts
  her in the genealogy of Jesus. Naamah the Ammonitess is the mother of Rehoboam (1 Kings 14:21).
  **Both nations born in this cave enter the line of Judah's kings.**

See [[Moab and Ammon - The Two Nations Born in the Cave]].

### Lot stops

**Genesis 19:36 is the last verse in the Bible that says his name in a narrative, and he does not
speak in this pericope at all.**

He has no death notice. Genesis is scrupulous about these — Sarah gets a chapter (23), Abraham
*"died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years"* (25:8), Ishmael is numbered and gathered
to his people (25:17), and even Terah is buried in a verse (11:32). **Lot simply stops.** He is
unconscious in the second-to-last sentence about him, and the last two sentences are about his
grandsons. The New Testament will mention him once more and call him righteous (2 Peter 2:7–8);
Genesis leaves him asleep in a cave. See [[Lot]] and
[[Righteous Lot - 2 Peter's Verdict and Genesis's Silence]].

**And Abraham never learns any of it.** He watched the smoke at 19:28 and went home; 19:29 told the
reader that the rescue happened for his sake and told him nothing; and now the man he was not told
about disappears from the book without their meeting again. The two of them last spoke at 13:9.

## People

- [[Lot]] — his last scene. He leaves the town he begged for, climbs to the mountain he refused,
  says nothing, is made drunk twice, does not know, and exits the Bible's narrative without a death
  notice.
- [[Lot's Daughters]] — the one passage in which they speak and act. The firstborn proposes, states
  the reason, goes first, and reports back; the younger goes second. Neither is named, and both
  become the mother of a nation.
- [[Moab (person)]] — son of the firstborn daughter, father of the Moabites; named in Hebrew without
  a gloss, and glossed *from my father* by the Septuagint.
- [[Ben Ammi]] — son of the younger daughter, father of the Ammonites; his name is transparent
  Hebrew, *son of my kinsman*.
- [[Abraham]] — absent, and permanently. He is told nothing at 19:29 and is never told this either;
  the two men last spoke at 13:9.
- [[Lot's Wife]] — absent by death, and the reason 19:31's *"there is not a man"* has a mother's
  silence behind it.
- [[Noah]] — off-stage, and everywhere in the vocabulary: the only other man in Scripture to survive
  a total judgment and then be found drunk and uncovered by his own children.

## Places

- [[Zoar]] — spared at his request nine verses earlier, named out of his plea, and abandoned by him
  here *"for he was afraid to live in Zoar"*. The one city of the plain left standing is the one
  city he will not live in.
- [[Moab (place)]] — the tableland east of the Dead Sea, named for the child born in this cave, and
  granted to *"the children of Lot"* by name at Deuteronomy 2:9.
- [[Ammon]] — the country north of Moab around Rabbah, granted on the same terms at Deuteronomy
  2:19, and Israel's neighbour for the whole monarchy.
- [[The Plain of the Jordan]] — the *kikkar* he chose at 13:11, burned at 19:25, and now the country
  he is living above.
- [[The Salt Sea]] — the sea the cave country overlooks; its eastern escarpment is where both new
  nations settle.
- [[Sodom]] — ash, and the reason for everything in the pericope: the fear, the isolation, the dead
  betrothed, and the daughters' reckoning that no man is left.

## Themes

- [[Life and Death]] — *"that we may preserve our father's family line"*, twice, in the flood's own
  idiom (*keep seed alive*, 7:3). The passage's stated motive is the continuation of life, and the
  means is the thing the law will later name first among the forbidden degrees.
- [[Deception]] — wine as the instrument, administered on two consecutive nights by people who tell
  the target nothing. Genesis's usual shape — nothing false is said, because nothing is said at all.
- [[The Nations]] — two peoples given an origin, an etymology, and a *"to this day"*. The Table of
  Nations method applied to Israel's nearest neighbours, with a narrative attached rather than a
  name in a list.
- [[Marriage]] — by its absence. The daughters state the problem in exactly the terms 2:24 set up —
  no man to come in to them — and solve it by the one arrangement Leviticus 18:6–7 puts first on the
  list of what may not be done.
- [[City and Civilization]] — the arc completed backwards. Genesis's cities run Enoch, Babel, Sodom;
  the one man the book followed into a city comes out of it into a cave, and what he founds there is
  not a city but two nations.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — a *gēr* in Sodom (19:9), a refugee in Zoar, and a cave-dweller in the
  hills. His descendants get a land grant by divine assignment; he never holds a foot of ground.
- [[Judgment]] — the aftershock. The fire is over by 19:29, and the pericope is what the survivors
  do in the country it left behind.
- [[Sin]] — and Genesis's silence about it. The book has now declined to grade Lot six times running,
  and this is the hardest of the six to leave ungraded.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** 19:30 opens with a new place (*"Lot went up out of Zoar"*) and
  19:38 closes with an etiological formula (*"to this day"*), after which 20:1 moves the camera back
  to Abraham and keeps it there. The unit is as clean as any in the book. The one live argument for
  running 19:1–38 together is that 19:30 cancels the concession of 19:21 and is barely intelligible
  without it; the wiki keeps them separate, as the previous entry said it would, because 19:29 is
  written to end something and because what happens in the cave is a different kind of scene.
- **Genesis grades nothing here, and this is the sharpest test of that habit so far.** The book has
  now reported, without evaluation, a man choosing the land of a doomed city, moving into it,
  sitting in its gate, offering his daughters to a mob, and being carried out of it by the wrist.
  Here it reports two women getting their father drunk and conceiving by him, and the narrator's
  only comment is a summary — *"Thus both of Lot's daughters were with child by their father"* —
  and two national etymologies. **No character is condemned, no character is excused, and God does
  not appear in the pericope at all.** He is not named once in nine verses, in a chapter that named
  him in almost every one of the first twenty-nine.
- **On what the daughters intend, and the two traditions of reading it.** The passage gives them a
  stated motive, and it is not desire: *"that we may preserve our father's family line."* Jewish
  reading has generally taken that motive seriously and read the sisters as acting to save the human
  race from what they believed was a second flood — *Genesis Rabbah* 51 develops this at length, and
  the rabbinic tradition goes further, weighing the elder's brazenness in naming her son *Moab*
  against the younger's discretion in naming hers *Ben Ammi* and finding the difference reflected in
  how the two nations are afterwards treated (*Bava Kamma* 38b; *Nazir* 23a–b, which reads the elder
  sister's act as a transgression performed for a good end and traces Ruth from it). Christian
  reading has more often taken the scene as straightforwardly shameful and read the two nations'
  later history back into it. **The wiki records both traditions and adopts neither**, because
  Genesis supplies the motive and withholds the verdict, and because the canon's own subsequent use
  of Moab and Ammon — cursed in Deuteronomy 23, protected in Deuteronomy 2, and mother-in-law to
  Israel's greatest king — refuses to run in one direction.
- **The polemic reading, and its limits.** That the pericope is a slur on two neighbouring peoples
  is a common and defensible critical account: it gives them a shameful birth, it attaches the
  shame to their names by wordplay, and it does so in the national epic of a people who fought both
  of them for centuries. What the account under-explains is Deuteronomy 2:9 and 2:19, where the same
  Torah forbids Israel to take a foot of either nation's land and grounds the prohibition in a
  divine grant to *"the children of Lot"*; and Ruth, which brings a Moabite woman into the royal
  line and shows no embarrassment about it. A polemic that its own canon keeps contradicting is at
  minimum an unstable one.
- **Where the wine came from.** Unanswerable, and worth naming as unanswerable rather than
  harmonised. Proposals run from provisions carried out of Zoar to the vineyards of the surviving
  hill country to the narrator simply not caring, which is the likeliest. Genesis has the same
  attitude to Cain's wife (4:17) and to who was left to kill Cain (4:14): the story requires
  something and does not stop to source it.
- **Source criticism, briefly.** 19:30–38 is assigned to J with near-consensus and reads as the
  continuation of the same narrative that runs from 18:1 — the divine name is absent here, but so is
  any divine speech or action, so the criterion has nothing to work with. The P notice at 19:29 sits
  between this scene and the chapter it belongs to, which is one of the reasons the seam at 19:29/30
  reads as cleanly as it does.
- **The two caves.** Worth keeping as an observation rather than a claim about authorial intent:
  *məʿārâ* enters Scripture here, and its next four occurrences in Genesis are all Machpelah — the
  cave Abraham buys for four hundred shekels in a public transaction so that he will have somewhere
  in the land that is his (23:3–20), and where Sarah, Abraham, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah, and Jacob are
  all eventually laid. The uncle and the nephew of Genesis 13 both end underground, and the two
  caves are not the same kind of thing: one is a purchase and a claim on the promise, and the other
  is a hiding place.
