# Joseph in Potiphar's House

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 39:1–23

## Summary

**This is the chapter where God comes back, and he comes back under his own name.**
[[Joseph Sold into Slavery]] ran thirty-six verses without a single divine name. Genesis 39 says
***Yahweh*** **eight times in twenty-three verses** — and then the name effectively leaves the book.
**In the eleven chapters from 40 to 50 it occurs exactly once**, in a single line of a poem (49:18).
**Everywhere else in the Joseph cycle the narrator and the characters say *ʾĕlōhîm*, God.**

| chapter | *Yahweh* | *God* |
|---|---|---|
| **37** | **0** | **0** |
| 38 | 3 — all of them *Yahweh killed him* (38:7, 10) | 0 |
| **39** | **8** | **1** — and it is Joseph's, not the narrator's |
| 40–48 | 0 | 33 across nine chapters |
| 49 | 1 — *"I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh"* (49:18) | 1 |
| 50 | 0 | 5 |

**The covenant name appears in the Joseph narrative in one concentrated burst, and it is spent on a
slave in a foreign house who owns nothing.** Not on the dreams, not on the famine, not on the throne —
on the thirteen years nobody would have picked.

**And the eight occurrences are all the narrator's.** Joseph, speaking aloud to an Egyptian woman, says
***ʾĕlōhîm*** (39:9). **The book tells the reader the covenant God is in the room and does not let the
character say so.**

### The chapter is built as a single sentence said twice

**Genesis 39 is two prosperity reports with a catastrophe wedged between them, and the second report is
the first one rewritten in a worse building.**

| | **the house (39:2–6)** | **the prison (39:21–23)** |
|---|---|---|
| the formula | *"Yahweh was with Joseph"* (2) | *"Yahweh was with Joseph"* (21) |
| who notices | *"his master saw that Yahweh was with him"* (3) | *"gave him favor in the sight of the keeper"* (21) |
| what he is given | *"overseer over his house, and all that he had"* (4) | *"all the prisoners who were in the prison"* (22) |
| the delegation | *"he didn't concern himself with anything"* (6) | *"didn't look after anything that was under his hand"* (23) |
| the verdict | *"Yahweh made all that he did prosper in his hand"* (3) | *"that which he did, Yahweh made it prosper"* (23) |

**Five correspondences, in order, across a fall that was supposed to end him.** The chapter's argument is
made by its shape before it is made by a word of commentary: **the sentence that was true of the estate
is true of the dungeon, unchanged.** See
[[I Will Be With You - The Promise of Presence]].

**What the frame does not say is that the prison is not worse.** *"Joseph's master took him, and put him
into the prison"* (39:20) — **the second involuntary descent in three chapters**, after the cistern. He
leaves the house he ran, and **Genesis never records that he was cleared.**

### The one thing the chapter will not tell you

**Nobody in Genesis 39 says whether Potiphar believed his wife.** *"His wrath was kindled"* (39:19) — and
the Hebrew does not name the target. **A slave charged with assaulting his owner's wife would be
executed**; this one is put in a prison **inside his owner's own house** (40:3), where he is promptly
given the run of the place by its keeper. **The wiki records the gap and adopts no reading of it.**

**Joseph, for his part, does not answer the charge.** He speaks once in the chapter, at 39:8–9, before
anything is alleged. **From the moment the garment is in her hand he says nothing at all** — the same
silence chapter 37 gave him in the pit, and which 42:21 eventually reveals was not silence.

### The beauty that is a plot device

***"Joseph was well-built and handsome"*** (39:6) — ***yəpēh-tōʾar wîpēh marʾeh***, *beautiful in form
and beautiful in appearance*. **Genesis has used that exact pair once before, of his mother:** *"Rachel
was beautiful in form and attractive"* (29:17), ***yəpat-tōʾar wîpat marʾeh***. **The son of the loved
wife is described in the words the book used for the loved wife**, and both descriptions are placed
immediately before the trouble they cause.

**Its position in the verse is the whole point.** It is the last clause of the prosperity report, and the
next words are *"After these things, his master's wife set her eyes on Joseph."* **Genesis supplies the
cause and does not comment on it.** See [[Rachel]] and [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who acts |
|---|---|---|

| **2–4** | *Yahweh was with Joseph*; the master sees it; overseer of the house | Yahweh, then Potiphar |
| **5** | *Yahweh blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake* | Yahweh |
| **6** | Everything handed over except *the food which he ate*; and he was handsome | Potiphar |
| **7** | *"Lie with me"* | the master's wife |
| **8–9** | The refusal: three clauses about his master, one about God | Joseph |

| **11–12** | The empty house; the garment seized; *he left it in her hand, and ran* | both |

### 39:1 — the chapter that starts by repeating the one before last

> **1** Joseph was brought down to Egypt. Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh’s, the captain of the guard, an
> Egyptian, bought him from the hand of the Ishmaelites that had brought him down there.

***"Joseph was brought down to Egypt."*** ***Hûrad*** — **a passive, and the verb is *yārad*, to go
down**, the Joseph cycle's directional signature. **He does not go; he is taken.** [[Abram in Egypt]]
went down of his own decision in a famine; the household will go down at 46:6 by invitation. **This is
the only descent in Genesis performed on somebody.** See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

**The verse is a resumptive repetition, and it is repairing a seam.** 37:36 ended *"the Midianites sold
him into Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh's, the captain of the guard"*; **39:1 says it again,
almost word for word, after [[Judah and Tamar]] has spent a whole chapter somewhere else.** The
technique is ordinary Hebrew narrative craft — pick the thread back up by restating its last knot — and
**it is also the clearest internal evidence that Genesis 38 is an insertion into a running story rather
than the next thing that happened.**

***"From the hand of the Ishmaelites."*** **And the seam it repairs is not the only one.** The caravan
was *Ishmaelite* at 37:25, 27, 28b, *Midianite* at 37:28a and 37:36 — **and Potiphar, who bought from
Midianites in the last verse of chapter 37, buys from Ishmaelites in the first verse of chapter 39.**
Whether that is two designations for one caravan, two parties in one transaction, or two strands joined
without reconciling, is set out on
[[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]], **and the wiki adopts none of the three.**

***"An Egyptian."*** **Added here and absent at 37:36**, and it is not decoration: **the word *Egyptian*
occurs four times in five verses** (39:1, 2, 5 twice), and the blessing at 39:5 falls on *"the Egyptian's
house"* rather than on Potiphar by name. **Genesis is establishing a nationality before it narrates a
household**, because the household is about to stand for a country. See [[Egypt]] and [[The Nations]].

### 39:2–6 — a slave runs an estate, and the narrator says why

> **2** Yahweh was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man. He was in the house of his master the
> Egyptian.
> **3** His master saw that Yahweh was with him, and that Yahweh made all that he did prosper in his
> hand.
> **4** Joseph found favor in his sight. He ministered to him, and he made him overseer over his house,
> and all that he had he put into his hand.
> **5** From the time that he made him overseer in his house, and over all that he had, Yahweh blessed
> the Egyptian’s house for Joseph’s sake. Yahweh’s blessing was on all that he had, in the house and in
> the field.
> **6** He left all that he had in Joseph’s hand. He didn’t concern himself with anything, except for the
> food which he ate. Joseph was well-built and handsome.

***"Yahweh was with Joseph."*** ***Wayhî YHWH ʾet-Yôsēp*** — **and this is the sentence Genesis 37
withheld for thirty-six verses.** The formula itself is not new: it was **promised** to Isaac (26:3, 24)
and to Jacob (28:15; 31:3), always in the future tense and always as a commissioning. **Here, for the
first time, it is reported in the past tense about someone who was never told it.** **Joseph receives no
theophany, no dream from God, no covenant speech — anywhere in Genesis.** He is the only major figure in
the book God never addresses. **What he gets instead is a narrator.** See
[[I Will Be With You - The Promise of Presence]] and [[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]].

***"He was a prosperous man."*** ***ʾÎš maṣlîaḥ*** — from ***ṣālaḥ***, and **the root frames the chapter**:
*Yahweh made all that he did prosper* (39:3) and *Yahweh made it prosper* (39:23). **The word is applied
to a man who has just been stripped of his clothes, his family, his country and his legal personhood**,
and Genesis uses it without irony. **Prosperity in this chapter is not a state; it is what happens to
whatever he is put in charge of.**

***"His master saw that Yahweh was with him."*** **An Egyptian slaver diagnoses the God of Abraham
correctly, by name, and Genesis does not remark on it.** [[Laban]] did the same at 30:27 — *"I have
divined that Yahweh has blessed me for your sake"* — **and the Philistines saw it about Isaac at
26:28.** **Genesis keeps handing accurate theology to people outside the covenant line and never explains
where they got it**; it is one of the book's most consistent and least commented-on habits. See
[[The Nations]] and [[Melchizedek]].

***"Overseer over his house."*** **The Egyptian institution is real and the title is the right one**:
*imy-r pr*, **steward of the house**, the chief of a private estate, attested throughout the Middle and
New Kingdoms and often held by foreigners. **Genesis knows what an Egyptian great house looked like.**

***"Yahweh blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake."*** ***Biglal Yôsēp*** — **and *biglal* is
not a common construction.** Genesis puts it in exactly two places: here, and **in Laban's mouth at
30:27**, *biglālekā*, *for your sake*. **Father and son, one chapter apart in the book, produce the same
economic effect on the same kind of foreign employer.** The full comparison is on
[[Blessed for His Sake - Laban's House and Potiphar's]]. **This is what Genesis 12:3 looks like when the
book actually narrates it**: not a nation blessed, but an inventory. See [[Blessing]] and
[[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]].

***"In the house and in the field."*** **Two domains, and it is the merism for everything an Egyptian
estate consisted of** — the residence and the agricultural holding. **The same pair returns as a curse
in Exodus**, where the plagues strike *"in the field"* and *"in the houses"* (Exodus 9:19, 25; 8:9, 13).
**The first Israelite in Egypt is a blessing to an Egyptian house; the last time Genesis's sequel uses
this pair it is describing the opposite.** See
[[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].

***"He didn't concern himself with anything, except for the food which he ate."*** **The strangest clause
in the chapter, and there are two standing readings.**

1. **A dietary boundary.** Genesis itself says at 43:32 that *"the Egyptians don't eat bread with the
   Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians"* — **so the one thing a Hebrew steward could not
   be given charge of is the master's table.** The reading is internal to the book and needs nothing
   imported.
2. **A euphemism.** *Bread* is used for a wife at Proverbs 30:20 and arguably elsewhere, and a long
   rabbinic tradition reads *"except the food which he ate"* as *except his wife* — **which would make
   verse 6 a single sentence containing the exception and the reason it fails.**

**The wiki records both and adopts the first as the plainer**, on the grounds that Genesis supplies it
itself four chapters later, while noting that the second is why the clause sits where it does.

### 39:7–10 — two words, said twice, and the longest speech Joseph has made

> **7** After these things, his master’s wife set her eyes on Joseph; and she said, “Lie with me.”
> **8** But he refused, and said to his master’s wife, “Behold, my master doesn’t know what is with me in
> the house, and he has put all that he has into my hand.
> **9** No one is greater in this house than I am, and he has not kept back anything from me but you,
> because you are his wife. How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?”
> **10** As she spoke to Joseph day by day, he didn’t listen to her, to lie by her, or to be with her.

***"His master's wife set her eyes on Joseph."*** ***Wattiśśāʾ … ʾet-ʿêneyhā*** — *lifted up her eyes*,
**which in Genesis is ordinarily the gesture before a promise or a rescue**: Abram *"lift up your eyes"*
over the land (13:14), Hagar sees the well (21:19), Abraham sees the ram (22:13), and the brothers *"lifted
up their eyes"* and saw the caravan that took Joseph to Egypt (37:25). **The idiom of seeing what is
given is used here for seeing what is not.** See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

***"Lie with me."*** ***Šikbâ ʿimmî*** — **two words in Hebrew, and they are the whole of her recorded
speech to him.** She says them at 39:7 and again at 39:12, **and Genesis gives her no other line addressed
to Joseph across an unspecified stretch of *day by day*.**

**She is the only woman in the Bible who propositions a man in the imperative and in plain terms.** The
comparisons all fall short of it: [[Lot]]'s daughters act on a sleeping man and say nothing to him
(19:32–35); [[Tamar]] negotiates a price in a veil and asks for a pledge (38:16–18); [[Ruth]] uncovers
feet and asks for a redeemer's cloak. **This woman states the request, unveiled, to a man she owns.**
**The power runs the same direction as the property**, which is the fact most retellings of the chapter
invert.

***"But he refused."*** ***Wayməʾēn*** — **and it is Joseph's first recorded act of will since 37:16.**
He has been carried, sold, bought, and promoted; **the first thing he does on his own initiative in Egypt
is decline.**

***"He said to his master's wife…"*** **This is the longest speech Joseph has given in Genesis, and the
first words the book records from him since the errand to Dothan.** **His last sentence before it was
*"I am looking for my brothers"*** (37:16). **Between that line and this one he has been stripped, pitted,
priced, and shipped.** Genesis gives him nothing to say in between and then hands him a moral argument.

***The shape of the argument is the surprising part.*** **Three clauses about his master, then one about
God.**

- *"My master doesn't know what is with me in the house"*
- *"He has put all that he has into my hand"*
- *"No one is greater in this house than I am, and he has not kept back anything from me but you"*
- *"How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?"*

**Three quarters of the refusal is about a breach of trust with an Egyptian slaveholder**, argued from
the inventory. **Joseph reasons about adultery as a steward reasons about property he has been given
charge of, and then names the one item he was not given** — *"but you, because you are his wife"* —
**and only then raises God.** The wiki notes the order without flattening it into either a cynical
reading or a pious one: **the same sentence contains the accounting and the theology, and Genesis
presents them as one argument.**

***"This great wickedness."*** ***Hārāʿâ haggədōlâ hazzōʾt*** — **the phrase belongs to a small Genesis
family of foreigner-facing moral verdicts.** [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] asks Abraham *"what have
you done to us … you have brought on me and on my kingdom a great sin"* (20:9), and 34:7 calls the rape
of Dinah ***nəbālâ***, *folly in Israel*. **In two of the three the moral vocabulary is in a foreigner's
mouth and in this one it is in a Hebrew slave's**, addressed to an Egyptian.

***"And sin against God."*** ***Wəḥāṭāʾtî lēʾlōhîm*** — and **three things about it are worth holding
together.**

**First, the name.** **Joseph says *ʾĕlōhîm*, the generic term**, in the one chapter of the cycle where
the narrator says *Yahweh* eight times. **Speaking to an Egyptian, he uses the word an Egyptian could
hear.** The narrator's covenant name and the character's neutral one sit eight verses apart and never
touch.

**Second, the party wronged.** Adultery with a master's wife injures **the master**, **the wife**, and
**the household**; Joseph names **God**. **It is the first time in Scripture that a sexual offence is
described as an offence against God by the person declining it**, and it anticipates the sentence David
will write about the same sin from the other side — *"Against you, you only, have I sinned"* (Psalm
51:4). See [[Sin Against God - Joseph at 39.9 and David at Psalm 51]].

**Third, that the category exists at all before Sinai.** **There is no commandment yet.** Genesis has
twice shown foreign kings assuming the prohibition anyway — Pharaoh at 12:18 and Abimelech at 20:9, both
of whom treat adultery as self-evidently wrong and both of whom are more scrupulous about it than the
patriarch who set them up. **Joseph does not cite a law, because there is none to cite.** See
[[Because Abraham Obeyed My Voice - Law Before Sinai]] and [[Sexual Sin]].

***"Day by day."*** ***Yôm yôm*** — **the chapter's only indication that any time passes**, and it
converts a scene into a siege. **The refusal is not a single heroic moment but a repeated one**, and the
three infinitives of verse 10 are graded downward: **not to lie with her, not to be by her, not even to
be with her.** He stops entering the room.

### 39:11–12 — an empty house, a garment, and the second stripping

> **11** About this time, he went into the house to do his work, and there were none of the men of the
> house inside.
> **12** She caught him by his garment, saying, “Lie with me!” He left his garment in her hand, and ran
> outside.

***"There were none of the men of the house inside."*** **The narrator establishes the absence of
witnesses before the act, which is why the chapter can turn entirely on an object.** **Both parties know
there is no testimony available**, and both behave accordingly: **he leaves, and she manufactures an
exhibit.**

***"She caught him by his garment."*** ***Beged*** — **not the *kətōnet passîm* of chapter 37**, which is
gone; **the ordinary word for a garment**. **And Genesis is now doing the same thing to Joseph for the
second time in three chapters.**

| | **Genesis 37** | **Genesis 39** |
|---|---|---|
| the garment | *kətōnet passîm*, his father's gift | *beged*, an ordinary slave's clothing |
| who takes it | his brothers strip him (37:23) | his master's wife seizes it (39:12) |
| what is done to it | dipped in goat's blood | kept beside her until evening |
| to whom presented | *"Examine it, now"* — to Jacob (37:32) | *"the Hebrew servant … came in to me"* — to Potiphar (39:17) |
| what it proves | that Joseph is dead | that Joseph is guilty |
| what is true | neither | neither |
| what follows | the pit, then Egypt | the prison |

**Twice a garment is taken off Joseph, and twice it is produced as evidence of something that did not
happen.** **Both times the man shown the cloth believes it without asking the one person who could
correct him**, and **both times Joseph is not recorded as speaking a word in his own defence.** The full
account is on [[A Garment Used as Evidence - The Coat and the Cloak]]; see also
[[Discern, Please - Hakker-na from Jacob's Coat to Judah's Pledge]] and [[Nakedness and Covering]].

***"He left his garment in her hand, and ran outside."*** ***Wayyānos ha-ḥûṣâ*** — **and the flight is
the thing the rest of Scripture keeps.** **The verse is the Bible's paradigm case for the instruction
Paul gives twice** — *"Flee sexual immorality"* (1 Corinthians 6:18) and *"flee from youthful lusts"*
(2 Timothy 2:22) — **and it is a flight that costs the man everything he has and does not clear his
name.** **Genesis does not reward it inside the chapter.** What it does is put *"Yahweh was with
Joseph"* on the other side of the prison door.

**Escaping half-dressed is also a small, exact humiliation the book does not soften.** **He runs into a
courtyard in front of a household he has been in charge of, without his clothes**, which is precisely
what makes her story tellable.

### 39:13–18 — the accusation, told twice, to two different audiences

> **13** When she saw that he had left his garment in her hand, and had run outside,
> **14** she called to the men of her house, and spoke to them, saying, “Behold, he has brought a Hebrew
> in to us to mock us. He came in to me to lie with me, and I cried with a loud voice.
> **15** When he heard that I lifted up my voice and cried, he left his garment by me, and ran outside.”
> **16** She laid up his garment by her, until his master came home.
> **17** She spoke to him according to these words, saying, “The Hebrew servant, whom you have brought to
> us, came in to me to mock me,
> **18** and as I lifted up my voice and cried, he left his garment by me, and ran outside.”

***"She called to the men of her house … until his master came home."*** **She tells the household
first and her husband second, and the order is the strategy.** **By the time Potiphar hears it, it is
not his wife's word against a slave's — it is a story the whole staff has already been told**, with the
garment in evidence. **Genesis does not editorialise; it simply reports the sequence, and the sequence is
a jury assembled before a charge is filed.**

***"He has brought a Hebrew in to us."*** — and then, to Potiphar — ***"The Hebrew servant, whom you have
brought to us."*** **The pronoun moves.** To the servants, *he* — an unnamed *he* who could be her husband
or the slave, **and who is most naturally read as her husband**. To her husband, it is explicit: ***you***
**brought him.** **In both versions the fault is routed back to the man who bought Joseph**, and in both
she includes herself with the household — ***to us***, ***against us*** — **speaking as one of the
staff to the staff and as the injured party to the master.**

***"A Hebrew."*** ***ʿIbrî*** — **and this is where the word starts doing the work it does for the rest
of the Torah.** It entered Scripture at 14:13 as a neutral identifier, *Abram the Hebrew*. **From here on
it is almost always in a foreigner's mouth, or in an Israelite's mouth when speaking to a foreigner**:
Joseph at 40:15 (*"I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews"*), the cupbearer at 41:12 (*"a young
man, a Hebrew"*), the Egyptians at 43:32, and then **the whole apparatus of Exodus** — the Hebrew
midwives (1:15–19), *"one of the Hebrews' children"* (2:6), *"the God of the Hebrews"* (3:18). **Genesis
39:14 is the first time it is spoken with contempt**, and the contempt is the point of using it. See
[[Abram the Hebrew - The First Use of the Name]].

***"To mock us."*** ***Ləṣaḥeq bānû*** — **and the verb is the Isaac verb.** ***Ṣāḥaq*** is *to laugh*:
Abraham laughs (17:17), Sarah laughs (18:12), **Isaac is named for it** (21:6), Ishmael is *mocking*
(21:9), **and at 26:8 Abimelech looks out of a window and sees Isaac *sporting with* Rebekah** —
***məṣaḥēq ʾēt Ribqâ*** — **which is how he works out that she is his wife and not his sister.**
**The sexual sense of the root is established in Genesis by that verse and nowhere else**, and this is
its next appearance. **The word that gave the second patriarch his name is the word an Egyptian woman
uses to accuse the fourth generation of attempted rape.** See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]],
[[Isaac in Gerar]], and [[Language and Speech]].

***"I cried with a loud voice."*** **The detail is not decorative and it is not accidental.**
**Deuteronomy 22:23–27 makes exactly this the legal test** for a sexual assault alleged inside a town:
**if she cried out, she is innocent; if she did not, the silence convicts her too.** **She supplies, twice,
the one element the law will later require** — and **she supplies it in a house she has just told the
reader was empty.** The wiki notes that the law postdates the narrative and that Genesis makes no
reference to it; **what it records is that her account is constructed around the question a hearer would
ask.**

***"He left his garment by me."*** **Told twice, and it is the only part of her account that is true.**
**Everything she says is technically verifiable except the direction of the encounter** — he was here, we
were alone, the garment came off, he ran. **She does not fabricate an event; she reverses one.** It is
the same species of deception the family has practised for four generations: **[[Jacob]] never said *I am
Esau* without being asked, the brothers said only *we have found this* — and here a woman narrates a real
sequence with one pronoun changed.** See [[Deception]].

### 39:19–20 — a master's anger, and the sentence he does not pass

> **19** When his master heard the words of his wife, which she spoke to him, saying, “This is what your
> servant did to me,” his wrath was kindled.
> **20** Joseph’s master took him, and put him into the prison, the place where the king’s prisoners were
> bound, and he was there in custody.

***"His wrath was kindled."*** ***Wayyiḥar ʾappô*** — **and Genesis does not say at whom.** The clause has
a subject and no object. **The wiki leaves it open and sets out what the text does and does not support.**

**What the text gives:**

- **He does not kill him.** In any ancient legal framework the wiki can consult — and in the plain logic
  of the household — **a slave who assaulted the master's wife would be executed.** Potiphar has the
  title ***śar haṭṭabbāḥîm***, *chief of the slaughterers*, **and does not use it.**
- **He does not sell him either**, which would recover the purchase price and remove the problem.
- **He puts him in his own house.** 40:3 places the royal prison *"in the house of the captain of the
  guard"* — **so Joseph is moved from one part of Potiphar's establishment to another**, and into the
  facility that holds Pharaoh's own detainees rather than a slave pen.
- **Potiphar never speaks in Genesis, here or anywhere.** He has no line of dialogue in the entire book.

**What the text does not give:** any statement of belief, any hearing, any verdict, and any indication
that the arrangement was mercy rather than procedure. **Reading Potiphar as privately unconvinced is a
reasonable inference and it is an inference**; the wiki reports it as one and does not adopt it.

***"The place where the king's prisoners were bound."*** **The chapter's last descent, and it is
institutional rather than personal.** He passes out of a household and into the state. **It is also,
narratively, the only door to Pharaoh**: the cupbearer and the baker are in this prison at 40:3, and the
cupbearer is the man who eventually says *"I remember my faults today"* (41:9). **The false charge is
what puts Joseph in the room with the king's servants**, and Genesis makes the connection by geography
without making it by comment.

### 39:21–23 — the same sentence, in a worse building

> **21** But Yahweh was with Joseph, and showed kindness to him, and gave him favor in the sight of the
> keeper of the prison.
> **22** The keeper of the prison committed to Joseph’s hand all the prisoners who were in the prison.
> Whatever they did there, he was responsible for it.
> **23** The keeper of the prison didn’t look after anything that was under his hand, because Yahweh was
> with him; and that which he did, Yahweh made it prosper.

***"But Yahweh was with Joseph."*** **The adversative is the chapter's last move and its whole argument.**
**The formula that opened the prosperity now opens the disaster**, unmodified, **and the paragraph that
follows is the earlier paragraph again.**

***"Showed kindness to him."*** ***Wayyēṭ ʾēlāyw ḥāsed*** — **literally *extended ḥesed toward him*,
and it is the first time in Genesis that God's ***ḥesed*** **is the object of this verb.** ***Ḥesed*** is
**the covenant word** — loyal, obligated love — used by Abraham's servant of God's dealings with his
master (24:12, 14, 27) and by Jacob at 32:10. **Here it is extended to a man in a prison with no
covenant speech ever addressed to him.** See [[Favor and Grace]] and [[Covenant]].

***"Gave him favor in the sight of the keeper."*** ***Ḥēn*** — **the same word as 39:4**, *"Joseph found
favor in his sight"*, **and the same word that is about to describe Israel leaving Egypt**: *"Yahweh gave
the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians"* (Exodus 3:21; 11:3; 12:36). **The mechanism by which one
slave survives an Egyptian institution is the mechanism by which a nation later walks out of one.**

***"Committed to Joseph's hand."*** ***Bəyad*** — ***hand*** **is the chapter's keyword and it is used
nine times.** He is bought *"from the hand of the Ishmaelites"* (39:1); everything prospers *"in his
hand"* (39:3); all that Potiphar has is put *"into his hand"* (39:4, 6); **she catches the garment and it
is left *"in her hand"* (39:12, 13)**; and the prison is committed *"to Joseph's hand"* (39:22), where
the keeper *"didn't look after anything that was under his hand"* (39:23). **The chapter is a ledger of
what is in whose hand**, and the one thing that ever leaves his is a piece of cloth.

***"Because Yahweh was with him; and that which he did, Yahweh made it prosper."*** **The book's last use
of the covenant name for eleven chapters**, and it is spent on the administration of a jail.

## People

- [[Joseph]] — **thirteen years of his life begin here, and the chapter gives him one speech and one
  act.** He refuses, argues from his master's trust and then from God, is besieged *day by day*, leaves a
  garment in a woman's hand and runs, and **is never recorded as answering the charge.** He is the only
  major figure in Genesis God never speaks to; **what he gets instead is a narrator who says *Yahweh was
  with Joseph* four times.** He ends the chapter running a prison with the same authority he ran an
  estate
- [[Potiphar's Wife]] — **new page.** Unnamed, as Genesis leaves her; **two words of recorded speech to
  Joseph, said twice**, and two full paragraphs of speech about him. **She tells the household before she
  tells her husband**, keeps the garment as an exhibit, routes the blame back to the man who bought him,
  and calls Joseph *a Hebrew* twice. **Her account reverses a real sequence rather than inventing one**,
  and Genesis never returns to her
- [[Potiphar]] — **buys, promotes, delegates everything, and then imprisons — without a line of
  dialogue anywhere in the book.** *"His wrath was kindled"* (39:19) names no target. **He does not
  execute a slave accused of assaulting his wife**, and the prison he uses is inside his own house
  (40:3). **Genesis does not say whether he believed her**, and the wiki does not decide
- [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] — present as an institution rather than a person: Potiphar is *his*
  officer (39:1), and the prison holds *"the king's prisoners"* (39:20). **The cell Joseph is put in is
  the one that eventually opens onto the throne room**
- [[Rachel]] — not present, and **described in this chapter anyway.** *"Joseph was well-built and
  handsome"* (39:6) is ***yəpēh-tōʾar wîpēh marʾeh***, **the exact pair Genesis used of her at 29:17**,
  and in both places it stands immediately before the trouble it causes
- **The keeper of the prison (39:21–23)** — ***śar bêt-hassōhar***, the second Egyptian functionary in
  two paragraphs to hand Joseph everything and stop counting. **No page**, on the wiki's standing
  practice for unnamed single-scene functionaries; he is a structural echo of Potiphar rather than a
  character, and **Genesis gives him no speech, no name, and no motive**
- **The men of the house (39:11, 14)** — named only by their absence and then addressed as an audience.
  **Their function is to be the jury she empanels before the trial.** No page
- [[Jacob]] — absent and, for the length of this chapter and the ten after it, wrong. He is at Hebron
  mourning a son who is running an Egyptian estate

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — **named four times in five verses at the chapter's opening**, twice as a nationality
  (*"my master the Egyptian"*, *"the Egyptian's house"*) rather than a country. **The blessing of
  Genesis 12:3 lands on an Egyptian household before it lands on anyone else outside the family**, and
  the estate, the prison and the royal detainees are the first Egyptian institutions the Bible describes
  from inside
- [[Canaan]] — the land Joseph has been carried out of; *"the land of the Hebrews"* is his own name for
  it at 40:15, which is the phrase 39:14 makes necessary

## Themes

- [[Sexual Sin]] — **new page.** *"How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?"* —
  **the first refusal in Scripture, and the first time the offence is named as one against God by the
  person declining it.** The theme has been accumulating in Genesis without a page since Sodom
- [[Testing]] — **not the word Genesis uses.** There is no *nissâ* here, no divine initiative, and no
  statement that Joseph is being tested; **the chapter presents an unsought situation and a repeated
  refusal.** The wiki records the distinction rather than importing the vocabulary of Genesis 22
- [[Favor and Grace]] — ***ḥēn*** twice (39:4, 21) and ***ḥesed*** once (39:21), **both of them granted
  to a man with no standing to be granted anything**, and *ḥēn* is the word Exodus will use for how the
  nation leaves
- [[Blessing]] — *"Yahweh blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake"*; ***biglal***, the same
  construction Laban used at 30:27, and the only two places Genesis uses it
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — **the fullest picture Genesis gives of what being owned looked like**: a
  purchase, a stewardship, a legal non-personhood that makes an accusation unanswerable, and a punishment
  handed down with no hearing
- [[Deception]] — a true sequence with one pronoun reversed, presented with a real object in evidence;
  **the family's own technique, used on the family**
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — **the second garment stripped from Joseph in three chapters**, and the
  second one used to lie about him
- [[Language and Speech]] — ***ʿibrî*** spoken with contempt for the first time; ***ṣāḥaq***, the Isaac
  verb, in an accusation of assault; and a charge framed around *"I cried with a loud voice"*
- [[Work]] — *imy-r pr*, steward of the house; the chapter measures God's presence entirely in
  administrative competence
- [[Suffering]] — **a man punished for the thing he refused to do**, and never cleared
- [[Righteousness]] — conduct with no law to cite, no audience, and no reward inside the chapter
- [[The Nations]] — an Egyptian slaveholder who diagnoses Yahweh correctly, and an Egyptian household
  that prospers because of a Hebrew slave
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"his master saw that Yahweh was with him"* (39:3) and *"his master's wife
  set her eyes on Joseph"* (39:7); **the same idiom, four verses apart, for reading a man correctly and
  for wanting him**
- [[Judgment]] — a verdict with no trial, and a narrator who declines to say what the judge thought

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *Joseph in Potiphar's House —
  Genesis 39:1–23*, and the chapter is a self-contained unit with an unusually explicit frame: **39:2 and
  39:21 open matching paragraphs with the same four Hebrew words**, and 40:1 starts *"after these things"*
  with a new cast. A split at 39:20 was considered and rejected — **the prison paragraph is the point of
  the chapter**, not an appendix to it, because it is the house paragraph restated after the disaster.
- **On the title, kept from the plan.** *Joseph in Potiphar's House* is where a reader would look, and
  **it is accurate about the frame**: the prison is in Potiphar's house too (40:3). **Alternatives
  considered and rejected**: *Joseph and Potiphar's Wife* (names the antagonist and reduces the chapter to
  the scene everyone already remembers, when the chapter's own emphasis is the *Yahweh was with Joseph*
  frame around it); *The False Accusation* (covers thirteen of the twenty-three verses); *Yahweh Was With
  Joseph* (a phrase-title, which this wiki reserves for connection pages). **No other book has a passage
  page by this title**, so no `(Book)` qualifier is needed.
- **On what was created, and the judgment calls in it.**
  - **[[Potiphar's Wife]]** — the chapter's antagonist, the mover of its entire plot, and the speaker of
    more words in it than anyone else. **She is unnamed and the page keeps her that way**, titled by whom
    she appears with, per the `AGENTS.md` convention for figures known only by a relation or a title —
    the same rule that produced [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] and [[Shua's Daughter]]. **The later
    tradition's names for her (Zuleika in the Islamic and Persian retellings, Aseneth's mother in some
    Jewish ones) are recorded on the page and not adopted**, because Genesis withholds the name
    deliberately and the wiki does not supply what the text refuses.
  - **[[Sexual Sin]]** — **a new theme page, and the largest judgment call in this pass.** Genesis has
    been accumulating this material for twenty-five chapters with nowhere to put it: Sodom (19:5),
    [[Lot and His Daughters]], the two wife-sister episodes, [[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]],
    Reuben and [[Bilhah]] (35:22), Er and Onan, and [[Judah and Tamar]]. **[[Marriage]] covers the
    ordinance and not its violation, and [[Testing]] explicitly excludes temptation** — the existing
    page says so in its own summary line. **This chapter is the first outright refusal in the sequence**
    and the first time the category is named from inside, which is why the page is opened here rather
    than retrofitted to Genesis 19. **Older passage pages were not retro-linked to it in this pass**;
    that is left for a lint pass, and the theme page carries the full list of passages itself so nothing
    is lost.
  - **[[A Garment Used as Evidence - The Coat and the Cloak]]** — the strongest structural parallel in
    the Joseph cycle, and **it is internal to two consecutive Joseph chapters**, which is exactly what a
    connection page is for.
  - **[[Sin Against God - Joseph at 39.9 and David at Psalm 51]]** — 39:9 is the first statement of a
    theology the Psalter makes famous, and **the two texts are on opposite sides of the same act.**
- **On what was deliberately not created.**
  - **No page for the keeper of the prison.** Unnamed, no speech, no motive, and **structurally a
    repetition of Potiphar rather than a person** — the wiki's practice for anonymous single-scene
    figures, set at the midwife of 35:17 and the man in the field of 37:15.
  - **No connection page for the Egyptian *Tale of Two Brothers*** (Papyrus D'Orbiney, 19th Dynasty), in
    which Bata refuses his elder brother's wife and is falsely accused by her. **The parallel is real and
    it is noted here**, but a connection page in this wiki is for links *within* Scripture, per
    `AGENTS.md`, and **a shared folk-tale pattern is not a dependence claim the wiki is in a position to
    make in either direction.**
  - **No connection page for the *Yahweh was with Joseph* frame.**
    [[I Will Be With You - The Promise of Presence]] already exists and **already contains the Genesis 39
    material**, written forward before this pericope arrived; it was updated rather than duplicated.
  - **No `Temptation` theme page separate from [[Sexual Sin]].** One page, opened where the material
    actually is, rather than two thin ones.
- **An ambiguity flagged and not resolved: did Potiphar believe his wife?** *"His wrath was kindled"*
  (39:19) has no object. **The evidence for doubt is circumstantial and real** — no execution, no sale,
  and confinement in his own house's royal prison rather than a slave's punishment — **and the evidence
  is entirely from what does not happen.** Genesis gives him no dialogue in the whole book. **The wiki
  sets out both readings and adopts neither.**
- **An ambiguity flagged and not resolved: *"except for the food which he ate"* (39:6).** Either the
  Egyptian dietary boundary Genesis itself states at 43:32, or the old euphemistic reading in which
  *bread* means *his wife*. **The first is adopted as the plainer because the book supplies it**; the
  second is recorded because it explains the clause's position.
- **A contradiction carried forward, not newly created: who sold Joseph.** 39:1 says **Ishmaelites**;
  37:36, one verse earlier in the narrative sequence, says **Midianites** — **the two statements are
  about the same purchase by the same buyer.** Set out on
  [[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]], **and the wiki adopts none of the three
  readings.**
- **On the divine-name statistics above, which are counted rather than remembered.** The table was
  produced by counting occurrences in `raw/bible/Genesis.md` chapter by chapter, not from memory. **The
  WEB renders the Tetragrammaton as *Yahweh* throughout**, which makes the count mechanical; a
  translation using *the LORD* would give the same distribution. **The wiki reports the distribution as a
  fact about the text and does not argue a source-critical conclusion from it** — the concentration is
  equally compatible with a deliberate authorial frame around the chapter and with a strand of material
  that used the name.
- **On stale forward-reference markers cleared elsewhere in the wiki.** *(not yet ingested)* markers
  pointing at Genesis 39 were cleared on [[Joseph]], [[Potiphar]], [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]],
  [[Egypt]], [[Blessed for His Sake - Laban's House and Potiphar's]], and
  [[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]].
- **What was left for later pericopes.** The prison located in Potiphar's house and *"the land of the
  Hebrews"* (40:3, 15); the cupbearer's memory (41:9–14); *Potiphera* (41:45); the Egyptians' refusal to
  eat with Hebrews (43:32); the last *Yahweh* of the book (49:18); and *"God meant it for good"* (50:20).
  **All are given as plain scripture references rather than wikilinks, so none is a dangling link.**
