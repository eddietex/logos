# Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 34:1–31

## Summary

**A chapter about a woman in which the woman never speaks, conducted entirely between men, and the
first chapter in Genesis with no divine name in it anywhere.**

Thirty-one verses in four movements: what Shechem does to Dinah and what he then wants (1–4), the
two households' reactions and the negotiation (5–17), the city's assembly and its consent (18–24),
and the third day (25–31). **[[Dinah]] is named six times and is the grammatical object almost every
time** — *taken*, *humbled*, *loved*, *defiled*, *given*, and finally *taken out of Shechem's house*.
She is not reported to speak, to consent, to refuse, or to react, and Genesis reports no outcome for
her at all. **The wiki treats that silence as the chapter's central fact rather than a gap to fill**,
on the practice already used for [[Lot's Wife]] and [[Job's Children]].

**No one in the chapter mentions God.** Genesis 34 is one of four chapters in the book where the
divine name never appears (34, 36, 37, 47) and **the first of them** — and it is the chapter in
which the covenant's own sign is proposed, agreed, performed on an entire town, and then used to
kill it. **[[Circumcision]] is invoked without the God who commanded it being named once.** Genesis
17 threatened that an uncircumcised male *"shall be cut off from his people"*; here it is the
freshly circumcised males who are cut down, on the third day, while they are *sore*.

**Every party in the chapter deceives, and the narrator says so of only one.** *"The sons of Jacob
answered Shechem and Hamor his father with deceit"* (34:13) — ***bĕmirmâ***, **the same word the
narrator put in Isaac's mouth about Jacob himself**: *"Your brother came with deceit, and has taken
away your blessing"* (27:35). The family's signature method is now in the hands of the next
generation, at scale. But Hamor and Shechem argue their own case two ways as well: to Jacob's sons
it is intermarriage and open land (34:9–10); to their own citizens at the gate it is
*"Won't their livestock and their possessions and all their animals be ours?"* (34:23). **Genesis
labels only the Israelite deceit as deceit.** See [[Deception]].

**And the chapter ends on a question nobody answers.** Jacob's objection at 34:30 is entirely
prudential — *"you have troubled me, to make me odious to the inhabitants of the land… I am few in
number"* — with no word about right, wrong, or his daughter. The brothers reply,
*"Should he deal with our sister as with a prostitute?"* (34:31), and the chapter simply stops.
**Jacob's real answer arrives fifteen chapters later, from his deathbed**: *"Cursed be their anger,
for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel. I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them
in Israel"* (49:7). See
[[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]].

### 34:1–4 — she goes out, and the verbs change hands

> **1** Dinah, the daughter of Leah, whom she bore to Jacob, went out to see the daughters of the
> land.
> **2** Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite, the prince of the land, saw her. He took her, lay
> with her, and humbled her.
> **3** His soul joined to Dinah, the daughter of Jacob, and he loved the young lady, and spoke
> kindly to the young lady.
> **4** Shechem spoke to his father, Hamor, saying, “Get me this young lady as a wife.”

**Dinah is introduced by two parents and no age**, *"the daughter of Leah, whom she bore to Jacob"* —
identified through her mother first, which is how [[Leah]]'s children are counted at 46:15, the one
other verse in the Bible that mentions her. **She goes out *"to see"*** — ***lirʾôt*** — **and the
next verb in the chapter is Shechem's *saw her*.** The seeing passes from her to him in the space of
one verse, and everything after it is done to her. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

**Genesis passes no judgment on her going out**, and later readers have supplied one steadily —
*Jubilees* 30 and a strand of rabbinic midrash read the outing itself as the fault, some of it by
attaching Leah's *"you must come in to me"* (30:16) to her daughter. **The text says she went to see
the daughters of the land and stops.** The wiki records the readings as reception and adopts none of
them.

**Verse 2 is three verbs and a title.** ***He took her, lay with her, and humbled her*** —
***wayyiqqaḥ***, ***wayyiškab***, ***wayĕʿannehā***. **The third is the decisive one.** ***ʿānâ***,
*to afflict, humble, bring low*, is the verb of Sarai's treatment of [[Hagar]] (16:6) and of Egypt's
of Israel in the prophecy of 15:13 — *"they will afflict them four hundred years"* — and it is the
word the law uses of rape (Deuteronomy 22:24, 29) and the word 2 Samuel 13 uses repeatedly of
Tamar's case. **Genesis does not editorialize; it uses the verb that will become the legal term.**
And *"the prince of the land"* names him as the one person in the town with the power to do it
without consequence. See [[Dinah and Tamar - Two Rapes and Two Avenging Brothers]].

**Then verse 3 reverses direction, and this is the chapter's strangest sentence.** *"His soul joined
to Dinah"* — ***wattidbaq napšô***, from ***dābaq***, **the verb of Genesis 2:24**, *"a man… will
join to his wife"*. **The one-flesh word of the creation ordinance is used of what has just
happened.** *"He loved the young lady"*, and *"spoke kindly to"* her — ***waydabbēr ʿal-lēb***, *spoke
to her heart*, the idiom of comfort and courtship used of Boaz to Ruth (Ruth 2:13), of God to Israel
(Hosea 2:14), and of the Levite going after his concubine in Judges 19:3. **Genesis records the
affection without a word of approval and without retracting the verb in verse 2.** See [[Marriage]]
and [[One Flesh - Genesis 2 and the New Testament on Marriage]].

**The order is the exact inverse of the one parallel the Bible gives it.** Amnon *"loved"* Tamar
before the act and *"hated her with exceedingly great hatred"* after it — *"the hatred with which he
hated her was greater than the love with which he had loved her"* (2 Samuel 13:15). **Here the
sequence runs the other way**, and the wiki does not treat either order as the more humane; it notes
that Genesis 34 gives its perpetrator a reputation (*"he was honored above all the house of his
father"*, 34:19) and 2 Samuel 13 does not.

**And verse 4 is addressed to his father, about her, without her.** *"Get me this young lady as a
wife"* — the same construction Samson uses of the woman at Timnah (Judges 14:2). **Dinah is spoken
of in the third person by every character in the chapter and by none of them to her face.**

### 34:5–7 — a silence, and a phrase the Bible will reuse six times

> **5** Now Jacob heard that he had defiled Dinah, his daughter; and his sons were with his
> livestock in the field. Jacob held his peace until they came.
> **6** Hamor the father of Shechem went out to Jacob to talk with him.
> **7** The sons of Jacob came in from the field when they heard it. The men were grieved, and
> they were very angry, because he had done folly in Israel in lying with Jacob’s daughter; a
> thing ought not to be done.

***"Jacob held his peace"*** — ***heḥĕrîš***, *he kept silent*. **It is the same posture he takes
when Reuben sleeps with Bilhah — *"Israel heard of it"* (35:22), and nothing follows** — and the
same posture David takes when he hears about Tamar (*"he was very angry"*, and does nothing, 2 Samuel
13:21). **Genesis gives no reason for the silence and does not call it wrong.** It gives a
circumstance instead: *"his sons were with his livestock in the field"*, which is a reason to wait
and not a reason to say nothing. See [[Jacob]].

**The sons' reaction is the chapter's one unambiguous moral statement, and the narrator makes it, not
a character**: *"The men were grieved, and they were very angry, because he had done **folly in
Israel**… a thing ought not to be done."* Two formulas, both of which have careers ahead of them.

- ***Nĕbālâ bĕyiśrāʾēl***, *folly in Israel* — **its first occurrence in the Bible, and an
  anachronism on its face**: there is no Israel yet. Jacob was renamed two chapters ago, and the
  phrase names a *national* standard for a household of thirteen people camped outside a town that
  is not theirs. **Genesis uses the language of a people that does not exist to judge an act against
  a family that does.** It recurs at Deuteronomy 22:21, Joshua 7:15, Judges 19:23 and 20:6, 10,
  2 Samuel 13:12, and Jeremiah 29:23 — every one of them sexual or covenantal treachery, and every
  one of them inside Israel. See
  [[Folly in Israel - Nebalah from Genesis 34 to 2 Samuel 13]].
- ***Wĕkēn lōʾ yēʿāśeh***, *a thing ought not to be done* — **Tamar says it nearly word for word**:
  *"no such thing ought to be done in Israel"* (2 Samuel 13:12), the only other place the formula
  appears in this shape.

**The grief comes before the anger** — *"the men were grieved, and they were very angry"* — and the
verb is ***ʿāṣab***, **the root of God's own grief at 6:6**, *"it grieved him in his heart"*, and of
the *pain* assigned to the woman and the man at 3:16–17. **It is the last time anyone in the chapter
is said to feel anything but anger.**

### 34:8–12 — an offer with the offence left out of it

> **8** Hamor talked with them, saying, “The soul of my son, Shechem, longs for your daughter.
> Please give her to him as a wife.
> **9** Make marriages with us. Give your daughters to us, and take our daughters for yourselves.
> **10** You shall dwell with us, and the land will be before you. Live and trade in it, and get
> possessions in it.”
> **11** Shechem said to her father and to her brothers, “Let me find favor in your eyes, and
> whatever you will tell me I will give.
> **12** Ask me a great amount for a dowry, and I will give whatever you ask of me, but give me
> the young lady as a wife.”

**Hamor never mentions what his son did.** His speech opens on desire — *"the soul of my son,
Shechem, longs for your daughter"* — and moves immediately to a treaty: **intermarriage in both
directions, residence, freedom of movement, trade, and property**. *"Make marriages with us. Give
your daughters to us, and take our daughters for yourselves."* **It is a generous offer and it is
the precise thing the covenant line has refused three times** — Abraham's oath that Isaac's wife not
come from *"the daughters of the Canaanites"* (24:3), Rebekah's *"I am weary of my life because of
the daughters of Heth"* (27:46), and Isaac's charge to Jacob in the same words (28:1). **What Hamor
proposes as peace, the family's whole marriage policy exists to prevent.** See [[Marriage]] and
[[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]].

**Shechem's own offer is legally exact.** *"Ask me a great amount for a dowry, and I will give
whatever you ask of me"* — ***mōhar***, the bride-price, and ***mattān***, a gift on top of it.
**This is, in the Torah's own later terms, the prescribed remedy**: Exodus 22:16–17 has the seducer
pay the *mōhar* and marry her unless her father refuses, and Deuteronomy 22:28–29 has the man who
seizes an unbetrothed virgin pay fifty shekels, marry her, and never divorce her. **Genesis 34
neither cites that law nor rules it out** — the law is four books away, and the chapter is not
arguing a case. **The wiki records the tension and does not resolve it**: by the standard the Torah
will later state, Shechem is offering what the law requires; by the standard the brothers state, no
settlement is available at all. See the Notes.

### 34:13–17 — the answer, which the narrator calls deceit before anyone reads it

> **13** The sons of Jacob answered Shechem and Hamor his father with deceit, and spoke, because
> he had defiled Dinah their sister,
> **14** and said to them, “We can’t do this thing, to give our sister to one who is
> uncircumcised; for that is a reproach to us.
> **15** Only on this condition will we consent to you. If you will be as we are, that every male
> of you be circumcised;
> **16** then will we give our daughters to you, and we will take your daughters to us, and we
> will dwell with you, and we will become one people.
> **17** But if you will not listen to us, to be circumcised, then we will take our sister, and we
> will be gone.”

***Bĕmirmâ***, *with deceit*, **is the narrator's word and not a character's**, and it is **the word
Isaac used of Jacob**: *"Your brother came with deceit, and has taken away your blessing"* (27:35).
**Genesis puts the family's signature method into the mouths of the next generation in a single
adverbial phrase, and then lets the plan run for twelve verses without comment.** See [[Deception]]
and [[A Garment and a Goat - Jacob Deceives and Is Deceived]].

**The stated ground is real even though the answer is false.** *"We can't do this thing, to give our
sister to one who is uncircumcised; for that is a **reproach** to us"* — ***ḥerpâ***, **and the only
other occurrence of that noun in Genesis is [[Rachel]] at 30:23**, *"God has taken away my
reproach"*. **The two verses in the book that use the word are the birth of Rachel's first son and
the vengeance taken for the daughter who was born four verses earlier with no explanation of her
name at all.** The endogamy the brothers appeal to is the household's actual policy (24:3; 27:46;
28:1) — **which is what makes the sentence usable as a lie.**

**And the condition offered is the covenant sign itself.** Genesis 17 made circumcision the mark of
a covenant with God, given to Abraham's household and to *"he who is bought with your money"* — a
sign with an explicit provision for outsiders joining. **Here it is proposed as a commercial term,
accepted as one, and Genesis 34 never once names the God whose sign it is.** See [[Circumcision]].

### 34:18–24 — the gate, and a second argument for a different audience

> **18** Their words pleased Hamor and Shechem, Hamor’s son.
> **19** The young man didn’t wait to do this thing, because he had delight in Jacob’s daughter,
> and he was honored above all the house of his father.
> **20** Hamor and Shechem, his son, came to the gate of their city, and talked with the men of
> their city, saying,
> **21** “These men are peaceful with us. Therefore let them live in the land and trade in it. For
> behold, the land is large enough for them. Let us take their daughters to us for wives, and let
> us give them our daughters.
> **22** Only on this condition will the men consent to us to live with us, to become one people,
> if every male among us is circumcised, as they are circumcised.
> **23** Won’t their livestock and their possessions and all their animals be ours? Only let us
> give our consent to them, and they will dwell with us.”
> **24** All who went out of the gate of his city listened to Hamor, and to Shechem his son; and
> every male was circumcised, all who went out of the gate of his city.

**The city gate is the town's court and assembly**, the same institution Abraham buys Machpelah in
front of (23:10–18) and Boaz convenes in Ruth 4. **Hamor and Shechem put the terms to it, and the
terms they put are not the terms they were given.** To Jacob's sons the proposal was kinship and
shared land; to their own citizens it is an investment: *"Won't their livestock and their possessions
and all their animals be ours?"* (34:23). **Both sides are selling the merger to their own people on
a reason they did not say out loud to the other.**

**And the town agrees — unanimously, and to major surgery, on their prince's word.** *"All who went
out of the gate of his city listened… and every male was circumcised."* **Genesis records no
objection from anyone in it.** The narrator's one editorial note in the passage is about Shechem
himself: *"The young man didn't wait to do this thing, because he had delight in Jacob's daughter,
and he was honored above all the house of his father"* (34:19) — **a man the text calls honoured,
acting fast, for a reason the text calls delight, in a chapter that began with him taking her by
force.** Genesis holds the two together and does not reconcile them.

### 34:25–29 — the third day

> **25** On the third day, when they were sore, two of Jacob’s sons, Simeon and Levi, Dinah’s
> brothers, each took his sword, came upon the unsuspecting city, and killed all the males.
> **26** They killed Hamor and Shechem, his son, with the edge of the sword, and took Dinah out of
> Shechem’s house, and went away.
> **27** Jacob’s sons came on the dead, and plundered the city, because they had defiled their
> sister.
> **28** They took their flocks, their herds, their donkeys, that which was in the city, that
> which was in the field,
> **29** and all their wealth. They took captive all their little ones and their wives, and took
> as plunder everything that was in the house.

**The third day after circumcision is the medical peak of the pain**, and the chapter says so:
*"when they were sore"*. **The plan was never a marriage negotiation.**

***"Two of Jacob's sons, Simeon and Levi, Dinah's brothers"*** — **the narrator specifies the
relationship that explains the arithmetic.** [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]] are Dinah's **full** brothers,
both [[Leah]]'s (29:33–34; 30:21), in a household of four mothers. **Of twelve brothers, the two who
act are the two who share her mother**, and the wiki notes that Genesis states the fact without
drawing the inference.

***"Came upon the unsuspecting city"*** — ***beṭaḥ***, *in security, confidently*, **the word for
trust.** The city is killed in the state the treaty produced in it. **They kill *all the males*, take
Dinah out of Shechem's house — the only indication in the chapter of where she has been for the
preceding twenty-two verses — and go.**

**Then the arithmetic widens.** Verses 27–29 are a different subject: *"Jacob's sons came on the
dead, and plundered the city."* **Flocks, herds, donkeys, everything in the city and in the field,
all their wealth, and — stated flatly — *"they took captive all their little ones and their wives"*.**
**Two brothers killed, and the rest of them looted, and the women and children of the town became
property.** Genesis 49 curses two men for it; this chapter records the participation of more. See
[[War]] and [[Wealth and Possessions]].

### 34:30–31 — an objection about consequences, and a question with no answer

> **30** Jacob said to Simeon and Levi, “You have troubled me, to make me odious to the
> inhabitants of the land, among the Canaanites and the Perizzites. I am few in number. They will
> gather themselves together against me and strike me, and I will be destroyed, I and my house.”
> **31** They said, “Should he deal with our sister as with a prostitute?”

**Jacob's objection contains no moral term.** *"You have troubled me"* — ***ʿăkartem***, the verb
behind Achan's name for the valley of Achor, *"Why have you troubled us? Yahweh will trouble you
today"* (Joshua 7:25) — *"to make me **odious**"*, ***lĕhabʾîšēnî***, literally *to make me stink*
among the [[Canaan]]ites and the Perizzites. Then the reason: *"I am few in number"* —
***mĕtê mispār***, the phrase Psalm 105:12 uses of the patriarchs themselves, *"a few men in number,
yes, very few, and foreigners in it"*. **He names the danger to the household and says nothing about Dinah, nothing
about the town, and nothing about the oath his sons swore and broke.**

**The brothers answer with a question and the chapter ends on it.** *"Should he deal with our sister
as with a prostitute?"* — ***hakzônâ yaʿăśeh ʾet-ʾăḥôtēnû***. **It is unanswered here by anyone**,
and it is the only sentence in the chapter that names what was done to her as the thing being
avenged. **Genesis gives the last word to the killers and withholds its verdict for fifteen
chapters**, until 49:5–7. See [[Vengeance]] and [[Retribution]].

## People

- [[Dinah]] — **the subject of one verb in the chapter and the object of the rest.** She goes out to
  see; after that she is taken, lain with, humbled, loved, spoken to, asked for, defiled, negotiated
  over, and finally *taken out of Shechem's house*. **No speech, no consent, no refusal, no reaction,
  and no reported outcome.** Genesis mentions her once more, in the descent-into-Egypt list (46:15),
  and Jacob's deathbed — which has something to say about ten of his sons — does not mention her at
  all.
- [[Shechem (son of Hamor)]] — **new page.** The prince of the land; takes Dinah by force, then loves
  her, asks his father for her, offers any bride-price named, is the first in the town to be
  circumcised, and is killed with his father on the third day. **The narrator calls him *honoured
  above all the house of his father*** in the same paragraph in which he is buying a woman he has
  already raped.
- [[Hamor]] — **filled out from the thin page created at 33:19.** The Hivite ruler; opens the
  negotiation without mentioning the offence, offers intermarriage, residence, trade and property,
  and sells the deal to his own citizens on the livestock. Killed with his son.
- [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]] — **Dinah's full brothers, and the only two who kill.** They propose the
  circumcision, wait three days, take their swords, and answer their father with the chapter's last
  line. **Jacob's deathbed pairs them in the only shared curse among the twelve** (49:5–7), and the
  scattering it decrees lands on both and means opposite things — see
  [[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]].
- [[Jacob]] — **hears, holds his peace, is absent from the negotiation his sons conduct in his
  name, and objects afterwards on grounds of safety.** He does not answer the brothers' question
  here; he answers it in 49:5–7. **And at 48:22 he claims this same ground *"which I took out of the
  hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow"*** — a sentence that fits neither his purchase
  at 33:19 nor his repudiation at 34:30. See
  [[Bought, Sacked, or Taken by the Sword - How Jacob Got Shechem]].
- [[Leah]] — named in the chapter's first clause as Dinah's mother, and not again. **The chapter's two
  avengers are hers**, which is the household fact the narrator supplies at 34:25 and does not
  interpret.
- [[God]] — **absent.** Not named, not addressed, not said to act, in the first of the four chapters
  of Genesis where the divine name never appears. **The covenant sign is used without him**, and the
  protection Jacob fears he has lost is supplied in the very next chapter without his asking for it:
  *"a terror of God was on the cities that were around them"* (35:5).

## Places

- [[Shechem (city)]] — **renamed this pass** from `Shechem.md`, because the man of the same name now
  has a page and `AGENTS.md` requires both to carry qualifiers. **The town is a character here and
  not a backdrop**: its prince acts, its ruler negotiates, its gate assembly votes, and every male in
  it dies. **The field Jacob bought *in peace* one chapter earlier (33:18–19) is the ground he is
  made to stink on.**
- [[Canaan]] — *"the inhabitants of the land, among the Canaanites and the Perizzites"* (34:30) is
  the chapter's only wide-angle view, and it is Jacob counting the odds against his household.

## Themes

- [[Circumcision]] — **the covenant sign proposed as a commercial term, performed on an entire
  Gentile town, and used as the tactical means of killing it.** Genesis 17's *cut off* becomes
  Genesis 34's *cut down*, and God is not named in the chapter that does it.
- [[Deception]] — ***bĕmirmâ*** at 34:13, the narrator's own word and the word used of Jacob at
  27:35; and the two different arguments Hamor and Shechem make to two different audiences, which the
  narrator does not label at all.
- [[Vengeance]] — **disproportion stated without comment**: one act against one woman, answered by
  every male of a town, its wealth, its women, and its children. The theme's Genesis arithmetic —
  Cain's sevenfold, Lamech's seventy-sevenfold, Noah's one-for-one — meets a case the chapter never
  totals.
- [[Marriage]] — a marriage proposal that is also a treaty of assimilation, *"we will become one
  people"* (34:16, 22), and the endogamy policy (24:3; 27:46; 28:1) used as the cover story for a
  massacre.
- [[War]] — **the first time Israelites take a city**, four centuries before the conquest, by two men
  and by stratagem rather than by battle.
- [[The Nations]] — **the Hivites are given speech, motive, an assembly, and a unanimous decision**,
  and the chapter's one explicitly acquisitive line is theirs (34:23). Both peoples in it act badly
  and only one of them is called deceitful by the narrator.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — Hamor offers *"the land will be before you. Live and trade in it, and get
  possessions in it"* (34:10): **the promise's substance offered as a commercial arrangement by the
  people who hold it.** Jacob's fear at 34:30 is the sojourner's standing fear, and Psalm 105:12
  quotes his phrase back about the patriarchs.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — the livestock argument at the gate (34:23) and the inventory of the
  plunder (34:28–29), which are the same list read from two sides.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — **the chapter is the wiki's clearest counter-case** to the
  Abram-and-Lot settlement at 13:8–9 and the treaties at Beersheba and Galeed: a dispute in which a
  negotiated settlement is reached, ratified, and then used as the weapon.

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *Dinah and Shechem — Genesis
  34:1–31*, the whole chapter, and it needed no revision. **34:1 opens on a new character and a new
  town's business**, with 33:20's altar behind it, and **34:31 ends on an unanswered question**, with
  35:1 opening on a direct command from God to move. A split at 34:24 — the negotiation from the
  massacre — was considered and rejected: the circumcision is only intelligible as the first half of
  the killing, and the chapter is one plan executed from end to end.
- **On the title, which was redrawn.** The plan's box said *Dinah and Shechem*. **That was changed to
  *Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem*** for two reasons: it reads, as written, like a pairing of two
  people rather than what the chapter is, and it now collides in the ear with the two newly qualified
  pages [[Shechem (city)]] and [[Shechem (son of Hamor)]]. **Alternatives considered and rejected**:
  *The Rape of Dinah* (names the first four verses and not the other twenty-seven) and *The Defiling
  of Dinah* (adopts the chapter's own loaded verb as the wiki's).
- **On the two new qualified pages, which the schema required.** `wiki/places/Shechem.md` became
  [[Shechem (city)]] and the man became [[Shechem (son of Hamor)]], with all 26 inbound `[[Shechem]]`
  links rewritten across the wiki. **This was scheduled a pericope in advance**, on [[Hamor]]'s page
  at the 33:19 ingest, exactly as `AGENTS.md` requires for a collision that arrives before its second
  page does. The patronymic form was chosen over a bare `(person)` on the
  `Enoch (son of Cain)` / [[Enoch (city)]] precedent, which the schema gives as its worked example.
- **On what the chapter does not say, kept open rather than filled.** Four things: **whether Dinah
  was held against her will** between 34:3 and 34:26 (she is *taken out of Shechem's house*, which
  implies she was in it, and the text says no more); **whether her going out is being blamed** (later
  readers supply the blame; Genesis does not); **whether Shechem's later love mitigates anything**
  (the chapter reports both and reconciles neither); and **whether Jacob's silence is prudence,
  paralysis, or indifference** (Genesis gives him a circumstance at 34:5 and no interior at all).
- **On the legal tension, recorded and not adjudicated.** By Exodus 22:16–17 and Deuteronomy 22:28–29
  — four books later, and not cited here — Shechem's offer of the bride-price and marriage is the
  remedy the Torah itself prescribes. **The brothers refuse the remedy the law will require and apply
  a penalty no law states.** Both halves of that sentence are true, and Genesis 34 contains no ruling
  on either. The wiki states the tension on this page, on [[Vengeance]], and on
  [[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]], and takes no side.
- **On the contradiction about how Jacob came to hold Shechem, flagged explicitly.** Three claims
  stand in the text: he **bought** the parcel for a hundred pieces of money (33:19, repeated at
  Joshua 24:32); his sons **sacked** the city and he disowned the act (34:27–30); and he tells Joseph
  he **took** the portion *"out of the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow"* (48:22).
  **None of the three is retracted by the text and the wiki does not harmonize them** — see the new
  connection page [[Bought, Sacked, or Taken by the Sword - How Jacob Got Shechem]] for the four
  standing readings and what each one costs.
- **On the reception history, recorded as reception.** *Jubilees* 30 and Judith 9:2–4 praise Simeon
  and Levi outright, and Theodotus the Hellenistic poet makes the massacre divinely sanctioned;
  rabbinic tradition divides, and Genesis 49:7 is the text every critic of the brothers cites.
  **None of this is Genesis's verdict**, and the page keeps it in the Notes rather than in the
  reading.
- **On what was created.** **One person page**, [[Shechem (son of Hamor)]]. **Four connection
  pages**: [[Folly in Israel - Nebalah from Genesis 34 to 2 Samuel 13]],
  [[Dinah and Tamar - Two Rapes and Two Avenging Brothers]],
  [[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]], and
  [[Bought, Sacked, or Taken by the Sword - How Jacob Got Shechem]]. **One page renamed**,
  [[Shechem (city)]].
- **On Tamar, named here without a page, deliberately.** The chapter's closest parallel is
  2 Samuel 13, and its victim shares a name with the Tamar of Genesis 38, who arrives four pericopes
  from now. **No `Tamar` page is created in this pass**: creating one for David's daughter would
  guarantee a rename at *Judah and Tamar* (Genesis 38:1–30), where Genesis's own Tamar has the better
  claim on the bare title. She is referred to in plain prose here, and the two are distinguished by
  patronymic on the connection page, so nothing dangles and nothing has to be renamed later.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Genesis 35:1–5 (Bethel, and the terror that answers 34:30),
  49:5–7 (the deathbed curse), 48:22 (the sword and the bow), 46:15 (Dinah counted among Leah's
  children). All are given as plain scripture references rather than wikilinks, so none is a
  dangling link.
