# The Call of Abram

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 12:1–9

## Summary

Nine verses, and the Bible changes shape. Genesis has spent eleven chapters on everyone — a
creation, a fall, a flood, a map of seventy nations, a scattering — and it has ended each movement
worse than it began. Here it stops addressing everyone. Yahweh speaks to one man, in a foreign city,
in a household the previous pericope has just finished emptying out, and what he says takes the
whole of the primeval history's damage as its subject.

**The command comes before the promise, and it is three subtractions.** *"Leave your country, and
your relatives, and your father's house, and go to the land that I will show you"* (12:1). Hebrew
*lek-ləkā* — a phrase that occurs exactly twice in the Bible, here and at 22:2 — followed by three
ablatives that get smaller and harder as they go: *ʾarṣəkā*, your land; *môladtekā*, your kindred,
the people you were born among; *bêt ʾābîkā*, your father's house, the household you eat in. Rashi's
observation on the order is the standard one and is hard to improve: geographically it is a
narrowing, emotionally it is an ascent — the last thing named is the thing hardest to leave. And the
destination is withheld. Where [[From Shem to Abram]] had [[Terah]] set out *"to go into the land of
Canaan"* and stop halfway, Abram is sent to *"the land that I **will show** you"* and arrives.
Hebrews 11:8 reads exactly this difference: *"he went out, not knowing where he went."*

**Then the promise, and it is made of the word the last eleven chapters lost.** The root *brk*,
bless, occurs **five times in two verses** (12:2–3): *I will bless you*, *you will be a blessing*,
*I will bless those who bless you*, and *all the families of the earth will be blessed through you*.
Genesis 1–11 contains exactly five curses — the serpent (3:14), the ground (3:17), Cain (4:11), the
ground again in Lamech's naming of Noah (5:29), and Canaan (9:25). Five for five. Nothing in the
text points this out and the symmetry is too clean to be accidental; it is the clearest structural
signal in the book that chapter 12 is an answer to chapters 1–11 rather than a fresh start after
them. See [[Blessing]] and [[Curse]].

**The last clause is the one the rest of the Bible argues about.** *"All the families of the earth
will be blessed through you"* (12:3). *Mišpəḥōt hāʾădāmâ* — *families*, the exact word
[[The Table of Nations]] used five times for the seventy peoples (10:5, 18, 20, 31, 32), and
*ʾădāmâ*, the **ground**, which is what God cursed at 3:17 and what Lamech was still complaining
about at 5:29. The promise is aimed by name at the two things the primeval history broke: the
scattered nations, and the cursed soil. One family is chosen, and the stated reason is everybody
else.

**Abram says nothing.** Not a word — no question, no objection, no acceptance. Genesis reports the
answer as a verb: *"So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him"* (12:4). He is seventy-five. He takes
Sarai, Lot, the property, and *"the people whom they had acquired in Haran"*, and covers roughly
four hundred miles to Shechem. His first recorded speech in Scripture is still two verses away, and
when it comes it will be an instruction to his wife to say she is his sister.

**And when he arrives, the land is occupied.** *"Abram passed through the land to the place of
Shechem, to the oak of Moreh. At that time, Canaanites were in the land"* (12:6). The narrator says
it flatly and immediately, before any promise about the land has been made — and then Yahweh
appears, the first theophany in the Bible granted to a named individual, and promises the land not
to Abram but to his offspring: *"I will give this land to your offspring"* (12:7). Abram himself is
given nothing he can stand on. Stephen puts it starkly: *"He gave him no inheritance in it, no, not
enough to set his foot on"* (Acts 7:5).

**What he does with the land instead is walk down it and build on it twice.** Shechem in the north,
then the ridge between [[Bethel]] and [[Ai]], then *"still going on toward the South"* — a transit
of the central highland spine from top to bottom, with an altar at the first stop and an altar and a
proclamation at the second. He builds no house and takes no field. *"He pitched his tent"* (12:8) is
the only dwelling the chapter gives him, and the pericope's last verse (Hebrew *nāsôaʿ wənāsôaʿ*,
journeying and journeying) leaves him still in motion. The next verse is a famine.

### The two journeys, side by side

The whole force of this pericope is in how exactly it reruns the one before it.

| | Genesis 11:31–32 | Genesis 12:1–9 |
|---|---|---|
| Who moves the family | Terah takes | Yahweh sends |
| Destination | named — *the land of Canaan* | withheld — *the land that I will show you* |
| Divine speech | none in 11:10–32 | the pericope is built out of it |
| Outcome | stops at Haran; Terah dies there | arrives; crosses the land end to end |
| What is left behind | Nahor's household, and a grave | country, kindred, father's house |

Two paragraphs, consecutive, with the same family and the same road. The difference between them is
a voice. See [[Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call]].

## Key verses

> **1** Now Yahweh said to Abram, “Leave your country, and your relatives, and your father’s house,
> and go to the land that I will show you.

> **2** I will make of you a great nation. I will bless you and make your name great. You will be a
> blessing.

> **3** I will bless those who bless you, and I will curse him who treates you with contempt. All
> the families of the earth will be blessed through you.”

> **4** So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him. Lot went with him. Abram was seventy-five years old
> when he departed from Haran.

> **5** Abram took Sarai his wife, Lot his brother’s son, all their possessions that they had
> gathered, and the people whom they had acquired in Haran, and they went to go into the land of
> Canaan. They entered into the land of Canaan.

> **6** Abram passed through the land to the place of Shechem, to the oak of Moreh. At that time,
> Canaanites were in the land.

> **7** Yahweh appeared to Abram and said, “I will give this land to your offspring.” He built an
> altar there to Yahweh, who had appeared to him.

> **8** He left from there to go to the mountain on the east of Bethel and pitched his tent, having
> Bethel on the west, and Ai on the east. There he built an altar to Yahweh and called on Yahweh’s
> name.

> **9** Abram traveled, still going on toward the South.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *Abram* here. He receives the first divine speech addressed to a named individual
  since Noah, and answers it with no words at all. Everything the Bible eventually says about him —
  the faith reckoned as righteousness, the going out not knowing where — is a reading of the gap
  between 12:1 and 12:4, which contains no reported deliberation. He is seventy-five, childless, and
  married to a woman the last pericope called barren.
- [[God]] — *Yahweh* throughout, and the pericope is almost entirely his speech. He was absent for
  the whole of 11:10–32; here he commands, promises five times over, appears (12:7, the Bible's first
  *wayyērāʾ* of God to a person), and is called on by name at an altar. What he does not do is
  explain the choice. No reason is given for Abram, then or later — see [[Call and Election]].
- [[Sarah]] — *Sarai*, named in the travel list at 12:5 and silent. The promise of 12:2 is *"I will
  make of you a great nation"*, addressed to a man whose wife the text has just declared unable to
  bear a child (11:30). Genesis puts the promise and the obstacle four verses apart and does not
  remark on the collision; the next ten chapters are that collision. See [[Barrenness]].
- [[Lot]] — *"Lot went with him"* (12:4), and again in the inventory of 12:5. He is not commanded,
  not promised anything, and not explained: the call said *leave your father's house*, and Abram
  brings a member of it. That unresolved presence is what Genesis 13 and 14 and 19 are made of.
- [[Terah]] — not named, but the pericope is unintelligible without him. Abram departs *from Haran*,
  the city his father stopped in and died in, on the road his father was already taking.
- **The people acquired in Haran** — *hannepeš ʾăšer ʿāśû bəḥārān*, literally *"the souls they had
  made in Haran"*. The plain sense is dependants and slaves gathered over the years in Haran; the
  odd verb *made* is what an old and much-loved rabbinic reading (Genesis Rabbah 39:14, followed by
  Rashi) turns into converts — Abram and Sarai making proselytes before there is anything to convert
  to. The wiki records it as a reading of the verb, not as the sense of the verse.
- **The Canaanites** — present in one clause (12:6) and given no words, no king, and no reaction.
  They are the reason the promise of 12:7 is a promise and not a transfer. See
  [[Canaan (son of Ham)]] for the man, [[Canaan]] for the land.

## Places

- [[Haran (city)]] — the point of departure, named twice (12:4, 12:5). The call is delivered here, on
  Genesis's own arrangement; Acts 7:2–4 puts it earlier, in Mesopotamia before Haran.
- [[Canaan]] — entered for the first time by anybody in the Bible: *"they went to go into the land of
  Canaan. They entered into the land of Canaan"* (12:5), the second clause doing what 11:31's never
  got to do.
- [[Shechem]] — the first place in the land Abram stops, at *the oak of Moreh*; a Canaanite sanctuary
  before it is anything of Israel's, and the site Joshua will choose for the covenant renewal.
- [[Bethel]] and [[Ai]] — the two towns Abram pitches between, twenty miles south. Bethel becomes
  Jacob's ladder and Jeroboam's calf; Ai becomes Joshua's one defeat. Abram is standing between them
  eight hundred years early, and the narrator names both by the names they will have later.
- [[The Negev]] — *"the South"* in the WEB, the arid country below the highlands. It is the direction
  the pericope ends in and the corridor into Egypt, which is where verse 10 goes.
- [[Ur of the Chaldees]] — not named here, but the place 15:7 and Nehemiah 9:7 say this journey
  started from, against a Genesis 12 that starts it in Haran.

## Themes

- [[Call and Election]] — the theme's founding text. God chooses one man out of the seventy nations
  just catalogued, gives no reason for choosing him, and states a purpose that reaches past him to
  everyone.
- [[Blessing]] — five occurrences of *brk* in two verses, answering the five curses of Genesis 1–11
  one for one. The word that had been running out since 3:14 is handed back in bulk, and handed to a
  man rather than to the race.
- [[Curse]] — 12:3 keeps it, in a carefully lopsided form: those who bless Abram are a plural
  participle, the one who *belittles* him (*məqallelkā*, from *qālal*, to treat as light) is
  singular, and the verb turned on him is the heavy one, *ʾārar*. Blessing is expected wholesale;
  cursing is contemplated retail.
- [[The Nations]] — *"all the families of the earth"* uses the Table of Nations' own word. Genesis 10
  divided the world into seventy peoples and Genesis 11 scattered them; Genesis 12:3 makes them the
  object of the promise, and the New Testament's whole argument for a mission beyond Israel is built
  on this clause.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the land is entered, walked end to end, and promised to somebody not yet
  born, while its current inhabitants are noted in passing. Abram's only structures in it are two
  altars and a tent.
- [[Covenant]] — not yet. Nothing here is sworn, cut, signed, or given a sign; 12:1–3 is a promise,
  and it becomes a covenant at 15:18 and takes a sign at 17:10. The distinction is worth holding
  onto, because the promise is complete without any of that machinery.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — two altars, no sacrifice mentioned at either. Genesis reports the
  building and not the burning, which is a pattern in the patriarchal altars and unlike Noah's.
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — by inversion. *"Let's make a name for ourselves"* (11:4) is
  answered three verses of genealogy later by *"I will make your name great"* (12:2), same noun,
  different subject. See [[A Name for Ourselves and a Great Name]].

## Notes

- **The raw file has a typo at 12:3.** `raw/bible/Genesis.md`, fetched from bible-api.com, reads
  *"I will curse him who treates you with contempt."* The WEB text reads **treats**. The quotation
  under **Key verses** is verbatim from the raw file per the repo's rule; the slip is in the source
  file, not in the translation, and is noted here rather than silently corrected in the quotation.
- ***Lek-ləkā*, and the two commands it introduces.** לֶךְ־לְךָ occurs twice in the Hebrew Bible: at
  12:1 and at 22:2. Both times it is followed by a destination deliberately withheld — *"the land
  that I will show you"*, *"one of the mountains which I will tell you of"* — and both times what is
  demanded is the surrender of the thing that makes a future possible: first the past he came from,
  then the son he was promised. Genesis Rabbah 55:7 makes the pairing explicit, and it is the
  strongest verbal link between the two ends of the Abraham cycle. The idiom itself is a *dativus
  ethicus*, an untranslatable reflexive: *go, for yourself*, *go, you*. Rashi reads it *"go for your
  own benefit"*; others hear an intensifier, *just go*. Nothing rides on the choice, but the
  doubling of the two-letter root is audible in Hebrew and is lost in every English rendering. See
  [[Leave Your Country - The Two Lek-Lekha Commands]].
- **Five blessings against five curses.** *Brk* five times in 12:2–3 answering *ʾrr* five times in
  1–11 (3:14, 3:17, 4:11, 5:29, 9:25) is an observation with a long pedigree and it holds on a
  count of the actual occurrences. Two cautions keep it honest. The five curses are not all of the
  same kind — two fall on the ground, one on a snake, one on a murderer, one on a grandson — and
  Genesis never lines them up itself. And the fifth blessing (*wənibrəkû*) is the disputed one; a
  reflexive reading of it changes what is being counted. It is a real pattern and not a proof.
- **Whether the nations are blessed or bless themselves.** *Wənibrəkû bəkā kōl mišpəḥōt hāʾădāmâ* —
  the verb is **niphal**, which in Hebrew can be passive (*shall be blessed*) or reflexive/middle
  (*shall bless themselves*, i.e. shall use your name as the formula: *may God make you like
  Abraham*, exactly as at 48:20). The same clause recurs in the niphal at 18:18 and 28:14 and in the
  unambiguously reflexive **hithpael** at 22:18 and 26:4, which is the strongest argument for the
  reflexive sense throughout. Against it: the Septuagint renders passively at every occurrence, and
  Acts 3:25 and Galatians 3:8 quote it that way and build on it. What is often missed is that the
  two readings converge in practice — a world that blesses itself by Abram's name is a world for
  which Abram has become the standard of blessedness — but they do not converge in force, and the
  passive is doing much more work in Paul than the Hebrew by itself will bear. See
  [[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]].
- **The promise is not yet a covenant.** Nothing in 12:1–3 is sworn, no animals are cut, no sign is
  given, and the word *bərît* does not appear. It arrives at 15:18 and is given circumcision as its
  sign at 17:10–11. This is worth keeping straight because the promise is unilateral and
  unconditional as stated — the only imperative in it is *go* — and every later formalization adds
  machinery without adding conditions. See [[Covenant]].
- **"At that time, Canaanites were in the land."** The clause is a narrator's aside, and it is doing
  two things at once. Inside the story it sets the terms: the land promised at 12:7 is somebody's
  home, and Genesis will keep saying so (13:7; 15:16; 23:4). Outside it, the phrase *at that time*
  implies a writer for whom that time is over — which is why **Ibn Ezra** attached his famous
  cryptic note to this verse (*"and the one who understands will keep silent"*), and why Spinoza
  quoted him five centuries later as an early witness against Mosaic authorship. The traditional
  reply is that the note distinguishes the Canaanite occupation from a still earlier one, or is a
  Mosaic anticipation of the conquest. The wiki records the verse's plain difficulty and adopts no
  side of it.
- **The oak of Moreh, and where Abram builds.** *ʾĒlôn môreh* is *the oak (or terebinth) of the
  teacher* — *môreh* from *yārâ*, to instruct or to give an oracle, the same root as *tôrâ*. A named
  sacred tree at the gate of a Canaanite city is a divination site, and Abram's first altar in the
  land goes up beside one. Genesis registers no discomfort about the location. The site keeps its
  charge: Jacob buries the household's foreign gods *"under the oak which was by Shechem"* (35:4),
  Joshua sets his witness-stone *"under the oak that was by the sanctuary of Yahweh"* there (24:26),
  and Deuteronomy 11:30 places Gerizim and Ebal *"beside the oaks of Moreh"*. See
  [[The Altar at Shechem and the Covenant at Shechem]].
- **Two altars, no sacrifice, and one proclamation.** At Shechem Abram builds *"to Yahweh, who had
  appeared to him"* — the altar answers a theophany. At Bethel he builds and *"called on Yahweh's
  name"* — the altar accompanies a public naming. Neither verse mentions a victim, a burning, or a
  smell, which is a consistent feature of the patriarchal altars (12:7, 12:8, 13:18, 26:25, 33:20)
  and a sharp contrast with Noah at 8:20–21. Abram's altars mark territory and declare a name; they
  are, in effect, claims staked in a land he does not own. He builds none in Egypt.
- **The route is the land's spine, and it is a survey.** Shechem sits in the pass between Gerizim
  and Ebal in the central highlands; Bethel and Ai are twenty miles south on the same ridge road;
  the Negev is below that again. Abram enters at the middle, goes to the top of the southern
  highlands, and then south to the edge of habitable country — a traverse of the promised land's
  length using the route every north–south traveller used. Genesis 13:17 will make the walking
  explicit and turn it into a command: *"Arise, walk through the land in its length and in its
  breadth; for I will give it to you."*
- **Seventy-five, and the arithmetic that will not close.** 12:4 gives Abram's age at departure, and
  it is the number that collides with Acts 7:4 — see the chronology in
  [[Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call]]. Worth adding here: 12:4 is also the first age
  Genesis attaches to an event in a life rather than to a fathering or a death. The register's
  formula has been left behind along with the register.
- **He does not answer.** There is no reply to the call anywhere in the pericope — no *here I am*
  (contrast 22:1, 22:11, 31:11, 46:2), no objection (contrast Moses at Exodus 3–4, Gideon at Judges
  6, Jeremiah at Jeremiah 1), no question. Genesis gives the command four verses and the compliance
  half of one: *"So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him."* The silence is what Hebrews 11:8 and
  Romans 4 are both reading, and it is the more striking because his first actual words in Scripture,
  seven verses later, are *"Please say that you are my sister."* The man the New Testament makes the
  pattern of faith is introduced obeying without speaking and then speaking to arrange a lie.
