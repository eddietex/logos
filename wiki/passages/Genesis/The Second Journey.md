# The Second Journey

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 43:1–34

## Summary

**[[Jacob]] ended the last chapter by saying no. This one opens with the grain running out, and the
famine does what ten sons could not.**

Genesis 42 closed on a refusal — *"My son shall not go down with you"* (42:38) — and nothing in
Jacob changes it. **What changes is the food.** *"When they had eaten up the grain which they had
brought out of Egypt, their father said to them, 'Go again, buy us a little more food'"* (43:2), and
the word *"again"* is the whole hinge: **the second journey happens because the first one's grain is
gone, not because anybody was persuaded.**

Then [[Judah]] speaks, and **the chapter divides into the half Jacob controls and the half he does
not.** He wins the argument by offering himself — *"I'll be collateral for him"* (43:9) — where
[[Reuben]] had offered two grandsons and been ignored. **From verse 15 Jacob is in Canaan and the
story is in Egypt**, and everything the chapter does after that happens in a house he will never
enter.

**The chapter's deepest move is a prayer answered in its own vocabulary, in a room where nobody who
prayed it can see.** Jacob sends them off with *"May God Almighty give you **mercy** before the
man"* (43:14) — ***raḥămîm***. Sixteen verses later, *"Joseph hurried, for **his heart yearned** over
his brother"* (43:30) — ***nikmərû raḥămāyw***, *his compassions grew hot*, the same root. **Genesis
does not connect them. It simply lets an old man in Hebron ask for a thing and an Egyptian governor
step out of the room to weep it.** See [[Providence]].

### Judah replaces Reuben, and the replacement is one Hebrew verb

**Two brothers make Jacob the same offer eleven verses apart, and the difference between them is the
rest of the book.**

| | [[Reuben]], 42:37 | [[Judah]], 43:9 |
|---|---|---|
| **what he pledges** | *"Kill my two sons, if I don't bring him to you"* | *"I'll be **collateral** for him"* — ***ʿārab*** |
| **who pays** | Jacob's grandsons | Judah |
| **when** | if Benjamin is lost | *"let me bear the blame **forever**"* |
| **Jacob's answer** | he does not reply at all | *"If it must be so, then do this"* (43:11) |

***ʿĀrab*** is a commercial verb — **to stand as guarantor for another man's debt**, the word the
wisdom literature spends four separate proverbs warning against (Proverbs 6:1; 11:15; 17:18; 20:16).
**Judah is not promising to try. He is putting himself on the note.** And where Reuben reached for
the Noahic reckoning at 42:22 — *"his blood is **required**"*, ***nidrāš*** — **Judah says
*"from my hand will you require him"* with a plainer verb, *bāqaš*: not blood cried up from the
ground, but a debt sought from the man who signed for it.** See
[[I Will Be Surety for Him - Judah's Pledge and the Guarantor in Scripture]].

**This is the pledge the whole cycle has been waiting for, and Genesis will make Judah keep it.** At
44:32–33 he quotes this verse back, word for word, and offers to serve the sentence himself —
*"please let your servant stay instead of the boy"* — **which is the moment the man who proposed
selling one favoured brother (37:26–27) refuses to abandon another.** [[Testing]] has an answer, and
it is spoken here on credit.

### Jacob's present is the caravan's cargo, coming back

> **11** Their father, Israel, said to them, "If it must be so, then do this. Take from the choice
> fruits of the land in your bags, and carry down a present for the man, a little balm, a little
> honey, spices and myrrh, nuts, and almonds;

**Set that beside the sentence that carried Joseph out of Canaan:**

> **25** They sat down to eat bread, and they lifted up their eyes and looked, and saw a caravan of
> Ishmaelites was coming from Gilead, with their camels bearing **spices and balm and myrrh**, going
> to carry it down to Egypt. (37:25)

**Balm, spices, myrrh — three of the six items, in a list of three, in the only other place Genesis
itemises a cargo.** The goods that were going down to Egypt on the camels Joseph was sold to are the
goods his father now sends down to Egypt to buy his life. **Nobody in the chapter knows this.** Jacob
is choosing luxuries for a stranger; the brothers ate bread beside those camels and said nothing.
**Genesis lays the two lists side by side twenty-one verses apart in the scroll and does not comment.**
See [[Balm, Spices, and Myrrh - The Caravan's Cargo and Jacob's Present]].

**And the present is a *minḥâ*** (43:11, 15, 25, 26) — **the same noun as the two hundred goats and
the milk camels Jacob sent ahead to the Jabbok** (32:13). *"I will appease him with the present that
goes before me"* (32:20). **The old man's method has not changed in twenty years: when you must face
a brother who has cause to kill you, send livestock ahead and stay the night behind.** It worked on
[[Esau]]; he does not know he is doing it to [[Joseph]]. See
[[Two Presents to a Feared Brother - Jacob's Minchah to Esau and to Egypt]].

### The dream finishes, and the text still will not say so

**At 42:6 ten brothers bowed and the first dream was short one sheaf.** Here they bow twice more —
*"bowed themselves down to the earth before him"* (43:26), *"They bowed down humbly"* (43:28) —
**and Benjamin is standing among them.** Eleven.

**Genesis marks it with nothing.** At 42:9 it at least said *"Joseph remembered the dreams"*; **here
there is not even that.** The completion of the image the whole cycle turns on is reported as
household manners. See [[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]] and
[[Prophecy and Vision]].

*(The second dream's sun and moon — 37:9 — are not here and cannot be: Jacob is in Canaan and
[[Rachel]] is dead since 35:19. Whatever 37:10 meant by them, the chapter that fulfils the sheaves
leaves them standing.)*

### The only person who says a true thing about God is a servant with no name

> **23** He said, "Peace be to you. Don't be afraid. Your God, and the God of your father, has given
> you treasure in your sacks. I received your money." He brought Simeon out to them.

**This is the theological centre of the chapter and it is spoken by staff.** The brothers have come
to the door with a rehearsed speech about the money (43:19–22), braced to be seized; **the steward
tells them their God did it and that the accounts are settled anyway.**

**Both halves are true and he cannot know either.** Joseph ordered the money returned (42:25) and
the steward says *"I received your money"* — **which is either a lie told kindly or a book that has
been squared out of his master's own funds.** And *"your God, and the God of your father"* is, from
an Egyptian, **the most accurate sentence anybody speaks in Genesis 43** — the last three chapters
have put God only inside characters' quotation marks (41:16, 25; 42:18, 28), and the narrator has
said nothing about him since 39:23. **He still says nothing here. He lets a butler say it.** See
[[God]] and [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

### 43:1–2 — four words of narration do what a chapter of argument could not

> **1** The famine was severe in the land. **2** When they had eaten up the grain which they had
> brought out of Egypt, their father said to them, "Go again, buy us a little more food."

**Jacob's first words in the chapter are an instruction he had sworn he would never give**, and he
gives it without reference to the oath. *"Buy us **a little** more food"* — **he is still negotiating
with the situation**, trying to make the errand small enough that it might not require Benjamin.
[[Famine and Plenty]] is the chapter's engine and it is stated in one clause: *"The famine was severe
in the land."*

**The severity is Joseph's own forecast running on schedule** (41:30–31, 54–57). **The man who
predicted this famine is being fed by it.**

### 43:3–10 — the argument, and the question that cannot be answered

> **3** Judah spoke to him, saying, "The man solemnly warned us, saying, 'You shall not see my face,
> unless your brother is with you.' **4** If you'll send our brother with us, we'll go down and buy
> you food, **5** but if you'll not send him, we'll not go down, for the man said to us, 'You shall
> not see my face, unless your brother is with you.'"

**Judah states the condition twice in three verses and does not argue it.** *"You shall not see my
face"* is not in Joseph's recorded terms at 42:15–20 — **which were about being tested and not dying**
— and it is the phrase of a royal audience, of access withdrawn. **Either Judah is compressing, or
Joseph said more than chapter 42 reported.** Genesis has already shown these men editing a report to
their father (42:29–34, which drops the three days in custody and invents a trading concession); the
reader is not told which is happening here.

**What Judah does not do is plead.** Verses 4 and 5 are a conditional, twice, with no adjective in
them: *if you send him we go, if you don't we don't.* **He has understood that the famine is
arguing for him.**

> **6** Israel said, "Why did you treat me so badly, telling the man that you had another brother?"
> **7** They said, "The man asked directly concerning ourselves, and concerning our relatives,
> saying, 'Is your father still alive? Have you another brother?' We just answered his questions. Is
> there any way we could know that he would say, 'Bring your brother down?'"

**Verse 6 is the last thing Jacob has left and it is a grievance about the past.** *"Why did you
treat me so badly"* — ***hărēʿōtem***, *why did you do evil to me* — **the same complaint, in the
same shape, that he will not make about the thing they actually did to him.**

**The brothers' answer contains an accidental knife.** *"Is your father still alive? Have you another
brother?"* — **they are quoting the interrogation to a man who does not know that the interrogator
was asking after his own father and his own brother.** *"We just answered his questions"* is
literally *we told according to the mouth of these words*; **they are saying they had no choice, and
they are right, and the reason is that the questions were not about espionage at all.**

***Šāʾôl šāʾal*** — *"asked directly"*, the infinitive doubled — **matches *hāʿēd hēʿid*, *"solemnly
warned"*, in verse 3.** The chapter opens with two intensified verbs describing the same off-stage
conversation, both of them Judah's and the brothers' account of a scene the reader has already
watched. See [[Language and Speech]].

> **8** Judah said to Israel, his father, "Send the boy with me, and we'll get up and go, so that we
> may live, and not die, both we, and you, and also our little ones. **9** I'll be collateral for
> him. From my hand will you require him. If I don't bring him to you, and set him before you, then
> let me bear the blame forever, **10** for if we hadn't delayed, surely we would have returned a
> second time by now."

**Verse 8 answers Jacob's arithmetic with arithmetic.** Jacob has been counting the sons he has lost;
Judah counts the people in the tents. *"So that we may live, and not die, **both we, and you, and
also our little ones**"* — **three generations against one boy** — and the clause *"so that we may
live, and not die"* is Jacob's own from 42:2, handed back to him. **The father's argument for the
first journey is the son's argument for the second.**

***"Send the boy"*** — ***hannaʿar***. **Benjamin is not a boy.** He is the father of ten sons at
46:21 and, by the cycle's own arithmetic, somewhere past thirty. **Everyone in the story calls him a
child** — Judah here and at 44:22–34 (*naʿar* fourteen times), Jacob at 42:38, Joseph at 43:29
(*"my son"*) and 44:20 (*"a little one"*). **The household has not updated its picture of him since
Rachel died bearing him**, and the whole plot depends on the family agreeing not to.

**Verse 9 is the chapter's load-bearing sentence.** *"I'll be collateral for him"* — ***ʾānōkî
ʾeʿerbennû***, *I myself will stand surety for him*, **the pronoun included when the verb did not
need it.** *"Let me bear the blame forever"* — ***wəḥāṭāʾtî ləkā kol-hayyāmîm***, literally
*I shall have sinned against you all the days*: **not a penalty he will pay but a status he will
occupy.** Reuben offered a transaction; **Judah offers to become, permanently, the man who did this.**

**Verse 10 is a rebuke, and it is the only sharp thing Judah says.** *"If we hadn't delayed, surely
we would have returned a second time by now"* — **the delay was Jacob's, and Judah tells him it cost
a round trip.** [[Simeon]] has been in an Egyptian prison the whole time this argument has taken.

### 43:11–14 — a father packs, and prays, and gives up

> **11** Their father, Israel, said to them, "If it must be so, then do this. Take from the choice
> fruits of the land in your bags, and carry down a present for the man, a little balm, a little
> honey, spices and myrrh, nuts, and almonds; **12** and take double money in your hand, and take
> back the money that was returned in the mouth of your sacks. Perhaps it was an oversight.
> **13** Take your brother also, get up, and return to the man.

***"If it must be so, then do this"*** — **five words of surrender followed by seventeen of
logistics.** Jacob does not say yes. **He starts packing**, which is how this book has always shown
him thinking: the goats and camels at 32:13–21, the flocks and the rods at 30:37–42. **Faced with a
thing he cannot control, he arranges goods.**

**The choice fruits go down in a famine.** *"The choice fruits of the land"* — ***zimrat hāʾāreṣ*** —
**from a land that has no food.** Balm and honey and nuts are not provisions; they are the last good
things in the house, sent to a man who has all the grain in the world. See
[[Wealth and Possessions]].

*"Perhaps it was an **oversight**"* — ***mišgeh***, a noun that occurs **nowhere else in the Hebrew
Bible**. **Jacob has to invent a word for the possibility that the money in the sacks was an
accident**, because the alternative — that someone in Egypt is doing something to his family on
purpose — is the thing he cannot look at. **He is right that it was done on purpose and wrong about
everything else.**

> **14** May God Almighty give you mercy before the man, that he may release to you your other
> brother and Benjamin. If I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved."

**This is Jacob's only prayer in the Joseph cycle and it is four clauses long.**

***"God Almighty"*** — ***ʾĒl Šadday***, **the covenant name, and Jacob's history with it is exact.**
It was spoken over him by [[Isaac]] the last time he was sent away from home — *"May God Almighty
bless you"* (28:3), as Isaac put him on the road to Paddan Aram — and God used it of himself to
Jacob at Bethel, *"I am God Almighty"* (35:11). **He will use it once more, at 48:3, telling Joseph
where he got it.** Here he says it over sons he is putting on a road. See
[[God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus]] and [[The Names of God]].

***"Mercy"*** — ***raḥămîm***, **from *reḥem*, womb.** It is the chapter's key word and Jacob is the
first to say it. **He asks God to put compassion into a stranger**, having no idea he is asking for
something the stranger has been fighting down since 42:24.

***"If I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved"*** — ***kaʾăšer šākōltî šākāltî***, **the same
verb twice, perfect and perfect, with no connective.** It is not hope and it is not quite despair;
**it is a man agreeing in advance to the worst outcome so that it cannot be done to him without his
consent.** The construction has one close cousin in Scripture: **Esther's *"if I perish, I perish"*
(Esther 4:16)** — also said by someone sending a beloved life into a foreign court, **also the last
word before the doors open.** See [[If I Am Bereaved, I Am Bereaved - Jacob and Esther]].

**And *šākōl*, *bereave*, is the word he used at 42:36** — *"You have bereaved me of my children"* —
**accusing his sons.** Now he uses it of himself, with no object to blame. **That is the whole
movement of the chapter's first half in one repeated verb.**

### 43:15–18 — the terror of an invitation

> **15** The men took that present, and they took double money in their hand, and Benjamin; and got
> up, went down to Egypt, and stood before Joseph. **16** When Joseph saw Benjamin with them, he said
> to the steward of his house, "Bring the men into the house, and butcher an animal, and prepare; for
> the men will dine with me at noon." **17** The man did as Joseph commanded, and the man brought the
> men to Joseph's house.

**Verse 15 puts Benjamin third in a list, after the present and the money** — *"the present … the
money … and Benjamin"* — **the order of a packing list**, and the whole weight of two chapters is in
the last noun.

**Joseph says nothing to his brothers in this verse or the next.** *"When Joseph saw Benjamin with
them"* — **and he turns to the servant.** The chapter's first response to the completed family is an
instruction about lunch. ***Ṭĕbōaḥ ṭebaḥ***, *"butcher an animal"*, is **a third doubled infinitive
in fifteen verses** and the only one that is an order rather than a report. *"At **noon**"* — a
detail Genesis does not usually give, **and it fixes the feast at the hour when nothing is hidden.**

> **18** The men were afraid, because they were brought to Joseph's house; and they said, "Because of
> the money that was returned in our sacks the first time, we're brought in; that he may seek
> occasion against us, attack us, and seize us as slaves, along with our donkeys."

**This is the chapter's cruellest irony and it is entirely the brothers' own doing.** **They are
being taken to dinner and they think they are being arrested.** Every kindness in the chapter
arrives at them as a threat — the returned money in 42:28, the invitation here, the seating in verse
33 — **because men carrying an unconfessed crime read hospitality as a trap.** See
[[Guilt and Conscience]].

***"Seize us as slaves"*** — ***laʿăbādîm***. **They fear being made slaves by the brother they made
a slave**, in a chapter where they call themselves *"your servants"* — the same word — **at 43:28.**
Genesis does not point at it. See [[Slavery and Servitude]].

**And the donkeys.** *"Along with our donkeys"* is the detail that makes the fear real rather than
literary: **men calculating what they will actually lose.**

### 43:19–25 — the speech at the door, and the answer nobody expected

> **19** They came near to the steward of Joseph's house, and they spoke to him at the door of the
> house, **20** and said, "Oh, my lord, we indeed came down the first time to buy food. **21** When we
> came to the lodging place, we opened our sacks, and behold, each man's money was in the mouth of his
> sack, our money in full weight. We have brought it back in our hand. **22** We have brought down
> other money in our hand to buy food. We don't know who put our money in our sacks."

**They get the confession in before the door closes** — *"at the door of the house"*, **the one place
they can still be men making a declaration rather than men inside a building.** The speech is
carefully built: the fact, the full weight, the restitution, the second payment, and then the
disclaimer.

**And it is not quite what happened.** At 42:27 **one man** opened **one sack** at the lodging place;
the rest was discovered at home in front of Jacob (42:35). **Here it is *"we opened our sacks"* and
*"each man's money."*** They have merged two scenes into one, **and the merge conveniently removes
the part where they carried the silver all the way home before returning it.** Genesis has now shown
this family editing an account three times in two chapters. See [[Deception]].

> **23** He said, "Peace be to you. Don't be afraid. Your God, and the God of your father, has given
> you treasure in your sacks. I received your money." He brought Simeon out to them.

***"Peace be to you. Don't be afraid."*** — ***šālôm lākem, ʾal-tîrāʾû.*** **The first words of
comfort spoken to these men in two chapters, and they come from a domestic servant.**

***"Treasure"*** — ***maṭmôn***, *hidden treasure*, **the word for buried silver** (Proverbs 2:4; Job
3:21). **He calls the thing they are terrified of a gift**, and attributes it to a God he does not
worship, **and then produces the brother they left in prison as though fetching a coat.** The
release of [[Simeon]] gets four words and no reunion. **Nobody in the chapter says anything to him;
he has no recorded speech in Genesis at all.**

> **24** The man brought the men into Joseph's house, and gave them water, and they washed their feet.
> He gave their donkeys fodder. **25** They prepared the present for Joseph's coming at noon, for they
> heard that they should eat bread there.

**Water, feet, fodder — this is the hospitality formula of Genesis 18:4 and 24:32**, performed for
Hebrews inside an Egyptian house. **Abraham did it for three strangers at Mamre; Laban's household
did it for Abraham's servant.** Here **the host is the brother they sold**, and the service is
rendered by staff to men who think they are about to be seized. See [[Hospitality]].

*"For they heard that they should **eat bread** there"* — ***leʾĕkol lāḥem***. **The last time this
phrase ran in the Joseph story, it was 37:25**: *"They sat down to eat bread"*, **beside the pit,
while the caravan came over the hill.** The words are ordinary. **Genesis has used them twice.**

### 43:26–31 — eleven bow, and a man leaves the room

> **26** When Joseph came home, they brought him the present which was in their hand into the house,
> and bowed themselves down to the earth before him. **27** He asked them of their welfare, and said,
> "Is your father well, the old man of whom you spoke? Is he yet alive?" **28** They said, "Your
> servant, our father, is well. He is still alive." They bowed down humbly.

**The sheaves are all present and the narrator will not say it.** See
[[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]].

***"He asked them of their welfare"*** — ***wayyišʾal lāhem ləšālôm***, **the same noun the steward
used at the door.** *Šālôm* is asked and *šālôm* is answered, twice, **in a house where nothing is at
peace.**

***"Is your father well, the old man of whom you spoke? Is he yet alive?"*** — **Joseph asks the only
question he has wanted to ask for twenty-two years, and he asks it in the third person, through the
protocol of a great man enquiring after a petitioner's household.** *"The old man"* — ***hazzāqēn*** —
**is how a foreign official refers to somebody else's father.** He asked after him at 42:13 too, and
got the same answer; **he asks again because once was not enough.**

> **29** He lifted up his eyes, and saw Benjamin, his brother, his mother's son, and said, "Is this
> your youngest brother, of whom you spoke to me?" He said, "God be gracious to you, my son."

***"His brother, his mother's son"*** — **the narrator's phrase, not Joseph's**, and it does in four
words what the chapter has been circling: **of eleven men in the room, one is Rachel's.** The
narrator used the same device at 42:4 — *"Benjamin, **Joseph's brother**"* — **naming the relation
that nobody on the page is allowed to name.**

***"God be gracious to you, my son"*** — ***ʾĕlōhîm yoḥnəkā bənî***, from ***ḥānan***. **Two mercy
words in fifteen verses**: Jacob prayed *raḥămîm* over the journey, Joseph says *ḥānan* over the boy.
**The pair *raḥûm wəḥannûn* is how God will name himself at Exodus 34:6**; here the halves are split
between a father in Canaan and a son in Egypt who cannot be in the same sentence. See
[[Favor and Grace]] and [[Compassion and Mercy]].

**And *"my son"* is the closest the chapter comes to the disclosure.** It is what an official says to
a young foreigner. **It is also what a man says who has just been handed the only other child of his
mother** — and the reason the next verse happens.

> **30** Joseph hurried, for his heart yearned over his brother; and he sought a place to weep. He
> entered into his room, and wept there. **31** He washed his face, and came out. He controlled
> himself, and said, "Serve the meal."

***"His heart yearned"*** — ***nikmərû raḥămāyw***, **literally *his compassions grew hot*.** The
idiom is rare and physical; **its only other use of a human being in Scripture is 1 Kings 3:26, of
the true mother before Solomon, when she would rather lose the child than see it cut.** **Both are
about a person who must hide what they feel in order to save the one they feel it for.**

**And it is Jacob's word.** *Raḥămîm* at 43:14, *raḥămāyw* at 43:30 — **the petition and the thing
itself, sixteen verses apart, with a border between them.** [[Providence]] in this book is usually
something a character declares afterwards (45:5–8; 50:20); **here it is a matched pair of nouns and
no narrator at all.**

**The weeping is the second of Joseph's seven** (42:24; 43:30; 45:2, 14–15; 46:29; 50:1, 17) **and it
is one room less hidden than the first.** At 42:24 he only *"turned himself away"*; here he has to
leave the building's public part and shut a door. **At 45:2 there will be no door.** See
[[Fell on His Neck and Wept - The Running Embrace in Genesis and Luke]].

***"He controlled himself"*** — ***wayyitʾappaq***, **a rare hitpael meaning to force oneself, to hold
in.** Genesis uses it exactly twice, **both of Joseph**: here, successfully, **and at 45:1 —
*"Joseph couldn't control himself"*** — where the same verb fails and the story ends. **The word is
the seam between the two halves of the cycle.**

***"Serve the meal"*** — ***śîmû lāḥem***, *set out bread*. **Three words, and the man who said them
has just washed his face.**

### 43:32–34 — three tables and a fivefold portion

> **32** They served him by himself, and them by themselves, and the Egyptians, that ate with him, by
> themselves, because the Egyptians don't eat bread with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to
> the Egyptians.

**The feast Joseph ordered is a feast he cannot sit at.** **Three tables in one room** — the
governor alone, the Egyptians apart, the Hebrews apart — **and the man at the solitary table belongs
to both of the other two and can admit to neither.** *"They served him by himself"* is the loneliest
stage-direction in Genesis.

***"Abomination"*** — ***tôʿēbâ***, **the word's first appearance in the Bible**, and it is used of
Egyptian table custom rather than of anything God forbids. **It will run: *"every shepherd is an
abomination to the Egyptians"* (46:34), and Moses's *"we shall sacrifice the abomination of the
Egyptians before their eyes"* (Exodus 8:26).** **Genesis is quietly building the fact that Israel
cannot be assimilated in Egypt** — the separateness that keeps the family a family through four
hundred years is, at its first mention, **an Egyptian dining prejudice.** See
[[An Abomination to the Egyptians - Separate Tables in Genesis and Exodus]] and
[[Clean and Unclean]].

> **33** They sat before him, the firstborn according to his birthright, and the youngest according
> to his youth, and the men marveled one with another.

**Eleven men are seated in the exact order of their births by a foreigner who has never met them,
and Genesis gives the reaction three words.** ***Wayyitməhû*** — *"they marveled"* — **a verb of
being stupefied.** They are looking at a seating plan that requires knowledge of Leah's, Rachel's,
Bilhah's and Zilpah's childbearing over seven years in Paddan Aram, **and their explanation for it
is the chapter's silence.**

**It is the second time Joseph has told them something without telling them.** **It is also,
mechanically, a warning he gives them and they fail to read** — which is precisely what will happen
again with the cup in the next chapter. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

> **34** He sent portions to them from before him, but Benjamin's portion was five times as much as
> any of theirs. They drank, and were merry with him.

**Joseph ends the chapter by doing to Benjamin what Jacob did to him.** **A father who singles out
one son of Rachel in front of the others is the sentence Genesis 37 opens with** — *"Israel loved
Joseph more than all his children … he made him a coat of many colors"* (37:3) — **and the result was
the pit.** Here the favouritism is served at table, **five times over, in front of ten men who once
solved this exact problem with a caravan.**

**Whether it is a test is the question the chapter leaves open, and the next one answers.** Genesis
does not say Joseph is watching for [[Envy]]. **It says he sent the portions and they drank.** And
44:1–2 will plant a silver cup in Benjamin's sack, **which only works as a test if this chapter was
one too.** See [[Testing]] and [[Envy]].

***Five*** is the Joseph cycle's number: **a fifth of the harvest (41:34), five years of famine still
to come (45:6), five changes of clothing for Benjamin (45:22), five brothers presented to Pharaoh
(47:2).** It is a storyteller's number in this book, and **its densest use is a plate of food.**

***"They drank, and were merry with him"*** — ***wayyištû wayyiškərû***, from ***šākar***, **the verb
of Noah's vineyard** (9:21). **The WEB's *"were merry"* is generous**; the root means to drink to
the point of drunkenness, and **Genesis's only other use of it ended with a man uncovered in his
tent and a curse on his grandson.** The chapter closes on ten men relaxing for the first time since
42:6, **at a table set by the brother they sold, with a cup already chosen for the morning.**

## People

- [[Joseph]] — **speaks eight times and never to the point.** Every line he has is domestic or
  formal: an order to a steward, a question about an old man's health, a blessing on a boy, *"Serve
  the meal."* **He sees [[Benjamin]] and turns away to arrange lunch** (43:16); he asks after his
  father twice in two verses and takes the answer without comment; **he calls Benjamin *"my son"* and
  has to leave the room.** *Nikmərû raḥămāyw* (43:30) is the first description of his interior life
  since 42:24, and *wayyitʾappaq* — the verb that fails at 45:1 — **is what he does with it.** He
  then seats eleven men in birth order, **which is either a slip or a message**, and gives Benjamin
  five portions, **which reproduces his father's mistake at the table of a man testing whether it
  still works**
- [[Judah]] — **the chapter is his, and it is the hinge of his whole arc.** He states Joseph's
  condition twice without embellishment (43:3–5), answers his father's grievance, and then makes the
  offer that moves him: ***ʿārab*** — *"I'll be collateral for him"* (43:9) — **himself, not his
  sons, and *"forever"*, not until the boy is back.** He backs it with Jacob's own argument from 42:2
  (*"so that we may live, and not die"*) **widened to three generations**, and closes with the one
  reproach in the chapter (43:10). **He is the man who proposed the sale at 37:26–27 and the man who
  fathered Perez by [[Tamar]] after she showed him what *hakker-nāʾ* costs (38:25–26)**; this verse
  is where that turn becomes a liability he signs. **He keeps it at 44:32–33.** [[Reuben]] is not
  mentioned in this chapter at all
- [[Jacob]] — **called *Israel* whenever he acts and *their father* whenever he is acted on**, and
  the chapter uses both within single verses (43:6, 43:11). He gives in without saying yes (*"If it
  must be so, then do this"*), **packs a *minḥâ* exactly as he did for [[Esau]] at 32:13–21**, sends
  double money, invents a word — ***mišgeh***, *oversight*, found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible —
  **for the possibility that the silver was an accident**, and prays the only prayer he prays in the
  cycle: ***ʾĒl Šadday*** and ***raḥămîm*** (43:14), **the name Isaac blessed him with when he too
  was sent from home** (28:3). *"If I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved"* is his last word in
  the chapter. **He never learns that the prayer was answered, or that the balm and myrrh he chose
  are the cargo his son travelled with**
- [[Benjamin]] — **present at last, and he does not speak.** Named six times, carried in a list after
  the present and the money (43:15), **seen** (43:29), **blessed** (*"God be gracious to you, my
  son"*), **wept over from another room**, seated last by right of youth, and served five times what
  anyone else gets. **Every other character calls him *the boy* — *hannaʿar* — though 46:21 gives him
  ten sons**; the family's picture of him froze when [[Rachel]] died bearing him. **He completes the
  eleven sheaves of 37:7 by standing in a room**, and he is the only man at the table who has done
  nothing
- **Joseph's steward** — **new page**, [[Joseph's Steward]]. **The only unnamed servant in the cycle
  with recorded speech, and what he says is the truest sentence in the chapter**: *"Peace be to you.
  Don't be afraid. Your God, and the God of your father, has given you treasure in your sacks. I
  received your money"* (43:23). **He brings [[Simeon]] out, washes feet, feeds donkeys, and sets
  three tables** — and at 44:1–12 he will plant the cup and run the brothers down. **The line on
  unnamed functionaries** (the interpreter of 42:23, the keeper of the prison, Egypt's magicians)
  **is that they get no page; speech and a two-chapter role put him the other side of it, with
  [[Abraham's Servant]]**
- [[Simeon]] — **released in four words** (43:23) **after roughly a year in an Egyptian prison, and
  nobody in the text greets him.** He says nothing, is asked nothing, and is not mentioned again in
  the chapter — **he is presumably one of the eleven who bow at 43:26 and one of the men seated in
  birth order at 43:33, second from the top.** His silence is total: **he has no recorded speech
  anywhere in Genesis**, including at Shechem (34:25–31)
- [[Reuben]] — **absent, and the absence is the point.** His surety was refused at 42:37 and the
  chapter does not so much as name him; **Judah's pledge occupies the space his would have.** He is
  seated first at 43:33, *"the firstborn according to his birthright"* — **a birthright the narrative
  has already transferred** (and which 49:3–4 will formally strip)
- [[Levi]], [[Dan (person)]], [[Naphtali]], [[Gad]], [[Asher]], [[Issachar]], [[Zebulun]] — **the
  plural again**, speaking once as a group at 43:7 and once at 43:19–22, **and then seated as
  individuals by a man who knows exactly what order they were born in.** Verse 33's *"the men
  marveled one with another"* is the whole of their inner life in this chapter
- [[God]] — **named three times, all inside quotation marks, and one of the speakers is Egyptian.**
  Jacob's *ʾĒl Šadday* (43:14), **the steward's *"your God, and the God of your father"* (43:23)**,
  and Joseph's *"God be gracious to you"* (43:29). **The narrator still says nothing** — the run of
  narratorial silence about God extends from 39:23 to 46:2 — **and the chapter's one act of
  providence is reported as an involuntary physical sensation** (43:30)
- [[Rachel]] — **dead since 35:19 and the reason the chapter exists.** The narrator invokes her
  without naming her: *"Benjamin, his brother, **his mother's son**"* (43:29). **Both of her children
  are in the room and only one of them knows it**
- [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]] — **not mentioned.** Joseph swore by his life twice in the previous
  chapter (42:15, 16) and does not here; **the second journey is transacted entirely inside a private
  house**
- **The Egyptians of Joseph's household** — **no page**, per the standing line on unnamed groups.
  They are the second table (43:32), **and they are the reason there are three tables** — their
  custom, not Joseph's, is what the narrator explains

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — **the second descent, and the first one that is a homecoming for anybody.** The verb is
  *yārad* again (43:15) and the destination this time is not a market but **a house**: *"Bring the men
  into the house"* (43:16, 17, 18, 24, 26). **Genesis has moved the family from Egypt's granaries to
  Egypt's dining room in one chapter** — and the chapter's closing note is that the two peoples
  cannot share a table (43:32), **which is the first statement of the problem that keeps Israel
  distinct for four centuries**
- [[Canaan]] — **named nowhere in the chapter, and its absence is structural.** It is where the food
  ran out, where Jacob stays, and where the reader's attention is not; **after 43:14 the narrative
  does not go back until 45:25.** The *"choice fruits of the land"* are the last thing it produces in
  Genesis
- **Joseph's house** — **no page** (a building, not a settlement). It is named seven times in
  nineteen verses, **the most repeated noun in the chapter**, and it is where the brothers expect to
  be enslaved and are instead given water for their feet

## Themes

- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — **the chapter is the book's turning point for it.** Judah's ***ʿārab***
  at 43:9 is **the first voluntary suretyship in Scripture**: a man interposing himself, by name and
  in advance, between a debt and the one who would otherwise pay it. **Set against [[Reuben]]'s
  offer of two grandsons (42:37), which Jacob does not dignify with a reply**, it establishes the
  difference between collateral you own and collateral you are
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — **new page, and this chapter is its foundation text.** ***Raḥămîm*** at
  43:14 (asked for) and ***nikmərû raḥămāyw*** at 43:30 (given), **with *ḥānan* at 43:29 in between**
  — the two roots that Exodus 34:6 will bind together as God's own name, **here split between a
  father's prayer and a son's body.** Genesis's densest cluster of mercy vocabulary is in a chapter
  where nobody is forgiven
- [[Providence]] — **the chapter's method is to answer a prayer and not mention that it has.** Jacob
  asks for *raḥămîm* before the man; the man's *raḥămîm* are kindled sixteen verses later and he
  leaves the room to hide it. **No narrator, no character, no comment** — the opposite of 45:5–8 and
  50:20, where the doctrine is stated. **Here it is only a repeated noun**
- [[Famine and Plenty]] — **the chapter's first sentence and its actual protagonist.** *"The famine
  was severe in the land"* (43:1) accomplishes in four words what [[Judah]] and [[Reuben]] together
  could not: it reverses Jacob's *"my son shall not go down"* (42:38). **Then the chapter ends in a
  banquet**, with the men who came to beg for food drinking at the table of the man who stored it
- [[Guilt and Conscience]] — **every good thing in this chapter frightens them.** They are brought to
  dinner and conclude they are being arrested for theft (43:18); **they rehearse a defence at the
  door before anyone has accused them** (43:19–22); they hear *"don't be afraid"* from a servant.
  **The confession of 42:21 has not made them easier — it has made them legible to themselves**
- [[Hospitality]] — **the formula of [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] (18:4–8) run
  in Egypt**: water, washed feet, fodder for the animals, a slaughtered beast, bread at noon. **And
  it is broken at the moment it succeeds** — three tables, because *"the Egyptians don't eat bread
  with the Hebrews"* (43:32). **The chapter offers the fullest hospitality in Genesis and cannot seat
  the guests with the host**
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — ***tôʿēbâ*, the Bible's first *abomination*, and it belongs to the
  Egyptians.** Not a divine prohibition but a national distaste (43:32; cf. 46:34), **and Genesis
  records it without judgment.** The vocabulary that will govern Israel's own table enters Scripture
  describing somebody else's
- [[Testing]] — **the chapter is a test that is never called one.** Joseph seats them in birth order
  and watches, gives Benjamin five portions and watches, **and Genesis reports neither as a trial.**
  The proof that they were is 44:1–2: **the cup only works on men who have already been shown a
  favoured brother and not acted**
- [[Envy]] — **the pit's cause, reconstructed at a dinner table.** *"Benjamin's portion was five times
  as much as any of theirs"* (43:34) is 37:3–4 restaged with the same ingredients — **one son of
  Rachel, conspicuously preferred, in front of the other ten** — and this time nothing happens. **The
  silence of the ten is the chapter's most important non-event**
- [[Prayer]] — **Jacob's only prayer in the Joseph cycle** (43:14), and **the only prayer in Genesis
  that asks God to change a third party's feelings.** Four clauses: the name, the petition, the
  purpose, and the pre-emptive surrender. **Compare his prayer at the Jabbok** (32:9–12), **which
  argued from covenant promises**; this one argues from nothing
- [[The Names of God]] — ***ʾĒl Šadday*** (43:14), **spoken by the man God spoke it to** (35:11), **in
  the same act of sending-away in which his own father spoke it over him** (28:3). Jacob uses the
  covenant name to commission a grocery run, **which is exactly the measure of what he thinks is at
  stake**
- [[Favor and Grace]] — ***ḥānan*** at 43:29, **Joseph's blessing on the brother he cannot claim**,
  and the counterpart to the *raḥămîm* his father asked for. **The chapter puts the two halves of
  Exodus 34:6 in two mouths that cannot hear each other**
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — **the sheaf dream of 37:7 is completed at 43:26 and the text says
  nothing.** Eleven brothers bow; at 42:6 there were ten and the narrator at least noted that Joseph
  remembered. **The fulfilment of the cycle's governing image is narrated as etiquette**
- [[Brotherhood]] — ***"our brother"*, *"your brother"*, *"his brother"* eleven times in thirty-four
  verses.** Judah says *"send the **boy** with me"* and *"I'll be collateral for **him**"*; Joseph
  says *"your youngest **brother**"* and the narrator says *"**his brother**, his mother's son."*
  **The chapter is a negotiation over the custody of one brother conducted by another brother in
  disguise**
- [[Deception]] — **thinner here than in 42, and running both ways.** Joseph maintains the
  impersonation without a single false statement, **which is a change of method**; the brothers merge
  two separate discoveries of the money into one account (43:21 against 42:27, 35); **and the steward
  says *"I received your money"* about money he returned**
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — **double money in the hand and the last luxuries of a starving house in
  the bags.** *"The choice fruits of the land"* (43:11) in the second year of a famine, **sent to the
  man who owns all the grain.** The chapter's economy is entirely one-directional and everyone in it
  is wrong about the accounts
- [[Language and Speech]] — **three intensified infinitives in fifteen verses** — *hāʿēd hēʿid*
  (43:3), *šāʾôl šāʾal* (43:7), *ṭĕbōaḥ ṭebaḥ* (43:16) — **and no interpreter mentioned.** The *mēlîṣ*
  of 42:23 is not named again, though nothing in the chapter suggests he is gone; **the reader is
  left to assume the wall Joseph built is still standing**
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"When Joseph **saw** Benjamin"* (43:16); *"He **lifted up his eyes**,
  and **saw** Benjamin"* (43:29); **and eleven men who look at a seating plan and cannot read it**
  (43:33). **The chapter's sight is all one-way**
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — *"seize us as slaves"* (43:18), feared by men who sold a brother into
  slavery, **and *"your servant, our father"* (43:28), the word they keep applying to their own
  household.** 44:16 will make it their own verdict: *"we are my lord's slaves"*
- [[Life and Death]] — *"so that we may live, and not die"* (43:8), **Judah quoting Jacob's own words
  from 42:2 back at him**, and *"If I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved"* (43:14). **The
  chapter's stakes are stated by both men in the same exchange and neither is talking about the same
  danger**
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — **the second *yārad* into Egypt in two chapters** (43:15), and the first
  one that ends indoors. *"The choice fruits of the land"* leave Canaan; **the family is being drawn
  in, one journey at a time**, toward 46:6 and four hundred years

## Notes

**On the chronology.** The first journey happens in the famine's early stage; Jacob will tell Pharaoh
at 47:9 that he is a hundred and thirty, and Joseph tells his brothers at 45:6 that **two years of
famine have passed and five remain.** The second journey therefore falls somewhere in year two.
**Simeon has been in prison for however long it took one household's grain to run out** — Genesis
gives no figure and the brothers' delay (43:10) is the only thing said about the interval.

**On *"you shall not see my face"* (43:3, 5).** Joseph's recorded terms at 42:15–20 do not contain
this phrase. **Either Judah is glossing, or chapter 42 compressed the interview.** Genesis has shown
these men editing a report before (42:29–34), so the reader cannot settle it — **but the phrase is
not casual**: *see the face* is court language for audience with a king (Exodus 10:28; 2 Samuel
14:24, 28), **and 43:3 is the first time anyone in the family speaks of Joseph in those terms.**

**On the money.** Three sums are in motion and the chapter never balances them: **the original
purchase price, returned in the sacks (42:25); the same sum carried back down (43:12, 21); and fresh
money for the new purchase (43:12, 22).** Jacob calls the first an *oversight*; the steward calls it
*treasure* from their God and says he was paid; **the brothers are left holding restitution that
nobody will accept.** 44:1 will return their money a second time, on top of the cup.

**On *hannaʿar*.** Everyone calls Benjamin *the boy*, and by the text's own numbers he is not one:
46:21 credits him with ten sons before the family enters Egypt. **The discrepancy is usually read as
the narrative preserving the family's way of speaking rather than his age** — Jacob's youngest, born
at his mother's death, is *the child* permanently. **The reading matters because the whole plot rests
on Jacob's belief that Benjamin cannot be risked.**

**On whether verse 33 is deliberate.** Joseph seats eleven men in birth order and Genesis records only
that they were amazed. **Nothing in the text says he intended them to notice**, and nothing says he
did not. **What the text does say is that they noticed and did not act on it** — the same response
they give to the five portions in the next verse, and the same one they will not be able to give when
the cup is found.

**A page deliberately not created.** The Egyptians who eat at the second table (43:32) get no page,
consistent with the interpreter of 42:23 and the magicians of 41:8: **an unnamed group with no
recorded speech.** [[Joseph's Steward]] crosses that line because he speaks (43:23) and because
44:1–12 gives him a role of his own.

**Links left for later pericopes.** Judah's fulfilment of this pledge (44:32–33), the silver cup
(44:1–2), *"we are my lord's slaves"* (44:16), the disclosure and the failure of *wayyitʾappaq*
(45:1–3), Benjamin's five changes of clothing (45:22) and the separateness of 46:34 are all named
above in prose and marked by reference rather than linked — **their passage pages do not exist yet.**
