# The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 25:1–18

## Summary

**Nobody speaks.**

Eighteen verses close the longest cycle in Genesis — fourteen chapters, from the call at 12:1 to the
grave at 25:10 — and they contain no dialogue at all. God does not speak; Abraham does not speak;
Isaac and Ishmael stand at the same grave and are given no word between them. The previous pericope,
[[A Wife for Isaac]], was two-thirds dialogue with a silent God. **This one removes the human speech
as well**, and what is left is a list of names, a will, two death notices, one sentence of divine
blessing reported rather than quoted, and a map.

**It is also the only pericope in the Abraham cycle that is mostly genealogy.** Sixteen descendants
by [[Keturah]] in four verses, twelve princes of [[Ishmael]] in four more: **twenty-eight names, in a
passage of eighteen verses**, framing the eleven verses in which Abraham makes his will, dies, and is
buried. The cycle that began with one childless man ends by counting his descendants and then
declining to follow any of them but one.

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who is on stage |
|---|---|---|
| **1–4** | Keturah, and sixteen descendants in four verses | nobody acts but Abraham, once: *he took* |
| **5–6** | the will: everything to Isaac, gifts to the rest, and the rest sent east | Abraham |
| **7–8** | a hundred seventy-five years; *gathered to his people* | Abraham |
| **9–10** | the burial at Machpelah, the deed recited a third time | Isaac and Ishmael, together |
| **11** | God blesses Isaac; Isaac settles at Beer Lahai Roi | God, for the first and only time in the pericope |
| **12–18** | *the history of the generations of Ishmael*: twelve princes, a lifespan, a territory | nobody |

### 25:1–4 — Keturah, and a second family nobody expected

> **1** Abraham took another wife, and her name was Keturah.
> **2** She bore him Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah.

**Genesis introduces her in nine words and never mentions her again.** She gets no genealogy, no
country, no father, no scene, and no word of speech — which puts her among the least-described named
wives in the book, and makes the four verses of descendants she produces the whole of her presence in
Scripture outside the parallel register at 1 Chronicles 1:32–33.

**The passage cannot decide what she is.** 25:1 calls her *ʾiššâ*, **wife**, the same word used of
Sarah. Five verses later the narrator writes *"the sons of Abraham's **concubines**"* (25:6) —
*pîlagšîm*, plural, which can only mean her and [[Hagar]] — and 1 Chronicles 1:32 says flatly
*"the sons of Keturah, Abraham's concubine"*. **Genesis states both and reconciles neither**, exactly
as it did with Hagar, who is *"his wife"* at 16:3 and *"the servant"* at 21:10. See
[[Marriage]] and [[Slavery and Servitude]].

**And the arithmetic is a problem the text does not acknowledge.** Sarah dies when Abraham is a
hundred thirty-seven (23:1, with 17:17); the narrative order puts Keturah after that, and six sons,
plus time enough for grandsons and great-grandsons to be named, has to fit into the thirty-eight
years left. **This is the man who fell on his face laughing at the idea that a hundred-year-old could
father a child** (17:17), and whose body Romans 4:19 calls *"as good as dead"*. The standing
proposals:

- **The order is not chronological.** Genealogical notices in Genesis are regularly placed where they
  are structurally useful rather than where they belong in time —
  [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] is filed at 22:20 for the sake of a bride two chapters
  later. Keturah could have been taken
  years before Sarah's death, which would make *"another wife"* a statement of rank, not sequence.
- **The blessing of 17:16 and 21:1–2 is read as a restored vigour** that persists — the traditional
  harmonisation, and the one *Genesis Rabbah* and the medieval commentators generally take.
- **The rabbinic identification of Keturah with Hagar** (*Genesis Rabbah* 61:4, and Rashi), reading
  *qəṭûrâ* from *qəṭōret*, **incense**, and taking her return as a reconciliation. It has no support
  in the text — the concubines of 25:6 are plural — and the wiki records it as reception history.

**Genesis raises none of this**, and the wiki names the tension without choosing. See the note below.

**Sixteen names, and the map is Arabia.** Six sons, seven grandsons, three great-grandsons. The one
that matters most later is **Midian** — the country Moses flees to and marries into (Exodus
2:15–21), the caravan that carries Joseph down to Egypt (37:28, 36), Gideon's enemy (Judges 6–8), and
Balaam's paymasters (Numbers 22–25; 31). **The Midianites are descendants of Abraham**, and no book of
the Bible that fights them ever says so.

**Two of the names are already in the book.** *Sheba* and *Dedan* appear at 10:7 as grandsons of Cush
through Raamah, and *Sheba* again at 10:28 under Joktan. Genesis has now placed the same two peoples
in three separate lines and does not comment; [[The Table of Nations]] already records that
Sheba and Havilah each occur twice inside chapter 10 alone. **Tribal names in this region attach to
territory, not to ancestry**, and a genealogy of peoples will assign one to whichever branch it is
currently tracing. See [[The Nations]].

### 25:5–6 — the will

> **5** Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac,
> **6** but to the sons of Abraham’s concubines, Abraham gave gifts. He sent them away from Isaac his
> son, while he yet lived, eastward, to the east country.

**The servant said this a chapter early.** *"He has given all that he has to him"* (24:36) was
volunteered to Rebekah's family as a marriage settlement, before the reader had been shown it
happening; the wiki filed it under the servant's six edits, as the one piece of information nobody
asked for. **25:5 makes it true**, and in the same words.

**Eight sons, one heir, and the disinheritance is done with gifts rather than a curse.** Abraham has
eight sons in Genesis: Ishmael, Isaac, and Keturah's six. Seven of them are paid off. **The verb is
not *drive out* — it is *give* — and Genesis is careful about the difference**: the removal of
Ishmael at 21:14 was *šālaḥ* with bread and a skin of water and a grief the narrator called *very
evil in his eyes*; this is *šālaḥ* with property, arranged in advance, *"while he yet lived"*. Same
outcome, and the book is not pretending they feel the same.

***Qēdəmâ, ʾel-ʾereṣ qedem*** — **eastward, to the east country.** In Genesis, east is where people
go when they are being removed from something. The man is driven out and the cherubim are stationed
*"at the east of the garden"* (3:24); Cain settles in Nod, *"east of Eden"* (4:16); Lot *"traveled
east"* and lands beside Sodom (13:11); and Esau will take everything he owns *"into a land away
from his brother Jacob"* (36:6). **The direction has a meaning in this book, and Abraham uses it on
six of his sons.** See [[Land and Sojourning]].

**And it is a clearing operation, not a punishment.** The pattern is the same one Genesis runs three
more times — Lot east, Ishmael south, Esau to Seir — and its function is structural: the narrative
cannot follow more than one line, so the others are given something, given a direction, and closed
out. See [[Call and Election]].

### 25:7–8 — a hundred seventy-five years

> **7** These are the days of the years of Abraham’s life which he lived: one hundred seventy-five
> years.
> **8** Abraham gave up his spirit, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years, and
> was gathered to his people.

**It is the promise of Genesis 15:15, delivered in the promise's own words.**

> **15:15** but you will go to your fathers in peace. You will be buried at a good old age.

*Śêbâ ṭôbâ*, **a good old age**, occurs in Genesis exactly twice: in that sentence and in this one.
**The clause is not a summary of a life; it is the discharge of a specific undertaking**, made in the
dark during the covenant ceremony, sixty years and eleven chapters earlier, in the middle of a
prophecy about four hundred years of slavery. Genesis marks the fulfilment by reusing the words and
says nothing about it. See
[[Buried in a Good Old Age - The Promise at Genesis 15.15 and Its Fulfilment]] and
[[The Covenant Between the Pieces]].

***"and was gathered to his people."*** ***Wayyēʾāsep ʾel-ʿammāyw*** — **the first occurrence in the
Bible**, and one of the strangest formulas in the Torah, because it is neither dying nor being
buried and the verse distinguishes it from both. Abraham *gave up his spirit*, *died*, *was gathered*
— three verbs in that order — **and only then, in the next verse, is he put in the ground**. Whatever
the gathering is, it happens before the funeral.

**And in his case it cannot mean the family tomb.** *His people* are in Ur and in Haran. The cave at
Machpelah holds one person when he is laid in it, and she is his wife, not his kin by descent. **The
one man in Genesis who left his kindred by command is the first man in the Bible said to be gathered
to it.** See [[Gathered to His People]], [[Burial and the Grave]], and [[Sheol]].

***Old and satisfied.*** The Hebrew at 25:8 is *zāqēn wəśābēaʿ* — **old and sated** — with no
object attached; the WEB's *full of years* supplies a noun that the parallel notices at 35:29 (Isaac)
and Job 42:17 do carry (*śəbaʿ yāmîm*, *full of days*) and that this verse does not. **Abraham is the
only person in Scripture described simply as satisfied.** Set it against Jacob before Pharaoh:
*"Few and evil have been the days of the years of my life"* (47:9). Two patriarchs, two verdicts, and
the book supplies both without ranking them.

**The numbers, which Genesis leaves for the reader to do.**

| fact | where it comes from | what it yields |
|---|---|---|
| Abraham is 100 at Isaac's birth | 21:5 | **Isaac is 75** when his father dies |
| Isaac is 60 when the twins are born | 25:26 | **Jacob and Esau are 15** — Abraham lived to see his grandsons |
| Abraham is 137 when Sarah dies | 23:1 with 17:17 | **he outlives his wife by 38 years** |
| Abram is 86 at Ishmael's birth | 16:16 | **Ishmael is 89** at this graveside, and outlives Abraham by 48 years |
| Shem's register at 11:10–11 | *see* [[From Shem to Abram]] | **Shem is still alive** — by the book's own arithmetic he outlives Abraham |

**Genesis states none of these and puts none of them in the pericope.** The most striking is the
second: the man dies fifteen years after the birth of the grandsons whose struggle is the next
chapter's subject, and the narrative order conceals it completely by burying him at 25:10 and only
then, at 25:19, going back to open Isaac's story.

### 25:9–10 — two sons at one grave

> **9** Isaac and Ishmael, his sons, buried him in the cave of Machpelah, in the field of Ephron, the
> son of Zohar the Hittite, which is before Mamre,
> **10** the field which Abraham purchased of the children of Heth. Abraham was buried there with
> Sarah, his wife.

**Ishmael has not been seen since 21:21 and is not explained.**

Four chapters ago he was a boy under a bush with the water gone, sent out of the household in a
scene the narrator called *very evil* in his father's eyes. Here he is standing at his father's grave
next to the brother who displaced him. **Genesis records no reconciliation, no message, no return,
and no word between them** — it simply puts the two names in one clause and moves on. It is the same
refusal to explain that produced *məṣaḥēq* at 21:9 and the price at Machpelah, and the wiki does not
fill it in. See [[Two Sons Nearly Lost - The Sending of Ishmael and the Binding of Isaac]].

**The younger is named first**, which is not how the book usually lists them. When Isaac is buried the
same way, the order is reversed: *"Esau and Jacob, his sons, buried him"* (35:29), elder first. **Two
funerals, two pairs of estranged brothers, and the birth order inverted in one of them** — with, in
both cases, no account of how the brothers came to be standing together.

**The deed is recited a third time.** Genesis states the purchase in full at 23:17–18, again at 23:19,
here at 25:9–10, and twice more at 49:29–32 and 50:13 — **five recitations across twenty-seven
chapters**, each naming the field, the cave, Ephron, and Mamre, and each specifying that Abraham
*bought* it. **The only ground the promise-bearer ever owns in the promised land is the plot he is
buried in**, and the book will not let the reader forget the receipt. See [[Machpelah]],
[[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]], and [[Ephron the Hittite]].

**And 25:10 puts him with Sarah.** *"Abraham was buried there with Sarah, his wife"* — the last
sentence about a marriage that Genesis mostly narrated through a barren womb, two sister-stories, and
an expulsion.

### 25:11 — one verse, and it is the whole succession

> **11** After the death of Abraham, God blessed Isaac, his son. Isaac lived by Beer Lahai Roi.

**This is God's first recorded act toward Isaac, and Isaac is seventy-five years old.**

Everything up to here was done *about* him: named before conception (17:19), circumcised before
consent (21:4), bound without a word (22:9), married by errand (24). **The first thing God is
reported doing to him directly happens after his father is in the ground**, and it is reported in
four words with no speech attached.

**And it is a blessing, not a covenant renewal.** God does not speak to Isaac until 26:2, and does not
restate the promise to him until 26:3–5 — where the ground given is *"because Abraham obeyed my
voice"*. Genesis is precise about this distinction and has been since 17:20, where Ishmael is
*blessed* in every term the patriarchal blessing has and the covenant is withheld in the next verse.
**Here the heir gets the blessing first and waits a chapter for the word.** See [[Blessing]] and
[[Covenant]].

***"Isaac lived by Beer Lahai Roi."*** **[[Hagar]]'s well.** The place is named at 16:14 by an
Egyptian slave running for the Egyptian border, for the God who found her — *the well of the Living
One who sees me* — and it is the only place in the Bible named by a woman. 24:62 had Isaac coming
from it; **this verse makes it his address.**

So the pericope's arrangement is this: **the verse that settles the son of the promise at the slave
woman's well is the last verse before the slave woman's son gets his genealogy.** Genesis puts them
adjacent and says nothing. See [[Beer Lahai Roi]] and [[The Two Flights of Hagar]].

### 25:12–18 — the history of the generations of Ishmael

> **12** Now this is the history of the generations of Ishmael, Abraham’s son, whom Hagar the
> Egyptian, Sarah’s servant, bore to Abraham.

**The formula is the book's structural spine, and it is being spent on the son who is not the heir.**
*ʾĒlleh tôlədōt* segments Genesis ten times, and its job is always the same: **clear the other branch
off the stage, briefly and completely, before following the one the book is actually about.** Cain's
line is given before Seth's; Japheth's and Ham's before Shem's; Ishmael's here, seven verses before
Isaac's at 25:19; and Esau's whole chapter (36) before Jacob's at 37:2. **The non-elect line always
goes first and always gets closed out.** See
[[The Other Line First - The Order of the Toledot in Genesis]] and [[The Table of Nations]].

**The heading names three people and defines Ishmael by two women.** *Abraham's son* — asserted, not
qualified. *Whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's servant, bore* — **his mother is identified by her
nationality and by her owner, and the owner has been dead for thirty-eight years**. Genesis gives
Hagar the same two-part description here that it gave her at 16:1, on the far side of everything that
happened in between. See [[Hagar]] and [[Egypt]].

> **13** These are the names of the sons of Ishmael, by their names, according to the order of their
> birth: the firstborn of Ishmael, Nebaioth, then Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam,
> **14** Mishma, Dumah, Massa,
> **15** Hadad, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah.
> **16** These are the sons of Ishmael, and these are their names, by their villages, and by their
> encampments: twelve princes, according to their nations.

**Twelve, promised at 17:20 and delivered here in the promise's own noun.** *"He will become the
father of twelve princes"* — *nəśîʾîm* — and 25:16 closes *"twelve princes, according to their
nations."* **It is the most exactly kept promise in the Abraham cycle**, and it is kept to the man
the covenant was refused to. Genesis makes the same point at 21:13 and 21:18 and refuses to soften
it either way. See [[Twelve Sons - Nahor, Ishmael, and Jacob]] and [[Call and Election]].

***"by their villages, and by their encampments."*** *Bəḥaṣrêhem ûbəṭîrōtām* — unwalled settlements
and camps. **The word for a walled city is not used**, and this is a description of the way a
pastoral confederation actually lives on the desert margin, which is what 16:12's *wild donkey among
men* predicted for them.

**Several of the twelve are attested as North Arabian peoples elsewhere in Scripture.** *Kedar* and
*Nebaioth* are the flocks of Isaiah 60:7 and the tents of Psalm 120:5 and Song of Songs 1:5; *Tema*
is a caravan stop in Job 6:19 and Isaiah 21:14; *Dumah* has an oracle of its own at Isaiah 21:11;
*Jetur* and *Naphish* are fought by the Transjordanian tribes at 1 Chronicles 5:19; *Massa* may stand
behind the headings of Proverbs 30:1 and 31:1. **The list is a real map**, and the wiki records the
identifications as identifications rather than as certainties.

**One name needs guarding.** *Tema* here is Ishmaelite; **[[Teman]]**, the Edomite town proverbial for
wisdom and the home of Job's first friend, descends from Esau (36:11) and is a different word. They
are separate pages in this wiki and should stay separate.

> **17** These are the years of the life of Ishmael: one hundred thirty-seven years. He gave up his
> spirit and died, and was gathered to his people.

**The same three verbs Abraham got, in the same order.** *Gave up his spirit*, *died*, *gathered to
his people* — and *gathered to his people* is a formula the Torah otherwise reserves for Abraham,
Isaac, Jacob, Aaron, and Moses. **Ishmael is the only person outside the covenant line ever given
it.** He is also the only one of the six with no burial reported at all: no cave, no field, no
mourners named.

> **18** They lived from Havilah to Shur that is before Egypt, as you go toward Assyria. He lived
> opposite all his relatives.

**The territory is bounded by his mother's two journeys.** [[Shur]] is the desert frontier she was
running toward when the angel turned her back (16:7); [[Havilah]] is the gold country of 2:11 and the
Arabian names of 10:7 and 10:29. **The son's map runs from the edge of one wilderness to the road his
mother was found on**, and Genesis does not remark on it.

***"He lived opposite all his relatives."*** This is the pericope's hardest half-verse, and the wiki
has been overstating it. The four-word phrase ***ʿal-pənê kol-ʾeḥāyw*** — *before the face of all his
brothers* — **is identical to the closing phrase of the oracle at 16:12**, word for word. **The verb
is not.** 16:12 has *yiškōn*, *he will dwell*; 25:18 has ***nāpāl***, *he fell* — the ordinary verb
for falling, used here of settling or encamping, and used nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible in quite
this construction.

So the renderings divide, and they divide on the verb rather than on the phrase:

- ***He settled / he encamped*** — taking *nāpal* in its attested sense of an army or a caravan
  coming down on a place. Neutral: a statement of where the twelve tribes live, alongside their kin.
- ***He fell / he settled in hostility*** — reading it against 16:12's *hand against every man*, which
  the same phrase closes. Adversarial: the oracle restated as an accomplished fact.

**The WEB's *opposite* keeps the ambiguity, and so does this wiki.** What can be said flatly is that
Genesis ends the Ishmael material by pointing back at the oracle that opened it, and that the clause
has been read for centuries as a permanent verdict on a people, which is more than the Hebrew
supports and more than Genesis does with it. See [[Hagar and Ishmael]] and [[The Nations]].

### What the pericope is doing

**It closes the cycle by counting.** Genesis 12 opened on a man with no children and a promise of a
great nation. Genesis 25:1–18 answers with twenty-eight named descendants in eight verses, **and then
follows none of them.** The promise of offspring is shown being kept lavishly and off the main line:
six sons paid off and sent east, twelve princes with villages and encampments and a territory four
hundred miles wide, and one son, seventy-five years old, living at a well in the Negev with a wife
and no children.

**And it makes the reader do the theology.** Nobody in these eighteen verses says what any of it
means. Abraham dies satisfied and the narrator does not say he was faithful; God blesses Isaac and
does not say why; Ishmael's twelve arrive exactly as promised and nobody points at 17:20; the last
clause about him is left ambiguous in the original. **This is the method [[A Wife for Isaac]]
introduced — events reported, interpretations withheld — carried one step further by removing the
characters' speech as well.** The next thing the book does, at 25:19, is start over with a barren
wife.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — **his death notice, and the end of the cycle named for him.** He speaks no word in
  these verses and is the subject of four verbs: *took*, *gave*, *gave*, *sent*. He dies at a hundred
  seventy-five *"in a good old age"*, which is the exact wording of a promise made to him at 15:15,
  and is the first person in the Bible *"gathered to his people"*.
- [[Keturah]] — the second wife, introduced in nine words and never mentioned again; *wife* at 25:1
  and one of the *concubines* at 25:6, with 1 Chronicles 1:32 taking the second reading. Sixteen
  descendants, one of whom is Midian.
- [[Isaac]] — **seventy-five, and given everything.** He inherits the whole estate, buries his father
  with the brother he displaced, receives the first divine act ever directed at him personally, and
  settles at Hagar's well. He says nothing.
- [[Ishmael]] — **back, without explanation, after four chapters away.** He buries Abraham with
  Isaac, gets the *toledot* formula the book spends on major figures, sees the twelve princes of
  17:20 delivered by name, dies at a hundred thirty-seven, and is *gathered to his people* in the
  same three verbs as his father — the only person outside the covenant line ever given that formula.
- [[Hagar]] — not present, and named in the heading of her son's genealogy exactly as she was
  introduced at 16:1: *the Egyptian*, *Sarah's servant*. Her owner has been dead thirty-eight years
  and the description stands.
- [[Sarah]] — dead, and named in the last clause of the burial: *"Abraham was buried there with
  Sarah, his wife."* Her tomb becomes the family's.
- [[God]] — acts once, in four words, and does not speak. *"After the death of Abraham, God blessed
  Isaac, his son"* is the whole of the divine presence in eighteen verses, and it is a blessing
  rather than a covenant word; the covenant word waits until 26:2–5.
- [[Ephron the Hittite]] — named again in the recitation of the deed, twenty-seven chapters into a
  transaction he closed in one.
- [[The Children of Heth]] — named again as the vendors: *"the field which Abraham purchased of the
  children of Heth."*
- [[Rebekah]] — not in the pericope, and the reason the next one exists; on the book's arithmetic she
  has been married to Isaac for thirty-five years at 25:7 and has no children.

## Places

- [[Machpelah]] — the cave and the field, recited in full for the third of five times; Abraham is
  buried in the only ground he ever owned.
- [[Mamre (place)]] — the survey benchmark again: *"which is before Mamre"*, the phrase Genesis
  repeats at 23:17, 23:19, 25:9, 49:30, and 50:13.
- [[Hebron]] — unnamed here, and the location: 23:19 supplied the gloss *"(that is, Hebron)"* once and
  the book has not repeated it since.
- [[Beer Lahai Roi]] — Hagar's well, and from 25:11 Isaac's address; the only place in the Bible named
  by a woman, and the home of the son of the promise.
- [[Havilah]] — the eastern limit of Ishmaelite range, and a name Genesis has already used twice
  elsewhere, for the gold country of Eden's second river (2:11) and for two separate lines in the
  Table of Nations (10:7, 10:29).
- [[Shur]] — the western limit, *"that is before Egypt"*; the desert frontier Hagar was running for
  when she was turned back (16:7).
- [[Egypt]] — the boundary marker at 25:18, and the identity clause on Hagar at 25:12; the country
  Ishmael's wife came from (21:21) and the country his descendants will carry Joseph into (37:25–28).
- [[Assyria]] — the far bearing, *"as you go toward Assyria"*: the north-eastern term of a line drawn
  across the whole Arabian trade corridor.
- [[The Negev]] — where Isaac is living, from 24:62 and now permanently.
- [[Canaan]] — the land the whole cycle was about, and in these verses it is a burial plot.

## Themes

- [[Burial and the Grave]] — the pericope's centre of gravity: two death notices, one burial, the
  Bible's first *gathered to his people*, and the deed to the grave recited in full.
- [[Life and Death]] — a hundred seventy-five years, a hundred thirty-seven years, and the only
  person in Scripture said to die *satisfied* with no object attached to the word.
- [[Blessing]] — *"God blessed Isaac, his son"*, the succession in four words; and the twelve princes
  of 17:20's blessing on Ishmael, delivered by name.
- [[Covenant]] — kept sharply distinct from blessing: Isaac is blessed here and not spoken to until
  26:2; Ishmael is fruitful, numbered, and princely, and the covenant was never his.
- [[Call and Election]] — seven sons given gifts and a direction, one given everything; and Genesis
  declining, again, to give a reason.
- [[The Nations]] — sixteen Arabian names by Keturah, twelve by Ishmael, and Sheba and Dedan filed
  under a third line at 10:7; the covenant family is the demographic source of half the Near East and
  the book keeps the promise and the lineage in different columns.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — Abraham dies owning one field, and it is the one he is buried in. His
  other sons are sent *eastward, to the east country*; his heir settles at a desert well.
- [[Marriage]] — Keturah, *wife* and *concubine* in six verses; and 25:10, which reunites Abraham with
  Sarah in the ground.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — Hagar identified as *Sarah's servant* in her son's genealogy, decades

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's box read *Genesis 25:1–18* and it stands as drawn; **no
  unchecked boxes were redrawn this pass.** The break at 25:18 / 25:19 is the book's own — 25:19 opens
  *"This is the history of the generations of Isaac"*, the *toledot* formula that begins a new
  section, and the book page's own structural note already ends the Abraham division at 25:18. The
  alternative split, taking 25:1–11 (Abraham's end) and 25:12–18 (Ishmael's genealogy) as two units,
  was considered and rejected: 25:11 and 25:12 are deliberately adjacent — the son of the promise
  settling at the slave woman's well, and then the slave woman's son's genealogy — and separating them
  loses the juxtaposition. The next box, *Jacob and Esau and the Sold Birthright — Genesis 25:19–34*,
  stands as drawn.
- **A correction to three existing pages.** [[Ishmael]], [[Hagar and Ishmael]], and [[The Nations]]
  each state that 25:18 repeats 16:12 *word for word*. **That is an overstatement and has been fixed
  in this pass.** The prepositional phrase *ʿal-pənê kol-ʾeḥāyw* is identical in both verses; the verb
  is not — *yiškōn* (*dwell*) at 16:12 against *nāpāl* (*fall*, here *settle* or *encamp*) at 25:18 —
  and the difference between the two verbs is exactly where the interpretive question lives. The
  claim now reads accurately on all three pages and the crux is set out on this one.
- **On Keturah's chronology, unresolved.** Sarah dies when Abraham is a hundred thirty-seven and
  Abraham dies at a hundred seventy-five, and the narrative order puts a second marriage, six sons,
  seven grandsons, and three great-grandsons into the thirty-eight years between. Three live
  proposals are set out above — non-chronological placement, restored vigour, and the Hagar
  identification — **and the wiki adopts none of them.** What is checkable is that Genesis states the
  ages, states the marriage, and never puts the two together.
- **On what she is called.** *Wife* at 25:1, one of the *concubines* at 25:6, and *concubine* at
  1 Chronicles 1:32. **Both readings are recorded and neither is adopted**, on the same footing as
  Hagar's *wife* at 16:3 against *servant* at 21:10. The distinction is about the status of the sons,
  which 25:5–6 then settles by other means.
- **On *gathered to his people*.** The formula is stated above as a fact about the narration and not
  as a claim about the afterlife. What the text supplies: it is distinguished from dying and from
  burial by being listed between them; it is used of six people in the Torah; and in Abraham's case
  his people are buried a thousand miles away, which rules out the family-tomb reading for the first
  occurrence. What the text does not supply is any statement about what happens to the person
  gathered. The proposals — a euphemism for death, a reference to Sheol as a common destination, a
  survival of ancestor-burial language — are set out on [[Gathered to His People]] and none is
  adopted.
- ***Śābēaʿ* at 25:8.** The Hebrew is *zāqēn wəśābēaʿ*, *old and satisfied*, without the *yāmîm*
  (*days*) that the parallel notices at 35:29 and Job 42:17 carry; the WEB's *full of years* supplies
  it, as do most English versions, and the ancient versions read it that way too. **The observation is
  recorded as a textual detail and not as a correction to the WEB** — the sense is the same either
  way, and what is worth keeping is that Genesis says of Abraham, uniquely, that he had had enough.
- **Why the sixteen and the twelve did not get pages.** Twenty-eight names arrive in these eighteen
  verses and none of them acts, speaks, or is described. **The wiki's standing practice, set at
  [[The Table of Nations]], is that a bare genealogical name does not earn a page and a name with
  narrative weight does** — Nimrod and Canaan got pages out of Genesis 10; Sheba, Dedan, and Raamah
  did not. That rule is applied here unchanged. **Midian is the closest call**, since the Midianites
  are a major people and the Bible fights them for four books without ever mentioning that they
  descend from Abraham; he is deliberately left without a page until the Midianites enter the
  narrative at Genesis 37:28, at which point the page should be `Midian (son of Abraham)` — the
  qualifier is needed because Midian is also a region, and an unqualified `Midian.md` is exactly the
  silent basename collision the `AGENTS.md` qualifier rules exist to prevent. **This note exists so
  the next agent does not create a bare `Midian.md`.** The same applies to Nebaioth and Kedar if
  Isaiah 60 or Isaiah 21 is ever ingested.
- **On the reappearance of Ishmael.** Genesis gives no account of it. The tradition that Abraham
  visited him, that they were reconciled, or that Keturah is Hagar returned, all exist to fill this
  gap, and all of them are later than Genesis. **What the text does is put the two names in one clause
  at a graveside**, in the same way it put Isaac and Abraham in one clause coming down from Moriah and
  then stopped mentioning Isaac. The wiki records the silence.
- **On Isaac's age here, which nobody states.** Seventy-five — the age Abraham was when he was called
  out of Haran (12:4). Genesis supplies both numbers, ten chapters apart, and never sets them beside
  each other; **the coincidence is recorded as arithmetic, not as a claim about the author's
  intention.**
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Rebekah's barrenness and the oracle of the two nations
  (25:21–23) belong to the next box and are not treated here beyond a cross-reference; the
  *(not yet ingested)* markers on [[Rebekah]] and [[Barrenness]] for 25:21 and 25:23 were left in
  place deliberately. Esau's *toledot* at Genesis 36 is referenced as a structural parallel and not
  developed. No dangling links were left.
