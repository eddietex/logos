# The Rescue of Lot and Melchizedek

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 14:1–24

## Summary

The chapter where Abram stops being a herdsman with a promise and becomes a man with three hundred
eighteen armed retainers — and then, at the moment he has the most to gain from it, refuses to be
paid.

Genesis 14 does not sound like the chapters on either side of it. It opens with a king list, dates
itself by regnal years, glosses its own place names for a reader who no longer knows them
(*"Bela (also called Zoar)"*, *"the valley of Siddim (also called the Salt Sea)"*, *"the valley of
Shaveh (that is, the King's Valley)"*), and moves across the whole map of the ancient Near East
instead of between two tents. It carries archaic vocabulary that occurs nowhere else in the Bible.
It is the only chapter in Genesis where a patriarch fights a battle, and it introduces, in seven
verses, the first priest, the first tithe, the first blessing spoken over a human being by another
human being who is not his father, and the first oath sworn by a man in Scripture. Critical
scholarship has never been able to file it: it belongs to none of the usual sources, and the
standing description is that it is an old, independent tradition set down here whole. What it is
placed *between* is not accidental — the promise enlarged at the end of chapter 13, and the
covenant cut in chapter 15 that begins *"Don't be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly
great reward"* (15:1), which is an answer to a man who has just turned down a reward.

**Four kings against five, and the five lose.** *"In the days of Amraphel, king of Shinar, Arioch,
king of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer, king of Elam, and Tidal, king of Goiim, they made war with Bera,
king of Sodom, and with Birsha, king of Gomorrah, Shinab, king of Admah, and Shemeber, king of
Zeboiim, and the king of Bela (also called Zoar)"* (14:1–2). Nine kings in two verses, and the
arithmetic of the fifth city is the first thing to notice: four of the five are named and the king
of Bela is not, which is exactly the pattern of a source that has real names for the important
cities and none for the small one. [[Shinar]] is the Babel plain of 10:10 and 11:2, back in the
book after three chapters and now supplying an invader. [[Elam]] is a son of Shem in the Table of
Nations (10:22); its king leads the coalition, and the chapter's whole campaign is later summarized
as *"the slaughter of Chedorlaomer"* (14:17), which makes him the antagonist even though he is
listed third. *Goiim* is not a place at all — it is *gôyim*, the ordinary Hebrew word for
**nations**, the word behind [[The Nations]] — so Tidal is *king of nations*, a title with no
country attached.

**Twelve years of tribute, one year of rebellion, and a punitive expedition in the fourteenth.**
*"They served Chedorlaomer for twelve years, and in the thirteenth year, they rebelled"* (14:4).
This is the standard shape of an imperial reprisal, and Genesis reports it in the flat idiom of a
chronicle rather than the idiom of a story. Nothing in 14:1–11 is about Abram. He is not mentioned;
he does not know it is happening.

**The campaign is a long southward sweep down the far side of the Jordan and back.** Ashteroth
Karnaim in the north, Ham, Shaveh Kiriathaim, then Mount Seir, then *"to El Paran, which is by the
wilderness"* at the desert's edge, then a turn north again to En Mishpat and Hazazon Tamar
(14:5–7). The peoples struck along the way are the legendary pre-Israelite giants of the
Transjordan — the Rephaim, the Zuzim, the Emim, the Horites — the same names Deuteronomy 2 will use
when it explains who lived in Moab, Ammon, and Edom *before* Moab, Ammon, and Edom. Genesis
is describing a raid down the King's Highway through territory the reader of Deuteronomy would
recognize as ancient even by Moses's standards.

**One of the names in that itinerary cannot be right, and the wiki flags it rather than smoothing
it.** *"They … struck all the country of the Amalekites"* (14:7). Amalek is Esau's grandson by his
concubine Timna (36:12), which puts him five generations after this chapter. Two readings are
standard and both are honest: the verse is a **narrator's geographical gloss**, naming the region by
the people who held it in the writer's day (the same convention that lets 13:18 call the place
Hebron and 12:8 call it Bethel long before either name is given in the narrative), or there was an
older Amalek unrelated to Esau's line. The first is much the more economical, and it is the same
phenomenon as *Dan* at 14:14 below.

**The battle is lost in the tar pits.** *"Now the valley of Siddim was full of tar pits; and the
kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and some fell there, and those who remained fled to the hills"*
(14:10). The Hebrew is emphatic — *beʾĕrōt beʾĕrōt ḥēmār*, *pits, pits of bitumen* — and the detail
is geologically exact: the southern end of the Dead Sea rift seeps asphalt, which floated up in
slabs after earthquakes and was traded out of the region for millennia. Genesis 11:3 already used
*ḥēmār* once, as the mortar of Babel. The same substance builds the tower and swallows the army.
See [[The Salt Sea]].

**And Lot is carried off in a sentence that will not let the reader forget whose fault it is.**
*"They took Lot, Abram's brother's son, who lived in Sodom, and his goods, and departed"* (14:12).
Every clause is a callback to chapter 13. *Abram's brother's son* is the relationship 13:8 called
*brothers*. *Who lived in Sodom* — 13:12 had him *"moved his tent as far as Sodom"*, outside it;
one chapter later the tent is inside the walls, and Genesis reports the move only in the clause that
says he lost everything by it. *And his goods* — the goods are the reason he went east in the first
place. The bill for the well-watered plain arrives in the first verse in which anything happens to
him. See [[Abram and Lot Separate]].

### Abram the Hebrew

*"One who had escaped came and told Abram, the Hebrew"* (14:13). **The first occurrence of the word
in the Bible.** *ʿIbrî* is never what Israelites call themselves among themselves; in Genesis and
Exodus it is what foreigners call them (39:14, 17; 41:12; Exodus 1:15–19; 2:6) and what they call
themselves when speaking to a foreigner (40:15; Jonah 1:9). It appears here, in the one chapter with
an international cast, at the moment Abram has to be identified to an outsider. Its derivation is
disputed and both candidates are live: from [[Eber]], Shem's great-grandson, whose line Genesis
10:21 introduces the whole Semitic branch by; or from *ʿābar*, **to cross over**, which would make
it *the one from the other side* — of the Euphrates, as Joshua 24:2–3 puts it. See
[[Abram the Hebrew - The First Use of the Name]].

**And he already has a treaty.** *"At that time, he lived by the oaks of Mamre, the Amorite,
brother of Eshcol, and brother of Aner; and they were allies of Abram"* (14:13). The Hebrew for
*allies* is *baʿălê bərît* — **masters of a covenant**, covenant-partners. This is the first
*bərît* in the Abraham story, and it is not with God: it is a mutual-defense pact with three
Amorite brothers. Genesis will not use the word of Abram and Yahweh until 15:18, four verses of
narrative later. The man who has been promised the land of the Amorites (15:16) is, at this point,
in a treaty with three of them, camping under their trees, and about to go to war with their help.
See [[Mamre (person)]], [[Eshcol]], and [[Aner]].

**Three hundred eighteen men, born in the house.** *"When Abram heard that his relative was taken
captive, he led out his trained men, born in his house, three hundred and eighteen, and pursued as
far as Dan"* (14:14). Two things in that verse are unique in Scripture.

- ***Trained men* occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible.** *Ḥănîkāyw* is a hapax; the root shows
  up in Proverbs 22:6 (*train up a child*) and the word itself is attested outside the Bible in
  Egyptian execration texts and in a fifteenth-century Taanach letter, in both cases meaning
  retainers or armed dependents of a chief. Genesis is using a genuinely archaic military term.
- **The number is oddly precise, and it makes Abram a chieftain.** Three hundred eighteen fighting
  men *born in his house* implies a household of well over a thousand. The tent-dweller of chapter
  13 turns out to command a private army, and the text mentions it only when he needs it. A famous
  rabbinic reading (*Nedarim* 32a) notes that 318 is the numerical value of the name Eliezer, the
  servant who is Abram's presumptive heir at 15:2, and takes the number as a way of saying he went
  out with one man. The wiki records it as a curiosity of the tradition, not as the verse's meaning.

***As far as Dan*** is the chapter's second anachronism and the clearer of the two. The town is
Laish until the tribe of Dan captures it and renames it (Judges 18:29; Joshua 19:47), which is
centuries after Abram. Like *the Amalekites* at 14:7, it is a narrator naming a place by the name his
readers use. See [[Dan (city)]].

**The tactics are a night raid, and Genesis tells them in one line.** *"He divided himself against
them by night, he and his servants, and struck them, and pursued them to Hobah, which is on the
left hand of Damascus"* (14:15). *The left hand* is north in the Hebrew orientation — the same
idiom Abram used offering Lot the land at 13:9 — so the pursuit runs past [[Damascus]] and out of
Canaan altogether, roughly a hundred and fifty miles from Hebron. *"He brought back all the goods,
and also brought back his relative, Lot, and his goods, and the women also, and the other people"*
(14:16). The verb *brought back* is *hēšîb*, **caused to return**, used twice, and the list is in
the order of a recovery inventory with the person the chapter cares about second and the anonymous
captives last. He recovers everything, including what belongs to the city that had swallowed his
nephew.

### Two kings come out to meet him

The last eight verses are built on a symmetry the narrative never comments on. Two kings meet Abram
in the same valley on the same day. One brings out food and a blessing and asks for nothing; the
other brings nothing and asks for something. Abram gives a tenth of everything to the first and
refuses a thread from the second.

| | Melchizedek, 14:18–20 | The king of Sodom, 14:17, 21–24 |
|---|---|---|
| Title | *king of Salem*, and *priest of God Most High* | *king of Sodom* |
| Named | yes, and his name is a sentence | *Bera* at 14:2, then only *the king of Sodom* |
| What he brings | *bread and wine* | nothing |
| What he says | a blessing, of Abram and of God | an imperative: *"Give me the people"* |
| What Abram does | *gave him a tenth of all* | *"I will not take a thread nor a sandal strap"* |
| Direction of the exchange | Abram receives, then gives | Abram is offered, and refuses |

The king of Sodom is introduced first (14:17) and then made to wait outside his own scene for four
verses while Melchizedek arrives, blesses, is tithed, and leaves. Whatever else Genesis 14 is doing,
it is arranging a comparison.

**Melchizedek.** *"Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine: and he was priest of God
Most High"* (14:18). He gets three verses, has no genealogy in a book made of genealogies, arrives
from nowhere and returns to it, and is never mentioned again in the Old Testament except in one
line of one psalm. Almost every word of his introduction is a first:

- **The first priest in the Bible.** *Kōhēn* has not appeared before this verse. There is no
  tabernacle, no altar mentioned here, no sacrifice, no Levi — Levi's great-grandfather is standing
  in front of him — and no Israel. See [[Priesthood]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- **His name is a sentence.** *Malkî-ṣedeq*: *my king is righteousness*, or *king of
  righteousness*, which is how Hebrews 7:2 translates it. Adoni-Zedek, king of Jerusalem in Joshua
  10:1, carries the matching formation — *my lord is righteousness* — which suggests *ṣedeq* was a
  dynastic element in that city's royal names rather than a personal accident.
- **His city is almost certainly Jerusalem.** *Shālēm* shares its consonants with *shālôm*, peace —
  again Hebrews 7:2, *king of peace* — and Psalm 76:2 puts the identification beyond much doubt:
  *"His tabernacle is in Salem. His dwelling place in Zion."* See [[Salem]].
- **His God is named with a title the Bible has not used.** *ʾĒl ʿElyôn*, **God Most High**, four
  times in three verses, with the epithet *qōnēh šāmayim wāʾāreṣ*, *possessor of heaven and earth*.
  *Qānâ* means both *acquire* and *create*, and the same divine title turns up in Ugaritic and
  Phoenician inscriptions of the surrounding cultures. See [[The Names of God]].
- **Bread and wine, brought out to a returning army.** The simplest reading is provisioning — this
  is what a city does for allies coming back from a campaign (compare Judges 8:5, where Gideon asks
  for exactly this and is refused). The chapter says only that he *brought them out*; no altar, no
  offering, no meal is described. That the two elements are the ones the Last Supper uses is a fact
  of vocabulary that later readers built on heavily, and the wiki records both the plain sense and
  the later use without collapsing one into the other. See [[The Vine and the Cup]].

**The blessing.** *"Blessed be Abram of God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth: and blessed be
God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand"* (14:19–20). Two blessings in one
breath, downward and upward, and the second credits the victory to God rather than to three hundred
eighteen men — the first time in Genesis anyone attributes a human military success to God. The
formula *bārûk X lə-ʾĒl ʿElyôn* is the shape [[Blessing]] will keep for the rest of the Bible.

**The tithe, and the ambiguity in it.** *"Abram gave him a tenth of all"* (14:20). The Hebrew has no
subject and no object — *wayyitten-lô maʿăśēr mikkōl*, *and he gave him a tenth of everything* — so
the verse is grammatically capable of meaning that Melchizedek gave Abram a tenth. Hebrews 7:2, 4
reads it the standard way and the WEB supplies *Abram* accordingly; virtually every version and
tradition agrees, and the ancient exception is a minority reading in Jewish sources. The wiki notes
the grammar and follows the consensus. What is not ambiguous is that this is the **first tithe in
Scripture**, paid without a command, without a law, and to a foreign priest. See [[Tithing]] and
[[The Tithe Before the Law]].

**The king of Sodom's offer, and Abram's oath.** *"Give me the people, and take the goods for
yourself"* (14:21). By the customary law of the ancient Near East, the whole recovery is Abram's;
the king of Sodom is asking a favor and dressing it as a division. Abram's answer is the longest
speech he has made to anyone:

> **22** *"I have lifted up my hand to Yahweh, God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth,*
> **23** *that I will not take a thread nor a sandal strap nor anything that is yours, lest you
> should say, 'I have made Abram rich.'*
> **24** *I will accept nothing from you except that which the young men have eaten, and the portion
> of the men who went with me: Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre. Let them take their portion."*

Four things are happening.

**He swears, and it is the first oath a human being takes in the Bible.** *I have lifted up my hand*
is the gesture, not a metaphor; the same idiom is used of God swearing in Deuteronomy 32:40 and
Ezekiel 20:5, and the perfect tense means the oath was made before he opened his mouth — he had
decided in advance. See [[Oaths and Vows]].

**He identifies Melchizedek's God as his own.** He takes the priest's exact title, *ʾĒl ʿElyôn qōnēh
šāmayim wāʾāreṣ*, and prefixes his own covenant name to it: *Yahweh, God Most High, possessor of
heaven and earth*. This is a theological act performed inside a refusal. Abram accepts a Canaanite
priest's blessing, accepts his God's name, and files it under Yahweh. It is the closest thing in
Genesis to a statement that the God worshipped elsewhere under another title is the God who called
him out of Ur.

**He refuses the thing he was promised.** *"I will make of you a great nation … I will bless you and
make your name great"* (12:2), and here is a king offering him a nation's worth of movable wealth,
which he declines with an idiom for the two cheapest objects a person carries. The stated reason is
about credit: *lest you should say, "I have made Abram rich."* Compare chapter 12, where the wealth
came from another foreign king, was accepted in exchange for a lie about his wife, and produced the
quarrel that cost him his nephew. He has learned something and Genesis does not say so. See
[[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].

**And he does not impose the vow on anybody else.** The young men have already eaten; Aner, Eshcol,
and Mamre take their share. His allies are not Yahweh's covenant partners and he does not treat
them as bound by an oath they did not swear. It is a small, precise piece of ethics that most of the
chapter's readers pass over.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *Abram*, and this is the chapter that shows what he actually is: not a wandering
  shepherd but a chief with a treaty, a private army, and enough standing that a king comes out to
  meet him in a valley. He acts without being told to and without praying about it, which is
  unusual; God does not speak once in Genesis 14, the only chapter in the Abraham cycle where that
  is true. His three recorded acts are a rescue, a tithe, and a refusal, and the last of them is a
  deliberate reversal of Genesis 12:16.
- [[Lot]] — the reason for everything Abram does here, and a man who does not appear in the chapter
  except as an object: he is *taken* (14:12) and *brought back* (14:16). He says nothing, and
  Genesis does not record him thanking anyone or leaving Sodom. By 19:1 he is sitting in its gate.
- [[Melchizedek]] — priest-king of [[Salem]], with no father, no genealogy, no successor, and three
  verses. He blesses Abram, is given a tenth, and leaves the Old Testament until Psalm 110:4. See
  [[Melchizedek in Psalm 110 and Hebrews]].
- [[Bera]] — *king of Sodom*, named once at 14:2 and thereafter only by his title. He flees his own
  battle, survives, comes out to meet the man who recovered his city's property, and asks for the
  only part of it Abram was never going to keep. His four words of dialogue are the only speech any
  Sodomite is given until 19:9.
- [[Chedorlaomer]] — king of [[Elam]] and the coalition's actual leader; the overlord whose tribute
  was refused and whose reprisal drives the chapter. **Amraphel of Shinar, Arioch of Ellasar, and
  Tidal of Goiim** are named twice each and given nothing else — no acts, no words, no fate — so the
  wiki covers them on his page rather than making three pages out of a name and a kingdom apiece.
  Amraphel was identified with Hammurabi of Babylon by nineteenth-century scholars; the equation is
  linguistically untenable and has been abandoned.
- [[Mamre (person)]], [[Eshcol]], and [[Aner]] — three Amorite brothers, Abram's *baʿălê bərît*,
  covenant-partners. They go with him, and he defends their right to a share of spoil he will not
  touch himself. Mamre is also the name of the grove Abram lives in ([[Mamre (place)]]), and Genesis
  uses it both ways in the same verse.
- **Birsha of Gomorrah, Shinab of Admah, Shemeber of Zeboiim, and the unnamed king of Bela** — the
  rest of the defeated five. Nothing is told of them beyond the loss; no pages.
- **The Rephaim, Zuzim, Emim, and Horites** — the peoples struck on the way south, all of them
  remembered in Deuteronomy 2:10–12, 20–23 as the giants and aboriginals of the Transjordan whom
  Moab, Ammon, and Edom displaced. The Rephaim already have a thread in the wiki through
  [[The Nephilim and the Giants of Canaan]]; none gets a page yet, and they earn one when
  Deuteronomy 2 or Joshua does something with them.
- [[God]] — silent. He is spoken *about* four times in three verses, under a title Genesis has not
  used before, by a man who is not an Israelite, and Abram adopts the title in his own oath. But
  Yahweh neither speaks nor acts on stage in Genesis 14, and the first words of chapter 15 read like
  a reply to the silence: *"Don't be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great
  reward."*

## Places

- [[Sodom]] — sacked, stripped of *"all the goods … and all their food"* (14:11), and given a king
  who speaks. This is the first time anything at all happens to the city the narrator condemned at
  13:13, and it is a rehearsal of chapter 19 in a minor key: judgment arrives from outside, and Lot
  is pulled out of it by someone else's action.
- [[Gomorrah]] — sacked alongside Sodom, as always, and its king flees into the tar pits with
  Sodom's.
- [[The Salt Sea]] — *"the valley of Siddim (also called the Salt Sea)"* (14:3), the battlefield and
  its gloss: the chapter locates the fight in a valley that the writer's readers know as a sea. The
  bitumen pits of 14:10 are the same rift-valley asphalt that mortared Babel at 11:3.
- [[Salem]] — Melchizedek's city, and on the evidence of Psalm 76:2 the first appearance of
  Jerusalem in the Bible, four hundred years before David takes it and under a king who is already
  a priest of the Most High.
- [[Elam]] — the Iranian highland kingdom east of Babylonia, and Chedorlaomer's seat. It is also a
  son of Shem at 10:22, which makes the chapter's chief aggressor a Semite by the Table's own
  reckoning.
- [[Shinar]] — Babel's plain, back in the narrative as a kingdom that sends armies. Everything that
  happens there in Genesis is about power.
- [[Dan (city)]] — the northern limit of Abram's pursuit, and the chapter's clearest anachronism:
  Laish until Judges 18:29.
- [[Damascus]] — the great oasis city of the Syrian plateau, entering Scripture here as a landmark
  for a place further on. Abram's steward Eliezer is *of Damascus* at 15:2, the next chapter.
- [[Mamre (place)]] and [[Hebron]] — where he is living when the news reaches him (14:13), and where
  he returns to.
- **The valley of Shaveh, the King's Valley** — the meeting place of 14:17, glossed by the narrator.
  Absalom raises his pillar in a *King's Valley* at 2 Samuel 18:18, near Jerusalem, which fits the
  Salem identification. No page yet.
- **Ashteroth Karnaim, Ham, Shaveh Kiriathaim, Mount Seir, El Paran, En Mishpat (Kadesh), Hazazon
  Tamar, Hobah, Bela/Zoar, Admah, Zeboiim, Ellasar, Goiim** — the campaign itinerary and the rest of
  the king list. Named in plain text rather than linked, and each earns a page when it does work of
  its own: Kadesh at Numbers 13, Seir with Esau at Genesis 32, Zoar at Genesis 19:18–23, Admah and
  Zeboiim in Deuteronomy 29:23 and Hosea 11:8.

## Themes

- [[War]] — the first war in Scripture, and a doubly odd introduction: nine kings fight it, none of
  them Israelite, and the man the book is about joins it only to recover a hostage. Genesis
  neither praises nor condemns Abram's raid; it reports the night attack in one clause and spends
  four times as long on what he does with the proceeds.
- [[Priesthood]] — *kōhēn* enters the Bible at 14:18, attached to a Canaanite king with no
  genealogy, no sanctuary, and no law. Everything Israel's priesthood will be defined by is absent,
  and Psalm 110 and Hebrews 7 will use exactly that absence.
- [[Tithing]] — the first tenth in Scripture, given voluntarily, out of spoil, before any command
  exists. Jacob will vow one at 28:22; the law will require one at Leviticus 27:30.
- [[Blessing]] — the first human blessing spoken over someone outside the speaker's family, and the
  first that runs in both directions in one breath: *"Blessed be Abram of God Most High … and
  blessed be God Most High."* It is also the first time 12:3's *"I will bless those who bless you"*
  is visibly cashed by a stranger.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — *"I have lifted up my hand to Yahweh"* (14:22), the Bible's first human oath,
  sworn before it is spoken and used to refuse a gift.
- [[The Names of God]] — *ʾĒl ʿElyôn*, *possessor of heaven and earth*, introduced by a foreigner
  and annexed by Abram to the name Yahweh in the same chapter.
- [[Covenant]] — *bərît* appears in the Abraham cycle for the first time here, and it is a treaty
  with Amorites (14:13), not with God. Chapter 15 will use the word of Yahweh four verses of
  narrative later, and the juxtaposition is worth keeping.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — nine kings, tribute, rebellion, and reprisal, in a book whose hero has
  no throne. Abram beats four kings and takes nothing, which is the chapter's whole political
  argument.
- [[City and Civilization]] — the cities of the plain lose everything they have to a bigger power in
  a single afternoon, and the tent-dweller in the hills is the one who gets it back.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the resident alien defends the country he has been promised and does not
  own, and then refuses to take a thread of it as payment.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the counterpart to chapter 13. There Abram avoided a fight by giving
  away what was his; here he starts one to recover what is not his, for the same nephew. The wiki
  reads the two chapters as one argument about what he is willing to spend himself on.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — bread and wine brought out by a priest, with no altar and no victim,
  in a chapter that has a priesthood and no sacrifice at all.

## Notes

- **Genesis 14 does not sound like Genesis.** The chapter's oddities are consistent with each other:
  regnal dating, a nine-name king list, four glosses of the *"also called"* type, an itinerary of
  places most of which never recur, a hapax military term (*ḥănîkîm*, 14:14), the divine title *ʾĒl
  ʿElyôn* found nowhere else in the patriarchal narratives, and an Abram who behaves like a
  chieftain rather than a nomad. Source criticism has never assigned it to J, E, P, or D; the usual
  description is that it is an independent block of old material. Nothing in that judgment settles
  its historicity in either direction, and the wiki takes no position: the kings' names are
  linguistically plausible for the second millennium (Tudḫaliya behind *Tidal*, *Arriwuk* behind
  *Arioch*, the Elamite element *Kudur-* behind *Chedorlaomer*), and none of the four has been
  identified with a known ruler.
- **Two anachronisms, and why they are not the same problem as an error.** *The Amalekites* (14:7)
  and *Dan* (14:14) both name a place by a population that arrives later. The wiki reads them as
  narratorial glosses of the same kind Genesis uses openly elsewhere — *Bethel* at 12:8 and 13:3
  before 28:19 names it, *Hebron* at 13:18 before 23:2, *"the valley of Siddim (also called the Salt
  Sea)"* in this very chapter — and notes that the alternative (a pre-Esau Amalek, a pre-Danite Dan)
  is available but unnecessary. What the glosses do show is a narrator writing at a distance from
  the events, for readers who need help with the map.
- **The tithe's grammar.** *Wayyitten-lô maʿăśēr mikkōl* has an unexpressed subject, and the two
  candidates are the two men in the room. Hebrews 7:4 — *"consider how great this man was, to whom
  even Abraham the patriarch gave a tenth out of the best plunder"* — is the earliest surviving
  explicit reading and the one every major tradition follows. The minority reversal (Melchizedek
  provisioning Abram with a tenth) survives in a few Jewish sources and has no significant modern
  defense. The verse is genuinely underdetermined and its interpretation is not.
- **What Melchizedek became.** Three verses in Genesis and one in the Psalter generated an
  unusually large afterlife. Psalm 110:4 makes his priesthood the model for a Davidic king's, which
  is the pivot Hebrews 5–7 turns on: because Genesis gives him no father, mother, genealogy, birth,
  or death, Hebrews reads him as *"made like the Son of God"* and a priest *"forever"* (7:3) — an
  argument from silence that Hebrews knows is an argument from silence and uses deliberately. At
  Qumran, 11QMelchizedek casts him as a heavenly figure who proclaims liberty and executes judgment
  in the last jubilee. A rabbinic tradition (*Nedarim* 32b; Targum Neofiti) identifies him with
  Shem, who by Genesis 11's numbers is still alive in Abram's lifetime, and reads 14:20's blessing
  as the reason the priesthood passed from Shem's line to Abram's. 2 Enoch tells a birth legend.
  The wiki records the range on [[Melchizedek]] and treats Hebrews as canonical interpretation
  rather than as a claim about what Genesis 14 was doing.
- **Was Abram right to fight?** The chapter offers no evaluation, and neither does the rest of
  Scripture. It is worth noticing what the text does and does not say: he arms in response to a
  kidnapping, he attacks a force already withdrawing, he takes no territory, and he keeps nothing.
  Melchizedek credits the victory to God (14:20) and Abram accepts the credit going upward while
  refusing the goods going sideways. Readers in the pacifist traditions have generally treated the
  episode as narrated rather than commended; readers in the just-war traditions have used it as a
  founding case of legitimate rescue. Genesis supplies the material for both and adjudicates
  neither.
- **The chapter is a hinge in the Abraham cycle, and the seam is visible.** Chapter 13 ends with
  Abram given a land he does not own. Chapter 14 has him defend it, decline to profit from it, and
  meet a priest of a God he had not been introduced to. Chapter 15 opens with God calling himself
  *shield* and *reward* — the two things a man who has just fought a battle and refused the spoil
  would be short of. The three chapters read continuously even though the middle one is stylistically
  foreign to the other two.
- **Lot's silence.** He is recovered and does not speak, does not thank Abram, and does not move.
  Genesis simply resumes with him in Sodom five chapters later. The chapter's structure makes the
  point without a word of comment: Abram travels three hundred miles for a man who goes straight back
  to the city he was carried out of.
