# Jacob's Flight from Laban

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 31:1–55

## Summary

**Fifty-five verses in which everybody steals something and the chapter ends in a treaty.**

**The longest pericope in Genesis so far**, and the one that finally closes the twenty years. It
contains **God's first words to Jacob since Bethel** (31:3), **the only speech Rachel and Leah ever
give together** (31:14–16), a theft, a flight, a seven-day pursuit, a house-to-house search, **the
longest speech Jacob makes in the book** (31:36–42), a boundary covenant, a heap of stones with two
names in two languages, and a kiss goodbye.

**Its keyword is *steal*.** ***Gānab*** runs **seven times** through the chapter and its objects keep
changing: Rachel steals her father's gods (31:19), Jacob *steals Laban's heart* (31:20, 26 — the WEB
renders the idiom *deceived*), Laban asks *why have you stolen my gods* (31:30), Jacob pronounces
death on whoever has them (31:32), and in his own defence he counts the animals *"stolen by day or
stolen by night"* that he paid for himself (31:39). **Nobody in the chapter is innocent of the verb
and nobody is charged with it**, and the one theft that is real — the *teraphim* under a camel
saddle — is never discovered, never confessed, and never mentioned again in the pericope.

**And the whole shape is an exodus.** A man serves a foreign master for twenty years; he asks to be
released and is refused (30:25–27); God tells him to go; he leaves in secret with his family and the
wealth he earned; the master pursues him; God intervenes on the road in a night warning; and the
master, unable to touch him, lets him go and makes terms. **Jacob's own summary of it is the exodus's
own word**: *"surely now you would have sent me away ***empty***"* (31:42), ***rêqām*** — and
Exodus 3:21 promises Israel *"you shall not go empty."* See
[[Served, Released, Pursued - The Laban Years as Proto-Exodus]].

### The shape

| verses | what happens | who moves |
|---|---|---|
| **1–3** | The sons complain, Laban's face changes, **and God speaks for the first time since 28:15** | God |
| **4–13** | Jacob's case to his wives: ten changed wages, the angel, *"I am the God of Bethel"* | Jacob |
| **14–16** | **Rachel and Leah answer as one voice** — *"are we not considered as foreigners?"* | the sisters |
| **17–21** | The flight; **Rachel steals the *teraphim*** and Jacob steals Laban's heart | Rachel, Jacob |
| **22–24** | The seven-day pursuit, and **God's night warning to Laban** | God |
| **25–35** | The charge, the death sentence, the search of four tents, and the camel saddle | Laban, Rachel |
| **36–42** | **Jacob's apologia** — twenty years, the shepherd's liability, *the fear of Isaac* | Jacob |
| **43–54** | The heap, two names in two languages, the boundary oath, the sacrifice and the meal | both |
| **55** | Laban kisses his grandchildren and leaves the book | Laban |

**Three divine interventions frame it** — a command at 31:3, an angel recalled at 31:11–13, a warning
at 31:24 — **and every one of them is spoken to somebody who is about to travel.**

### 31:1–3 — the complaint, the face, and the first word from God in six years

> **1** He heard the words of Laban’s sons, saying, “Jacob has taken away all that was our father’s.
> He has obtained all this wealth from that which was our father’s.”
> **2** Jacob saw the expression on Laban’s face, and, behold, it was not toward him as before.
> **3** Yahweh said to Jacob, “Return to the land of your fathers, and to your relatives, and I will
> be with you.”

***"He has obtained all this wealth."*** ***Kol-hakkābōd hazzeh*** — literally ***all this glory***.
**The noun is *kābôd*, weight, the word that will stand over Sinai and fill the tabernacle**, and in
the mouths of Laban's sons it means livestock. **Genesis lets them use the heaviest word in the
theological vocabulary for a herd count**, and the sentence they build with it is factually right and
morally empty: the animals *were* their father's, and their father did agree to the contract.

***"The expression on Laban's face… was not toward him as before."*** ***Pənê lābān… ʾênennû ʿimmô
kitmōl šilšōm*** — *not with him as yesterday and the day before.* **The idiom is the book's standard
measure of a relationship's temperature**, and the last face Genesis reported falling belonged to
[[Cain]]: *"Cain was very angry, and the expression on his face fell"* (4:5), **also over an offering
and a brother's success.** See [[Envy]].

**The complaint and the face are exactly what happened to his father.** *"The man became great… and
the Philistines envied him"* (26:13–14), and Abimelech sent him away. **[[Jacob's Flocks]] closed on
*the man burst out exceedingly*, in the same construction Genesis used of Isaac** — and the sequel
here is the same sequel, one generation on, with the difference that this host does not send the man
away. See [[Isaac in Gerar]] and [[Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt]].

***"Yahweh said to Jacob."*** **The first divine speech to him since the stairway at Bethel** — and
between the two lie **two whole chapters, two marriages, twelve children, six years of animal
husbandry, and not one word from God to this man.** *Yahweh* and *ʾĕlōhîm* are absent from Genesis 29
entirely and are named in 30 only inside human sentences. **He is spoken to again the moment leaving
becomes dangerous**, and what he is given is a command, a destination, and a promise, in that order.

***"Return… and I will be with you."*** ***Šûb… wəʾehyeh ʿimmāk.*** **The last clause is Bethel's
own** — *"behold, I am with you, and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you again into
this land"* (28:15). **God is not making a new promise; he is calling in an old one, in the verb its
own last clause used.** And **Jacob asked for this six years ago and was refused by a man** (30:25);
he is now told to do it by God, which is the difference between an employee's request and a
departure. See [[I Will Be With You - The Promise of Presence]] and [[Land and Sojourning]].

### 31:4–13 — the case Jacob makes to his wives

> **4** Jacob sent and called Rachel and Leah to the field to his flock,
> **5** and said to them, “I see the expression on your father’s face, that it is not toward me as
> before; but the God of my father has been with me.
> **6** You know that I have served your father with all of my strength.
> **7** Your father has deceived me, and changed my wages ten times, but God didn’t allow him to hurt
> me.
> **8** If he said this, ‘The speckled will be your wages,’ then all the flock bore speckled. If he
> said this, ‘The streaked will be your wages,’ then all the flock bore streaked.
> **9** Thus God has taken away your father’s livestock, and given them to me.

***"To the field to his flock."*** **He does not call a family meeting in the house; he summons two
women out to the pasture.** The chapter's first action is a man choosing ground where his
father-in-law's household cannot overhear, **and Genesis reports the precaution without commenting on
it** — the same flat register it used for the peeled rods.

***"Your father has deceived me."*** ***Hētel bî.*** **The verb is not the *rāmâ* of 29:25** — *"why
then have you deceived me?"*, the morning after the wedding — but ***tālal***, *to trifle with, to
mock*, and this is its first appearance in Scripture. **Jacob has two words for what Laban does to
him and uses a different one each time.**

***"Changed my wages ten times."*** ***Ḥelip ʾet-maśkurtî ʿăśeret mōnîm.*** **Genesis has narrated
exactly one wage agreement in chapter 30 and no changes to it at all.** The reader is handed nine
more renegotiations that the book never showed, **in reported speech, by an interested party, in an
argument** — and then hears the same figure again at 31:41 and from Laban not at all. **The wiki
records that the ten changes are Jacob's testimony and that Genesis neither corroborates nor
contradicts them.** *"Ten times"* is idiom for *repeatedly* elsewhere (Numbers 14:22; Job 19:3).

***"God didn't allow him to hurt me."*** ***Ləhāraʿ ʿimmādî*** — **and God will use the same root to
Laban three verses later**: *"be careful that you don't speak to Jacob either good or ***bad***"*
(31:24, ***raʿ***). **Jacob asserts a protection at 31:7 that the narrator then shows being enforced
at 31:24**, which is the one point in the chapter where his account and the narrator's touch.

***"Thus God has taken away your father's livestock, and given them to me."*** ***Wayyaṣṣēl ʾĕlōhîm
ʾet-miqnēh ʾăbîkem.*** **This is the sentence [[Jacob's Flocks]] does not contain.** Nineteen verses
of poplar rods, watering-troughs and selective breeding, with no divine subject anywhere in them,
are summarised by the man who did it as a transfer God made. ***Nāṣal*** is the verb of **rescue and
plunder** — *"I have delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians"* (Exodus 18:10), and *"you will
plunder the Egyptians"* (Exodus 3:22, ***wəniṣṣaltem***). **The wiki does not choose between the two
accounts.** See [[Two Explanations for One Flock - The Rods and the Dream]].

> **10** During mating season, I lifted up my eyes, and saw in a dream, and behold, the male goats
> which leaped on the flock were streaked, speckled, and grizzled.
> **11** The angel of God said to me in the dream, ‘Jacob,’ and I said, ‘Here I am.’
> **12** He said, ‘Now lift up your eyes, and behold, all the male goats which leap on the flock are
> streaked, speckled, and grizzled, for I have seen all that Laban does to you.
> **13** I am the God of Bethel, where you anointed a pillar, where you vowed a vow to me. Now arise,
> get out from this land, and return to the land of your birth.’”

***"The angel of God said to me in the dream, 'Jacob,' and I said, 'Here I am.'"*** ***Malʾak
hāʾĕlōhîm***, and ***hinnēnî***. **The answer is Abraham's at Moriah** (22:1, 11) — **the third
*hinnēnî* in Genesis and the first from this man.** See [[The Binding of Isaac]].

***"I am the God of Bethel."*** ***ʾĀnōkî hāʾēl bêt-ʾēl.*** **God identifies himself by a place, and
he does it nowhere else in Scripture.** *I am the God of your father*, *the God of Abraham*, *God
Almighty* — those are the formulas; **this one names a hillside in Canaan and two things Jacob did on
it.** *"Where you anointed a pillar, where you vowed a vow to me"* is **the vow of 28:20–22 quoted
back to the man who made it**, and it is quoted by God, who was not offered it as a bargain but is
now treating it as one. **Jacob's vow said *"if God will be with me… then Yahweh will be my God"*;
God's answer six years later is to take the title from the place where the *if* was spoken.** See
[[Jacob's Ladder at Bethel]], [[Oaths and Vows]], and
[[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]].

**And the seam runs straight through the sentence.** *The angel of God* speaks at 31:11; by 31:13 the
speaker is saying ***I am the God of Bethel*** and ***you vowed a vow to me***. **Genesis does not
mark the transition, and this is the plainest example of the alternation in the book.** See
[[The Angel of Yahweh]].

***"For I have seen all that Laban does to you."*** ***Rāʾîtî.*** **The chapter's theology in three
words**, and it is the family's oldest one: *"You are a God who sees"* (16:13), *"God heard the voice
of the boy"* (21:17), *"God has seen my affliction"* (31:42). **The verb of seeing keeps arriving
where somebody is being used.** See [[Seeing and Being Seen]] and [[Suffering]].

### 31:14–16 — the only time the sisters speak with one voice

> **14** Rachel and Leah answered him, “Is there yet any portion or inheritance for us in our
> father’s house?
> **15** Aren’t we considered as foreigners by him? For he has sold us, and has also used up our
> money.
> **16** For all the riches which God has taken away from our father, that is ours and our
> children’s. Now then, whatever God has said to you, do.”

**Three verses, and they are the most important three verses on either woman's page.**

**Everything [[Rachel]] and [[Leah]] have said in Genesis until now has been about children, and
almost all of it has been about each other.** *Give me children, or else I will die.* *Is it a small
matter that you have taken away my husband?* *I have wrestled with my sister… and have prevailed.*
Twelve naming speeches between them, every one a move in the same rivalry. **Here they answer as one
grammatical subject, they are given no separate lines, and the subject is money.** See
[[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]].

***"Any portion or inheritance."*** ***Ḥēleq wənaḥălâ.*** **The legal pair**, and it is the language
of land tenure in Joshua and Numbers. **Two daughters ask whether their father's estate holds
anything for them and answer their own question no** — which, in a household with sons (31:1), is
simply the law of the place and not a grievance anyone could take to court. **Genesis lets them state
it as a grievance anyway.**

***"Aren't we considered as foreigners by him?"*** ***Hălôʾ nokriyyôt neḥšabnû lô.*** ***Nokrî*** is
the sharpest of the Bible's three words for an outsider — not the *gēr* who resides among you, whom
the law protects, but **the alien with no claim.** **Two Aramean women use it of their own father**,
and the same root will be the one Joseph uses when he *made himself strange* to his brothers (42:7).

***"He has sold us, and has also used up our money."*** ***Məkārānû wayyōʾkal gam-ʾākôl
ʾet-kaspēnû*** — literally ***he has eaten, yes eaten, our silver.*** **The charge is precise and it
is about the *mōhar*.** A bride-price in this world passed to the bride's father, and some portion of
it was expected to come back to the daughter as her security. **Jacob had no silver and paid in
fourteen years of labour** (29:18, 27) — **so the daughters are saying that their own bride-price was
consumed as a working asset, which is exactly what the narrative shows happening.** *"He has sold
us"* — ***mākar***, the ordinary verb for selling a slave, **and the same verb Joseph's brothers will
use of him at 37:28.** See [[Marriage]], [[Slavery and Servitude]], and [[Wealth and Possessions]].

***"All the riches which God has taken away from our father, that is ours and our children's."***
**They adopt their husband's account of the flocks without a pause** — *the riches which God has
taken away*, the same *nāṣal* he used at 31:9 — **and then reassign the property from him to
themselves and their sons.** *"Now then, whatever God has said to you, do"* is the last thing either
of them says in the chapter, and **it is a ruling, not a submission**: they have just established
that the herd is their inheritance and that leaving with it is not theft.

**The verdict is theirs and the book does not qualify it.** **The only two witnesses in Genesis who
have lived in Laban's house all their lives are asked what he is, and they say *he sold us and ate
the money*.** **It is the harshest sentence anyone passes on him**, it comes from his daughters, and
the narrator neither seconds it nor disputes it.

### 31:17–21 — the flight, and the two thefts

> **17** Then Jacob rose up, and set his sons and his wives on the camels,
> **18** and he took away all his livestock, and all his possessions which he had gathered, including
> the livestock which he had gained in Paddan Aram, to go to Isaac his father, to the land of Canaan.
> **19** Now Laban had gone to shear his sheep: and Rachel stole the teraphim that were her father’s.
> **20** Jacob deceived Laban the Syrian, in that he didn’t tell him that he was running away.
> **21** So he fled with all that he had. He rose up, passed over the River, and set his face toward
> the mountain of Gilead.

***"To go to Isaac his father, to the land of Canaan."*** **[[Isaac]] has not been named since 28:5
and is still alive**; he will not die until 35:29, and Jacob will not reach him for two more chapters
of trouble. **The destination is stated as a father, and the land is the apposition.** See [[Canaan]].

***"Laban had gone to shear his sheep."*** **Sheep-shearing in the Bible is a festival**, several
days long, held away from the house, with drinking — and **it is the setting of three later scenes in
which somebody is caught out while the shearing goes on**: Judah at Timnah (38:12), Nabal's feast
while David waits (1 Samuel 25:2–11), and Absalom killing Amnon at his own shearing (2 Samuel 13:23–29).
**Genesis gives the detail as a timetable**: three days' journey away (30:36) and busy, **which is
why nobody notices for three days** (31:22).

***"Rachel stole the teraphim that were her father's."*** ***Wattignōb rāḥēl ʾet-hattərāpîm.*** **The
first *teraphim* in the Bible**, and Genesis does not say what they are, what they were for, or why
she took them. **They recur across Scripture as household gods and divinatory objects** — Micah's
shrine (Judges 17:5; 18:14–20), the image Michal puts in David's bed (1 Samuel 19:13, 16), the king
of Babylon consulting them at the crossroads (Ezekiel 21:21), and Hosea's Israel left *"without
ephod or teraphim"* (Hosea 3:4). **1 Samuel 15:23 puts them in a line with divination and calls the
whole business iniquity.**

**The standing explanations are three and Genesis supports none of them explicitly**: that they were
worth something as objects; that possession carried a claim on the estate (the reading built on the
Nuzi tablets, and the one most often repeated); or that they were the household's protective gods and
she wanted the protection, or wanted her father not to have it. **The wiki records all three as
proposals and adopts none**, and notes what the text does supply: **the family that keeps them is the
one whose head has just told us he divines** (30:27), **and Rachel is the one who bought mandrakes**
(30:14). See [[The Teraphim - Household Gods in Genesis and After]], [[Idolatry]], and
[[Divination in Genesis - Laban's Nahash and Joseph's Cup]].

***"Jacob deceived Laban the Syrian."*** ***Wayyignōb yaʿăqōb ʾet-lēb lābān*** — ***Jacob stole the
heart of Laban the Aramean.*** **The WEB renders the idiom; the verse's verb is the same one used of
his wife two verses earlier.** **Genesis has just put *stole* twice in three verses, with a wife and a
husband as its two subjects and a father as both objects** — and *lēb*, the heart, is the seat of
knowing rather than of feeling, so the idiom means *he kept him in the dark*. **The chapter will use
the root five more times.**

***"Laban the Syrian."*** ***Hāʾărammî.*** **The fourth of four times Genesis attaches the ethnic
label to him** (25:20; 28:5; 31:20, 24), **and both of this chapter's uses sit next to a deception or
a divine warning.** Deuteronomy 26:5 will make the same word Israel's confession of these very years:
*"A Syrian ready to perish was my father."* See [[The Nations]] and [[Aram Naharaim]].

***"Passed over the River, and set his face toward the mountain of Gilead."*** ***Hannāhār*** with no
qualifier is **the Euphrates**, and crossing it is what the family did in the other direction under
Terah. **The geography is impossible as a week's march** — the Euphrates to Gilead is some four
hundred miles — and **Genesis reports it as a week anyway** (31:22–23). The wiki notes the distance
and does not smooth it. See [[The Euphrates]] and [[Gilead]].

### 31:22–24 — the pursuit, and a warning delivered to the wrong man

> **22** Laban was told on the third day that Jacob had fled.
> **23** He took his relatives with him, and pursued him seven days’ journey. He overtook him in the
> mountain of Gilead.
> **24** God came to Laban, the Syrian, in a dream of the night, and said to him, “Be careful that
> you don’t speak to Jacob either good or bad.”

***"Pursued him."*** ***Wayyirdōp.*** **The verb of Pharaoh's chariots** (Exodus 14:8–9). **A master
who refused to release a servant now chases him across a desert with an armed household**, and the
next thing that happens is that God stops him in the night. See
[[Served, Released, Pursued - The Laban Years as Proto-Exodus]].

***"God came to Laban… in a dream of the night."*** **The second and last time in Genesis that God
appears to a foreigner in order to protect this family**, and the parallel to the first is close
enough to be structural: **[[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] is warned in a night dream not to touch
Abraham's wife** (20:3), **Laban is warned in a night dream not to touch Abraham's grandson**, and
**both men obey immediately, both complain afterwards that they were not told, and both walk away
with a covenant.** See [[Warned in a Dream - Abimelech and Laban]].

***"Either good or bad."*** ***Miṭṭôb ʿad-rāʿ.*** **The idiom is Laban's own**, from the first scene
he ever appeared in: *"we can't speak to you bad or good"* (24:50), his answer when Abraham's servant
laid the case out. **His first recorded position and his last are the same sentence**, and the second
time it is imposed on him from outside. He repeats it back verbatim at 31:29. See [[Laban]].

### 31:25–35 — the charge, the sentence, and the camel saddle

> **26** Laban said to Jacob, “What have you done, that you have deceived me, and carried away my
> daughters like captives of the sword?
> **27** Why did you flee secretly, and deceive me, and didn’t tell me, that I might have sent you
> away with mirth and with songs, with tambourine and with harp;
> **28** and didn’t allow me to kiss my sons and my daughters? Now have you done foolishly.
> **29** It is in the power of my hand to hurt you, but the God of your father spoke to me last night,
> saying, ‘Be careful that you don’t speak to Jacob either good or bad.’
> **30** Now, you want to be gone, because you greatly longed for your father’s house, but why have
> you stolen my gods?”

***"What have you done?"*** ***Meh ʿāśîtā.*** **The formula three foreign kings used on this family**
— Pharaoh to Abram (12:18), Abimelech to Abraham (20:9), Abimelech to Isaac (26:10) — **and the one
Jacob himself used on Laban after the wedding** (29:25). **It is the fifth time in Genesis that a man
of this house is asked it, and the fourth time by an outsider who is in the right.**

***"Carried away my daughters like captives of the sword."*** ***Kišbuyôt ḥāreb.*** **The complaint of
a man whose daughters have just told the reader he sold them.** **Genesis prints both sentences
eleven verses apart and reconciles neither**, and the wiki flags the contradiction rather than
resolving it: **at 31:15 the daughters say *he sold us*; at 31:26 the father says *you took them like
prisoners of war*.** Both are speeches; the narrator supports neither.

***"That I might have sent you away with mirth and with songs, with tambourine and with harp."***
***Wāʾăšallēḥăkā.*** **The verb is *šālaḥ*, the piel — the exact verb Jacob used at 30:25 when he
asked to be released and was refused.** **Laban is now claiming he would have performed the release
he declined to grant**, and he is claiming it with an orchestra. **The nearest thing in Scripture to
what he describes is Miriam with a tambourine on the far side of the sea** (Exodus 15:20). **Genesis
does not comment, and the reader has 30:27 in front of them.**

***"It is in the power of my hand to hurt you, but the God of your father spoke to me last night."***
***Yeš-lʾēl yādî.*** **He states the threat and cancels it in one sentence**, which is the most honest
thing he says in the chapter: **he would have, and he was stopped.** **He also gets the title right** —
*the God of your father* — and he does not claim him as his own.

***"Why have you stolen my gods?"*** ***Lāmmâ gānabtā ʾet-ʾĕlōhāy.*** **The plural is grammatical and
the possessive is the point.** **A man who has twice named Yahweh correctly** — *"the thing proceeds
from Yahweh"* (24:50), *"Yahweh has blessed me for your sake"* (30:27) — **calls a set of portable
figures *my gods*, and Genesis prints the sentence without a syllable of comment.** **The book's
clearest picture of what the branch that stayed in Mesopotamia actually held is this line and the
oath at 31:53.** See [[Idolatry]] and [[The Names of God]].

> **31** Jacob answered Laban, “Because I was afraid, for I said, ‘Lest you should take your daughters
> from me by force.’
> **32** Anyone you find your gods with shall not live. Before our relatives, discern what is yours
> with me, and take it.” For Jacob didn’t know that Rachel had stolen them.

***"Because I was afraid."*** ***Kî yārēʾtî.*** **The one true and complete answer he gives in the
chapter**, and it answers the first question (*why did you flee?*) and not the second (*where are my
gods?*). **The man who has manoeuvred through five pericopes says plainly that he ran because he was
frightened.**

***"Anyone you find your gods with shall not live."*** **A death sentence pronounced by a husband over
his own wife without knowing it**, and Genesis flags the irony itself in the next clause: ***"for
Jacob didn't know that Rachel had stolen them."*** **The narrator almost never explains a character's
ignorance and does it here**, which is the surest sign in the chapter that the sentence is meant to
be held onto.

**Rachel dies in childbirth four chapters later** (35:16–19), **and the reading that connects the two
— an oath uttered in ignorance and afterwards fulfilled — is ancient and is not the text's.**
Genesis draws no line between 31:32 and 35:18. **The wiki records the reading as a reading**, notes
that the book has one other case of a rash vow (Judges 11:30–39, outside Genesis), and lets both
stand. See [[Oaths and Vows]] and [[Rachel]].

> **33** Laban went into Jacob’s tent, into Leah’s tent, and into the tent of the two female servants;
> but he didn’t find them. He went out of Leah’s tent, and entered into Rachel’s tent.
> **34** Now Rachel had taken the teraphim, put them in the camel’s saddle, and sat on them. Laban
> felt around all the tent, but didn’t find them.
> **35** She said to her father, “Don’t let my lord be angry that I can’t rise up before you; for I’m
> having my period.” He searched, but didn’t find the teraphim.

**Four tents in the order of the household's own hierarchy** — Jacob, [[Leah]], the two slaves
([[Zilpah]] and [[Bilhah]], named here only as *the two female servants*), and **Rachel last**, which
is where the reader knows the gods are. **The search is narrated as slapstick and the verb is
*mūš*, to grope**: he *felt around all the tent*, twice.

***"I'm having my period."*** ***Kî-derek nāšîm lî*** — *for the way of women is upon me.* **She is
sitting on her father's gods and telling him she cannot get up**, and the excuse is one no man in
that world would test. **Leviticus 15:19–23 will make everything she sits on unclean**, which — if the
law is read back into the scene, and Genesis gives no sign that it should be — **puts the household
gods under a menstruating woman for the length of a search.** **The wiki states the resonance and
declines to make it the point**, because the text's own point is simpler: **they were not found.**

**And they are never mentioned again in the chapter.** No confession, no discovery, no return.
[[Rachel]] keeps them, and the next time foreign gods surface in this household Jacob buries them
under an oak at Shechem (35:2–4) without the narrator saying whose they were.

### 31:36–42 — the longest speech Jacob makes in Genesis

> **36** Jacob was angry, and argued with Laban. Jacob answered Laban, “What is my trespass? What is
> my sin, that you have hotly pursued me?
> **37** Now that you have felt around in all my stuff, what have you found of all your household
> stuff? Set it here before my relatives and your relatives, that they may judge between us two.
> **38** “These twenty years I have been with you. Your ewes and your female goats have not cast their
> young, and I haven’t eaten the rams of your flocks.
> **39** That which was torn of animals, I didn’t bring to you. I bore its loss. Of my hand you
> required it, whether stolen by day or stolen by night.
> **40** This was my situation: in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my
> sleep fled from my eyes.
> **41** These twenty years I have been in your house. I served you fourteen years for your two
> daughters, and six years for your flock, and you have changed my wages ten times.
> **42** Unless the God of my father, the God of Abraham, and the fear of Isaac, had been with me,
> surely now you would have sent me away empty. God has seen my affliction and the labor of my hands,
> and rebuked you last night.”

**He has been silent or transactional for six chapters, and here he is angry.** ***Wayyiḥar ləyaʿăqōb
wayyāreb*** — *he burned, and he brought a suit.* ***Rîb*** is **the legal word for a lawsuit**, and
the prophets will build whole oracles on it (Micah 6:1–2; Hosea 4:1). **He is not shouting; he is
laying a charge, in front of witnesses he has just asked to be empanelled** (31:37).

***"What is my trespass? What is my sin?"*** ***Pišʿî… ḥaṭṭāʾtî.*** **The two nouns of the
sacrificial vocabulary, from a man in a field with no altar in sight**, and **the search that just
failed is his evidence**: nothing was found, so there is nothing to answer.

***"That which was torn of animals, I didn't bring to you. I bore its loss."*** ***Ṭərēpâ lōʾ-hēbēʾtî
ʾēlêkā, ʾānōkî ʾăḥaṭṭennâ.*** **This is the most legally specific sentence in Genesis, and it is a
claim about a liability that the Torah will later refuse to impose.**

**The standing rule of the ancient Near East, and of Exodus 22:10–13, is that a hired keeper is not
liable for an animal killed by a predator.** He produces the carcass as evidence — *"let him bring
it as evidence; he shall not make good that which was torn"* (Exodus 22:13) — and the loss falls on
the owner. **Jacob says he never brought the evidence and absorbed the loss himself**, and adds that
theft by day and by night was charged to him too, **where the same law makes the keeper liable only
for a theft he could have prevented** (Exodus 22:12). **Twenty years of a shepherd paying for
conditions the law would go on to say were not his fault**, stated as one clause in the middle of a
speech. See [[The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22]] and [[Work]].

***"In the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep fled from my eyes."***
***Ḥōreb… qeraḥ.*** **The only description of physical hardship any patriarch gives of himself**,
and it is about weather. **Abraham is never cold in Genesis and Isaac is never tired.**

***"Twenty years… fourteen years for your two daughters, and six years for your flock."*** **The
arithmetic of the whole Laban cycle, given here for the first and only time**, and it is what dates
[[Jacob's Flocks]] to six years. **The number twenty is repeated at 31:38 and 31:41 in the same
words**, framing the complaint on both sides.

***"The God of my father, the God of Abraham, and the fear of Isaac."*** ***Ûpaḥad yiṣḥāq.*** **A
divine title that occurs twice in Scripture and both times in this chapter** (31:42, 53). **What it
means is genuinely uncertain and the wiki says so.** The ordinary sense of *paḥad* is **dread** — *the
Dread of Isaac*, the God his father feared — which fits [[The Fear of God]] as Genesis uses it
elsewhere (22:12; 42:18). **A long-standing alternative reads it against Arabic *faḫd*, thigh, hence
*clan* or *kinsman*, giving *the Kinsman of Isaac***; it is a reconstruction, not a gloss the text
gives, **and the wiki records it as a proposal.** **What is not in doubt is the omission**: he names
Abraham and Isaac and **not Nahor**, three verses before Laban names all three. See
[[The Names of God]].

***"You would have sent me away empty."*** ***Rêqām šillaḥtānî.*** ***Šālaḥ*** again, the release
verb, and ***rêqām***, *empty-handed*. **Exodus 3:21 is the promise this sentence is the negative
of**: *"when you go, you shall not go empty."* **A man leaves twenty years of forced service in a
foreign house with flocks, slaves, and camels, and says that but for God he would have left with
nothing** — **which is the exodus stated in advance, by a private person, about a private
employment.** See [[Served, Released, Pursued - The Laban Years as Proto-Exodus]].

***"God has seen my affliction and the labor of my hands."*** ***ʿOnyî wîgîaʿ kappay.*** ***ʿŎnî***
is **the affliction of Exodus 3:7** — *"I have surely seen the affliction of my people"* — and it is
the noun [[Leah]] used at [[Reuben]]'s birth (29:32) and [[Hagar]] was addressed with in the desert
(16:11). **The word Genesis reserves for people with no recourse.** See [[Suffering]].

### 31:43–54 — the heap, the two languages, and the oath

> **43** Laban answered Jacob, “The daughters are my daughters, the children are my children, the
> flocks are my flocks, and all that you see is mine: and what can I do today to these my daughters,
> or to their children whom they have borne?
> **44** Now come, let us make a covenant, you and I; and let it be for a witness between me and you.”

***"The daughters are my daughters, the children are my children, the flocks are my flocks."***
**Four possessive claims and then a surrender**: *and what can I do today?* **He concedes nothing in
principle and everything in fact**, which is precisely the structure of *"we can't speak to you bad
or good"* (24:50). **He never once admits he was wrong, and he stops.**

***"Let us make a covenant."*** ***Nikrətâ bərît.*** **The fourth *bərît* in Genesis** — Noah's (9:9),
Abram's (15:18), circumcision (17:2), and the treaty at Beersheba (21:27) — **and the first between two
men who have wronged each other.** See [[Covenant]] and [[The Treaty at Beersheba]].

> **45** Jacob took a stone, and set it up for a pillar.
> **46** Jacob said to his relatives, “Gather stones.” They took stones, and made a heap. They ate
> there by the heap.
> **47** Laban called it Jegar Sahadutha, but Jacob called it Galeed.

***"Set it up for a pillar."*** ***Maṣṣēbâ.*** **His second of four** (28:18; 31:45; 35:14; 35:20),
**and the only one that is not for God.** At Bethel a pillar marked a theophany and was anointed with
oil; **here a pillar marks a border with a man**, and it is set up in the same chapter that quoted the
Bethel pillar back to him (31:13). See
[[The Pillar Jacob Raised and the Pillars the Law Forbids]].

***"Jegar Sahadutha… Galeed."*** ***Yəgar śāhădûtāʾ*** and ***galʿēd***. **The two names mean the
same thing — *heap of witness* — in two languages, and the first of them is Aramaic.** **These are the
only Aramaic words in the Torah**, and Genesis supplies them without translating either, **letting the
reader see that the two branches of one family no longer speak the same language.** **The book has
been calling Laban *the Aramean* for four chapters and here it finally lets him talk like one.** See
[[Jegar Sahadutha - The Only Aramaic in the Torah]] and [[Language and Speech]].

**And *galʿēd* is one consonant from *gilʿād*, Gilead**, the region they are standing in. **Genesis
gives the place its etiology in a pun and moves on.** See [[Galeed]] and [[Gilead]].

> **48** Laban said, “This heap is witness between me and you today.” Therefore it was named Galeed
> **49** and Mizpah, for he said, “Yahweh watch between me and you, when we are absent one from
> another.
> **50** If you afflict my daughters, or if you take wives besides my daughters, no man is with us;
> behold, God is witness between me and you.”

***"And Mizpah."*** ***Miṣpâ***, from ***ṣāpâ***, *to watch* — and the etiology is in the next clause:
*"Yahweh ***watch*** between me and you"*, ***yiṣep YHWH***. **A third name for one heap.**

**The sentence is engraved on jewellery and read at partings, and it is a surveillance clause.**
*"Yahweh watch between me and you, when we are absent one from another"* is not a blessing; **it is
two men who do not trust each other asking God to keep an eye on the other one while they are out of
sight**, and 31:50 and 31:52 say what he is watching for. **The wiki records the popular reading
because it is nearly universal, and records that the text will not bear it.** See
[[The Mizpah Benediction - Genesis 31.49 and How It Is Read]].

***"If you afflict my daughters, or if you take wives besides my daughters."*** ***ʾIm-təʿanneh
ʾet-bənōtay*** — **the *ʿānâ* of 31:42's *ʿŏnî***, used one verse after Jacob said God had seen his
own. **Laban's last act as a father is to secure a protection for his daughters that no law of the
period gave them**, and **it is the first prohibition of an additional wife anywhere in Scripture** —
extracted by a private treaty, from the man who already has four women in his tents. **Genesis does
not remark on the incongruity.** See [[Marriage]].

> **51** Laban said to Jacob, “See this heap, and see the pillar, which I have set between me and you.
> **52** May this heap be a witness, and the pillar be a witness, that I will not pass over this heap
> to you, and that you will not pass over this heap and this pillar to me, for harm.
> **53** The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge between us.” Then
> Jacob swore by the fear of his father, Isaac.
> **54** Jacob offered a sacrifice in the mountain, and called his relatives to eat bread. They ate
> bread, and stayed all night in the mountain.

***"Which I have set between me and you."*** **Jacob set the pillar at 31:45; Laban says *I have
set*.** **The chapter's last small annexation**, and nobody corrects him.

***"A witness."*** ***ʿĒd***, five times in eight verses (31:44, 48, 50, 52 twice). **The heap is the
witness because there is no third party** — no king, no court, no priest. **Two men on a mountain make
a border out of a pile of rocks and appoint the rocks as the judiciary.** See
[[Oaths and Vows]] and [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

***"The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge between us."***
**The most theologically loaded sentence Laban speaks**, and **the verb *yišpəṭû* is plural in the
Hebrew** — *let them judge* — where a single God would take a singular. **The Samaritan text and the
Septuagint read it singular; the Masoretic Text does not.** **The wiki reports the plural and does not
resolve it**: on one reading Laban is invoking two family gods as a pair, **which would make this the
Bible's one glimpse of what the branch that stayed behind actually worshipped**; on another the
plural is a grammatical agreement with the compound subject and means nothing theological. See
[[Nahor (brother of Abram)]] and [[The Names of God]].

***"Then Jacob swore by the fear of his father, Isaac."*** **He does not swear by the formula Laban
just offered.** Laban named Abraham and Nahor together, **which would put the two lines' gods on the
same footing**; Jacob answers with a title that names **only his father** and **excludes Nahor
entirely.** **It is the sharpest thing anyone does in the covenant scene, and neither man remarks on
it.** See [[The Fear of God]].

***"Jacob offered a sacrifice."*** ***Wayyizbaḥ yaʿăqōb zebaḥ.*** **The first *zebaḥ* in
Scripture** — the noun and the verb of the shared, eaten sacrifice, as against the *ʿōlâ* that goes
up whole (8:20; 22:13). **The rite here is a meal**: *he called his relatives to eat bread.* **A
covenant in this world was ratified by eating together**, and this is the first time Genesis shows it
being done. **His grandfather's covenants were cut with halved animals and fire** (15:9–17); **this
one is dinner.** See [[Sacrifice and Offering]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

### 31:55 — the last thing Laban does

> **55** Early in the morning, Laban rose up, and kissed his sons and his daughters, and blessed them.
> Laban departed and returned to his place.

**He kisses and blesses, and the chapter closes on the two verbs it opened him with a generation
earlier**: he *ran*, *embraced*, and *kissed* Abraham's servant at the spring (24:29–31) and Jacob at
the door (29:13). **The man Genesis introduced by what he noticed goes out doing the only thing he has
ever done without an angle**, and **he blesses grandchildren he has just spent seven days chasing.**

**He never appears again.** Deuteronomy 26:5 keeps him in Israel's creed as *"a Syrian ready to
perish"*, which is the descendant's word for the ancestor and not for him.

**In the Hebrew Bible this verse is 32:1**, so the Hebrew chapter break falls after the pursuit and
before the departure, while the English break falls before the kiss. **The wiki follows the English
versification the WEB uses and notes the difference.**

### What the pericope is doing

**It is the chapter in which the promise at Bethel is kept, and every human being in it is arguing
about property.**

*I am with you and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you again into this land* (28:15).
**All three clauses are discharged here** — *"I will be with you"* is repeated at 31:3, the keeping is
what 31:24 does, and the return is what 31:17–21 begins — **and the discharge is narrated as a labour
dispute.** God speaks three times and each time it is to move somebody or stop somebody. **Everything
else is wages, herds, dowry, household gods, and a boundary.**

**And it is the chapter that finally names what the twenty years were.** [[Jacob's Flocks]] gave the
six years as husbandry with no divine subject; here the same six years are a dream, and the fourteen
before them are *service*, and the whole thing is ***affliction*** (***ʿŏnî***, 31:42) — **the word
Exodus will use of Egypt.** **The wiki does not treat Jacob's account as the narrator's**, and notes
that the narrator supplies one corroboration and no others: **God did in fact stop Laban** (31:24).

**Nobody repents and nobody is punished.** Rachel keeps the gods; Laban never admits the wages;
Jacob never learns what his wife did. **What ends the quarrel is a pile of stones with a different
name in each man's language**, an oath sworn on two different titles for God, and a meal — **and then
the two branches of Terah's family separate for good.** **After this chapter the Bible's Arameans are
foreigners**, and Israel's confession of them is a single line about a wandering father.

## People

- [[Jacob]] — **he is angry for the second time in his life and it is the first time it is
  justified.** He is commanded to leave, argues his case to his wives in a field, credits the flocks
  to God where the narrator credited them to rods, flees without notice, *steals Laban's heart*,
  pronounces death over an unknown thief who is his own wife, and then delivers **the longest speech
  he makes in Genesis** — a shepherd's apologia with a legal term in every second clause. **He swears
  by a divine title that excludes his uncle's side of the family**, raises his second pillar, and
  offers the Bible's first *zebaḥ*. **He asks for nothing and gets everything**, and he never finds
  out about the *teraphim*.
- [[Laban]] — **he loses, concedes nothing, and gets the last protection clause in the treaty.** He is
  stopped by God in a dream, admits in one sentence that he had the power to harm and was forbidden,
  calls a set of figurines *my gods*, searches four tents on his hands and knees, and is defeated by
  a daughter sitting on a camel saddle. **He proposes the covenant himself**, invokes *the God of
  Abraham and the God of Nahor* in a plural verb, secures his daughters against a second wife, and
  **leaves the book kissing grandchildren.** Genesis never records him apologising and never records
  him being condemned.
- [[Rachel]] — **the one theft in the chapter that is real, and it is hers.** She takes her father's
  household gods, says nothing about it to her husband, sits on them through a search, and stops her
  father with a sentence about her body. **She also speaks, with her sister and for the first time
  about anything but children**, the harshest verdict anyone passes on Laban. **Her husband's death
  sentence stands over her unknowingly**, and Genesis marks his ignorance in a clause of its own.
- [[Leah]] — **she is given a voice in this chapter and it is joint.** *"Is there yet any portion or
  inheritance for us in our father's house? … he has sold us."* **It is the only thing she says in
  Genesis that is not about a child or a husband's affection**, and she says it in the same breath as
  the sister she spent the previous pericope counting against. **Her tent is searched third of four**,
  and she is not named again in the chapter.
- [[God]] — **he acts three times and speaks three times, and every intervention is about movement.**
  He commands the return with Bethel's own clause (31:3); he is recalled inside Jacob's speech as *the
  angel of God* and then as *the God of Bethel* (31:11–13); and **he stops a pursuing Aramean in a
  night dream** (31:24) — the second and last time in Genesis he appears to a foreigner to protect
  this family. **He is invoked by four different titles in the chapter** — *the God of my father*,
  *the God of Abraham*, *the fear of Isaac*, *the God of Nahor* — **and Laban calls a rival set of
  objects by the same noun.**
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — **the singular figure returns at 31:11–13**, as *malʾak hāʾĕlōhîm*, and
  says *"I am the God of Bethel… where you vowed a vow to me."* **The alternation between messenger
  and sender is nowhere plainer in Genesis**, and it happens inside reported speech.
- [[Isaac]] — **named as the destination** (31:18) and **as a divine title** (31:42, 53). He is alive,
  he is not reached in this chapter, and *the fear of Isaac* is the only name for God in Scripture
  built on a patriarch's fear rather than his covenant.
- [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]] — **named in an oath by his own descendant** (31:53), four generations
  after he stayed behind. **Laban puts *the God of Nahor* beside *the God of Abraham* and Jacob
  declines to swear by the pair.**
- [[Zilpah]] and [[Bilhah]] — **present as *"the tent of the two female servants"*** (31:33) and not
  named. Their tent is searched and they say nothing; **it is the last time either is mentioned in
  Genesis until 35:22 and 37:2.**
- **Laban's sons** — **their complaint opens the chapter** (31:1) and they do not appear again. They
  remain unnamed and without a page; they are carried on [[Laban]].

## Places

- [[Gilead]] — **the highland east of the Jordan**, and the chapter's destination and stage. Jacob
  *"set his face toward the mountain of Gilead"* (31:21), Laban overtakes him there (31:23), both
  camp on it (31:25), and the covenant meal is eaten on it (31:54). **Its name is given an etiology
  in the pun of 31:47–48** — *galʿēd*, *heap of witness*.
- [[Galeed]] — **the heap itself**, which acquires three names in five verses: *Jegar Sahadutha* in
  Laban's Aramaic, *Galeed* in Jacob's Hebrew, and *Mizpah*, *watchpost*, in the clause that explains
  it. **It is a border marker, a witness, and the site of the meal.**
- [[The Euphrates]] — *"he rose up, passed over the River"* (31:21). **The definite noun with no
  qualifier is the Euphrates**, and crossing it westward reverses Terah's journey. **The distance from
  it to Gilead cannot be walked in the week the chapter allows**, and the wiki notes the difficulty
  without resolving it.
- [[Aram Naharaim]] — **Paddan Aram**, named at 31:18 as the place the livestock was gained, and left
  for good in this pericope. **After 31:55 nobody in the covenant line goes back.**
- [[Canaan]] — the stated destination (31:18), reached two pericopes later.
- [[Bethel]] — **not visited, but named by God as his own title** (31:13). **It is the only place in
  Scripture used this way.**

## Themes

- [[Deception]] — **the chapter is built out of the root *gānab*, seven times**, with a wife, a
  husband, and an accusation all using it. **Nobody is caught and nobody confesses.**
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the Bethel vow quoted back by God (31:13); a death sentence sworn in ignorance
  (31:32); a boundary oath sworn on two incompatible titles (31:53); **and a heap of stones appointed
  as the witness because there is no court.**
- [[Covenant]] — **the first *bərît* in Genesis between two men who have wronged each other**, with a
  witness, a boundary, a protection clause for the daughters, a sacrifice, and a shared meal.
- [[Idolatry]] — **the Bible's first *teraphim***, stolen by a matriarch, hidden under a saddle, never
  recovered — and a man who has twice named Yahweh correctly calling them *my gods*.
- [[Work]] — **the fullest account of a labour contract in Scripture**, given as a complaint: twenty
  years, fourteen for two wives and six for a flock, wages changed ten times, and a liability for
  predation and theft that the law of Exodus 22 will not impose.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — *"all this **glory**"* (31:1), *kābôd*, used of livestock by two men who
  want it back; and the daughters' counter-claim that **the herd is their consumed dowry**.
- [[Marriage]] — the *mōhar* eaten (31:15), and **the first prohibition of an additional wife in
  Scripture** (31:50), extracted by treaty from a man with four women in his tents.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — ***mākar***, *he sold us*, spoken by two free daughters of the household
  about their own father, in the verb used of [[Joseph]] at 37:28.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — *"return to the land of your fathers"* (31:3), *"the land of your birth"*
  (31:13): **the return command that answers the departure command of 28:2**, and the border that
  fixes the family on one side of it.
- [[The Names of God]] — **four titles in one chapter**: *the God of my father*, *the God of Abraham*,
  ***the fear of Isaac*** (found only here), and *the God of Nahor*. **And *I am the God of Bethel*,
  the only place-name title God gives himself in Scripture.**
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — **two dreams, one recalled and one narrated**, and the second is given to
  a foreigner. **Neither is interpreted; both are instructions.**
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"I have seen all that Laban does to you"* (31:12); *"God has seen my
  affliction"* (31:42). **The chapter's theology is a verb of sight, twice, and both times about
  someone being used.**
- [[Suffering]] — ***ʿŏnî***, affliction (31:42), the noun of Hagar in the desert and of Israel in
  Egypt, applied by a man to twenty years of employment.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — **a lawsuit** (*rîb*, 31:36), witnesses empanelled, a treaty, and a
  meal: **the fullest peacemaking procedure in Genesis**, and it ends with two men agreeing never to
  cross a line.
- [[The Nations]] — **Laban speaks Aramaic on the page** (31:47), and the family that stayed in
  Mesopotamia is here shown to have its own language and its own gods.
- [[Language and Speech]] — **the only Aramaic in the Torah**, given untranslated beside its Hebrew
  equivalent.
- [[The Fear of God]] — ***paḥad yiṣḥāq***, and **Jacob's refusal to swear by anything else.**
- [[Envy]] — the complaint of Laban's sons and the changed face (31:1–2), **the exact sequel that
  followed Isaac's enrichment at 26:13–14.**
- [[Names and Naming]] — **one heap, three names, two languages**, each with its etiology printed.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — **the first *zebaḥ* in the Bible** (31:54), and the first covenant meal.

## Notes

- **On the boundary, which was used as drawn.** The plan's box read *Jacob's Flight from Laban —
  Genesis 31:1–55*, and it needed no revision. **31:1 opens on new speakers** (Laban's sons) and a new
  situation, and **31:55 closes on Laban leaving the narrative permanently**, with 32:1 opening on
  angels and a new place-name. **A split at 31:24 or 31:36 was considered and rejected**: the pursuit,
  the search, the apologia, and the treaty are one continuous scene on one mountain over two days, and
  the *gānab* chain runs across all of them.
- **On the length.** At fifty-five verses this is **the longest single pericope ingested so far**, and
  it was kept whole because dividing it would separate the theft from the search and the complaint
  from the covenant. **The next boxes were left as drawn.**
- **On the title.** *Jacob's Flight from Laban* was kept from the plan. **Alternatives considered and
  rejected**: *The Covenant at Mizpah* (names only the last twelve verses, and the popular association
  of *Mizpah* would mislead), *Laban Overtakes Jacob* (puts the pursuer in the subject position for a
  chapter he loses), and *The Stolen Gods* (names the sub-plot the text never resolves). **No other
  book has a passage page by this title.**
- **On versification, which differs between the Hebrew and English texts.** **Genesis 31:55 in the
  English tradition is Genesis 32:1 in the Hebrew Bible**, so the Hebrew chapter break falls before
  Laban's farewell and the English one after it. **The wiki follows the WEB's English versification
  throughout** and records the difference here because it changes where this pericope ends by one
  verse.
- **On the ten changed wages, which are testimony and not narration.** Genesis 30 shows **one** wage
  agreement and **no** changes to it. The ten changes appear twice (31:7, 41), **both times in Jacob's
  mouth, in an argument**, and Laban never confirms or denies them. **The page reports them as his
  claim.** *Ten times* is also a standing idiom for *repeatedly*.
- **On the two accounts of the flocks, which this pericope supplies the second half of.** 31:9–12
  credits the increase to God and an angel with no mention of rods; [[Jacob's Flocks]] describes rods
  and selective breeding with no mention of God. **The wiki continues to hold both without
  harmonising**, and the connection page created for it in the previous pass now has both halves
  ingested. See [[Two Explanations for One Flock - The Rods and the Dream]].
- **On the contradiction between 31:15 and 31:26, which is flagged rather than resolved.** The
  daughters say *"he has sold us, and has also used up our money"*; eleven verses later their father
  says Jacob *"carried away my daughters like captives of the sword."* **Both are direct speech, both
  are self-interested, and the narrator supports neither.** The page states both and notes that the
  daughters are the only lifelong witnesses to Laban's household the book ever puts on the record.
- **On *paḥad yiṣḥāq*, which is left undecided.** *The fear of Isaac* (31:42, 53) occurs nowhere else
  in Scripture. **The natural reading is *dread*** — the God his father feared. **The alternative,
  built on Arabic *faḫd* (thigh, hence clan), gives *the Kinsman of Isaac***; the page records it as a
  proposal and not as a gloss the text supplies. **What is certain and is stated is the omission of
  Nahor from Jacob's oath**, three verses after Laban included him.
- **On the plural verb at 31:53.** *"The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their
  father, judge between us"* has ***yišpəṭû***, a **plural** verb, in the Masoretic Text; the
  Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint read a singular. **The page reports the plural, gives both
  standing explanations — two gods, or grammatical agreement with a compound subject — and adopts
  neither.**
- **On Rachel's death, which is not connected to 31:32 here.** The reading that Jacob's *"anyone you
  find your gods with shall not live"* is fulfilled at 35:16–19 is old and widespread. **Genesis draws
  no line between the two verses**, and the page records the reading as a reading. The passage will be
  revisited when Genesis 35 is ingested.
- **On the *teraphim*, whose purpose is not decided.** Three explanations are standing — value,
  inheritance claim (the Nuzi reading), and protection — and **Genesis gives no motive for Rachel at
  all.** The page lists all three, adopts none, and notes that the text supplies one relevant fact:
  this household divines (30:27).
- **On the geography, which does not work as a week.** 31:21–23 has Jacob cross the Euphrates and
  reach Gilead with a three-day head start, pursued over seven days' journey. **The ground is some
  four hundred miles.** The page states the difficulty and does not resolve it; the standing
  explanations (a compressed itinerary, *seven days* as an idiom for a long chase, a different
  starting point) are all guesses.
- **On what was created.** **Two place pages**, [[Gilead]] and [[Galeed]] — the first is the chapter's
  stage and a region the Bible returns to constantly, the second is the heap with three names and is
  kept separate because *Galeed*, *Jegar Sahadutha* and *Mizpah* all name the same object and none of
  them names the region. **One theme page**, [[Idolatry]] — the *teraphim* are the Bible's first
  household gods, the wiki had no page for the subject, and Genesis 35:2–4 will need it. **Six
  connection pages**: [[Served, Released, Pursued - The Laban Years as Proto-Exodus]] (deferred from
  the previous pass on the explicit ground that the shape only completes here),
  [[The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22]],
  [[The Teraphim - Household Gods in Genesis and After]],
  [[Jegar Sahadutha - The Only Aramaic in the Torah]], [[Warned in a Dream - Abimelech and Laban]],
  and [[The Mizpah Benediction - Genesis 31.49 and How It Is Read]].
- **On what was deliberately not created.** **No page for Laban's sons** — they speak once, are never
  named, and are carried on [[Laban]] as they have been since 30:35. **No separate page for *the fear
  of Isaac*** — the title occurs twice, in one chapter, and the material sits on
  [[The Names of God]] and [[The Fear of God]] rather than carrying a page of its own. **No connection
  page for the *gānab* chain**, which is confined to this pericope and is treated on this page.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Mahanaim and the angels (32:1–2); the Jabbok and the new name
  (32:22–32); the meeting with Esau (33); the foreign gods buried at Shechem (35:2–4); Rachel's death
  and the fourth pillar (35:16–20); Isaac's death (35:29); Joseph *sold* and Joseph *made strange*
  (37:28; 42:7); and the *zebaḥ* at Beersheba (46:1). **All are given as plain scripture references
  rather than wikilinks, so none is a dangling link.**
