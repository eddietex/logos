# The Waters Recede

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 8:1–22

## Summary

The flood account is built as a mirror, and this is the half that comes down. Everything from 6:9
onward has been rising — the corruption, the commissioning, the loading, the shutting of the door,
the hundred and fifty days of water — and at 8:1 the whole structure turns on four words and then
descends back through its own elements in reverse. The fountains that opened are stopped. The
animals that went in come out. The door [[God]] shut is answered by a covering [[Noah]] removes.
The hundred and fifty days of rise are matched by a hundred and fifty of retreat. And the man who
has not spoken since the narrative began still does not speak.

**The hinge is a memory.**

> **1** God remembered Noah, all the animals, and all the livestock that were with him in the ship;
> and God made a wind to pass over the earth. The waters subsided.

*Remembered* is **zākar**, and everything depends on not reading it as English. It is not recall
after a lapse — nowhere in Scripture does God forget and then recover. It is a verb of *acting on a
commitment*: what God remembers, God does something about. It is the word of Genesis 9:15 (*"I will
remember my covenant"*), of Genesis 30:22 (*"God remembered Rachel"*, and she conceives), of Exodus
2:24 (*"God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham"*, and the exodus
begins), of 1 Samuel 1:19 (*"Yahweh remembered her"*, and Samuel is born), and of the one request
made from a cross in Luke 23:42. In every case the remembering is the turn in the plot. See
[[Remembrance]].

Which means the flood does not end because the rain stopped. Nothing in the water can turn an
un-creation — not the ark's construction, not Noah's righteousness, not seaworthiness. What turns it
is that God calls someone to mind. The whole survival of the world is placed on a single act of
divine recollection, and the narrative puts that act at its exact centre.

Note also *what* is remembered: *"Noah, all the animals, and all the livestock that were with him"*.
The animals are in the object list, which is not decoration — it is the ground of Genesis 9:9–10,
where the covenant is made *"with every living creature"* and not only with the eight people.

**Then the re-creation runs forwards.** *"God made a wind to pass over the earth"* — **rûaḥ**, over
the waters, which is Genesis 1:2 word for word: *"God's Spirit was hovering over the surface of the
waters"*. The same untranslatable choice sits in both verses (wind? breath? Spirit?), and Genesis
uses it at both moments when a world is about to appear out of water. From there the days of
creation come back in order: the deep is closed (day two's division restored), the mountaintops
appear and then dry ground (day three), an olive leaf says the plants are back (day three
completed), and the creatures come out *"that they may breed abundantly in the earth, and be
fruitful, and multiply"* (8:17) — which is the blessing of 1:22, returned. Genesis is not narrating
a disaster with a happy ending. It is narrating the world being made a second time. See
[[The Flood as Un-Creation]] and [[Creation]].

**Somebody is keeping a calendar.** The chapter is unusually dense with dates, and they are
consistent. The flood began in Noah's six hundredth year, second month, seventeenth day (7:11); the
ship rests in the seventh month on the seventeenth day (8:4) — exactly five months, and 7:24 and 8:3
both call that period a hundred and fifty days, which makes the months thirty days each. The
mountaintops appear on the first of the tenth month (8:5). The covering comes off on the **first day
of the first month of the six hundred and first year** (8:13) — New Year's Day, which is when a
narrator who has been counting since 7:11 chooses to show a man looking out at dry ground. The earth
is finally dry on the twenty-seventh of the second month (8:14), one year and ten days after the
water came: a lunar year of three hundred and fifty-four days plus the eleven that make it solar.
This is not a story that lost track of time in a crisis.

**The raven and the dove.** Noah opens a window and runs an experiment, twice, with two birds chosen
for their difference.

The **raven** is unclean (Leviticus 11:15) and it *"went back and forth, until the waters were dried
up"*. It is not reported returning, and Genesis does not say why. It does not have to: a carrion
feeder released over an ocean of floating carcasses had no reason to come home.

The **dove** is clean, and it is the offering the poor bring in Leviticus 5:7 and 12:8. Noah sends
her three times at seven-day intervals — the flood's one surviving rhythm of sevens. The first time,
*"the dove found no place to rest her foot"*, and the Hebrew is **mānôaḥ**, a resting place, from the
root that gives the ark its verb in 8:4 (*"the ship rested"*), gives the aroma its adjective in 8:21,
and gives Noah his name. The dove's problem is Genesis 1:9's problem: there is no dry land yet. And
then comes the one gesture of tenderness in the entire flood narrative, from a book that has spent
forty-four verses reporting mass death without an adjective:

> **9** … He put out his hand, and took her, and brought her to him into the ship.

The second sending brings back *"a freshly plucked olive leaf"* — olives do not grow at altitude and
do not survive long submersion, so the leaf is evidence about the lowlands, not the mountain. The
third time she does not come back, and the dove that chose the world over the ark is how Noah learns
the world is habitable. It is also, by way of this verse, why an olive branch means peace in every
language that has the idiom.

**He waits anyway.** At 8:13 Noah removes the covering, looks, and sees dry ground. He then stays in
the ship for fifty-seven more days, until 8:15: *"God spoke to Noah, saying, 'Go out of the ship'"*.
He has evidence and does not act on it. The narrative that has twice summarized him as *"Noah did
all that God commanded him"* (6:22, 7:5) makes the same point here by having him decline to do
anything God has not.

**And then, unbidden, he does something.**

> **20** Noah built an altar to Yahweh, and took of every clean animal, and of every clean bird, and
> offered burnt offerings on the altar.

This is the **first altar in the Bible**. [[Cain]] and [[Abel]] brought offerings; no altar was
named. It is also the first *burnt offering* — the **ʿōlâ**, the sacrifice that is wholly consumed
and of which nobody eats anything. Noah's first act on dry land is not to plant, build, or eat, but
to give away a seventh of every clean species he possesses, which is finally the answer to why
[[Noah and the Flood]] had him load seven pairs of the clean and only two of the rest (7:2). The
surplus existed for this. And no one told him to do it: the only unprompted act in the whole of
Noah's story is a costly one. See [[Sacrifice and Offering]] and [[Clean and Unclean]].

**The last two verses are the point of the chapter, and they are a scandal.**

> **21** Yahweh smelled the pleasant aroma. Yahweh said in his heart, “I will not again curse the
> ground any more for man’s sake because the imagination of man’s heart is evil from his youth. I
> will never again strike every living thing, as I have done.

Set that beside 6:5, the verse that caused the flood: *"every imagination of the thoughts of man's
heart was continually only evil."* The Hebrew nouns are the same — **yēṣer**, the shaping or
inclination; **lēb**, the heart; **raʿ**, evil. The diagnosis has not changed by a syllable. What has
changed is the conclusion drawn from it. In chapter 6 the evil of the human heart is the reason to
send the flood; in chapter 8 it is the reason never to send another. The connective is *kî*,
**because** — and it means *because*, in the same way it meant *because* in 6:5. Some readers soften
it to *"even though"*, and the Hebrew will bear a concessive reading under pressure, but the plain
sense is the harder and better one: God resolves on permanent restraint not because humanity has
improved but on the explicit grounds that it will not. The flood worked exactly as intended and
changed nothing about the thing it was sent against. Mercy is what is left. See
[[Divine Grief and Relenting]] and [[Favor and Grace]].

Two more details in that sentence. *"Said in his heart"* — *ʾel-libbô* — is the second and last time
Genesis reports something happening inside God, and the first was 6:6, *"it grieved him in his
heart"*. The flood is bracketed by God's interior, grief on one end and resolve on the other, with
the same phrase both times. And *"curse the ground… for man's sake"* is verbatim the sentence of
3:17, which means [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] gets an answer to the hope he pinned on this son's name
in 5:29 — a real answer, and a partial one, because the verb here is *qālal*, not the *ʾārar* of
3:17. God undertakes to add no further curse of this kind. He does not lift the one already in force,
and the ground is still worked in sweat for the rest of the canon. See
[[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]] and [[Curse]].

Then 8:22, which is where the promise becomes something a farmer can use:

> **22** While the earth remains, seed time and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter,
> and day and night will not cease.”

Four pairs — agricultural, thermal, seasonal, diurnal — covering the whole of ordinary time. The
claim is not that nature is stable. It is that nature's stability is a **promise**, an undertaking
God has entered into and could in principle not have. Jeremiah takes it exactly this way when he
speaks of *"my covenant of the day and my covenant of the night"* (33:20, 25), and Jesus takes it as
ethics: sun and rain on the evil and the good alike (Matthew 5:45), which is 8:21–22 turned into an
instruction.

**One last thing, easily missed: Noah does not hear any of this.** 8:21 is spoken *in God's heart*,
not to anybody. No covenant has been made — the *bərît* promised at 6:18 is not established until
9:9, and its sign is not given until 9:13. So the chapter ends with the reader knowing the world is
permanently safe and the man on the altar not knowing it. He steps off the ship into a world that
has just been drowned once, with no assurance it will not happen again, and builds an altar anyway.
The assurance comes in the next pericope. The worship comes first.

## People

- [[Noah]] — still silent, and now busy. Fourteen verbs are attributed to him here and not one word:
  he opens, sends, sends, puts out his hand, takes, brings in, waits, sends, waits, sends, removes,
  looks, goes out, builds, takes, offers. Two of those are worth holding on to. Taking the exhausted
  dove into the ship (8:9) is the only tender act anyone performs in the flood narrative. Building
  the altar (8:20) is the only thing he does in the entire story that he was not told to do — and it
  is the expensive one. His first recorded speech in Scripture is still one chapter away, and it is
  a curse (9:25).
- [[God]] — the divine names divide the chapter cleanly along the same seam as 6:9–7:24. He is
  *ʾĕlōhîm* for the cosmic and administrative half: *God remembered* (8:1), *God made a wind* (8:1),
  *God spoke to Noah* (8:15). He is *Yahweh* from the altar onward: the altar is built *to Yahweh*,
  *Yahweh smelled*, *Yahweh said in his heart* (8:20–21). The name attached to the covenantal,
  personal, interior material is the same one that said *"Come with all of your household"* at 7:1
  and *"shut him in"* at 7:16.
- [[Shem]], [[Ham]], [[Japheth]] — carried out as they were carried in, named only as *"his sons"*
  (8:16, 8:18), still given no act and no word. Everything that individuates them is Genesis 9–10.
- **Noah's wife, and the wives of Shem, Ham, and Japheth** — unnamed still, and present in both exit
  lists. One small thing changes: every boarding list keeps the men together and the women together
  (*"you, your sons, your wife, and your sons' wives"*, 6:18; likewise 7:7, 7:13), and God's command
  to disembark pairs them off instead — *"you, your wife, your sons, and your sons' wives"* (8:16).
  An old reading (Rashi, following the Talmud) takes this as marital relations suspended for the
  duration of the flood and resumed on dry land, which suits a verse that immediately tells the
  animals to *breed abundantly*. Worth noting honestly that the narration of the exit two verses
  later reverts to the old order — *"Noah went out, with his sons, his wife, and his sons' wives"*
  (8:18) — so the reordering is in the command only. No page yet for any of the four; there is still
  nothing to put on one.
- [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] — dead before the flood and not mentioned, but 8:21 is the verse his
  one recorded sentence was waiting for. He named this son in hope of *comfort* from *"the ground
  which Yahweh has cursed"* (5:29), and here the cursing of the ground is the thing God undertakes
  not to repeat.

## Places

- [[Ararat]] — the first named location in Genesis since [[Nod]] in 4:16, and the first ever named
  outside the Eden/Cain orbit. The Hebrew is *hārê ʾărārāṭ*, **the mountains of Ararat**, plural: a
  region, not a peak. Ararat is Urartu, the kingdom around Lake Van in eastern Anatolia that Assyrian
  records document for six centuries, and it appears twice more in Scripture as an ordinary place on
  the map (2 Kings 19:37; Jeremiah 51:27). The identification of a single mountain as *the* Ararat is
  medieval and later, not biblical.

The rest of the chapter still has no geography, for the same reason the previous pericope had none:
the arena is *hāʾāreṣ*, the whole earth. What changes here is that a piece of it comes back into
view and can be named — which is itself the plot.

## Themes

- [[Remembrance]] — introduced here, at the structural centre of the flood. *Zākar* in 8:1 is the
  Bible's first use of the verb with God as its subject, and it establishes the pattern the rest of
  Scripture runs on: God's remembering is never mental, always an intervention.
- [[Creation]] — replayed forwards. The *rûaḥ* of 1:2, the waters divided, dry land, vegetation, and
  the creatures released under the blessing of 1:22, in that order.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — the first altar and the first burnt offering in Scripture, and the
  first appearance of *rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ*, the *pleasant aroma*, which becomes the standing formula of the
  Levitical sacrificial system.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — load-bearing at last. The distinction assumed without explanation in 7:2
  turns out to have been for 8:20, and it also chooses the birds: an unclean raven for the errand
  that does not come back, a clean dove for the one that does.
- [[Curse]] — 8:21 addresses 3:17 directly, in 3:17's own words (*"for man's sake"*), and stops short
  of revoking it. The verb is *qālal* rather than *ʾārar*: no further curse of this kind, not the
  lifting of the one in force.
- [[Divine Grief and Relenting]] — the theme's decisive turn. 6:6 has God grieved *in his heart*;
  8:21 has him speak *in his heart*, restating 6:5's verdict and concluding the opposite way.
- [[Sin]] — 8:21 is the Bible's second statement that the human *yēṣer* is evil, and it is made
  *after* the flood, by God, as settled fact. The judgment did not change the diagnosis; nothing in
  this narrative does.
- [[Favor and Grace]] — 6:8 gave Noah favor before any act of his was reported. 8:21 goes further:
  the promise to the whole race is grounded on humanity's incorrigibility rather than on anything it
  has done or will do.
- [[Judgment]] — ended, and formally limited. *"I will never again strike every living thing, as I
  have done"* is the first time God binds his own future conduct in Scripture.
- [[Blessing]] — restored at 8:17, to the animals first: *"be fruitful, and multiply on the earth"*,
  unmentioned since 1:22 and absent throughout the flood. Humanity's turn is 9:1.
- [[Covenant]] — anticipated, not yet enacted. 8:21–22 is a resolve spoken in God's heart that Noah
  never hears; the *bərît* of 6:18 is established in 9:8–17, and the rainbow is its sign. That gap is
  deliberate, and it is why Noah builds an altar before he has any assurance.
- [[Life and Death]] — 8:17's *"breed abundantly"* against 7:22's *"all on the dry land… died"*, and
  8:21's *"never again strike every living thing"* against 6:7's resolve to do exactly that.
- [[Sabbath]] — not named, but the chapter's one human rhythm is sevens: *"He waited yet another
  seven days"*, twice (8:10, 8:12), for a man measuring a new world by the week.

## Notes

- **The chiasm, and why 8:1 sits where it does.** The reading of Genesis 6:10–9:19 as a symmetrical
  structure with 8:1 at its apex is Gordon Wenham's, and it has been widely adopted because the
  correspondences are countable rather than impressionistic: Noah's three sons (6:10) against Noah's
  three sons (9:18); seven days of waiting (7:4, 7:10) against seven days of waiting (8:10, 8:12);
  forty days (7:17) against forty days (8:6); a hundred and fifty days of rising (7:24) against a
  hundred and fifty days of falling (8:3); the animals entering against the animals leaving; the
  covenant promised (6:18) against the covenant established (9:9). Whatever one concludes about the
  account's composition history — and the flood is the classic demonstration case for source
  analysis, as [[Noah and the Flood]] sets out — the finished text is shaped, and the shape puts
  *"God remembered Noah"* at dead centre with nothing else competing for the position.

- **The dates, and the two calendars underneath them.** The intervals in 7:11–8:14 are internally
  consistent on thirty-day months (five months = a hundred and fifty days), which is a schematic
  three-hundred-and-sixty-day year rather than either a real lunar or a real solar one. But the total
  span from the first day of the flood to the dry earth is a year and ten days, and eleven days is
  precisely the gap between a lunar year (354) and a solar one (365). Both facts are in the same
  chapter. The usual explanation is that the flood account was transmitted through more than one
  calendrical convention; a simpler one is that a schematic year is being used for the internal
  arithmetic and a real one for the total. The dating in Genesis 7–8 is one of the few places in the
  primeval history where the text volunteers numbers precise enough to be checked against each
  other, which is why it has been checked so often.

- **Gilgamesh XI, and where the resemblance stops.** The Mesopotamian flood story is not a distant
  parallel; at two points it is nearly the same scene. Utnapishtim's boat grounds on Mount Nimush and
  he sends out three birds — a dove, then a swallow, then a raven. The first two find no resting
  place and return. The raven *"saw the waters receding; it ate, it circled, it cawed, and it did not
  turn back."* Genesis has two birds instead of three, puts the raven first instead of last, and
  declines to explain why it stays away — the detail Gilgamesh spells out is the one Genesis leaves
  for the reader to supply. Then Utnapishtim disembarks and sacrifices, and *"the gods smelled the
  sweet savour; the gods gathered like flies about the sacrificer."* Genesis 8:21 has the identical
  idiom — *Yahweh smelled the pleasant aroma* — and nothing else of it. In the Akkadian the gods
  swarm because they are starving: they drowned the humans who fed them and have gone without
  offerings for a week. That is not a hostile caricature of the story, it is what the story says. The
  Genesis sentence takes the same three words and continues not with appetite but with a resolve —
  *"I will not again curse the ground"* — and grounds that resolve in a judgment about the human
  heart. Which text depends on which, and in what direction, is genuinely disputed and probably not
  settleable; that the two are in the same conversation is not.

- **The raven, and a small history of embarrassment.** Genesis's silence about the raven's fate left
  a gap that later readers were unwilling to leave open. The Septuagint and the Vulgate read the bird
  as not returning until the waters dried; the Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 108b) has the raven argue with
  Noah about the errand; Jewish and Christian commentators variously made it disobedient, faithless,
  or a glutton feeding on the dead, and the Christian tradition then set the treacherous black raven
  against the faithful white dove and read the pair allegorically for a thousand years. None of that
  is in the verse. Genesis reports a bird going back and forth over water and says nothing about its
  character, in the same register in which it reports a hundred and fifty days and a mountain range.

- **"Because" or "even though" (8:21).** The clause is *kî yēṣer lēb hāʾādām raʿ minnəʿurāyw*. *Kî*
  is a general subordinator and can be causal, concessive, temporal, or asseverative depending on
  context, so *"even though the imagination of man's heart is evil"* is not a mistranslation in the
  abstract. Three things weigh against it here. It is the ordinary causal use that dominates the
  Hebrew Bible, and it is what nearly every major translation gives, WEB included. The phrase is a
  near-quotation of 6:5, where the same construction unambiguously supplies the *grounds* for God's
  action. And the concessive reading makes the sentence bland — God will refrain from judging despite
  provocation, which is unremarkable — while the causal reading makes it the theological turn the
  whole narrative has been building toward: the flood is not repeated because it did not work, and
  God says so. The harder reading is also the one the structure demands.

- **What 8:21 does and does not undo.** *"I will not again curse the ground any more for man's
  sake"* is 3:17's phrase (*baʿăbûr hāʾādām*) with a different verb: 3:17 uses *ʾārar*, the strong
  formal curse, and 8:21 uses *qālal*, to treat lightly or to slight. The scope is also limited by
  its parallel line — *"I will never again strike every living thing, as I have done"* — which is
  about the flood specifically. So the undertaking is: no second flood, and no further curse laid on
  the ground on humanity's account. It is not the lifting of the curse of 3:17, which stays in force
  through the whole Bible and is still being felt in Romans 8:20–22. [[Lamech (father of Noah)]] gets
  part of what he hoped for, in the generation he hoped for it, and not the part that would have
  ended the toil.
