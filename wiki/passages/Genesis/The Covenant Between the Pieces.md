# The Covenant Between the Pieces

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 15:1–21

## Summary

The chapter where Abram finally answers back — and where God, having been answered back, swears an
oath in fire while the man he swore it to is unconscious.

Genesis 15 is the hinge of the Abraham cycle. Everything promised loosely since 12:1 is here made
formal: the offspring get a number, the land gets a border, and the whole arrangement is for the
first time called a *bərît* between Yahweh and this man and ratified by a ceremony. It is also the
first chapter in which Abram speaks to God at all. He has been spoken to at 12:1, 12:7, and
13:14–17 and has never once replied; here he replies three times (15:2, 15:3, 15:8), and the first
thing out of his mouth is a complaint that the promise is not working. Genesis does not treat the
complaint as a failure. It treats it as the thing that gets the covenant cut.

**The chapter is built as two matching panels**, and seeing the symmetry is most of reading it:

| | Panel A — 15:1–6 | Panel B — 15:7–21 |
|---|---|---|
| God's self-declaration | *"I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward"* | *"I am Yahweh who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldees"* |
| The subject | offspring | land |
| Abram's answer | *"what will you give me, since I go childless"* | *"how will I know that I will inherit it?"* |
| The sign given | the stars, outside, at night | five animals, cut, at sunset |
| How it closes | *"He believed in Yahweh"* | *"Yahweh made a covenant with Abram"* |

Both panels open with a divine *I am*, both are met with a question, and both questions are
answered rather than rebuked. What differs is that the first ends in Abram's faith and the second
ends in God's oath — and that between them the chapter's two most quoted verses sit four verses
apart.

### Panel A — the shield, the stars, and the credit

***"After these things Yahweh's word came to Abram in a vision"*** (15:1). Two firsts in eight
words.

- **The prophetic formula enters the Bible here.** *Hāyâ dəbar-YHWH ʾel-* — *the word of Yahweh came
  to* — is the standing superscription of the prophetic books, used hundreds of times of Jeremiah,
  Ezekiel, Hosea, Jonah, Zechariah and the rest. Genesis 15:1 is its first occurrence, and it is
  attached to a man Genesis 20:7 will call a *nābîʾ*, a prophet — the Bible's first use of that word
  too, and also of Abraham. See [[Prophecy and Vision]].
- ***In a vision*** is *ba-maḥăzeh*, a rare noun that occurs four times in the Hebrew Bible and only
  once outside Balaam's oracles and Ezekiel. Genesis is signalling that what follows is not the
  ordinary appearing of 12:7 or 18:1.

***"Don't be afraid, Abram."*** The first *ʾal-tîrāʾ* in Scripture, and the beginning of a formula
that will run to Isaiah's *"Don't be afraid, for I am with you"* (43:5) and the angel at the empty
tomb. Genesis has not said Abram was afraid, which is worth noticing: the reassurance arrives
before the fear is reported, and the reader is left to supply the reason from the previous chapter.

***"I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward."*** Both nouns are answers to Genesis 14.

- ***Shield*** is *māgēn*, its first occurrence in the Bible — and Melchizedek's blessing one verse
  of narrative earlier used the same root as a verb: *"blessed be God Most High, who has delivered
  your enemies into your hand"* (14:20) is *miggēn ṣārêkā*. The priest said *he shielded your
  enemies into your hand*; God now says *I am your shield*. A man who has just fought four kings
  with three hundred eighteen men is told what stood between him and them.
- ***Reward*** is *śākār*, wages — the word for what you are paid. Abram has just refused payment
  from the king of Sodom *"lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich'"* (14:23). The next thing
  he is told is that his wages are God's to give. See
  [[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].

The Hebrew of the second half is ambiguous and both readings are standard: *I am your shield; your
reward will be very great*, or, as the WEB takes it appositionally, *I am your shield, your
exceedingly great reward* — God himself as the payment. The second reading is the more theologically
loaded and the first is the more likely grammar; the wiki records both, because the sentence in
Hebrew does not choose.

***"Lord Yahweh, what will you give me, since I go childless"*** (15:2). **Abram's first recorded
words to God in the Bible, and they are a complaint.** He has obeyed a command without a word
(12:4), built three altars without a prayer, given away the land without an objection, and refused
a fortune without hesitation; the first time he opens his mouth to God he says the promise has
produced nothing. *ʿĂrîrî*, *childless*, is a bleak word — stripped, denuded — and the participle
*hôlēk*, *I go*, means *I am going on this way*, not *I have arrived at it*.

The address is also a first: ***ʾĂdōnāy YHWH***, *Lord Yahweh*, the Bible's first occurrence of the
pair, used here and again at 15:8 and then almost nowhere else in the Torah. Both times it
introduces a question. The most deferential form of address Abram ever uses is the form he uses to
argue. See [[The Names of God]].

***"He who will inherit my estate is Eliezer of Damascus … one born in my house is my heir"***
(15:2–3). The Hebrew of 15:2b, *ben-mešeq bêtî hûʾ Dammeśeq ʾĔlîʿezer*, is one of the hardest
clauses in Genesis; *mešeq* occurs nowhere else and the wordplay with *Dammeśeq* is either the point
or the corruption. What is clear is the institution: with no son, the head of a household adopts a
trusted servant as heir. And Genesis has already told us what *born in my house* means — 14:14's
*"trained men, born in his house, three hundred and eighteen"*. Abram's army and Abram's heir come
from the same category of person. See [[Eliezer of Damascus]] and [[Damascus]].

***"This man will not be your heir, but he who will come out of your own body will be your heir"***
(15:4). The correction is anatomical and blunt. Yahweh's answer to a legal solution is a biological
promise, and the chapter never mentions [[Sarah]] — a silence chapter 16 will exploit within four
verses.

***"Look now toward the sky, and count the stars, if you are able to count them"*** (15:5). He is
brought outside; the vision has a night in it. Dust was shown to a man surveying a country (13:16);
stars are shown to a man who has just said the promise is not working. See
[[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]].

### Genesis 15:6

***"He believed in Yahweh, who credited it to him for righteousness."***

Nine Hebrew words, and no verse in the Old Testament has more downstream traffic. Four things about
it are worth having straight before any of that traffic arrives.

**The verb is *heʾĕmin*, and this is its first occurrence in the Bible.** It is the hiphil of
*ʾāman*, the root behind *ʾĕmûnâ* (faithfulness) and behind **amen** — a root whose basic sense is
firmness, reliability, being able to bear weight. To *believe in* Yahweh in Hebrew is not primarily
to hold a proposition true; it is to lean on someone and find they hold. See [[Faith]].

**The grammar is irregular and the irregularity is old news.** Genesis narrates in a chain of
waw-consecutive verbs; 15:6 breaks the chain with a perfect and a simple *waw* (*wə-heʾĕmin*)
instead of the expected *wayyaʾămēn*. Grammarians read this either as marking the clause off from
the sequence — a summary or an evaluation rather than the next event — or as durative, *he had
believed* and *kept believing*. Either way the effect is the same and the New Testament noticed it
long before the grammarians did: the verse reads less like a report of one moment than like a
verdict on a man.

**The second verb is an accounting term.** *Wayyaḥšəbehā llô ṣədāqâ* — *and he credited it to him
as righteousness*. *Ḥāšab* is to reckon, count, enter in a ledger, and it has a technical cultic use
(Leviticus 7:18; 17:4; Numbers 18:27) for what does or does not count in God's books. Nothing about
it implies pretence. It implies a reckoning: something is put in a column.

**And the Old Testament itself uses the identical formula for a deed.** Psalm 106:31, of Phinehas
running a spear through two people: *"That was credited to him for righteousness, for all
generations forever more."* Same verb, same noun, same construction, and the thing credited is an
act of lethal zeal. Genesis 15:6 and Psalm 106:31 are the two halves of the argument Paul and James
will have, and both halves are already in the Hebrew Bible. See
[[Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James]].

One ambiguity should be named and then not inflated. The Hebrew has no explicit subjects — *he
believed … and he credited it to him* — so it is grammatically possible to read Abram as crediting
righteousness to God. The Septuagint's passive (*elogisthē autō*, *it was reckoned to him*) settles
it in the standard direction, every ancient version follows, and the reversal has no serious
defense; the wiki notes the grammar and follows the consensus, as it did with the tithe at 14:20.

### Panel B — the question that is answered with a ceremony

***"I am Yahweh who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldees, to give you this land to inherit it"***
(15:7). The self-presentation formula, and it is word-for-word the shape of Exodus 20:2 — *"I am
Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage"* — with Ur
where Egypt will be. The exodus's own grammar is being used of Abram four hundred years early, in a
chapter that is about to announce the exodus.

It also quietly corrects the narrative. Genesis 11:31 said *Terah* took the family out of Ur;
15:7 says Yahweh did. Both are allowed to stand, and Joshua 24:3 says the same thing Yahweh says
here. See [[Ur of the Chaldees]] and
[[Out of Ur - Terah's Journey and Abram's Call]].

***"How will I know that I will inherit it?"*** (15:8). The man who was just credited with
righteousness for believing asks for proof four verses later. Genesis puts no distance at all
between the two and offers no reconciliation, and the honest reading is that the chapter does not
think a request for a sign is the opposite of faith. It is worth setting beside the other askers:
Gideon asks twice with a fleece and is indulged twice (Judges 6:36–40); Hezekiah asks and is given a
shadow that goes backwards (2 Kings 20:8–11); Zechariah asks *"How can I be sure of this?"* and is
struck mute (Luke 1:18–20). Scripture does not run a single rule here. What distinguishes the cases
is not the question but who is asking and what they have already been told.

***"Bring me a heifer three years old, a female goat three years old, a ram three years old, a
turtledove, and a young pigeon"*** (15:9). **Five animals, and they are exactly the five species the
Levitical system will use** — herd, flock, and bird, in the two grades that let a poor worshipper
bring a pair of birds where a rich one brings a bull (Leviticus 1; 5:7; 12:8). The whole sacrificial
economy of Israel is prefigured in one shopping list, four centuries before any of it is legislated.
*Məšullāš*, rendered *three years old*, could also mean *threefold* — three of each — and the
ancient versions divide; the WEB takes the age, which is the majority reading and which makes each
animal a mature specimen. See [[Sacrifice and Offering]].

***"He brought him all these, and divided them in the middle, and laid each half opposite the other;
but he didn't divide the birds"*** (15:10). The verb is *bātar*, to cut in two, which occurs in the
Hebrew Bible only here; its noun *betārîm*, *the pieces*, gives the chapter its traditional Jewish
name, *bərît bên ha-betārîm* — **the covenant between the pieces**. That the birds are not divided
matches Leviticus 1:17 exactly, where the bird offering is torn by the wings *"but shall not divide
it apart"*, and it is one of the more striking small agreements between this chapter and a law book
that does not yet exist.

Nothing here is a sacrifice. Nothing is burned, nothing is offered, no altar is built, no blood is
manipulated, and God does not eat. The animals are not victims; they are the *terms* of an oath. See
[[Passing Between the Pieces]].

***"The birds of prey came down on the carcasses, and Abram drove them away"*** (15:11). One verse,
and it does two things. It tells the reader that a long time passes — he stands over the halves
through the heat of a whole day, guarding them — and it is the only thing Abram does in the second
half of the chapter. After this he sleeps and God works.

***"When the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram. Now terror and great darkness fell on
him"*** (15:12). *Tardēmâ*, **deep sleep**, is the word used of Adam at 2:21, and those are its only
two occurrences in Genesis. In both, God does the decisive thing while the man is unconscious:
builds a woman out of him, and binds himself to him. Abram is a spectator at his own covenant. And
the sleep is not peaceful — *ʾêmâ ḥăšēkâ gədōlâ*, *terror, great darkness*, falls on him before a
word of the prophecy is spoken.

***"Know for sure that your offspring will live as foreigners in a land that is not theirs, and will
serve them. They will afflict them four hundred years"*** (15:13). The answer to *how will I know*
begins with four centuries of slavery. This is the Bible's first prediction of the exodus, given to
the man whose descendants it will happen to, and it is given as part of a reassurance about the
land. The verb for *live as foreigners* is *gûr*, the theme-word Abram's own life has been running
on since 12:10. See [[Land and Sojourning]] and
[[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].

***"Afterward they will come out with great wealth"*** (15:14). *Rəkūš gādôl* — and *rəkūš* is the
word Genesis 14 used four times for the plunder of Sodom (14:11, 12, 16, 21), the plunder Abram
refused to keep. He declines *rəkūš* from a king and is told his offspring will leave Egypt loaded
with it (Exodus 12:35–36). See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

***"But you will go to your fathers in peace. You will be buried at a good old age"*** (15:15). A
personal promise dropped into a national prophecy, and the first time in Genesis anyone is told how
their own death will go. *Go to your fathers* is a formula whose plain sense is burial with the
clan — awkwardly, since his fathers are buried in Ur and Haran and he will be buried at Machpelah —
and which therefore already carries some sense of the dead as a company one joins. See
[[Sheol]] and [[Life and Death]].

***"In the fourth generation they will come here again, for the iniquity of the Amorite is not yet
full"*** (15:16). Two hard things in one verse.

**Four generations against four hundred years.** The two numbers in four verses do not obviously
agree, and the standard resolutions are all live: *dôr* here means a lifetime rather than a
begetting cycle (four hundred-year spans); the four hundred is a round figure and the four
generations are the Levi–Kohath–Amram–Moses line of Exodus 6:16–20; or the sojourn was shorter than
Exodus 12:40's four hundred thirty years, which the Septuagint and Samaritan texts of that verse
compute as time *in Egypt and in Canaan* and which Paul follows at Galatians 3:17. The wiki records
the tension rather than picking a chronology.

**And the conquest is put on a moral clock.** *"The iniquity of the Amorite is not yet full"* is the
Bible's own reason for a four-century delay: the land is not taken yet because the people in it are
not yet liable to lose it. The theology is uncomfortable in both directions — it presumes a
threshold of guilt at which dispossession becomes just, and it presumes God waits for it — and it is
the one reason the Torah actually gives, alongside Leviticus 18:24–28 and Deuteronomy 9:4–5, for what
Joshua does. Noah's curse on Canaan is never cited for it. See
[[The Curse of Canaan and the Conquest]] and [[Judgment]].

***"Behold, a smoking furnace, and a flaming torch passed between these pieces"*** (15:17). The
theophany, and the whole point of the chapter is **who walks**.

In the ceremony Jeremiah 34:18–20 describes — the only place in Scripture that explains it — the
parties to a covenant pass between the halves of a cut animal, and the passage enacts a self-curse:
*may I be made like this if I break it*. Genesis 15 stages the rite in full and then sends only one
party through. Abram is asleep. A *tannûr* — a clay bread-oven, not a smelter — smoking, and a torch
of fire, move down the corridor between the halves alone. **God swears the self-maledictory oath and
Abram swears nothing**, which makes this the most one-sided covenant in the Bible after Noah's, and
the reason Paul can say the Abrahamic promise has no conditions in it for the law to annul
(Galatians 3:15–18).

Fire and smoke are how this God appears from here to Sinai: the pillar of cloud and fire (Exodus
13:21–22), the mountain smoking *"like the smoke of a furnace"* (Exodus 19:18), the bush that burns
and is not consumed. See
[[Smoking Furnace and Flaming Torch - Fire as Theophany]].

***"In that day Yahweh made a covenant with Abram"*** (15:18). *Kārat bərît* — literally **cut a
covenant** — and this is **its first occurrence in the Bible.** Genesis 6:18 and 9:9 used *hēqîm*,
*establish*, of Noah, with no ceremony at all; 14:13 used the noun of a treaty between Abram and
three Amorites. Here, for the first time, the standard biblical idiom appears with its literal
ceremony attached, and the reader can see why the idiom is what it is. See [[Covenant]].

***"from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates"*** (15:18). The land promise
gets a border for the first time — 12:7 and 13:15 said *this land* and pointed. The northern limit is
[[The Euphrates]], one of Eden's four rivers at 2:14. The southern is *nəhar
Miṣrayim*, which uses the word for a perennial river where the standard boundary formula elsewhere
uses *naḥal Miṣrayim*, the Wadi of Egypt (Numbers 34:5; Joshua 15:4; 1 Kings 8:65) — so it is either
the Nile or, much more probably, that wadi under an unusual word. No Israel ever holds this
territory except briefly under Solomon (1 Kings 4:21). See
[[From the River of Egypt to the Euphrates]].

***"the Kenites, the Kenizzites, the Kadmonites, the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Rephaim, the
Amorites, the Canaanites, the Girgashites, and the Jebusites"*** (15:19–21). **Ten peoples — the
longest such list in the Bible**, where the standard formula has six (Exodus 3:8) or seven
(Deuteronomy 7:1; Joshua 3:10). Four of the ten appear in no other list, and two of those four are
awkward:

| People | Elsewhere |
|---|---|
| Kenites | Jethro's people; Israel's **allies**, spared at 1 Samuel 15:6 and settling with Judah (Judges 1:16) |
| Kenizzites | Caleb is *the Kenizzite* (Numbers 32:12; Joshua 14:6) and is given Hebron |
| Kadmonites | *easterners*; occur nowhere else in Scripture |
| Rephaim | the Transjordan giants of 14:5 and Deuteronomy 2:10–11, 3:11 |

Two of the peoples promised as dispossessed produce, in the event, an Israelite tribe's in-laws and
the man who takes Hebron. Genesis does not notice, and the wiki does not smooth it: the list is
maximal, it is the widest claim in Scripture, and Israel's actual history is smaller and messier than
it in every direction.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *Abram*, and this is the chapter in which he first talks back. Three speeches, two of
  them questions and one of them a complaint, and every one of them is answered. He is credited with
  righteousness for believing (15:6) and asks for proof two verses later (15:8), and Genesis puts the
  two side by side without a seam. In the second half he does exactly one thing — drives the vultures
  off the carcasses — and then sleeps through the covenant made with him.
- [[God]] — who speaks twelve of the chapter's twenty-one verses, takes a man outside to look at the
  sky, and then walks between the pieces alone in fire. He identifies himself twice by what he has
  done (*your shield*, *who brought you out of Ur*), predicts four centuries of slavery inside a
  reassurance, defers the conquest until a people's guilt is complete, and binds himself under a
  self-curse to a man who is unconscious and has undertaken nothing.
- [[Eliezer of Damascus]] — named once in Scripture, in the sentence where he is set aside. Abram's
  steward and, in the absence of a son, his heir by adoption. Jewish tradition identifies him with
  the unnamed senior servant of Genesis 24 and Genesis never does.
- [[Sarah]] — **not mentioned.** The chapter is entirely about an heir and never names the woman who
  is not producing one. Genesis 16:1 opens on that silence — *"Now Sarai, Abram's wife, bore him no
  children"* — and her proposal about Hagar follows immediately. The promise of 15:4 says *your own
  body* and does not say *hers*, which is precisely the gap chapter 16 walks into.
- **The Amorites** — named here as a people whose guilt is *"not yet full"*, and at 14:13 as the
  nation of Abram's three covenant-partners. Genesis is capable of holding both. No page yet; they
  earn one when the conquest texts do something with them.

## Places

- [[Ur of the Chaldees]] — invoked by God as what he brought Abram out of (15:7), in a self-
  introduction shaped exactly like the one Exodus 20:2 will build on the exodus. Genesis 11:31 had
  credited the departure to Terah.
- [[Damascus]] — Eliezer's city, and the far end of Abram's pursuit four verses of narrative earlier
  (14:15). Its two mentions in Genesis are these.
- [[Canaan]] — the land itself, given a border for the first time and inventoried by its ten peoples.
- [[Egypt]] — unnamed but unmistakable in 15:13–14: *"a land that is not theirs"*, four hundred years
  of affliction, a judged nation, and a departure *"with great wealth"*.
- [[The Euphrates]] — the northern boundary of the promise, and one of the four rivers of Eden at
  2:14. The only time Israel's territory approaches it is under David and Solomon.
- **The river of Egypt** — the southern boundary. Almost certainly the Wadi of Egypt (Wadi el-Arish)
  of Numbers 34:5 and Joshua 15:4, under an unusual word for it; possibly the Nile. Covered on
  [[From the River of Egypt to the Euphrates]] rather than given a page of its own.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the theme's decisive Old Testament passage. *Kārat bərît*, **to cut a covenant**,
  occurs here for the first time in Scripture, with the ceremony that explains the idiom staged in
  full; and the only party who passes between the pieces is God.
- [[Faith]] — *heʾĕmin*'s first occurrence in the Bible (15:6), and the taproot of everything the New
  Testament will say about believing. The chapter's own definition is behavioural: Abram leans on a
  promise about a son he does not have, and it holds.
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣədāqâ* credited rather than earned, by an accounting verb, to a man who has
  done nothing in the chapter but complain and then believe.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — *"Yahweh's word came to Abram in a vision"* (15:1): the prophetic
  formula's first appearance, and the Bible's first predictive oracle about a nation's future.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the promise finally given a border, and in the same breath a four-hundred
  year delay in a land that is not theirs. Title and possession are separated by the length of the
  book of Exodus.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — five animals in the five Levitical species, cut and laid out, with no
  altar, no fire from Abram, no burning, and no offering. The nearest thing to a sacrificial scene in
  the Abraham cycle before chapter 22, and it is not a sacrifice.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — chapter 14 gave the Bible's first human oath; chapter 15 gives its first
  divine one, sworn in a ceremony whose whole grammar is *may I be so divided*.
- [[Judgment]] — *"I will also judge that nation"* (15:14), the first announced judgment on a
  specific people; and 15:16's clock, on which judgment waits until guilt is complete.
- [[Call and Election]] — the call of 12:1 formalized. Nothing is asked of Abram in the covenant, and
  the one thing he contributes — belief — is credited to him rather than counted as a condition.
- [[Barrenness]] — 11:30's flat notice is now a spoken grievance: *"I go childless."* The chapter
  raises the problem and leaves it unsolved for another chapter and a half.
- [[The Nations]] — ten of them, listed as the content of a land grant, in a book whose founding
  promise was that all of them would be blessed (12:3). Genesis holds the two without comment.
- [[Life and Death]] — *"you will go to your fathers in peace"* (15:15): the first time anyone in
  Genesis is told the manner of his own death, and a formula that treats the dead as a company.
- [[The Names of God]] — *ʾĂdōnāy YHWH*, *Lord Yahweh*, first occurrence in Scripture, used twice and
  both times to ask a question.
- [[Blessing]] — offspring and land, the two contents of blessing in Genesis, sworn here in one
  ceremony.

## Notes

- **The chapter's two halves have been read as two sources, and the seam is real whichever way you
  explain it.** Panel A is a night vision about offspring; Panel B begins at what reads like a fresh
  start (*"He said to Abram, 'I am Yahweh who brought you out…'"*) and runs from daylight through
  sunset to full dark. Classical source criticism assigned the halves to J and E and found the joins
  at 15:7 and 15:13–16; more recent work often reads the whole chapter as a late, deliberately
  composed bridge between the patriarchal promises and the exodus, precisely because it contains both
  and belongs neatly to neither. Nothing in the wiki's reading depends on the outcome: the two panels
  answer each other whether one hand or three arranged them.
- **What kind of oath this is, in one sentence.** In a *bərît* cut between pieces, the party who walks
  the corridor invokes the fate of the animals on himself. Genesis 15 has one walker, and it is not
  the human. Every later argument that the Abrahamic promise is unconditional — Galatians 3:15–18,
  Hebrews 6:13–18, and the whole Reformed reading of covenant — is standing on that observation, and
  the observation is simply what 15:12 and 15:17 report.
- **Genesis 15:6 is one verse and two doctrines.** Paul quotes it three times (Romans 4:3, 22;
  Galatians 3:6) to prove that Abraham was justified apart from works and apart from circumcision,
  which Genesis 17 has not yet introduced — the chronology is load-bearing for his argument.
  James 2:23 quotes the same verse to prove that faith is completed by works, with Genesis 22 as the
  completion. Between them, 1 Maccabees 2:52 had already read 15:6 through the Aqedah — *"was not
  Abraham found faithful in temptation, and it was reckoned to him for righteousness?"* — which means
  James is arguing with a reading that existed, not inventing one. The wiki keeps all three and does
  not harmonize them on this page; see
  [[Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James]].
- **On the four hundred years, the wiki declines to compute a chronology.** Genesis 15:13 says four
  hundred; Exodus 12:40 says four hundred thirty in Egypt; the Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch
  read that verse as four hundred thirty in Canaan *and* Egypt, which halves the Egyptian stay and
  fits Genesis 15:16's four generations and Exodus 6:16–20's Levi-to-Moses line; Paul at Galatians
  3:17 counts four hundred thirty from the promise to Sinai, which is the Septuagint's arithmetic;
  Acts 7:6 quotes the round four hundred. Every one of these is a genuine ancient reading of the
  same tradition, and no reconciliation satisfies all of them without adjusting something. The wiki
  reports the spread. See [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].
- **The chapter's moral difficulty is 15:16, and it should not be hidden behind 15:6.** *"The iniquity
  of the Amorite is not yet full"* makes the dispossession of a people contingent on a measured
  accumulation of guilt. Read one way it is remarkable restraint — four centuries in which a people
  is left alone, and Israel enslaved in the meantime, because judgment is not yet earned. Read
  another it is a theology in which a land can be given away over the heads of the people living on
  it once a threshold is met. The Torah itself immediately turns the same principle on Israel
  (Leviticus 18:28: *"that the land not vomit you out also"*), which is the strongest thing that can
  be said in its defense: the standard is not ethnic and Israel is explicitly subject to it. 2 Kings
  17 and 25 are the text taking its own point.
- **Two of the ten peoples of 15:19–21 end up on Israel's side.** The Kenites are spared by Saul
  because *"you showed kindness to all the children of Israel when they came up out of Egypt"*
  (1 Samuel 15:6), and Caleb the Kenizzite is given Hebron — the very ground Abram is probably
  standing on in this chapter. The maximal list promises the land free of peoples who turn out to
  help hold it. Genesis makes nothing of this and neither should a reader; it is a good measure of
  how far the list is a claim about extent rather than a census of enemies.
- **Nothing in this chapter is a sacrifice, and the resemblance is the trap.** Five clean species,
  cut and arranged, at evening, with fire arriving — everything looks cultic and nothing is. No altar
  is built, no piece is burned, no blood is applied, nothing is eaten, and the word *qorbān* and its
  relatives are absent. The animals are an oath's vocabulary. Genesis 22 is where the Abraham cycle's
  actual sacrifice happens, and the two chapters are the more instructive for not being the same
  thing. See [[Sacrifice and Offering]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- **Abram asks for a sign and is not rebuked, and the wiki does not manufacture a rule from it.**
  15:8 is the only place in the Abraham cycle where he asks God to prove something, and it produces
  the covenant. The nearest thing to a pattern in Scripture is that signs are granted to people
  already in the relationship and refused to people demanding credentials (Matthew 12:38–39), but the
  cases do not line up cleanly — Zechariah is a priest in the temple and is silenced for the same
  question. What can be said flatly is that Genesis 15 does not treat 15:8 as contradicting 15:6.
