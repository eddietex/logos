# The Treaty at Beersheba

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 21:22–34

## Summary

**A foreign king comes to Abraham and asks him to swear he will not cheat him.**

That is the whole premise, and it is worth stopping on before anything else. One chapter ago
Abimelech was told a woman was Abraham's sister, took her on that information, and nearly died for
it. Now he arrives with the commander of his army and opens with a compliment — *"God is with you in
all that you do"* — and then asks for the one thing the last chapter gave him reason to want:

> **23** Now, therefore, swear to me here by God that you will not deal falsely with me, nor with my
> son, nor with my son’s son.

**The man asking for the oath is the man who was lied to. The man giving it is the man who lied.**
Genesis does not connect the two scenes out loud. It puts them eleven verses apart and lets the
request explain itself.

**Nothing else in the pericope is what the reader expects either.** God neither speaks nor acts in
these thirteen verses — the only unit in the Abraham cycle since chapter 13 of which that is true,
and it sits immediately before the chapter in which God says the most terrible thing he says in
Genesis. Abraham, who at 14:22–23 swore an oath in order to refuse a foreign king's property, here
hands a foreign king sheep and cattle. And Abraham, who owns not one square foot of the land he has
been promised, plants a **tree** — the slowest thing a man can do with ground he does not hold.

### Two transactions, braided together

The pericope runs two pieces of business at once, and the text alternates between them rather than
finishing one first:

| verses | the treaty | the well |
|---|---|---|
| 22–24 | Abimelech asks for an oath; Abraham agrees | |
| 25–26 | | Abraham lodges a complaint; Abimelech denies knowledge |
| 27 | sheep and cattle given; *"Those two made a covenant"* | |
| 28–30 | | seven ewe lambs set apart as a witness to the digging |
| 31–32 | the place named for the swearing; *"So they made a covenant at Beersheba"* | |
| 33–34 | | the tamarisk, the name of God, and the long sojourn |

**The covenant is reported twice (21:27 and 21:32) and the place is named twice (21:31 and 21:32),**
which is the kind of seam source critics read as two accounts spliced — an oath-treaty tradition and
a well-claim tradition, joined because both explain the name *Beersheba* and both involve the same
king. The doublet is real and the wiki records it. What it does not settle is whether a redactor
braided two stories or a narrator told one story in the way ancient legal narrative normally tells
them: agreement, then consideration, then the ceremony that makes it evidentiary. See the notes.

### *"God is with you in all that you do"*

**The formula is two verses old when Abimelech uses it, and it was used of the other son.**

> **20** God was with the boy, and he grew. He lived in the wilderness, and became, as he grew up, an
> archer.

*ʾĕlōhîm ʿim* — God with — is said by the narrator of [[Ishmael]] at 21:20, in a wilderness, over a
boy who has just been sent away with a skin of water; and by a Philistine king of [[Abraham]] at
21:22, in a court, about a man conspicuously prospering. **Genesis puts the same three words on the
disinherited son and on the man who disinherited him, inside one chapter, and says nothing about the
juxtaposition.** See [[The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar]].

The formula then becomes one of the book's load-bearing phrases. It is what Isaac's version of this
same treaty turns on — *"We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you"* (26:28) — and it is the refrain
that carries the entire Joseph narrative, four times in one chapter (39:2, 3, 21, 23), in a prison.
**It is almost always spoken by an outsider or by the narrator, and almost never by the person it is
about.**

**And notice what Abimelech is actually reasoning from.** He has watched a man arrive as a
destitute alien, be caught in a lie, leave his court richer than he entered it, and then father a
son at a hundred. His conclusion is not that Abraham is good; it is that Abraham is *backed*, and
that a man who is backed is a man you make a treaty with rather than a man you evict. That is a
political judgment about power, and it happens to be theologically correct. See [[The Nations]].

### The oath, and the word Abimelech chooses

***"swear to me here by God that you will not deal falsely with me"***. The verb is *šāqar*, and
**this is its first occurrence in Scripture** — the root that Leviticus 19:11 will use for *"you
shall not deal falsely"* and that stands behind the *šeqer*, the falsehood, of the ninth
commandment's *"false witness"* (Exodus 20:16). Of all the things a king might ask a resident alien
to swear, this is the one Genesis has him ask, of this man, one chapter after chapter 20.

**Three generations are named.** *"with me, nor with my son, nor with my son's son"* — the standard
reach of an ancient Near Eastern treaty clause, binding the descendants of both houses. It lands
oddly here on purpose: Abraham is a hundred years old and has had a legitimate son for perhaps three
years. **The king of Gerar is asking the man with one child to guarantee the safety of his
grandchildren.** The land Abraham has been promised is the land Abimelech is quietly taking a
mortgage against.

***"But according to the kindness that I have done to you, you shall do to me"***. The word is
**ḥesed** — loyal, obligated kindness between parties who owe each other something. It is exactly
the word Abraham used at 20:13, to Sarah, of the arrangement that produced this whole mess:
*"This is your kindness which you shall show to me. Everywhere that we go, say of me, 'He is my
brother.'"* **Abraham's ḥesed was a wife's cooperation in a lie. Abimelech's ḥesed was a wife
returned, livestock, silver, and a standing invitation to live in his country.** The same word for
both, eleven verses apart, and the narrator does not mark it.

***"Abraham said, 'I will swear.'"*** Two words in Hebrew. He does not negotiate, qualify, or (yet)
mention the well. Set it beside 14:22–23, where the Bible's first human oath was sworn in order to
*refuse* a king's goods, and beside the elaborate reasoning he gave Abimelech at 20:11–13. **Here he
consents in a syllable and produces his grievance afterward.** See [[Oaths and Vows]] and
[[Not a Thread nor a Sandal Strap]].

### The well, and a second plea of ignorance

> **25** Abraham complained to Abimelech because of a water well, which Abimelech’s servants had
> violently taken away.
> **26** Abimelech said, “I don’t know who has done this thing. You didn’t tell me, and I didn’t hear
> of it until today.”

*Hôkîaḥ* — the WEB's *"complained"* — is the verb for pressing a case, reproving, arguing a matter to
judgment; it is the same root behind Isaiah 1:18's *"let us reason together"* and Job's repeated
demand to *argue* his case with God. **This is a legal complaint, lodged at the moment the other
party most wants something.** Whether Abraham times it that way or simply cannot say it until he has
been asked for a favour, the text leaves open.

**Water is the whole economy of this country.** A well in the Beersheba basin is not an amenity; it
is the reason a household can exist south of the hills at all, and seizing one is how you evict
somebody without touching them. Abraham's grievance is that he dug and Abimelech's men took.

**And Abimelech pleads not-knowing for the second time in two chapters, and is believed for the
second time.** At 20:5 it was *"In the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands I have
done this"*, and God himself confirmed it. Here it is *"I don't know who has done this thing. You
didn't tell me, and I didn't hear of it until today"* — and this time nobody confirms it, but nobody
contradicts it either, and Abraham proceeds as though it were true. **The king of Gerar is the
Bible's standing case of a man whose servants and whose ignorance keep getting him into other
people's stories.** See [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]].

### The gifts, reversed

> **27** Abraham took sheep and cattle, and gave them to Abimelech. Those two made a covenant.

**Read 20:14 next to it.**

> **14** Abimelech took sheep and cattle, male servants and female servants, and gave them to
> Abraham, and restored Sarah, his wife, to him.

*Ṣōʾn ûbāqār* both times, *lāqaḥ … wayyittēn* — took and gave — both times, and the two men in
opposite positions. **One chapter after the king paid the patriarch for a wrong, the patriarch pays
the king for a treaty.** The gift here is not compensation; it is what a covenant costs. Abraham is
buying standing.

That is also the third distinct posture Abraham has taken toward a foreign ruler's property in eight
chapters: he *accepted* from Pharaoh and said nothing (12:16), he *refused* the king of Sodom under
oath (14:22–23), he *accepted* from Abimelech without a word (20:14) — and now he *gives*. Genesis
grades none of the four.

### Seven ewe lambs, and the only question anybody asks

> **28** Abraham set seven ewe lambs of the flock by themselves.
> **29** Abimelech said to Abraham, “What do these seven ewe lambs which you have set by themselves
> mean?”
> **30** He said, “You shall take these seven ewe lambs from my hand, that it may be a witness to me,
> that I have dug this well.”

**The seven lambs are a second transaction, not part of the first.** The covenant was already made in
verse 27; these are set apart *afterward*, and they do a different job. Abimelech has to ask what
they are for, which is the pericope's one question and the narrator's device for explaining a legal
gesture the original audience presumably knew and we do not.

**The mechanism is acceptance as acknowledgment.** *"You shall take these seven ewe lambs from my
hand"* — if Abimelech receives them, he has publicly received them *for* something, and what they
are for has been stated aloud: *"that it may be a witness to me, that I have dug this well."*
*ʿĒdâ*, **witness**, of livestock. The same idea — an object made to testify because objects outlast
memory — produces the heap of stones between Jacob and Laban (*"This heap is witness between me and
you today"*, 31:48), the stone Joshua sets up under the oak (Joshua 24:26–27), and the song God
tells Moses to teach Israel *"that this song may be a witness for me"* (Deuteronomy 31:19–21).

**Abraham does not claim the well. He claims the digging.** In the customary law this reflects,
labour is the title: the man who cut the shaft holds the water. The lambs do not buy the well back;
they put Abimelech on record that there is nothing to buy.

### *Beersheba* — a name with two etymologies in one verse

> **31** Therefore he called that place Beersheba, because they both swore an oath there.

*Bəʾēr Šebaʿ* is **well of seven** or **well of the oath**, and in Hebrew those are barely two things.
*Šebaʿ* is seven; *šābaʿ* is to swear — and the reflexive *nišbaʿ*, the form used in this very verse,
means something like **to seven oneself**. Why swearing should be sevening is not recoverable, though
the seven lambs of verse 28 are the best guess anyone has ever had, and this verse may be Genesis
making exactly that guess.

**So the naming verse gives the oath etymology while the four verses before it have staged the seven
etymology, and both are correct.** It is one of the neatest pieces of wordplay in the book, and it
works only in Hebrew.

**Genesis then names the place a second time, five chapters later, and for a different reason.** At
26:32–33 Isaac's servants strike water on the day he swears his own treaty with an Abimelech and a
Phicol, and he calls the well *Shibah* — *"Therefore the name of the city is Beersheba to this day."*
**The book explains the name twice and does not notice.** See [[Beersheba]] and
[[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].

A third notice sits behind both: at 21:14, seventeen verses before the place is named, Hagar
*"wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba."* The narrator uses the later name for the country, the
same habit that calls a town *Dan* at 14:14 several hundred years before Judges 18 names it.

### *"they returned into the land of the Philistines"*

**The word arrives here in the narrative for the first time.** *Pəlištîm* has appeared once already,
in the Table of Nations' list of Egypt's descendants (10:14), but 21:32 and 21:34 are the first time
Genesis uses it of a living kingdom, and 26:1 will call this same Abimelech *"king of the
Philistines"* outright.

The Philistines as an archaeologically visible people — Aegean-derived, on the southern coast, with
their own pottery and their pentapolis — arrive around 1200 BC, centuries after any date proposed
for Abraham, and nothing in the Iron Age Philistine world resembles this scene: no pentapolis, no
*seren*, no city of Gaza or Ashdod, just a king with a herdsman's dispute and an army officer. **The
ordinary reading is that Genesis names the region by the people who later held it**, exactly as it
does with *Dan*, and the wiki records it as an anachronism of that kind rather than as a problem to
be argued away. See [[The Land of the Philistines]].

### The tamarisk

> **33** Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and called there on the name of Yahweh, the
> Everlasting God.

***ʾĒšel*, tamarisk, occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible, and the other two are Saul's** — the
tree at Gibeah under which he sits with his spear (1 Samuel 22:6) and the tree at Jabesh under which
his bones are buried (1 Samuel 31:13). Its identification as a tamarisk is traditional and probable
rather than certain; the Septuagint read it as a *field* or *ploughland*, which is a different word
division.

**Whatever species it is, the act is the point: Abraham plants.** He has built altars (12:7, 12:8,
13:18) and pitched tents, and Genesis has been careful for nine chapters that he owns nothing —
[[The Covenant Between the Pieces]] promised the land to offspring he did not have, and he will pay
four hundred shekels for a burial cave in two chapters because a grave is the only ground he ever
holds. **A tamarisk is a slow tree.** Planting one is a claim on decades, made by a resident alien in
a country he has just contracted not to seize, on the strength of a promise about people not yet
born. The gesture and the treaty say opposite things about who owns Beersheba, and Genesis puts them
in consecutive verses.

**The later law will forbid something that looks a great deal like this.** *"You shall not plant for
yourself an Asherah of any kind of tree beside Yahweh's altar which you shall make for yourself"*
(Deuteronomy 16:21). Genesis reports Abraham planting a tree at a place where he invokes the divine
name, with no discomfort whatever — as it reports the pillars, the oaks of Mamre and Moreh, and the
altars at Bethel and Shechem that later law would also look at sideways. **The wiki records the
tension and does not resolve it**; the patriarchal narratives consistently describe a cult that
Deuteronomy would not permit, and the book shows no sign of finding this awkward. See
[[The Trees of the Patriarchs]].

### *ʾĒl ʿÔlām* — the Everlasting God

***"and called there on the name of Yahweh, the Everlasting God."*** *ʾĒl ʿÔlām*, and **it occurs
here and nowhere else in the Bible.**

It is the fourth in Genesis's series of *ʾĒl*-names, each given at a place and most of them by a
human being rather than by God:

| name | verse | given by |
|---|---|---|
| *ʾĒl ʿElyôn*, God Most High | 14:18–22 | Melchizedek, then Abram |
| *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, God who sees me | 16:13 | Hagar |
| *ʾĒl Šadday*, God Almighty | 17:1 | God, of himself |
| ***ʾĒl ʿÔlām*, the Everlasting God** | **21:33** | **Abraham** |
| *ʾĒl ʾĕlōhê Yiśrāʾēl* | 33:20 | Jacob |
| *ʾĒl Bêt-ʾĒl* | 35:7 | Jacob |

***ʿÔlām* does not mean *eternal* in the philosophical sense.** It means duration out of sight —
time running past the horizon in either direction, *of old* as readily as *forever*. What makes
Abraham's choice of it pointed is where he has heard it. **Four chapters earlier God used *ʿôlām*
four times of the covenant itself**: *"an everlasting covenant"*, *"an everlasting possession"*
(17:7, 8, 13, 19). Abraham now applies the covenant's own adjective to its maker, at the moment he
has signed a treaty acknowledging that the land belongs, for the present, to somebody else. **The
name is a statement about the timescale he thinks he is operating on.** See
[[The Everlasting God - El Olam in Genesis and Isaiah]].

The formula *qārāʾ bešēm YHWH*, **called on the name of Yahweh**, is the same one used at 4:26,
12:8, and 13:4, and it will be used of Isaac at this same place at 26:25. It is public invocation at
a fixed spot, not private prayer. See [[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]].

**And the verse holds *Yahweh* and *ʾĒl ʿÔlām* together in one breath**, which is worth flagging
against Exodus 6:3's claim that the patriarchs did not know God by the name Yahweh. Chapter 20 used
*ʾĕlōhîm* for seventeen verses and *Yahweh* once at the very end; this pericope has Abimelech say
*ʾĕlōhîm* twice and Abraham say *Yahweh* once — the same distribution, and the same standing
question. See [[The Names of God]].

### *"many days"*

> **34** Abraham lived as a foreigner in the land of the Philistines many days.

*Wayyāgor* — he sojourned; **the word that has defined him since 12:10 and will define his
descendants for four hundred years** (15:13). The pericope ends by placing him, at rest and by treaty,
in a country that is not his, in the land that is.

And then: *"After these things, God tested Abraham"* (22:1). **The last thing Genesis tells us about
Abraham before Moriah is that he had made a durable peace, planted a tree, and settled down.** The
*many days* are the width of the calm, and the next verse ends it.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — asked to swear he will not deal falsely, by the man he dealt falsely with; he agrees
  in two words, then produces a grievance, then pays for the treaty, plants a tree, and names God
  *the Everlasting God*.
- [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]] — back with his army commander, opening with *"God is with you"*
  and asking for a three-generation non-aggression clause; and pleading ignorance for the second
  time in two chapters.
- [[Phicol]] — *the captain of his army*, named twice here and once more a generation later; he says
  nothing in either scene and is present in both, which is the whole of his function.
- [[God]] — named by both parties, invoked as the guarantor of the oath, addressed by a name that
  occurs nowhere else in Scripture — and neither speaking nor acting anywhere in the pericope.
- [[Isaac]] — off-stage, unnamed, and the reason the three-generation clause matters; he will repeat
  this treaty, at this place, with these two men, at 26:26–33.
- [[Ishmael]] — off-stage, and the other person of whom this chapter says *God was with him*.
- [[Sarah]] — absent for the first time in five pericopes; the last thing said to her was Abimelech's.

## Places

- [[Beersheba]] — named here for the first time in the book's own reckoning, for an oath and for
  seven lambs at once; Abraham's base from now until Moriah, and the patriarchs' southern address.
- [[The Land of the Philistines]] — where Abimelech and Phicol go home to, and where Abraham lives as
  a foreigner *many days*; the first narrative use of the name in Genesis, and an anachronism.
- [[Gerar]] — Abimelech's town, west along the wadi, and the place both delegations set out from.
- [[The Negev]] — the dry country the well makes habitable.
- [[Canaan]] — the land promised to Abraham's offspring, in which Abraham signs a treaty as a
  resident alien.
- [[Paran]] — off-stage; the wilderness where the other son is growing up as this is agreed.

## Themes

- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the first oath in Scripture sworn *between two men*, and a place named for the

- [[Covenant]] — *bərît* used for the first time in the Bible of an agreement between two human
  beings, twice in six verses.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — a seized well, a formal complaint, a denial, a payment, and a treaty,

- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the promised land held by treaty from its present occupant, and a tree
  planted in it by a man who owns none of it.
- [[The Names of God]] — *ʾĒl ʿÔlām*, the Everlasting God: one occurrence in the Bible, and it is

- [[The Nations]] — a Philistine king reads God's favour off a foreigner's prosperity and acts on the

- [[Kingship and Empire]] — a king who negotiates rather than seizes, with his general standing

- [[War]] — Phicol's title is the only military fact in the pericope, and its whole point is that

- [[Blessing]] — *"God is with you in all that you do"*: the promise of 12:2–3 being read back to
  Abraham by one of the families of the earth.
- [[Deception]] — named by its absence, in the verb Abimelech asks Abraham to swear against.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** 21:22 opens with *"At that time"* and a new cast; 21:34 closes
  with a summary of duration; 22:1 opens with *"After these things"* and a new subject. The unit is
  clean at both ends and the plan's existing split stands. **No unchecked boxes were redrawn.** The
  one live alternative — attaching 21:22–34 to chapter 20, since it is the same king, the same
  officer's town, and arguably the same negotiation — was rejected for the reason
  [[Abraham and Abimelech]] already gives: 21:1–21 sits between them and is about something else
  entirely.
- **The doublet, stated plainly.** *"Those two made a covenant"* (21:27) and *"So they made a
  covenant at Beersheba"* (21:32) report the same act twice; 21:31 and 21:32 both fix the name.
  Source-critical readings split the pericope into an **E** treaty narrative (22–24, 27, 31a — an
  oath between kings, matching chapter 20's E material) and a **J** well narrative (25–26, 28–30,
  32–33), each with its own etymology, joined at the name. The literary reply is that the alternation
  is deliberate: an agreement is reached, a grievance is raised, consideration passes, and only then
  is a public evidentiary act performed and the site named — which is the order a legal narrative
  would take. **The wiki records both and adopts neither**, as it does for the two creation accounts
  and the two Hagar flights.
- **Is Genesis 26:26–33 the same event?** It has the same king's name, the same army commander's
  name, the same town, the same place, the same kind of dispute, an oath, and a name explained by
  *šebaʿ*. It differs in the patriarch, in the addition of *Ahuzzath his friend*, in who approaches
  whom, and in the etymology's occasion. The three standing accounts — one tradition attached to two
  patriarchs; two genuine incidents in a family that kept using the same well; a father's treaty and
  a son's renewal, which is what treaties normally needed — are all live, and Genesis's own
  *"besides the first famine that was in the days of Abraham"* (26:1) shows the book is at least
  aware that it is telling a second version. See [[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].
- **The first human covenant in the Bible, with one qualification.** *Bərît* has so far been God's
  word: with Noah (6:18; 9:9–17), with Abram (15:18), with Abraham and his offspring (17:2–21).
  21:27 and 21:32 are the first time Scripture *narrates* two human beings cutting one. The
  qualification is 14:13, where Mamre, Eshcol, and Aner *"were allies of Abram"* — Hebrew *baʿălê
  bərît*, **masters of a covenant** — which reports that such an agreement exists without ever
  showing one being made. See [[Covenant]].
- **What *ʿēdâ* is doing.** The seven lambs are not payment and not sacrifice; nothing is killed and
  no altar is mentioned. They are evidence, deliberately constructed, because in a society without
  archives the way to make a fact survive a generation is to attach it to something that can be
  pointed at afterward. Genesis is unusually explicit about the mechanism here, and the same
  explicitness recurs at 31:48 with Jacob's heap.
- **On Phicol.** *Pîkōl* has no accepted Hebrew etymology; the folk reading *pî-kōl*, **mouth of
  all** — that is, spokesman — is attractive and unprovable, and a non-Semitic (Anatolian or Aegean)
  origin has been proposed to match the Philistine setting. He is named at 21:22, 21:32, and 26:26,
  never speaks, and does nothing but stand there, which is exactly what a treaty witness of his rank
  is for. The wiki gives him a page on the same footing as [[Aner]] and [[Eshcol]] — named, recurring,
  and structurally load-bearing without being a character.
- **On the chronology of *many days*.** 21:34 is the only measure Genesis gives of the interval
  between the treaty and the Akedah, and it gives it in the vaguest available terms. Attempts to date
  the binding of Isaac from it — Isaac as a boy of thirteen, as a young man of twenty-five, as the
  thirty-seven-year-old of the rabbinic reckoning that puts Moriah immediately before Sarah's death
  at 23:1 — all begin from this verse and none is constrained by it. See [[Isaac]].
- **What the pericope does not do.** It does not have God speak; it does not mention the promise; it
  does not judge Abraham for making a treaty over land already promised to him, or for planting a
  tree where later law would object, or for accepting the second oath after the first one went the
  way chapter 20 went. **This is the fifth consecutive pericope in which Genesis declines to grade
  the person it is following** — and the last quiet one before it asks the hardest question in the
  book.
