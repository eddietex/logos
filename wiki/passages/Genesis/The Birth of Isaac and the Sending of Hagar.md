# The Birth of Isaac and the Sending of Hagar

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 21:1–21

## Summary

The promise arrives, and it costs a household.

**Twenty-five years after *"I will make of you a great nation"* and four chapters after the date was
set, the son is born in seven verses** — and then the next fourteen are about the son who is not the
heir, put out into a desert with a skin of water. Genesis puts the two halves side by side without a
seam and without a verdict, and the second half is twice as long as the first.

The chapter is the hinge of the Abraham cycle. Everything before it has been the working-out of six
words at 11:30 — *"Sarai was barren. She had no child"* — and everything after it is about what the
family does with a son it has. It is also the last of three removals that clear the field for Isaac:
[[Lot]] separated at 13:11, [[Eliezer of Damascus]] set aside at 15:4, and now
[[Ishmael]] sent away. Genesis narrates all three as departures and none of them as a punishment.

### *"Yahweh visited Sarah as he had said"*

> **1** Yahweh visited Sarah as he had said, and Yahweh did to Sarah as he had spoken.

**Six clauses in verse 1 and three of them are about the reliability of a sentence.** *As he had
said* … *as he had spoken* … and then in verse 2, *"at the set time of which God had spoken to him"*.
The birth is reported almost without event; what the verse is interested in is that the words held.

***Pāqad*, *visited*, and this is its first occurrence in Genesis.** It is not the ordinary verb for
God arriving — it means to attend to, to muster, to take note of with consequences, and it is the
word the rest of the Torah uses for the moment God turns toward a people he has left alone.
Joseph's last sentence in the book is built out of it: *"God will surely visit you, and bring you up
out of this land"* (50:24, 25), and Exodus 3:16 and 4:31 cash it. **The first thing God is said to
*visit* in Scripture is a closed womb.** See
[[Yahweh Visited Sarah - Paqad and the Visitations of God]].

**And *Yahweh* is used twice here and then not again in the pericope.** From 21:2 to 21:20 God is
*ʾĕlōhîm* throughout — *"of which God had spoken"*, *"as God had commanded"*, *"God has made me
laugh"*, *"God said to Abraham"*, *"God heard the voice of the boy"*, *"the angel of God"*, *"God
opened her eyes"*, *"God was with the boy"*. It is the same distribution as the chapter before, in
reverse: [[Abraham and Abimelech]] ran on *ʾĕlōhîm* for seventeen verses and put *Yahweh* in the
last. See [[The Names of God]].

### The most obedient seven verses in the Abraham cycle

Everything in 21:1–5 is done exactly as it was specified, and Genesis marks each one.

- **The time** — *"at the set time"* (*la-môʿēd*), which is 17:21's *"at this set time next year"* and
  18:14's *"At the set time I will return to you"*.
- **The name** — *"Abraham called his son … Isaac"*, which is 17:19's *"You shall call his name
  Isaac."*
- **The circumcision** — *"when he was eight days old, as God had commanded him"*, which is 17:12.
  **Isaac is the first person in Scripture circumcised on the eighth day**; his brother had been
  circumcised at thirteen (17:25) and his father at ninety-nine. See [[Circumcision]] and
  [[Circumcision, the Sign of the Covenant]].
- **The age** — *"Abraham was one hundred years old"*, which is 17:17's arithmetic, and the number he
  laughed at.

**Abraham does not speak in this pericope.** He names, circumcises, makes a feast, grieves, rises
early, loads a woman's shoulder, and sends her away, and the narrator does not give him one line of
dialogue in twenty-one verses. The last thing he said was a defence of himself to a foreign king
(20:11–13).

### *"God has made me laugh"*

> **6** Sarah said, “God has made me laugh. Everyone who hears will laugh with me.”
> **7** She said, “Who would have said to Abraham, that Sarah would nurse children? For I have borne
> him a son in his old age.”

**These are the last words Sarah speaks in Genesis that are not a demand, and they are the fourth
laugh in five chapters.** Abraham laughed at the arithmetic (17:17), she laughed at her own body
behind the tent flap (18:12), she denied it and was contradicted (18:15), and here she takes the
word back and makes God the subject of it: *ṣəḥōq ʿāśâ lî ʾĕlōhîm*, **laughter God has made for me**.
The name of the son is the word for what she did when she heard, and she is the one who converts it.
See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].

***"Everyone who hears will laugh with me"* is ambiguous in the Hebrew and the ambiguity is not
resolvable.** *Kol-haššōmēaʿ yiṣḥaq-lî* — the preposition can carry *with me* (the WEB's choice,
rejoicing) or *at me* (mockery). A ninety-year-old woman with a newborn has grounds to expect both,
and three verses later somebody does laugh, and it breaks the household.

Verse 7 shifts register — short cola, a rhetorical question, a rare poetic verb for *say* (*millēl*,
which otherwise appears in Job and the Psalms rather than in narrative). It is often called Sarah's
song, and it is the only thing in the Bible she says that sounds composed. *"Sarah would nurse
children"*, plural, for one child: the language of a wet nurse's proverb, not of an obstetric report.

### The feast, and one participle

> **8** The child grew, and was weaned. Abraham made a great feast on the day that Isaac was weaned.
> **9** Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham, mocking.

Weaning in the ancient Near East came at two or three years, which puts [[Ishmael]] at sixteen or
seventeen on the book's own arithmetic (16:16; 17:25; 21:5). The *mišteh gādôl* — literally a great
*drinking*-feast — is the second one in Genesis; [[Lot]] made the first for two angels the night
Sodom burned (19:3).

***Məṣaḥēq*.** One Hebrew participle, from the root behind *Isaac*, with **no object in the text**,
and the entire expulsion turns on it. The versions diverge because the Hebrew gives them nothing to
work with: the WEB has *mocking*, others *playing* or *laughing*; the Septuagint supplies *"playing
with Isaac her son"*; Galatians 4:29 makes it *persecuting*; rabbinic tradition supplies worse.
**Genesis does not say what he did.** What it says is that the son named *laughter* had a brother
caught *laughing*, and that this was enough. The wiki records the reason for the expulsion as
genuinely underdetermined by the text and declines every attempt to fill it in.

Note also what verse 9 does with names. **Sarah sees *"the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had
borne to Abraham"*** — a boy she has known for seventeen years, identified by his mother, his
mother's nationality, and his father, and not by his name.

### Nobody in this episode says Ishmael's name

**From 21:9 to the end of the pericope, Ishmael is never named.** He is *the son of Hagar the
Egyptian*, *her son*, *the son of this servant*, *the boy*, *the son of the servant*, *the child*,
and *him* — thirteen times in thirteen verses, and not once *Ishmael*. Genesis names him freely in
chapter 16 and chapter 17 and will name him again at 25:9; here, in the chapter that removes him, it
does not.

**And the name means *God hears*.** So the pericope's turn is a pun nobody in it can say out loud:
*"God heard the voice of the boy"* (21:17) is *Yišmāʿēl* unpacked into a sentence, in an episode that
has stopped using the word. He was named at 16:11 because God heard his mother's affliction; he was
conceded at 17:20 because God heard his father's plea (*šəmaʿtîkā*); here the name is cashed for the
third time, on his own account, by a narrator who withholds it.

### *"Cast out this servant and her son!"*

> **10** Therefore she said to Abraham, “Cast out this servant and her son! For the son of this
> servant will not be heir with my son, Isaac.”

***Gārēš*, cast out** — the verb used of the man driven from the garden (3:24) and by [[Cain]] of his
own sentence (4:14). It is the language of expulsion from a place one belongs to, and it is the
Torah's word for what happens to a divorced wife (Leviticus 21:7, 14; 22:13). [[Sarah]] uses no
names: *this servant*, *her son*, *the son of this servant*, against *my son, Isaac*. **She has never
once said Hagar's name in the Bible**, in either chapter.

**Her reason is inheritance, and it is a legal reason.** *Lōʾ yîraš* — *he will not inherit* — and
the second-millennium law codes are on her mind and not on her side: Lipit-Ishtar and the Code of
Hammurabi both make provision for the sons of a slave woman acknowledged by the father, and
Deuteronomy 21:15–17 will eventually protect the firstborn of an unloved wife by name. Ishmael was
circumcised into the household at 17:23 and named by Abraham himself at 16:15. **Sarah is asking for
something the surrounding law would not have given her, and she gets it.**

> **11** The thing was very grievous in Abraham’s sight on account of his son.

*Wayyēraʿ … məʾōd* — **it was very evil in his eyes**, the strongest thing the narrator says about
anyone's feelings in the Abraham cycle. And it is *"on account of his son"* only; God's reply will
correct the count.

### *"listen to her voice"* — the third time

> **12** God said to Abraham, “Don’t let it be grievous in your sight because of the boy, and because
> of your servant. In all that Sarah says to you, listen to her voice. For your offspring will be
> named through Isaac.
> **13** I will also make a nation of the son of the servant, because he is your child.”

**Genesis has used *listen to the voice of* your wife twice before, and both times it was the setup
for a disaster.** *"Because you have listened to your wife's voice, and ate from the tree"* (3:17,
in the curse); *"Abram listened to the voice of Sarai"* (16:2, and the result is the woman now being
sent away). **The third time, God commands it.** The wiki flags this without resolving it: the same
idiom carries a curse, a mistake, and a divine instruction inside eighteen chapters, and Genesis
never comments on the pattern. See [[Listening to the Voice - Genesis 3 and Genesis 16]].

**God's speech corrects Abraham's grief in its first clause.** Abraham grieved *"on account of his
son"*; God says *"because of the boy, and because of your servant"* — putting Hagar back into a
sentence her owner and her master had both left her out of.

***"For your offspring will be named through Isaac."*** *Kî bə-Yiṣḥāq yiqqārēʾ ləkā zāraʿ* — the
clause that the New Testament quotes twice as the definition of election (Romans 9:7; Hebrews 11:18).
It does not say Ishmael is not Abraham's offspring; the next verse says he is (*"because he is your
child"*). It says the line is *named* through Isaac. See [[Call and Election]].

**And the compensation is the promise of 12:2 given to the wrong son.** *"I will also make a nation
of the son of the servant"* — *gôy*, the word of *"I will make of you a great nation"*, repeated at
21:18 with *great* attached. Genesis holds both promises at once and never explains how they sit
together.

### The wilderness

> **14** Abraham rose up early in the morning, and took bread and a bottle of water, and gave it to
> Hagar, putting it on her shoulder; and gave her the child, and sent her away. She departed, and
> wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba.

***"rose up early in the morning"*** — *wayyaškēm ʾAbrāhām babbōqer*, and **the identical Hebrew
clause opens Genesis 22:3** (which the WEB renders *"Abraham rose early in the morning"*), where he
takes the other son to a mountain. Both times he is silent, both times he
loads what the journey needs, both times a child is nearly lost, and both times a voice from the sky
stops it at the last moment. See
[[Two Sons Nearly Lost - The Sending of Ishmael and the Binding of Isaac]].

**The provisions are almost nothing.** Bread and a skin of water, from a man who at 13:2 was *"very
rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold"* and who had just been given sheep, cattle, and servants
by Abimelech (20:14). Genesis reports the amount and says nothing about it.

**The Hebrew of verse 14 is famously awkward**, and the WEB's *"putting it on her shoulder; and gave
her the child"* is one way through it: the syntax can be read as putting the child on her shoulder
along with the provisions, which is how the Septuagint and much of the tradition took it, and which
fits a toddler rather than a teenager. Verse 15's *"she put the child under one of the shrubs"* pulls
the same way. **The wiki records the difficulty and does not resolve it** — the chronology of 16:16,
17:25 and 21:5 is explicit, and so is the picture the verbs paint. See [[The Two Flights of Hagar]].

***"wandered"*** — *wattētaʿ*, from *tāʿâ*, the verb for straying. **Abraham used it of his own call
one chapter earlier**: *"When God caused me to wander from my father's house"* (20:13). He described
the journey God sent him on with the word the narrator now uses for the journey he sends her on.

### *"Don't let me see the death of the child"*

> **16** She went and sat down opposite him, a good way off, about a bow shot away. For she said,
> “Don’t let me see the death of the child.” She sat over against him, and lifted up her voice, and
> wept.

**A bow shot** — and eight verses later the boy *"became, as he grew up, an archer"*. Genesis measures
the distance at which she gives him up in the unit of the life he will have.

**She does the seeing.** [[Hagar]] is the woman who named God *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, *a God who sees* (16:13),
and who was found by looking at a spring; here the one thing she asks is not to see. She puts the
child down, walks away, sits opposite, and weeps — a sequence of five verbs in which she is the
subject of every one. In Genesis 16 she was the object of every verb until the wilderness. See
[[Seeing and Being Seen]].

> **17** God heard the voice of the boy. The angel of God called to Hagar out of the sky, and said to
> her, “What ails you, Hagar? Don’t be afraid. For God has heard the voice of the boy where he is.

**The mother weeps and the son is heard.** Verse 16 ends with her voice; verse 17 opens with his.
Genesis does not report a word or a cry from him — it reports that God heard one. He does not speak
in either of his chapters.

**Three things in this verse are firsts or near-firsts.**

- ***The angel of God*** — *malʾak ʾĕlōhîm*, not the *malʾak Yahweh* who found her at 16:7. The
  chapter's divine name holds even here. See [[The Angel of Yahweh]].
- ***Out of the sky*** — *min-haššāmayim*, and **this is the first time in Scripture a divine voice
  calls from heaven**. The only other occurrences in Genesis are 22:11 and 22:15, at the other son's
  altar. In chapter 16 the messenger *found* her at a spring, on the ground; here he calls from above,
  and she is not approached.
- ***"What ails you, Hagar?"*** — she is addressed **by name**, for the second time in her life and
  the second time by a divine messenger. Nobody in Abraham's household has ever said it.

***"where he is"*** — *ba-ʾăšer hû šām*. Three words that the rabbinic tradition made a great deal of
(*Genesis Rabbah* 53:14; *b. Rosh Hashanah* 16b): God hears the boy as he is at this moment, and not
on account of what his descendants will later do to Israel. It is one of the most generous readings
in the midrash and it is built on a phrase Genesis leaves undecorated.

### The well, and the boy who lives

> **19** God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. She went, filled the bottle with water, and
> gave the boy drink.

**The rescue is not a new well; it is her eyes.** *Wayyipqaḥ ʾĕlōhîm ʾet-ʿênêhā* — the water was
there. Genesis has opened eyes once before, at 3:7, and to worse effect; the next time anyone's eyes
are opened to something already present will be Elisha's servant seeing the hills full of horses
(2 Kings 6:17). **The woman who gave God the name *the God who sees* is the one person in Scripture
whose seeing God has to repair.**

> **20** God was with the boy, and he grew. He lived in the wilderness, and became, as he grew up, an
> archer.
> **21** He lived in the wilderness of Paran. His mother took a wife for him out of the land of Egypt.

***"God was with the boy"*** — and **this is the first time in Genesis the formula is used at all**.
It is said of Ishmael before it is said of anyone in the covenant line: Abimelech will say it of
Abraham in the very next verse (21:22), Laban's people of Isaac at 26:28, God to Jacob at 28:15, and
the narrator four times of Joseph in Egypt (39:2, 3, 21, 23). **The Bible's formula for providential
favour is used first of the son who was just disinherited.**

***His mother took a wife for him.*** **No other mother in the Bible does this.** Abraham sends a
servant to find Isaac's wife (24:4), Isaac instructs Jacob where to find his (28:1–2), Judah takes a
wife for Er (38:6) — it is a father's act every other time it happens. Hagar has no husband, no
household, and no man to do it, so she does it, and Genesis reports it without a comment in the same
flat voice it uses for the archery.

**And the wife is Egyptian.** The slave taken out of Egypt (probably in Pharaoh's payment for Sarai
at 12:16) ends her story choosing an Egyptian daughter-in-law in a desert on the Egyptian frontier.
Ishmael's line begins as three-quarters Egyptian, and the traders who carry Joseph *down to Egypt* at
37:25–28 are called Ishmaelites. See [[Egypt]] and [[The Nations]].

### What the pericope refuses to do

**It does not grade anyone.** Sarah's demand is harsh, legally overreaching, and endorsed by God.
Abraham's compliance is silent, under-provisioned, and commanded. Hagar's expulsion is unjust by the
standards of the surrounding law and is the making of her son. Genesis says the thing was *very evil
in Abraham's eyes*, has God tell him not to feel that way, and then follows the woman out into the
desert for eight verses and stays with her. **The narrative's sympathy is not where the plot's
authority is**, and the book does not reconcile them. This is the fifth consecutive pericope in which
Genesis declines to grade the person it is following.

## People

- [[Isaac]] — born at the set time, named as instructed, circumcised on the eighth day, and the first
  person in Scripture to receive the sign at the age the law will later fix. He does nothing in the
  pericope but grow and be weaned, which is the most any of Genesis's least active patriarch usually
  does.
- [[Sarah]] — she laughs a fourth time and this time makes God the subject of the verb, speaks the
  only composed thing she says in the Bible, and then demands an expulsion in language that never
  uses either name. She is not mentioned again after verse 12.
- [[Hagar]] — sent away rather than fleeing, silent until the desert, and then the subject of every
  verb in it. She is addressed by name from the sky, has her eyes opened to water that was already
  there, and ends the episode free, in her own wilderness, taking a wife for her son.
- [[Ishmael]] — never named in the thirteen verses that remove him, though his name means *God hears*
  and the turn of the story is *"God has heard the voice of the boy"*. He does not speak; God hears
  him anyway. He grows up an archer, at a bowshot's distance from where his mother laid him down.
- [[Abraham]] — silent for twenty-one verses. He names, circumcises, feasts, grieves *very*, is told
  not to, rises early, loads a shoulder, and sends. Genesis gives him no line and no reaction after
  verse 11.
- [[God]] — *Yahweh* twice in verse 1 and *ʾĕlōhîm* for the rest. He keeps a dated promise to the day,
  endorses a demand the surrounding law would have refused, corrects the grieving father's arithmetic
  by adding the slave back into it, hears a boy who says nothing, and is *with* him.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — here *the angel of God*, and calling **out of the sky** rather than
  finding her at a spring: the first voice from heaven in Scripture.

## Places

- [[Beersheba]] — *"the wilderness of Beersheba"*, where Hagar wanders and the water runs out; the
  place is named here before it is named, since the etiology and the oath that give it its name come
  eleven verses later at 21:31.
- [[Paran]] — the great desert of the eastern Sinai where Ishmael settles and his descendants remain;
  the wilderness Israel will cross, send its spies out of, and be judged in.
- [[Egypt]] — his mother's country and his wife's, and the direction of everything in Hagar's story:
  she fled toward it in chapter 16 and reaches back into it in chapter 21.
- [[Gerar]] — where Abraham was living at 20:1 and still is at 21:34; the chapter does not say where
  Isaac was born, and Gerar is the last address given.
- [[Beer Lahai Roi]] — the other well, from the other flight, and where Isaac will live for the rest
  of his life (24:62; 25:11).
- [[Canaan]] — off-stage. The land of the promise is not mentioned in the pericope at all, and the
  eight verses that matter happen outside it.

## Themes

- [[Barrenness]] — the problem the book has carried since 11:30, closed in one verse, and the only
  thing about it the narrator dwells on is that the words held.
- [[Covenant]] — *"your offspring will be named through Isaac"*, the sharpest statement in Genesis of
  which son the line runs through, spoken to settle a domestic dispute.
- [[Call and Election]] — a blessing and a covenant separated as cleanly as the book ever separates
  them: the excluded son gets a nation, a promise, God's presence, and no covenant.
- [[Circumcision]] — the eighth day, performed for the first time, on the first child born inside the
  covenant of chapter 17.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — a slave and her son expelled at the demand of the woman who owns her,
  with a divine endorsement and two loaves of bread.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — she asks not to see her child die; God opens her eyes; and she is the
  woman who named him *a God who sees*.
- [[Blessing]] — *"I will make him a great nation"*, the promise of 12:2 in the same word, given to
  the son who has just been disinherited.
- [[Faith]] — nowhere named, and the whole point of verse 1: the promise is kept on the date it was
  given, which is the fact Romans 4 and Hebrews 11 both build on.
- [[The Nations]] — a nation founded in a desert by a slave, with an Egyptian mother and an Egyptian
  wife, and God with it.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the heir is born to a man who owns none of the country, and the other son
  is sent into a wilderness that will belong to his descendants.
- [[Life and Death]] — a child under a bush with the water gone, and a mother sitting a bowshot away
  so as not to watch.
- [[Marriage]] — the last verse: a mother arranging her son's marriage, which is nowhere else a
  mother's act.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — a voice from the sky, for the first time in Scripture, and it delivers a
  question, a reassurance, an instruction, and a promise in that order.

- 1 Samuel 1:19–20; 2:21 — Hannah, another closed womb, and *"Yahweh visited Hannah"*.
- 2 Kings 6:17 — *"Yahweh, please open his eyes, that he may see"*, the same act as 21:19.
- Isaiah 54:1 — the barren woman with more children than the married one, quoted at Galatians 4:27.
- Romans 4:19–21 — *"the deadness of Sarah's womb"*, and the promise held to.
- Romans 9:7–9 — *"in Isaac will your offspring be called"*, quoting 21:12 as the definition of
  election.
- Galatians 4:21–31 — the allegory, quoting 21:10 and glossing 21:9 as persecution.
- Hebrews 11:11–12, 17–18 — Sarah receiving power to conceive, and *"in Isaac will your offspring be
  called"* quoted a second time, against the binding.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's unit of 21:1–21 stands. There is a real seam at 21:8 —
  verses 1–7 are the birth notice and 8–21 the expulsion, and the two halves have different casts, and
  a defensible plan would split them. The wiki keeps them together because the second half is caused
  by the first (the weaning feast is Isaac's, the demand is about Isaac's inheritance, and the pun that
  triggers it is Isaac's name), and because 21:22 begins something genuinely separate, with a new cast
  and a treaty. Nothing downstream in the checklist was redrawn.
- **The chronology and the toddler.** Ishmael is at least sixteen at a weaning feast on the book's own
  numbers (86 at 16:16, thirteen at 17:25, and Abraham a hundred at 21:5), and verses 14–15 describe a
  child carried on a shoulder and laid under a bush. The classical source-critical answer is that
  21:8–21 is E's version of a story J tells in chapter 16 and knows nothing of chapter 17's
  chronology, which is P's. The literary answer is that *yeled* is used loosely and that the verbs
  describe a mother's despair rather than a child's size. **The wiki records both and adopts neither**,
  on the same footing as the two creation accounts and the two Adam registers. See
  [[The Two Flights of Hagar]].
- **Whether Genesis 21:9's *məṣaḥēq* has an object.** The Masoretic text gives the participle bare.
  The Septuagint and the Vulgate supply *with Isaac her son*, which several modern translations
  restore on the strength of it; Galatians 4:29 reads *persecuting*; the midrash variously supplies
  idolatry, bloodshed, and an attempt on Isaac's life (*Genesis Rabbah* 53:11), all of them
  manufacturing a proportionate cause for a disproportionate response. **The wiki keeps the bare
  participle and says the cause is not recoverable.** What is recoverable is that it is the same root
  as the name of the child at the centre of the feast.
- **What the law would have said.** Sarah's *"will not be heir"* is a legal claim, and the legal
  material that survives from the period does not support it. The Code of Hammurabi §§170–171 provides
  that a father who has acknowledged the sons of a slave woman gives them a share, and that even
  unacknowledged sons and their mother go free; Deuteronomy 21:15–17 will forbid preferring the son of
  a loved wife over the firstborn of an unloved one. Ishmael was acknowledged as thoroughly as a son
  can be — named by Abraham (16:15), circumcised into the covenant household (17:23), and asked for by
  his father in prayer (17:18). **The demand is an overreach, God endorses it, and Genesis does not
  reconcile the two.** The nearest the text comes is 21:13's *"because he is your child"*, which
  concedes exactly what Sarah denied.
- **On the divine names.** *Yahweh* twice in 21:1 and *ʾĕlōhîm* everywhere else, including *the angel
  of God* at 21:17 against *Yahweh's angel* at 16:7. Documentary readings take 21:1a and 21:2b as J or
  P and 21:6–21 as the second major E block, continuous with chapter 20 and with 22:1–14. The
  distribution is verifiable from the raw text and is recorded here as the standard critical account;
  what it does not decide is whether chapters 16 and 21 are one tradition told twice or two events, and
  the wiki does not decide it either. See [[The Names of God]].
- **On "where he is" (21:17).** *Genesis Rabbah* 53:14 and *b. Rosh Hashanah* 16b both read the phrase
  as a ruling on how God judges: by a person's condition at the moment, not by what they or their
  descendants will become. The passage is one of the sources for the rabbinic principle that a person
  is judged *ba-ʾăšer hû šām*. This is reception and is recorded as such, but it is unusually close to
  what the verse actually says.
- **On Islamic reception.** The well of 21:19 is identified in Islamic tradition with **Zamzam** at
  Mecca, and Hagar's search for water is re-enacted in the *saʿy* between Ṣafā and Marwa on the hajj;
  Ismāʿīl becomes the ancestor of the northern Arabs and, with Ibrāhīm, the builder of the Kaʿba
  (Qurʾān 2:127). The geography is not Genesis's — Paran is in the Sinai — and the wiki records the
  tradition as reception rather than as a reading of this text. Genesis 16:14's Beer Lahai Roi is
  identified with Zamzam in the same tradition; see [[Hagar]].
- **The three removals.** Lot goes east at 13:11, Eliezer is set aside at 15:4, Ishmael is sent at
  21:14. Each is a plausible heir, each leaves without a quarrel that Genesis blames on him, and each
  departure is narrated as a fact rather than as a judgment. After this chapter Abraham has one son in
  the story, and the next thing that happens is that God asks for him.
