# Jacob Blesses His Sons

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 49:1–33

## Summary

**A dying man says one true thing about each of his twelve sons, and the book calls all twelve of them
blessings.**

Genesis 49 is **the longest sustained poem in the book** — twenty-five verses of verse (49:3–27) set
inside eight verses of prose frame (49:1–2, 28–33) — and it is the only place in Genesis where the
narrative stops entirely and hands the floor to a single speaker for a whole chapter. **It is also the
last thing [[Jacob]] does.** Verse 33 has him draw his feet up onto the bed and die in the same
sentence that reports him finishing.

**The chapter does three things in sequence.** **49:1–2 summons all twelve sons and announces what
kind of speech this will be** — *"that which will happen to you **in the days to come**"*. **49:3–27
delivers twelve sayings of wildly uneven length**, from Judah's five verses and Joseph's five down to
Asher's single line. **49:28–33 closes the frame**: the narrator's summary (28), a burial instruction
naming five graves (29–32), and a death (33).

**What makes it strange is verse 28.** *"All these are the twelve tribes of Israel, and this is what
their father spoke to them and **blessed** them. He **blessed** everyone according to his blessing."*
**Three of the twelve sayings are not blessings by any ordinary reading.** [[Reuben]] is told *"you
shall not excel"*; [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]] are told *"**Cursed** be their anger"* and *"I will divide
them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel"*; [[Issachar]] is told he will become *"a servant doing
forced labor"*. **Genesis puts the word *blessed* over the top of all of it and does not explain
itself.** See [[Blessing]] and [[Curse]].

**Read against the pericope before it, the chapter is the second half of one deathbed and the reversal
of its terms.** In [[Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh]] a blind grandfather adopted two boys and
gave the greater share to the younger, and **nobody lost anything** — *"He also will become a people,
and he also will be great"* (48:19). **Here the losses are the point.** 48:5 measured the adoption
against *"[[Reuben]] and [[Simeon]]"*, the firstborn and the second; **four verses later the poem takes
the rank off both of them by name**, and 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 reads the two chapters together exactly so:
*"his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph."* See
[[Two Tribes in Place of One - The Adoption of Ephraim and Manasseh]] and
[[Birthright and the Firstborn]].

### *"In the days to come"*

> **1** Jacob called to his sons, and said: “Gather yourselves together, that I may tell you that which
> will happen to you in the days to come.
> **2** Assemble yourselves, and hear, you sons of Jacob. Listen to Israel, your father.

***Bəʾaḥărît hayyāmîm*** — **literally *in the after-part of the days* — and this is its first
occurrence in the Bible.** **The phrase will be the standing formula of prophetic futurity**: Balaam
uses it of the star out of Jacob (Numbers 24:14), Moses of Israel's apostasy (Deuteronomy 4:30;
31:29), Isaiah and Micah of the mountain of Yahweh's house established in the top of the mountains
(Isaiah 2:2; Micah 4:1), Hosea of David their king (Hosea 3:5), Daniel twice of what is to come
(Daniel 2:28; 10:14). **Genesis puts it in the mouth of a dying man in Egypt, and it is the only
occurrence in the Torah's narrative rather than in a poem or a warning.** Filed as
[[In the Days to Come - Aharit Hayyamim from Genesis 49 to the Prophets]]; see
[[Prophecy and Vision]].

**Two verbs of summoning and two names for the father, in two verses.** *Gather yourselves*
(*hēʾāsəpû*) and *assemble yourselves* (*hiqqābṣû*) — **and *hēʾāsēp* is the same root that closes the
chapter twice**: *"I am to be **gathered** to my people"* (49:29), *"was **gathered** to his people"*
(49:33). **The word that opens the summons is the word that ends the man.** See
[[Gathered to His People]].

***"You sons of Jacob. Listen to Israel, your father."*** **The name-switch of 48:2 done again and
inverted**: there *Jacob* was told and *Israel* sat up; here the sons are *Jacob's* and the father they
are to hear is *Israel*. **Across the chapter the two names fall five times each**, and **twice in a
single line**: *"I will divide them in **Jacob**, and scatter them in **Israel**"* (49:7), *"the Mighty
One of **Jacob** … the stone of **Israel**"* (49:24). See
[[Renamed and Still Called Jacob - The Two Namings of Israel]].

### Reuben — the firstborn, in three lines and a turned back

> **3** “Reuben, you are my firstborn, my might, and the beginning of my strength; excelling in
> dignity, and excelling in power.
> **4** Boiling over like water, you shall not excel; because you went up to your father’s bed, then
> defiled it. He went up to my couch.

**Verse 3 is the fullest statement of the rights of a firstborn anywhere in Genesis**, and **verse 4
removes them in four Hebrew words.** ***Rēʾšît ʾônî*** — *the first of my vigour* — **is the technical
phrase Deuteronomy 21:17 uses in the law that forbids exactly what Jacob is doing**: *"he shall
acknowledge the firstborn … by giving him a double portion … for he is the beginning of his strength;
the right of the firstborn is his."* **The wiki records the collision and does not resolve it**:
Genesis narrates a patriarch overriding the rule, and Deuteronomy legislates against the override,
using the same words. See [[Loved and Hated - Genesis 29 and Deuteronomy 21.15-17]].

***Paḥaz kammayim*** — **WEB *"Boiling over like water"*** — **is a hapax**, and the versions divide
between *unstable*, *reckless*, *wanton*, and *frothing*. **All of them are readings of a word that
occurs once.**

**The grammar of 49:4 is the harshest thing in the chapter.** **Jacob addresses Reuben in the second
person — *you went up to your father's bed* — and then, in the last clause, stops addressing him**:
***"He went up to my couch."*** **The father turns from *you* to *he* mid-verse, and speaks about his
son to the room.** Genesis offers no comment, and the effect is unmistakable.

**The crime is Genesis 35:22**, four words of narrative — *"Reuben went and lay with Bilhah his
father's concubine, and Israel heard of it"* — **followed by a sentence break that the Hebrew
manuscripts mark as a deliberate gap.** **Thirteen chapters later the heard-of-it finally speaks.** See
[[Lying with the Father's Concubine - A Claim on the Succession]] and [[Bilhah]].

### Simeon and Levi — a curse on the anger, and a sentence on the men

> **5** “Simeon and Levi are brothers. Their swords are weapons of violence.
> **6** My soul, don’t come into their council. My glory, don’t be united to their assembly; for in
> their anger they killed men. In their self-will they hamstrung cattle.
> **7** Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel. I will divide them
> in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel.

***"Simeon and Levi are brothers"*** — **the only saying in the poem addressed to two sons at once**,
and the observation is not affectionate. **All twelve are brothers; these two are *brothers* in the
sense that they act as one**, which is what Genesis 34:25 said of them (*"Simeon and Levi, Dinah's
brothers, each took his sword"*).

***Məkērōtêhem***, **WEB *"their swords"*, is the chapter's most contested word.** The root suggests
*mkr*, *to sell*, giving *their wares* or *their bargains*; a Greek loan (*machaira*) gives *swords*; a
different vocalisation gives *their dwellings* or *their plans*. **Every English version picks one and
none can defend it from Hebrew usage, because the word occurs once.**

***"In their self-will they hamstrung cattle."*** **Genesis 34 does not report this.** It reports that
they killed every male, took [[Dinah]] out of Shechem's house, and that the other brothers *"took their
flocks, their herds, their donkeys"* (34:28) — **taking livestock, not crippling it.** **Hamstringing
is the deliberate destruction of an animal's usefulness**, and it is what Joshua does to captured
horses (Joshua 11:6, 9) and David to chariot teams (2 Samuel 8:4). **Jacob's deathbed account of
Shechem is harsher than the narrator's, and Genesis lets both stand.** Filed under
[[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]].

***"Cursed be their anger."*** **He curses the *anger*, not the men** — ***ʾārûr ʾappām*** — **and then
sentences the men anyway**: *"I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel."* **The
distinction is real and the outcome is the same.** Compare 34:30, where his objection to the massacre
was that it had made him *"a stench to the inhabitants of the land"*; **on his deathbed the objection
has become moral.**

**And the sentence lands on the two tribes in opposite ways.** **[[Simeon]] is scattered by
absorption** — Joshua 19:1, 9 gives his inheritance *"in the middle of the inheritance of the children
of Judah"*, because *"the part of the children of Judah was too much for them"* — **and the tribe
vanishes from the later lists.** **[[Levi]] is scattered by appointment**: no territory at all, and
forty-eight cities distributed through every other tribe (Joshua 21; Numbers 35:1–8). **The same word
describes a disappearance and a priesthood.** **Exodus 32:26–29 is the hinge — the Levites take up the
sword again, this time at Moses' order, and are ordained for it.** Filed on
[[Simeon and Levi - The Sword at Shechem and the Deathbed Curse]], which this pericope completes; see
[[Priesthood]] and [[Retribution]].

### Judah — the lion, the scepter, and the crux

> **8** “Judah, your brothers will praise you. Your hand will be on the neck of your enemies. Your
> father’s sons will bow down before you.
> **9** Judah is a lion’s cub. From the prey, my son, you have gone up. He stooped down, he crouched as
> a lion, as a lioness. Who will rouse him up?
> **10** The scepter will not depart from Judah, nor the ruler’s staff from between his feet, until he
> comes to whom it belongs. To him will the obedience of the peoples be.
> **11** Binding his foal to the vine, his donkey’s colt to the choice vine; he has washed his garments
> in wine, his robes in the blood of grapes.
> **12** His eyes will be red with wine, his teeth white with milk.

***"Judah, your brothers will praise you"*** — ***yəhûdâ ʾattâ yôdûkā ʾaḥeykā*** — **the second pun on
the name, and the only one of the twelve names Genesis glosses twice.** [[Leah]] coined it at 29:35
(*"This time I will praise Yahweh"*, *ʾôdeh*); **the father re-coins it here and changes the object of
the praising from God to the man.** See [[Names and Naming]].

***"Your father's sons will bow down before you."*** **[[Joseph]] dreamed this and it came true about
him** (37:7–10; 42:6; 43:26; 44:14). **Here the same sentence is spoken over a different brother, in
Joseph's hearing, by the father who rebuked him for the dream** (37:10). **Genesis does not remark on
it, and the two claims sit in the same book without adjudication**: Joseph gets the double portion
(48:5) and Judah gets the scepter. **1 Chronicles 5:2 is the only verse in Scripture that states both
and divides them** — *"Judah prevailed above his brothers, and from him came the prince; but the
birthright was Joseph's."* See [[This Dreamer Comes - Joseph's Dreams and Their Fulfilment]].

**The lion is the poem's most durable image.** ***Gûr ʾaryēh*** — *a lion's cub* — **growing through the
verse into *ʾaryēh* and then *lābîʾ*, the full-grown lion and the lioness**, and ending on a question
nobody answers: *"Who will rouse him up?"* **Balaam will use the same line of Israel twice**
(Numbers 23:24; 24:9), **Hosea of God himself** (Hosea 5:14), **and Revelation 5:5 of one person**:
*"the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has overcome."* Filed as
[[The Lion of Judah - Genesis 49.9 and Revelation 5.5]].

**Verse 10 is the most disputed sentence in Genesis.** The Hebrew is ***ʿad kî-yābōʾ šîlōh***, and the
four verses of English above conceal four incompatible readings of those three words:

| reading | how *šîlōh* is taken | rendered |
|---|---|---|
| ***šellōh*** — *that which is his* | a relative + suffix | **WEB: *"until he comes to whom it belongs"*** |
| ***šay lōh*** — *tribute to him* | two words | *"until tribute comes to him"* (RSV margin, many moderns) |
| **Shiloh** | the place name | *"until he comes to Shiloh"* (Joshua 18:1's sanctuary) |
| **a title** | a personal name of the coming one | *"until Shiloh come"* (KJV; the rabbinic and older Christian reading) |

**The Septuagint reads the first** — *heōs ean elthē ta apokeimena autō*, *until the things stored up
for him come* — **and Ezekiel 21:27 is the closest thing in Scripture to a quotation**: *"until he
comes whose right it is; and I will give it to him."* **The wiki sets out the four and adopts the
WEB's**, noting that all four have been messianic in reception history and that the Hebrew does not
settle it. Filed as [[The Scepter and Shiloh - Genesis 49.10 and Its Readings]]; see
[[Kingship and Empire]].

***"Binding his foal to the vine … washed his garments in wine."*** **A man so rich in vines that he
tethers a donkey to one and launders in the vintage.** **Read as plenty it is the most extravagant
promise in the chapter; read forward it is not comfortable.** **Isaiah 63:1–3 puts a figure in
garments stained red from a winepress and calls it the day of vengeance**, and **Revelation 19:13,
15 clothes the rider in *"a garment sprinkled with blood"* and has him tread *"the winepress of the
fierceness of the wrath of God"*. **Genesis 49:11 is the seed of both images and commits to
neither.** See [[The Vine and the Cup]].

**Two of the Gospels' entry scenes are downstream of this verse.** ***"Binding his foal … his donkey's
colt"*** **stands behind Zechariah 9:9's king *"riding on a donkey, even on a colt the foal of a
donkey"***, which Matthew 21:5 and John 12:15 quote at the triumphal entry. **The wiki records the
image as a shared one and not as a citation**: Matthew quotes Zechariah, and Zechariah does not quote
Genesis.

### Zebulun and Issachar — the sea and the corvée

> **13** “Zebulun will dwell at the haven of the sea. He will be for a haven of ships. His border will
> be on Sidon.
> **14** “Issachar is a strong donkey, lying down between the saddlebags.
> **15** He saw a resting place, that it was good, the land, that it was pleasant. He bows his shoulder
> to the burden, and becomes a servant doing forced labor.

**[[Zebulun]] is given ten Hebrew words and a third pun on his name** — *yizbōl*, *will dwell*, from
the *zābal* his mother heard at 30:20. **He is also given a coastline he never gets.** **Joshua
19:10–16 lays his territory inland in lower Galilee and does not reach the sea**, and the border does
not touch [[Sidon (city)]]. **The wiki notes the mismatch, which neither Genesis nor Joshua
remarks on**, and declines to harmonise it.

***"Zebulun … Issachar."*** **The birth order is reversed.** [[Issachar]] is [[Leah]]'s fifth son and
Jacob's ninth (30:17–18); Zebulun is her sixth and Jacob's tenth (30:19–20). **The poem puts the
younger first, in a chapter otherwise scrupulous about rank** — and **Deuteronomy 33:18 keeps the same
inversion**: *"Rejoice, Zebulun, in your going out; and Issachar, in your tents."*

**Issachar's saying is the only one in the poem that is unambiguously bad and not a punishment.**
***Ḥămōr gārem*** — *a bony donkey*, meaning *raw-boned*, *strong* — **and the strength is the setup for
the humiliation.** **He sees that rest is good and the land is pleasant, and trades his back for
them**: ***mas-ʿōbēd***, *a serving levy*. **That noun is the Egyptian bondage's own word** —
Exodus 1:11 sets *"taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens"*, *śārê missîm* —
**and it is what Solomon will put the surviving Canaanites under** (1 Kings 9:21). **The one tribe
told in advance that it will choose comfort and pay in forced labour.** See
[[Slavery and Servitude]] and [[Work]].

**Judges 5:15 is the counter-evidence and the wiki records it**: *"the princes of Issachar were with
Deborah … into the valley they rushed forth at his feet."* **The tribe the poem calls a lying-down
donkey is in Deborah's song among the tribes that came.**

### Dan, and the sentence that interrupts

> **16** “Dan will judge his people, as one of the tribes of Israel.
> **17** Dan will be a serpent on the trail, an adder in the path, That bites the horse’s heels, so
> that his rider falls backward.
> **18** I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh.

***Dān yādîn*** — **the third name-pun**, and the one [[Rachel]] built into the name at 30:6
(*"God has judged me"*). ***"As one of the tribes of Israel"*** **is a small, strange reassurance**:
the son of a maidservant is told he counts.

**Then the poem's only animal of ambush.** ***Šəpîpōn***, *adder*, **is a hapax**, and the tactic
described is a small thing bringing down a large one from below — **which is how [[Dan (person)]]'s
tribe fights in Judges.** **Samson, the one judge Dan produces, wins every fight by trickery and dies
pulling a building down on himself** (Judges 13–16). **Judges 18 then has six hundred armed Danites
take a town by surprise and set up a graven image in it** — *"and they set up for themselves Micah's
engraved image"* (18:31). **Dan is the first tribe in Scripture to establish an idolatrous shrine**,
and [[Dan (city)]] is where Jeroboam later puts one of his two calves (1 Kings 12:29). **Revelation
7:5–8 lists twelve tribes and Dan is not among them.** See [[Idolatry]] and
[[Bethel from Jacob's Pillar to Jeroboam's Calf]].

**Verse 18 stops the poem dead.** ***Lîšûʿātəkā qiwwîtî YHWH*** — **five Hebrew words, first person,
addressed to God, in a chapter otherwise addressed to sons.** **It is the only time the divine name
Yahweh appears in the chapter**, and **the first occurrence in the Bible of the noun *yəšûʿâ*,
*salvation*** — the word behind *Joshua*, *Isaiah*, *Hosea* and *Jesus*, and the standing vocabulary of
the Psalms. **Genesis gives it no introduction and no object.** **Whether it belongs to Dan's saying,
divides the poem, or is a dying man's breath taken in the middle of a long speech, the text does not
say.** Filed as [[I Have Waited for Your Salvation - The First Yeshuah in Scripture]]; see
[[Hope]] and [[Prayer]].

### Gad, Asher, Naphtali — three lines and no narrative

> **19** “A troop will press on Gad, but he will press on their heel.
> **20** “Asher’s food will be rich. He will produce royal dainties.
> **21** “Naphtali is a doe set free, who bears beautiful fawns.

**[[Gad]]'s line is the densest wordplay in the Bible.** ***Gād gədûd yəgûdennû wəhûʾ yāgud ʿāqēb*** —
**five words, four of them built from the consonants of his name**, and the sense turns on itself:
*raiders will raid him, and he will raid their heel*. **The tribe settles east of the Jordan, exposed
on the frontier** (Numbers 32:34–36), **and 1 Chronicles 12:8 calls its men *"mighty men of valour …
whose faces were like the faces of lions"*.** ***ʿĀqēb***, *heel*, **is the word of 25:26 and 27:36** —
the heel Jacob was born holding and the name he was mocked with. **He uses it once, in the poem, of
somebody else's enemies.**

**[[Asher]] gets food and [[Naphtali]] gets a metaphor, and neither gets a history.** *"Royal
dainties"* — ***maʿădannê-melek*** — **fits an allotment on the fertile western slopes**
(Joshua 19:24–31), and **Deuteronomy 33:24 says the same thing differently**: *"let him dip his foot
in oil."*

***"A doe set free, who bears beautiful fawns."*** **The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous and the versions
split down the middle.** ***ʾayyālâ šəluḥâ*** is *a doe let loose*; ***ʾimrê-šāper*** is *words of
beauty* far more naturally than *beautiful fawns* — **so the line reads either as a freed deer with
handsome young, or as a spreading tree putting out goodly boughs (an old Greek reading), or as
something that speaks well.** **The wiki notes the three and adopts the WEB's.**

### Joseph — the blessings, and the two titles

> **22** “Joseph is a fruitful vine, a fruitful vine by a spring. His branches run over the wall.
> **23** The archers have severely grieved him, shot at him, and persecuted him:
> **24** But his bow remained strong. The arms of his hands were made strong, by the hands of the
> Mighty One of Jacob, (from there is the shepherd, the stone of Israel),
> **25** even by the God of your father, who will help you; by the Almighty, who will bless you, with
> blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep that lies below, blessings of the breasts, and of
> the womb.
> **26** The blessings of your father have prevailed above the blessings of your ancestors, above the
> boundaries of the ancient hills. They will be on the head of Joseph, on the crown of the head of him
> who is separated from his brothers.

***"The archers have severely grieved him, shot at him, and persecuted him."*** **The only backward
glance in the poem.** **Genesis records no archers**; it records a pit, a sale, a false accusation and
a prison. **The father describes his son's life in the vocabulary of an ambush and names nobody**, and
the men who did it are standing in the room. See [[Suffering]].

***ʾĂbîr yaʿăqōb*** — ***"the Mighty One of Jacob"*** — **is the first occurrence of a divine title
that afterwards belongs almost entirely to Isaiah**: *"I, Yahweh, am your Saviour, your Redeemer, the
Mighty One of Jacob"* (Isaiah 49:26; also 60:16; Psalm 132:2, 5). **A man coins a name for God out of
his own name, on his deathbed, and it outlives him by a thousand years.** See [[The Names of God]] and
[[The Mighty One of Jacob and the Stone of Israel]].

***"From there is the shepherd, the stone of Israel."*** **Two more titles in a four-word parenthesis,
and both are firsts.** ***Rōʿeh*** **completes what 48:15 began** — *"the God who has fed me all my
life long"* is God as the one shepherding; **here the participle has become a noun, and it is the
last thing Jacob calls God.** Filed on
[[The God Who Has Fed Me - The First Time God Is a Shepherd]]. ***ʾEben yiśrāʾēl***, **the *stone* of
Israel, occurs nowhere else** — **and Psalm 118:22's rejected stone, Isaiah 28:16's tried foundation
stone, and the New Testament's use of both** (Matthew 21:42; 1 Peter 2:4–8) **all stand downstream of a
title Genesis uses once and does not explain.**

***"By the Almighty, who will bless you."*** ***El Shaddai*** — **the fifth and final occurrence in
Genesis** (17:1; 28:3; 35:11; 48:3; 49:25), **and the second in two pericopes.** See
[[God Almighty - El Shaddai in Genesis and Exodus]].

**The blessings are stacked in three pairs and they map the cosmos.** ***Heaven above*** and ***the
deep that lies below*** — ***təhôm***, **the word of Genesis 1:2**, *"darkness was on the surface of
the deep"* — **and then *breasts and womb*.** **Sky, abyss, body**: rain, springs, and children, which
is the whole of what an agrarian household can be given. See [[Creation]].

***Nəzîr ʾeḥāyw*** — **WEB *"him who is separated from his brothers"*** — **is the noun that becomes
*Nazirite*** (Numbers 6). **It can mean *set apart*, in the sense of consecrated, or *separated*, in
the sense of cut off — and [[Joseph]]'s life makes both true at once.** **Deuteronomy 33:16 repeats the
phrase word for word over the same son, and lets the double sense stand.**

**Nine of the chapter's occurrences of *bless* are in these five verses and the narrator's summary.**
**None of the other eleven sayings uses the word at all.**

### Benjamin

> **27** “Benjamin is a ravenous wolf. In the morning he will devour the prey. At evening he will
> divide the plunder.”

**The shortest saying, and the last, and it is about a predator.** **[[Benjamin]] is the child the book
has treated as a possession to be protected for eleven chapters** — the one Jacob would not send, the
one Judah stood surety for — **and the poem's last word about him is *plunder*.**

**The tribe's history obliges.** **Ehud the left-handed assassin is a Benjamite** (Judges 3:15);
**the outrage at Gibeah and the war that nearly exterminates the tribe are Benjamin's**
(Judges 19–21); **Israel's first king is a Benjamite** (1 Samuel 9:1–2), **and so is the man who was
*"ravaging the assembly"* before Damascus** (Acts 8:3; Philippians 3:5). **The wiki records the pattern
and notes that Genesis states it before any of it happens.** See [[War]] and [[Prophecy and Vision]].

### The summary, the grave, and the death

> **28** All these are the twelve tribes of Israel, and this is what their father spoke to them and
> blessed them. He blessed everyone according to his blessing.
> **29** He instructed them, and said to them, “I am to be gathered to my people. Bury me with my
> fathers in the cave that is in the field of Ephron the Hittite,
> **30** in the cave that is in the field of Machpelah, which is before Mamre, in the land of Canaan,
> which Abraham bought with the field from Ephron the Hittite as a burial place.
> **31** There they buried Abraham and Sarah, his wife. There they buried Isaac and Rebekah, his wife,
> and there I buried Leah:
> **32** the field and the cave that is therein, which was purchased from the children of Heth.”
> **33** When Jacob finished charging his sons, he gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded up
> the spirit, and was gathered to his people.

***"All these are the twelve tribes of Israel."*** **The first occurrence of *tribes of Israel* —
*šibṭê yiśrāʾēl* — in the Bible**, and **the list that produces it is the pre-48 list**: twelve sons
including [[Levi]], with [[Joseph]] counted as one. **Chapter 48 made Joseph into two tribes and
chapter 49 counts him as one, eight verses later**, and Genesis does not reconcile them. **Every later
tribal roster has to drop Levi or fold the half-tribes to keep the number at twelve.** See
[[The Twelve Sons and the Lists That Reorder Them]].

***"He blessed everyone according to his blessing."*** ***ʾîš ʾăšer kəbirkātô*** — *each man, according
to what was his blessing.* **The clause is the narrator's, and it is the closest Genesis comes to
defending verse 28 against verses 4, 7 and 15.** **Read one way it means *he gave each the blessing
that suited him*; read another it means *what each got is by definition his blessing*.** **The wiki
records both and prefers the second**, because it is the only reading on which the curse of Simeon and
Levi is a blessing at all.

***"Bury me with my fathers."*** **Jacob has now asked for this twice in three chapters** — under oath,
hand under the thigh, at 47:29–31, and here as a charge to all twelve. **The repetition is the measure
of how much he distrusts Egypt to do it.** See
[[Put Your Hand Under My Thigh]] and [[Land and Sojourning]].

**49:31 is the only verse in the Bible that reports the burials of [[Rebekah]] and of [[Leah]].**
**Genesis narrates [[Sarah]]'s burial (23:19), [[Abraham]]'s (25:9) and [[Isaac]]'s (35:29); it never
narrates Rebekah's or Leah's, and this single sentence supplies both retrospectively, in direct
speech.** Filed on [[Buried at Machpelah - The Six Graves of Genesis]].

***"And there I buried Leah."*** **First person, and it is the last thing Jacob says about either of
his wives.** **[[Leah]] is in the cave with the patriarchs; [[Rachel]] is beside a road to Ephrath**,
which he explained one chapter earlier (48:7). **The wife he was tricked into is buried where he is
going; the wife he worked fourteen years for is not.** **Genesis reports the fact in both places and
never comments on it.** See [[Rachel Weeping - Her Grave, Ramah, and Bethlehem]] and [[Love]].

***"He gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded up the spirit, and was gathered to his
people."*** **Three verbs, and the first is physical.** **He has been sitting on the edge of the bed
since 48:2** — *"Israel strengthened himself, and sat on the bed"* — **and the last thing he does is
lie down.** ***Wayyigwaʿ wayyēʾāsep***, *expired and was gathered*, **is the same formula used of
[[Abraham]]** (25:8) **and [[Isaac]]** (35:29), **and *gathered to his people* is said of a man who is
about to be buried five hundred kilometres from all of them.** See [[Gathered to His People]] and
[[Life and Death]].

**The chapter ends without a reaction.** **Nobody weeps, nobody speaks, and the narrator does not
pause.** **The mourning is in the next pericope**, and its first verse — *"Joseph fell on his father's
face, wept on him, and kissed him"* (50:1) — **is the response 49:33 withholds.**

## People

- [[Jacob]] — **speaking for thirty-two verses and dead in the thirty-third.** He summons (49:1–2),
  demotes his firstborn and turns from *you* to *he* mid-sentence (49:3–4), curses an anger and
  sentences two sons (49:5–7), hands the scepter to the fourth (49:8–12), prays once (49:18), coins
  four names for God (49:24–25), charges all twelve with his burial (49:29–32), and **lies down**
  (49:33). **Named *Jacob* five times and *Israel* five times**, twice in the same line
- [[Reuben]] — **given the fullest statement of firstborn right in Genesis and stripped of it in the
  next verse** (49:3–4). ***"Boiling over like water"*** is a hapax; **the charge is 35:22**; and the
  last clause stops speaking to him. **1 Chronicles 5:1 makes this the transfer of the birthright to
  Joseph.** See [[Birthright and the Firstborn]]
- [[Simeon]] — **cursed jointly with [[Levi]] and scattered by absorption.** Joshua 19:1, 9 puts his
  inheritance inside [[Judah]]'s; **Moses' blessing omits him entirely** (Deuteronomy 33), the only son
  left out. **The brother [[Joseph]] bound in Egypt** (42:24) **and the brother 48:5 named as the
  measure of rank**
- [[Levi]] — **under the same curse and the opposite outcome.** *"Scatter them in Israel"* becomes
  forty-eight cities in every tribe's territory and no land of his own (Joshua 21), **and the sword
  that earned the curse is taken up again at Exodus 32:26–29 and rewarded with the priesthood.** See
  [[Priesthood]]
- [[Judah]] — **five verses, the second pun on his name, and the kingship.** *"Your father's sons will
  bow down before you"* (49:8) **is said in front of the brother whose dreams said it about him.** The
  lion (49:9), the scepter and *šîlōh* (49:10), the vine and the wine (49:11–12). **Every king from
  David on, and the genealogies of both Gospels, run through this saying**
- [[Zebulun]] — **a harbour, ships, and a border on [[Sidon (city)]]** (49:13) — **none of which the
  allotment of Joshua 19:10–16 actually gives him.** **Named before [[Issachar]] though born after
  him**, an inversion Deuteronomy 33:18 repeats
- [[Issachar]] — **the only saying that is bad news without being a punishment** (49:14–15). Strong,
  and he trades the strength for *"a resting place, that it was good"*; ***mas-ʿōbēd***, **the corvée
  word of Exodus 1:11**. **Judges 5:15 records the tribe doing the opposite**
- [[Dan (person)]] — ***Dān yādîn***, **the pun [[Rachel]] made at 30:6**, plus the reassurance *"as one
  of the tribes of Israel"* (49:16) — **a maidservant's son told he counts.** Then the serpent on the
  trail (49:17): **the tribe of Samson, of the stolen shrine of Judges 18, and of one of Jeroboam's two
  calves — and the tribe Revelation 7:5–8 leaves out**
- [[Gad]] — **five words, four of them puns on his name** (49:19), **and the only saying whose subject
  is a raid.** ***ʿĀqēb***, *heel*, is the word of Jacob's own birth and his own name (25:26; 27:36)
- [[Asher]] — **rich food and *royal dainties*** (49:20). **One line, no history, and Deuteronomy 33:24
  says the same thing as oil**
- [[Naphtali]] — ***"a doe set free, who bears beautiful fawns"*** (49:21), **on a Hebrew line the
  versions cannot agree about** — a deer, a tree, or good speech
- [[Joseph]] — **five verses and nine tenths of the chapter's blessing vocabulary.** The vine by the
  spring, **archers Genesis never mentions** (49:23), **four names for God in three verses** — the
  Mighty One of Jacob, the shepherd, the stone of Israel, El Shaddai (49:24–25) — **and blessings of
  sky, abyss and body.** ***Nəzîr ʾeḥāyw***, *separated from his brothers*, **is the word behind
  *Nazirite***
- [[Benjamin]] — **a ravenous wolf, in nine Hebrew words** (49:27). **The youngest, the protected one,
  and the one the poem describes as taking prey in the morning and dividing plunder at night.** Ehud,
  Gibeah, Saul and Paul are all Benjamites
- [[Leah]] — **named once, in the last clause of Jacob's last instruction**: *"and there I buried
  Leah"* (49:31). **The only report of her burial in the Bible**, and **the answer, unremarked, to
  48:7's report of where [[Rachel]] is not**
- [[Abraham]], [[Sarah]], [[Isaac]], [[Rebekah]] — **the four other occupants of [[Machpelah]]**
  (49:31). **Rebekah's burial is recorded nowhere else in Scripture**; **Genesis narrates the other
  three** (23:19; 25:9; 35:29)
- [[Ephron the Hittite]] — **named twice in two verses, thirty-six years after the transaction**
  (49:29–30). **Jacob's instruction recites the provenance of the grave like a deed**: the field, the
  cave, the seller, the buyer, and the purpose. See [[The Children of Heth]]
- [[God]] — **named three times in the whole chapter, in two verses**: *Yahweh* at 49:18, *the God of
  your father* and *the Almighty* at 49:25, plus the four titles of 49:24–25. **Against seven mentions
  in the twenty-two verses of chapter 48, this is a chapter that talks about God and mostly does not
  talk to him**

## Places

- [[Machpelah]] — **named in the instruction and described four ways in four verses** (49:29–32): the
  cave, the field, *before [[Mamre (place)]]*, *in the land of [[Canaan]]*, bought from
  [[Ephron the Hittite]], purchased *from [[The Children of Heth]]*. **Jacob's last act is to recite
  the title deed of Genesis 23**
- [[Mamre (place)]] — **the geographic fix on the cave** (49:30), as at 23:17, 19 and 25:9. **The grove
  where [[Abraham]] pitched, argued with God and entertained three visitors is now simply the landmark
  a grave is measured from**
- [[Canaan]] — **named once, as the country the grave is in** (49:30). **The whole chapter is spoken in
  Egypt about a plot of ground four hundred miles away**, and the only use the land is put to in it is
  burial. See [[Land and Sojourning]]
- [[Sidon (city)]] — **new page.** *"His border will be on Sidon"* (49:13) — **the northernmost point
  named in Genesis's account of the tribal future**, and the Phoenician city that Genesis 10:15 already
  made [[Canaan (son of Ham)]]'s firstborn and 10:19 made the top of the Canaanite frontier
- **The haven of the sea** (49:13) and **the boundaries of the ancient hills** (49:26) — **two
  geographies named and not located.** The first is a coastline [[Zebulun]] does not receive; the
  second, ***gibʿōt ʿôlām***, is **a phrase for permanence rather than a place**, and Deuteronomy
  33:15 uses it of [[Joseph]] too

## Themes

- [[Blessing]] — **the chapter's own name for itself and its hardest problem.** ***Bārak*** **nine
  times in thirty-three verses, of which seven are in [[Joseph]]'s five verses and two in the
  narrator's summary** — **and the summary covers a demotion, a curse, and a sentence of forced
  labour**: *"He blessed everyone according to his blessing"* (49:28)
- [[Curse]] — ***"Cursed be their anger"*** (49:7) **is the only curse pronounced by a human being on
  another human being in Genesis**, and it is aimed at a disposition rather than at the men who have
  it. **The book's other curses are God's** (3:14, 17; 4:11; 9:25 is [[Noah]]'s, on a grandson)
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — **the first *bəʾaḥărît hayyāmîm* in the Bible** (49:1), **and twelve
  predictions delivered with no *thus says Yahweh*, no dream, no vision and no messenger.** **A man
  simply says what will happen**, and the book files it under blessing
- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — **the theme's demolition scene.** 49:3's *rēʾšît ʾônî* is
  Deuteronomy 21:17's technical phrase for what cannot be transferred; **49:4 transfers it**, and
  1 Chronicles 5:1–2 names both halves of where it went
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — ***"The scepter will not depart from Judah"*** (49:10) **is the Bible's
  first attachment of rule to a named tribe**, three hundred years before there is a king. **Compare
  17:6 and 35:11**, where kings are promised to [[Abraham]] and [[Jacob]] without a tribe attached
- [[Names and Naming]] — **four of the twelve sayings pun on the son's name** — Judah/*praise* (49:8),
  Zebulun/*dwell* (49:13), Dan/*judge* (49:16), Gad/*troop* (49:19) — **and three of the four re-use
  the etymology his mother gave at his birth.** **A dying father plays his wives' naming speeches back
  at their children**
- [[The Names of God]] — **four in three verses, and three of them are firsts**: *the Mighty One of
  Jacob*, *the shepherd*, *the stone of Israel* (49:24), and *El Shaddai* for the fifth and last time
  in Genesis (49:25). **All four are in one son's saying**
- [[Burial and the Grave]] — **four of the last five verses are about a grave** (49:29–32), and
  **49:31 is the only report in Scripture of where [[Rebekah]] and [[Leah]] are buried.** **The
  chapter's last word on the land of promise is that it holds the family's dead**
- [[Retribution]] — **three sons are told what their own acts have cost them**: [[Reuben]] for 35:22,
  [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]] for 34:25–29. **The wiki notes that the sentences are announced decades after
  the acts, by a father who said nothing at the time** — 35:22's *"and Israel heard of it"* is the
  whole of his response then
- [[Priesthood]] — [[Levi]]'s scattering is the curse that becomes an office (Joshua 21; Numbers
  35:1–8), **and the wiki files the reversal explicitly** rather than letting Genesis 49:7 and
  Deuteronomy 33:8–11 contradict each other unremarked
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — ***mas-ʿōbēd*** (49:15), **the corvée word, applied to a tribe of Israel
  by its own father**, and **the same institution Exodus 1:11 puts Israel under in Egypt** and 1 Kings
  9:21 puts the Canaanites under
- [[Life and Death]] — **the only chapter in Genesis in which a man speaks for thirty-two verses and
  dies in the thirty-third**, with no intervening narration. ***"Gathered up his feet into the bed"***
  is the physical detail the book supplies instead of a reaction
- [[Gathered to His People]] — **the formula twice** (49:29, 33), **bracketed by the *gather* of the
  summons at 49:1–2.** **The same root opens the chapter and closes the man**
- [[Brotherhood]] — ***"Simeon and Levi are brothers"*** (49:5) **is the chapter's flattest sentence
  and its bitterest**, and *"your brothers will praise you"* (49:8) and *"separated from his brothers"*
  (49:26) put the same noun to opposite work three times in twenty verses
- [[War]] — **swords, a lion, a serpent, a raiding troop, a bow, and a wolf.** **Six of the twelve
  sayings are military**, and the one that is not — [[Issachar]]'s — is about being conquered
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — **a man dying in [[Egypt]] assigns coastlines, hills, pastures and a
  harbour** to sons who own none of it, **and then asks to be carried to the one field the family has a
  receipt for** (49:30)
- [[Hope]] — ***"I have waited for your salvation, Yahweh"*** (49:18). **The first *yəšûʿâ* in the
  Bible, spoken by a man five verses from his death, about a rescue he is not going to see**

## Notes

**On calling the whole chapter a blessing.** Verse 28 is the narrator's, and it covers three sayings
that are plainly not blessings: [[Reuben]]'s demotion, [[Simeon]] and [[Levi]]'s curse, and
[[Issachar]]'s forced labour. **The wiki takes *"He blessed everyone according to his blessing"* as
definitional rather than descriptive** — what each son received *is* his blessing, whatever it
contained — **and flags this as a reading.** **The competing readings** (that *bārak* here means only
*took formal leave of*; that the narrator is summarising loosely; that verses 3–7 are a later
insertion into an originally favourable poem) **are all defensible, and Genesis states none of them.**

**On *šîlōh* (49:10).** Four readings, set out in the table above and at length on
[[The Scepter and Shiloh - Genesis 49.10 and Its Readings]]. **The wiki adopts the WEB's *"until he
comes to whom it belongs"*, which follows the Septuagint and the most defensible Hebrew emendation,
and records that the Hebrew consonants do not decide between the four.** **It does not claim the verse
is or is not a messianic prophecy**: the reception history on both the Jewish and the Christian side
reads it as one, and the text itself says only that something arrives.

**On the archers (49:23).** Genesis narrates no archers and no battle in [[Joseph]]'s life. **The verse
is figurative** — the standard reading is that the brothers, [[Potiphar's Wife]], and the forgetting
[[The Chief Cupbearer]] are the bowmen — **and the wiki records it as figurative and notes that the
figure is supplied by nothing in the narrative.** **48:22's *"with my sword and with my bow"* is the
same phenomenon in the same deathbed**: military vocabulary for events the book told differently. See
[[Bought, Sacked, or Taken by the Sword - How Jacob Got Shechem]].

**On Naphtali (49:21).** ***ʾayyālâ šəluḥâ hannōtēn ʾimrê-šāper.*** **The second half is the problem.**
*ʾImrê* is the ordinary plural construct of *ʾēmer*, *word* or *saying*, and *šāper* is *beauty* — so
the natural reading is *who gives words of beauty*, which does not obviously suit a doe. **WEB, with
most modern versions, repoints to get *fawns*; the Septuagint and Vulgate read the whole line as a
tree** — *a spreading terebinth putting forth goodly boughs* — **which requires taking *ʾayyālâ* as
*ʾēlâ*.** **Three readings, one line, and the wiki adopts the WEB's while recording the other two.**

**On the order of the twelve.** [[Leah]]'s six come first (49:3–15), **but with [[Zebulun]] ahead of
[[Issachar]], reversing their birth order**; then the four sons of the maidservants in an alternating
pattern — [[Dan (person)]] ([[Bilhah]]), [[Gad]] and [[Asher]] ([[Zilpah]]), [[Naphtali]] (Bilhah) —
**which is neither the birth order nor the order by mother used at 35:23–26**; then [[Rachel]]'s two.
**No arrangement Genesis has used before produces this sequence**, and **the poem's own logic — rank
first, then the four short sayings, then the two long ones — accounts for it better than descent
does.** The wiki records the observation and claims nothing further from it.

**On the counts.** In the thirty-three verses: *bless* in all forms **nine** (seven of them in
[[Joseph]]'s five verses, two in 49:28), *father* or *fathers* **seven**, *Jacob* **five**, *Israel*
**five**, *son* or *sons* **five**, *field* **four**, *vine* **four**, *cave* **three**, *brother* or
*brothers* **three**, *Judah* **three**, *lion* or *lioness* **three**, *bow* **three** — **and the
three *bows* are three different words doing three different things**: *bow down* (49:8), *bows his
shoulder* (49:15), *his bow remained strong* (49:24). ***Curse*** **once** (49:7). ***Yahweh*** **once**
(49:18), ***God*** **once** and ***Almighty*** **once** (both 49:25). **Counted from the WEB text in
`raw/bible/Genesis.md`.** **God is named three times in thirty-three verses, against seven times in the
twenty-two verses of chapter 48.**

**On the pericope boundary.** The book page queued 49:1–33 and **the boundary was kept.** The chapter
is one speech with its own frame, and 50:1 starts the reaction to it — the weeping, the embalming, the
journey to [[Machpelah]] — **which belongs with the rest of the burial and with [[Joseph]]'s own death,
not with the poem.** **The temptation to run 49 and 50 together as *the death of Jacob* would bury the
twelve sayings inside a funeral**, and the temptation to split 49:1–27 from 49:28–33 would separate the
poem from the sentence that calls it a blessing. **Neither was taken.**

**Links left for later pericopes.** Genesis 50:1–14 (the mourning, the embalming and the burial at
[[Machpelah]]), 50:15–21 (*"you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good"*) and 50:22–26
([[Joseph]]'s death and the oath about his bones) **are cited above by reference and not linked** —
their passage page does not exist yet. **It is the last unchecked box in [[Genesis]].**
