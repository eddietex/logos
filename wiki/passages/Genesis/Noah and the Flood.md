# Noah and the Flood

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 6:9–7:24

## Summary

The Bible's first act of destruction, told at a pace that refuses to hurry, by a narrator who
seems more interested in dates and dimensions than in horror. Forty-four verses cover the
commissioning of the ark, its construction in a single sentence, the loading, the shutting of the
door, and the rise of the water. Nobody screams. Nobody pleads. The one man the story is about
does not speak a word from beginning to end.

**The unit opens by starting Noah over.** Genesis 6:8 had already introduced him — *"But Noah found
favor in Yahweh's eyes"* — and then 6:9 introduces him again, from scratch, under a formal heading:
*"This is the history of the generations of Noah"*. That is the *tôlədōt* formula, the phrase that
segments the whole book, and it means the flood narrative is being presented as a new section
rather than as the continuation of the last one. The effect is that the reader gets two Noahs in
consecutive verses and has to decide how they relate. In 6:8 he is a man who *found favor*, with
nothing said about him. In 6:9 he is *"a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time"*
who *"walked with God"*. The wiki has been holding that question open since
[[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]], and this unit does not close it; it simply
supplies the second half and leaves the connective out. See [[Favor and Grace]] and
[[Righteousness]].

The three descriptions are worth separating, because each is doing different work. *Righteous*
(*ṣaddîq*) is the Bible's first occurrence of the word that will carry most of its weight about
right standing — the word of Genesis 15:6, of Habakkuk 2:4, of Romans 1:17 — and it arrives
attached to a man about whom no act has yet been reported. *Blameless* (*tāmîm*) is the sacrificial
word, used of animals without defect, and it comes with a qualifier that has been argued over for
two millennia: *"among the people of his time"* — either *despite* his generation, which is praise,
or *by the standards of* his generation, which is faint praise (see Notes). And *"Noah walked with
God"* is the exact idiom of [[From Adam to Noah]], said of [[Enoch (son of Jared)]] and now of one
other man and of nobody else in Scripture. Both men who walk with God are exempted from a general
death. See [[Walking with God]].

**Then the diagnosis, and it is built on a single verb.** *"The earth was corrupt before God, and
the earth was filled with violence. God saw the earth, and saw that it was corrupt, for all flesh
had corrupted their way on the earth."* Three occurrences of *šāḥat* in two verses — corrupt,
corrupt, corrupted. And then God speaks: *"Behold, I will destroy them and the earth"* (6:13), and
again *"I, even I, do bring the flood of waters on this earth, to destroy all flesh"* (6:17). The
verb in both is *šāḥat* again. English needs two words; Hebrew uses one. **The earth ruined itself,
and God's sentence is to ruin it** — the punishment is not merely proportionate to the crime, it is
grammatically identical to it. Genesis is saying that the flood does not introduce destruction into
a world that lacked it. It finishes what the world had already started.

The other word in the diagnosis is *ḥāmās*, **violence** (6:11, 13), and it is the first time the
narrative names a specific social evil rather than a condition of the heart. Genesis 6:5 had
diagnosed the interior — *every* imagination, *continually*, *only* evil. Genesis 6:11 gives the
exterior: the earth is *full* of the thing Lamech boasted about in
[[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]]. Read the two verdicts together and the primeval history
has closed its circuit: the heart of 6:5 produces the violence of 6:11, and the violence is what
God says he is acting on.

**The ark is not a ship.** The WEB translates *tēbâ* as "ship" throughout, which is defensible and
also loses something: *tēbâ* is not any of the ordinary Hebrew words for a vessel, and it occurs in
only one other place in the entire Bible — Exodus 2:3, 5, where it is the papyrus **basket**,
sealed with pitch, that carries the infant Moses through the water that was killing every other
Hebrew boy. Two objects in Scripture are called a *tēbâ*, both are waterproofed with bitumen, both
carry a single rescued figure through a judgment by water, and both are the means by which a people
survives. The word is doing typology before anyone has written any.

Its specifications are given with a precision the narrative gives to almost nothing else — three
hundred cubits by fifty by thirty, three decks, one door, one opening at the top, sealed inside and
out — and it has no rudder, no sail, no oar, and no destination. It is not a vessel for going
somewhere. It is a container for staying alive, and the only navigational fact in the account is
that the waters lift it (7:17) and it floats (7:18).

**In the middle of the demolition order, a word nobody has heard before: covenant.** *"But I will
establish my covenant with you"* (6:18). *Bərît* occurs here for the first time in the Bible, and
its placement is exact — it is spoken *after* the announcement that all flesh will die and *before*
the boarding instructions, so the first covenant in Scripture is offered inside a sentence of
universal death, to one family, before the water comes. Note also the verb: God says he will
*establish* (*hēqîm*, cause to stand) his covenant, not *cut* (*kārat*) it, which is the ordinary
idiom for making one. Whether that implies a prior arrangement being confirmed is a real question
and is taken up in the Notes. See [[Covenant]].

**Then the passage does something strange with the animals, and it does it twice.** Genesis 6:19–20
says *"two of every sort"*, male and female, of all flesh. Genesis 7:2–3 says *"seven pairs of every
clean animal … Of the animals that are not clean, take two"*, and seven pairs of birds. The two
instructions are eleven verses apart, in the same narrative, from the same speaker, and they do not
say the same thing. This is the single most cited piece of evidence for the documentary analysis of
the Pentateuch, and it is also soluble on a plain reading — 6:19 gives the general rule and 7:2
refines it for the clean animals — and both of those observations are worth holding at once (see
Notes).

The more interesting fact is buried in the refinement. Genesis 7:2 assumes a distinction between
**clean and unclean animals** roughly nine hundred years before Leviticus 11 defines one, and it
offers no explanation, as though Noah already knew which was which. The narrative reason surfaces
at Genesis 8:20, when Noah sacrifices *"of every clean animal"* — he needs a surplus of the clean
ones because he is going to burn some, and a breeding pair would not survive it. See
[[Clean and Unclean]].

**Noah's entire characterization in this unit is two sentences, and they are the same sentence.**
*"Thus Noah did. He did all that God commanded him"* (6:22). *"Noah did everything that Yahweh
commanded him"* (7:5). Between them, an ark is built — an undertaking the text gives not one verse
to, no description of labour, no mockery from neighbours, no doubt, no delay. Compare what the
Bible does with almost everyone else it calls to a hard thing: Abraham bargains (18:23–33), Moses
objects five times (Exodus 3–4), Gideon asks for a sign twice, Jonah runs. Noah is told, and Noah
does, and the narrator reports it in four words. He does not speak a single line in the whole flood
account; his first recorded words in Scripture come at 9:25, after the water, and they are a curse.

**The flood itself is dated like a legal document.** *"In the six hundredth year of Noah's life, in
the second month, on the seventeenth day of the month"* (7:11) — year, month, day. Nothing in
Genesis so far has been dated at all. The flood narrative gives seven such notations across chapters
7 and 8, and the effect is a deliberate change of register: the account of the sons of God and the
Nephilim was told in the vaguest possible terms (*"in those days, and also after that"*), and this
is told like a ship's log. Whatever Genesis thinks it is narrating here, it is not narrating it as
myth.

**And what the water does is undo Genesis 1.** *"…all the fountains of the great deep burst open,
and the sky's windows opened"* (7:11). *The great deep* is *təhôm rabbâ* — *təhôm* is
the word of Genesis 1:2, the formless deep over which the Spirit hovered. On day two God made an
expanse and said *"let it divide the waters from the waters"* (1:6), holding the waters above off
the waters below.
Genesis 7:11 opens that barrier from both sides at once: the deep from beneath, the windows from
above. On day three he gathered the waters so that dry land appeared; 7:19 covers *"all the high
mountains that were under the whole sky"*, and 7:22 specifies *"all on the dry land"* died. The
flood is not a large storm. It is the third day and the second day being run backwards, in order,
and the roll of the dead in 7:21 — *"birds, livestock, animals, every creeping thing … and every
man"* — is the population of days five and six, listed for erasure exactly as it was listed for
creation. See [[The Flood as Un-Creation]] and [[Creation]].

**Two sentences in the middle of it are unlike the rest.** The first is 7:16: *"Those who went in,
went in male and female of all flesh, as God commanded him; then Yahweh shut him in."* After nine
verses of inventory, the divine name changes from *ʾĕlōhîm* to *Yahweh* and God closes the door
himself. It is the only gesture of care in the chapter, and it is also the moment the door stops
being an entrance. Whatever mercy is in that sentence, it is the same act that seals everyone else
outside.

The second is 7:23: *"Only Noah was left, and those who were with him in the ship."* The verb is
*šāʾar*, **to remain, to be left over** — the root behind *šəʾār* and *šəʾērît*, the **remnant**,
which becomes one of the load-bearing words of the prophets. Isaiah names a son Shear-Jashub, *a
remnant shall return*; Elijah is told seven thousand are left; Paul argues the whole question of
Israel from *"a remnant according to the election of grace"*. The idea enters the Bible here, in a
verse about eight people in a box on the water.

**And then the pericope stops without relief.** *"The waters flooded the earth one hundred fifty
days"* (7:24). That is the last verse of the unit, and it is chosen deliberately: the flood
narrative is built as a chiasm, and its exact centre is the next verse — *"God remembered Noah"*
(8:1) — after which every element of the ascent runs again in reverse. Ending the pericope at 7:24
leaves the reader at the bottom of that structure, on a covered earth, with a hundred and fifty days
of water and no word yet that anyone is coming back. Genesis wants that pause. The turn belongs to
the next unit, Genesis 8:1–22.

## People

- [[Noah]] — the whole unit is about him and he does not speak in it. He is given three character
  descriptions in one verse (righteous, blameless, walking with God), two identical obedience
  formulas (6:22, 7:5), an age (600), and no interior life whatever: no fear, no grief for the
  drowned, no question. The narrator's restraint is total and is almost certainly the point.
- [[God]] — named *ʾĕlōhîm* through the commissioning (6:9–22) and *Yahweh* at the two moments of
  personal address and personal action: *"Yahweh said to Noah, 'Come …'"* (7:1) and *"then Yahweh
  shut him in"* (7:16). He announces, specifies, promises a covenant, sets a date, and closes a
  door. He does not question anyone, and unlike Genesis 3 and 4 there is no one to answer.
- [[Shem]] — named at 6:10 and again at 7:13 as one of the three carried through; given no act and
  no words here. His significance is entirely prospective: the line of Abraham runs through him.
- [[Ham]] — named at 6:10 and 7:13. Everything that will make him a character, and the curse on his
  son, belongs to Genesis 9:18–29.
- [[Japheth]] — named at 6:10 and 7:13, and third in every list. The most thinly drawn of the three.
- **Noah's wife, and the wives of Shem, Ham, and Japheth** — four of the eight people in the ark,
  present in every boarding list (6:18, 7:7, 7:13), and unnamed in Scripture from beginning to end.
  Genesis names the men and counts the women, and later tradition could not leave the gap alone: the
  *Book of Jubilees* supplies Emzara for Noah's wife, the Genesis Apocryphon has Bitenosh, and
  medieval sources multiply the names further. None of it is in the text. No pages are created for
  them here because there is nothing yet to put on one; that may change at 9:18–29.
- [[Adam]] — not named, but 7:22's *"in whose nostrils was the breath of the spirit of life"* is
  quoting the making of the man at 2:7, and using it to describe what dies.

## Places

None named. This is now the third consecutive pericope in which Genesis names no location, and the
reason is the same as before and stronger: the arena is *hāʾāreṣ*, the whole earth, and the point of
7:19 is that there is nowhere left that is anywhere in particular. Ararat, the first named place on
the far side of this, is 8:4 — in the next pericope, and it is a mountain range rather than a
settlement. The primeval history has been widening its frame since Eden, and here it reaches the
maximum: every high mountain under the whole sky, and one floating object with no position given.

## Themes

- [[Righteousness]] — introduced here. *Ṣaddîq* at 6:9 is the word's first occurrence in the Bible,
  and 7:1 restates it in God's own mouth: *"I have seen your righteousness before me in this
  generation"*. Both are declared rather than demonstrated; the narrative gives Noah no righteous
  act to point at except the obedience it then reports.
- [[Covenant]] — introduced here. *Bərît* at 6:18 is the first in Scripture, spoken inside a
  sentence of universal death, and *established* rather than *cut*. The covenant it anticipates is
  ratified at 9:8–17 with the rainbow.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — introduced here. 7:2 assumes the distinction with no definition given and
  no law yet in existence, and the reason is practical: the clean animals need a surplus because
  8:20 is going to consume some of them.
- [[Judgment]] — the sentence of 6:7 carried out. This is the Bible's first *executed* judgment on a
  scale beyond individuals, and it keeps the Genesis pattern intact: the provision (the ark) is
  specified in the same speech as the destruction (6:13–18), before the water.
- [[Sin]] — given an exterior at last. 6:5 named the *yēṣer* of the heart; 6:11 and 6:13 name
  *ḥāmās*, violence, and 6:12 says *"all flesh had corrupted their way"* — the fault is universal,
  it is behavioural, and the verb for it is the same verb as its punishment.
- [[Creation]] — run backwards. The deep of 1:2 and the waters divided on day two return through the
  openings that held them apart, dry land disappears, and the creatures of days five and six are
  listed for erasure in the order they were made.
- [[Life and Death]] — *"Everything that is in the earth will die"* (6:17), and 7:21–23 executes it
  three times over in three different phrasings. The breath of 2:7 is named at 7:22 as the thing
  being taken back.
- [[Walking with God]] — the second and last occurrence of the exact idiom, at 6:9. Both men it is
  said of are carried past a death everyone else undergoes: Enoch before the flood, Noah through it.
- [[Favor and Grace]] — the 6:8 / 6:9 question, now with its second term on the page. The unit does
  not resolve which way the relation runs and is careful not to.
- [[Blessing]] — conspicuously absent. Every creature in the ark is under the blessing of 1:22 and
  1:28, and none of it is mentioned; the blessing is not renewed until 9:1, on dry ground.

## Notes

- **"Blameless among the people of his time" (6:9).** *Tāmîm* means whole, complete, without
  defect — it is the standing requirement for a sacrificial animal (Leviticus 1:3) and is used of
  moral integrity throughout the Psalms. The disputed element is the phrase *bədōrōtāyw*, *in his
  generations*. Read one way it is intensive: he was blameless *even in* a generation like that,
  which is high praise and is how Hebrews 11:7 and 2 Peter 2:5 take him. Read the other way it is
  restrictive: he was blameless *by the standards of* his generation, which would have been a low
  bar. The restrictive reading is genuinely old — it is preserved in the Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 108a)
  as the view of Rabbi Yohanan, who held that had Noah lived in Abraham's generation he would have
  been nothing, against Resh Lakish, who held that a man who stayed upright in that company would
  have been greater still in a better one. The Hebrew permits both, and the narrative supplies
  ammunition for each: Noah obeys perfectly and never once, unlike Abraham at Sodom, asks God to
  spare anybody.

- **The two animal instructions (6:19–20 and 7:2–3).** The general rule at 6:19 is *"two of every
  sort … male and female"*; the instruction at 7:2 is *"seven pairs of every clean animal … Of the
  animals that are not clean, take two"*. Three ways of reading the difference, all with real
  support. **(1) Refinement.** 6:19 states the principle — every kind is preserved by at least a
  breeding pair — and 7:2 refines it for a subset, adding the surplus that 8:20's sacrifice will
  consume. Nothing in 7:2 contradicts 6:19; it adds to it, and Hebrew narrative commonly gives a
  general instruction and then a specific one. **(2) Sources.** The documentary analysis assigns
  6:9–22 to P (the *tôlədōt* heading, *ʾĕlōhîm*, the covenant language, the exact dimensions and
  dates, the pairs) and 7:1–5 to J (*Yahweh*, the clean/unclean distinction, the sevens, the forty
  days), and the flood account is the classic demonstration case for the theory because the two
  strands can be separated into two nearly complete narratives with different numbers throughout —
  two of each against seven pairs of the clean, forty days against a hundred and fifty, *Yahweh*
  against *ʾĕlōhîm*. That the separation *can* be done this cleanly is the strongest argument for
  the theory anywhere in the Pentateuch. **(3) Deliberate composition.** A significant body of
  literary work (Cassuto, and Wenham's chiastic analysis in particular) argues the doublets are
  structural rather than accidental — that the account is built out of paired panels on purpose, and
  that a redactor who left two contradictory animal counts eleven verses apart was either
  extraordinarily careless or not contradicting himself at all. The wiki takes no position. The
  observation that matters for reading is that the text as it stands says both things and expects
  the reader to carry both.

- **Forty days, a hundred and fifty days, and the flood's arithmetic.** 7:12 and 7:17 give forty
  days and nights of rain; 7:24 gives a hundred and fifty days of the waters *flooding* — and 8:3
  repeats the hundred and fifty before the ark grounds on the seventeenth day of the seventh month
  (8:4), which is exactly five months of thirty days after the flood began on the seventeenth day of
  the second month (7:11). The internal calendar is consistent: the hundred and fifty days is a
  period of *prevailing*, of which the first forty were the period of *rain*, and the total time from
  7:11 to the earth being dry at 8:14 is a year and ten days. The apparent conflict comes from
  reading the forty and the hundred and fifty as competing durations of the same thing rather than as
  nested.

- **"Fifteen cubits" (7:20).** The waters rose fifteen cubits above the mountains — and the ark is
  thirty cubits high (6:15). Fifteen is exactly half, which is a plausible draft for a loaded vessel
  of those proportions, and the number is therefore usually read as a statement about the ark rather
  than about the mountains: the water was deep enough that the hull cleared every peak. It is one of
  the few details in the account that only makes sense if someone was thinking practically.

- **How large was the flood?** The text's language is unambiguous and universal — *all* the high
  mountains under the *whole* sky, *every* living thing, *all* flesh — and the question is what that
  language is doing. The **universal** reading takes it at face value and is the plain sense; its
  costs are the well-known physical and biological ones. The **local or regional** reading holds
  that *ʾereṣ* means *land* as often as it means *earth*, that biblical universals are frequently
  hyperbolic within a known horizon (Genesis 41:57 has *"all countries"* coming to Egypt for grain;
  Romans 1:8 has the Roman church's faith proclaimed *"throughout the whole world"*), and that a
  catastrophic Mesopotamian inundation would be *all the earth* to everyone it happened to. The
  **theological or literary** reading holds that the question is misdirected: the account is
  narrating the reversal of Genesis 1, and its geography is the geography of Genesis 1, so asking for
  its flood-extent is like asking how many cubits wide the expanse of day two was. These are not
  fully exclusive. What is worth noticing is that Scripture itself never argues from the flood's
  physical extent; every later use of it — Isaiah 54:9, Matthew 24, 1 Peter 3, 2 Peter 3 — argues
  from its completeness as a judgment and from the fact that eight people came through it.

- **The Mesopotamian flood accounts.** The story has close and undeniable relatives: the *Epic of
  Gilgamesh* tablet XI (Utnapishtim), the older *Atrahasis* epic, the Sumerian Eridu Genesis
  (Ziusudra), and the Berossus summary. The shared elements are specific enough to rule out
  coincidence — a divine decision to destroy humanity, one man warned, a vessel built to
  specification and sealed with bitumen, animals and family aboard, a great flood, a landing on a
  mountain, birds sent out to test the water, and a sacrifice on disembarking that the god or gods
  smell. The differences are equally specific and are where the theological argument lives.
  **Cause:** in Atrahasis the gods send the flood because humanity has become too numerous and too
  noisy for them to sleep; in Genesis the cause is stated as moral — violence, and the corruption of
  all flesh. **Agency:** the Mesopotamian gods squabble, one of them betrays the plan to his
  favourite behind the others' backs, and they are frightened by the flood they raised (in Gilgamesh
  they *"cowered like dogs"*); Genesis has one God who announces, executes, and remembers. **The
  sacrifice:** Utnapishtim's offering draws the famished gods who *"gathered like flies"* over it,
  because the flood has cut off their food supply; Genesis 8:21 has God smell the aroma and make a
  promise, needing nothing. **The survivor:** Utnapishtim is granted immortality and removed to the
  ends of the earth; Noah farms, gets drunk, and dies at 950. Which direction any borrowing ran is
  unsettled and probably unanswerable — the Atrahasis text is older than any plausible date for
  Genesis, but a shared older tradition is at least as likely as direct dependence. What is not in
  doubt is that Genesis is telling a story its neighbours also told, and disagreeing with them about
  what it means.

- **"Then Yahweh shut him in" (7:16).** Three verbs of shutting are available in Hebrew and the one
  used here, *sāgar*, is the ordinary one, but the construction is unusual: God shuts *on behalf of*
  or *around* Noah — the preposition is *baʿădô*, the same form used at 2 Kings 4:4–5 of Elisha
  telling the widow to shut the door *behind* herself and her sons for the miracle of the oil, and at
  Job 1:10 of God putting a hedge *around* Job. Its force is protective rather than merely custodial.
  The commentators who linger here are all making the same double observation and it is a fair one:
  the sentence is tender, and it is also the last moment at which anyone else could have come in.

- **The chiasm.** Gordon Wenham's structural analysis of Genesis 6:9–9:19 — now widely adopted even
  by commentators who reject its implications for source criticism — sets the whole account out as a
  symmetrical mirror: Noah and his sons (6:10) against Noah and his sons (9:18–19); the corruption
  of the earth against the covenant; seven days of waiting (7:4) against seven days of waiting
  (8:10, 12); forty days (7:17) against forty days (8:6); the waters rising a hundred and fifty days
  (7:24) against the waters falling a hundred and fifty days (8:3). At the exact centre, with no
  matching element on either side, stands 8:1: **"God remembered Noah."** If the structure is real,
  and the numerical pairing makes it hard to dismiss, then the flood narrative's own architecture
  says the point of it is not the destruction but the remembering. This pericope ends one verse
  short of that centre, which is why it ends where it does.

- **"God remembered" and what it does not mean.** Worth noting in advance, since 7:24 is written to
  make the reader wait for it: *zākar* used of God in the Bible never implies he had forgotten. It is
  a covenantal action-word — to remember is to act on an existing commitment (Exodus 2:24; 1 Samuel
  1:19; Luke 1:72). The hundred and fifty days of 7:24 are not a period of divine absent-mindedness;
  they are the silence the narrative imposes on the reader, and the covenant of 6:18 is what the
  remembering of 8:1 is remembering.

- **Where the source-critical seam is usually placed.** Continuing the note begun on
  [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]]: the standard division within this unit assigns
  6:9–22 and 7:6, 7:11, 7:13–16a, 7:18–21, 7:24 to P, and 7:1–5, 7:7–10, 7:12, 7:16b–17, 7:22–23 to
  J. The markers are consistent enough to be checked by any reader — the divine name, the animal
  counts, the presence or absence of dates, and the two boarding notices at 7:7 and 7:13 that report
  the same event twice. The wiki records the analysis because it explains features a reader will
  notice anyway, and takes no position on it. The account as it stands is a single narrative and is
  read here as one.
