# Hagar and Ishmael

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 16:1–16

## Summary

The chapter in which the promise is helped along, and the only person God speaks to is the Egyptian
slave it was helped along with.

Genesis 15 ended with a covenant sworn in fire over a sleeping man. Genesis 16 opens on the sentence
that chapter never said: ***"Now Sarai, Abram's wife, bore him no children."*** God had told Abram
his heir would *"come out of your own body"* (15:4) — *your* body, with no clause about hers — and
[[Sarah]] takes the omission at its word. She has an Egyptian slave. The law of the age allows a
barren wife to build a family through one. Ten years in Canaan have produced nothing. So she acts,
and Genesis reports the result without ever telling the reader whether she was wrong to.

**The chapter has three panels, and the middle one is the point.**

| | 16:1–6 | 16:7–14 | 16:15–16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who speaks | Sarai, Abram | Yahweh's angel, Hagar | nobody |
| Who is spoken *to* | each other | Hagar | — |
| Hagar is called | *my servant*, *your maid* | **Hagar** | Hagar |
| What happens to her | given, used, despised, afflicted, driven out | found, questioned, promised, and she answers | she bears a son |

**Neither Sarai nor Abram ever says her name.** Sarai calls her *my servant* twice (16:2, 16:5),
Abram calls her *your maid* (16:6), and neither of them addresses a single word to her in the whole
chapter. The narrator names her seven times; the angel names her once, in the vocative, at 16:8. She
is spoken *about* by everyone in the household and spoken *to* only by God — and then she is the one
who does the naming, of God himself.

**And God says nothing at all to Abram in this chapter.** Between the covenant of 15:21 and the
appearance at 17:1, the only human being Yahweh addresses in the book of Genesis is a runaway
Egyptian slave girl in a desert on the road home. That is the fact the chapter is built to produce.

### Panel A — the plan, and the four verbs from Genesis 3

***"Sarai said to Abram, 'See now, Yahweh has restrained me from bearing'"*** (16:2). The verb is
*ʿāṣar*, to shut up or hold back — and this is **the first time in Scripture anyone attributes a
closed womb to God.** 11:30 stated the barrenness flatly with no cause; Sarai supplies one, and she
supplies it correctly by the standards of the rest of the Bible (29:31; 30:2, 22; 1 Samuel 1:5–6).
She is not complaining. She is reasoning: if the obstacle is God's, the promise will have to come
some other way, and the other way is legally available.

***"Please go in to my servant. It may be that I will obtain children by her."*** The Hebrew of the
second clause is *ʾûlay ʾibbāneh mimmennâ* — literally ***perhaps I shall be built up from her***,
from *bānâ*, to build, punning on *bēn*, son. Sarai's plan is a construction project, and the verb is
the one Genesis 2:22 used of the making of the first woman. Rachel will use the identical idiom of
Bilhah at 30:3, and it is worth saying plainly that Genesis narrates this arrangement three times —
Sarai and Hagar, Rachel and Bilhah, Leah and Zilpah (30:1–13) — and that the second and third produce
four of the twelve tribes of Israel with no disaster attached. The convention is not what Genesis 16
is criticizing. What happens inside it is.

***"Abram listened to the voice of Sarai."*** *Wayyišmaʿ ʾabrām lə-qôl śāray.* **The only other
person in Genesis of whom this is said is Adam**, in the sentence that curses the ground: *"Because
you have listened to your wife's voice, and ate from the tree"* (3:17). And the echo does not stop
at one phrase. Set the two scenes side by side:

| Genesis 3:6 | Genesis 16:2–3 |
|---|---|
| the woman **saw** | Sarai **saw** (16:4, of Hagar's conceiving — and 16:2 begins *"See now"*) |
| she **took** some of its fruit | Sarai **took** Hagar the Egyptian |
| she **gave** to her husband | and **gave** her to Abram her husband |
| the man **listened to the voice** of his wife | Abram **listened to the voice** of Sarai |

Four verbs in the same order — *see, take, give, listen* — with *her husband* in the same slot in
both. Genesis has done this deliberately, and it has done it without a word of comment: nobody in
chapter 16 is rebuked, no curse is pronounced, and God's speech in the chapter contains no criticism
of anybody. The judgment is entirely in the vocabulary. See
[[Listening to the Voice - Genesis 3 and Genesis 16]].

***"after Abram had lived ten years in the land of Canaan"*** (16:3). Ten years since 12:4, which
makes Abram eighty-five here and eighty-six at 16:16. The clock is being run deliberately: the wait
so far is a decade, the wait remaining is fourteen years more (17:24–25; 21:5), and the note is
placed in the verse where the waiting stops.

***"gave her to Abram her husband to be his wife"*** — *lə-ʾiššâ*. Hagar is not a concubine in this
verse; the text says *wife*, and 16:3 is the second marriage in Genesis after Lamech's (4:19). At
21:10 Sarah will demand the expulsion of *"this servant"*, having herself supplied the word *wife*.

***"When she saw that she had conceived, her mistress was despised in her eyes"*** (16:4). *Qālal* —
to be light, of small weight, hence to be slighted. This is the pivot, and it is the one moment
Genesis lets us inside Hagar: a slave who has done in one act what her mistress has failed to do in
decades, and who lets it show. Genesis does not defend it. It also does not pretend that the
household arrangement did not create it.

***"This wrong is your fault … Yahweh judge between me and you"*** (16:5). Sarai's Hebrew is
*ḥămāsî ʿālêkā* — ***my violence be upon you***, using *ḥāmās*, the word for the violence that
filled the earth before the flood (6:11). She is invoking a lawsuit formula (*yišpōṭ YHWH bênî
ûbênêkā*, cf. 31:53; Judges 11:27; 1 Samuel 24:12) against the husband who did what she told him to.
Nothing she says is about Hagar's welfare and everything is about her own standing.

The Masoretes marked this verse. There are dots — *puncta extraordinaria* — written above
*ûbênêkā*, *and you*, in the standard Hebrew text; the same scribal device appears about fifteen
times in the Torah, always over something the tradition found strange. Whatever it was meant to flag,
it is a very old signal that somebody found Sarai's sentence hard.

***"Behold, your maid is in your hand. Do to her whatever is good in your eyes"*** (16:6). **Abram's
only sentence in the chapter, and it hands a person over.** Three chapters earlier his only sentence
also gave something away — *"Please separate yourself from me. If you go to the left hand, then I
will go to the right"* (13:9) — and that speech was the most admirable thing he ever does; it cost
him the choice of a country to keep the peace. This one costs him nothing and keeps the peace by
returning a pregnant woman to the person who wants to hurt her. Genesis puts the same generosity of
form to two opposite uses within four chapters. See [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

The phrase *do whatever is good in your eyes* is the standard idiom for granting someone absolute
discretion (19:8; Judges 19:24 — both, notably, about handing over women).

***"Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled from her face"*** (16:6). Two words carry the rest of
the Bible in them.

- ***Dealt harshly*** is *wattəʿannehā*, from **ʿānâ** — the verb of 15:13, *"They will afflict them
  four hundred years"*, and of Exodus 1:11–12, where Egypt sets taskmasters over Israel *to afflict
  them*. One chapter after Abram is told his offspring will be afflicted in Egypt, his wife afflicts
  an Egyptian.
- ***Fled*** is *bāraḥ*, the verb used of Moses fleeing Pharaoh into the wilderness of Midian
  (Exodus 2:15).

The whole exodus is here in miniature and running backwards: a Hebrew household afflicts an Egyptian
slave, who escapes into the wilderness, meets God at a spring on the way, is given a promise of
innumerable offspring — and is sent back into the affliction. See
[[Affliction and Flight - Hagar and the Exodus]].

### Panel B — the angel, the well, and the name

***"Yahweh's angel found her by a fountain of water in the wilderness"*** (16:7). ***Malʾak YHWH*** —
**the first appearance of the angel of Yahweh in the Bible**, and it is to a foreign slave woman, not
to a patriarch. *Malʾāk* means messenger and is used of human envoys throughout Scripture; what makes
this figure a permanent puzzle is that from 16:10 onward he speaks in the first person as God (*"I
will greatly multiply your offspring"*), and 16:13 has Hagar name *Yahweh who spoke to her* — the
narrator collapsing the messenger into the sender without a seam. See [[The Angel of Yahweh]].

*The verb is **found**.* Nobody in Abram's household is looking for her.

***"Hagar, Sarai's servant, where did you come from? Where are you going?"*** (16:8). The first
divine question in Genesis since *"Where is Abel, your brother?"* (4:9), and only the third in the
book after *"Where are you?"* (3:9). All three are asked of someone who has run from something, and
none of them is a request for information.

Two details in the address. **She is called by name** — the first woman since Eve to be addressed
directly by God, and the only person in Genesis God addresses by their owner's name. And her answer
is a half answer: she says where she has come *from* (*"I am fleeing from the face of my mistress
Sarai"*) and says nothing about where she is going, which is the question with the future in it. The
road to Shur is the road to Egypt. She is going home.

***"Return to your mistress, and submit yourself under her hands"*** (16:9). **The hardest verse in
the chapter, and it should not be softened.** *Hitʿannî* is the reflexive of the same *ʿānâ* that
described what Sarai did to her: *afflict yourself under her hands*. God's first recorded word to
this woman sends her back to the person who drove her out, and offers her no protection, no rebuke of
Sarai, and no term.

What the text does supply is what comes with the command, and it is not nothing:

- She is given a promise in the patriarchal formula, which no other woman in Scripture receives
  directly (16:10).
- She is told her son's name and its meaning before he is born (16:11) — she does not go back
  ignorant.
- 21:9–21 shows that the arrangement does not hold: she is expelled anyway, God intervenes again, and
  she ends up free in the wilderness of Paran with a son and a daughter-in-law of her own choosing.

The wiki records the difficulty rather than resolving it. A command to return to an abusive
household, given by God, in a text that has just called that household's treatment of her by the
exodus's own word for slavery, is a genuine hard edge of Genesis; reading it as approval of the
household is not required by anything in the chapter, and reading it as an endorsement of endurance
in abuse is a use the passage does not authorize. What the chapter itself says is that she goes back
carrying something she did not have when she left. See [[Slavery and Servitude]].

***"I will greatly multiply your offspring, that they will not be counted for multitude"*** (16:10).
*Harbâ ʾarbeh ʾet-zarʿēk* — the infinitive-absolute construction that Genesis uses in exactly three
places: here, at 3:16 of the woman's pain in childbirth, and at 22:17 of Abraham's offspring after
the binding of Isaac. **This is the offspring promise, in the covenant's own grammar, spoken to a
slave.** No other woman in the Bible is given it in her own right — Sarah is told at 17:16 that she
will be a mother of nations, but the promise is spoken to Abraham about her. See
[[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]].

***"You shall call his name Ishmael, because Yahweh has heard your affliction"*** (16:11). **The
Bible's first annunciation** — the form that will be used of Samson (Judges 13:3–5), of Immanuel
(Isaiah 7:14), and of John and Jesus (Luke 1:13, 31): *you are with child, you will bear a son, you
shall call his name X, because Y.* It is given to Hagar before it is given to Sarah.

***Ishmael*** is *Yišmāʿēl*, **God hears**, and the explanation names *Yahweh* while the name itself
contains *ʾĒl* — a small mismatch the chapter does not smooth. And the verb is the chapter's own:
16:2 said *Abram **heard** the voice of Sarai*; 16:11 says *Yahweh has **heard** your affliction*.
The two hearings are the chapter's spine. One produces the trouble, the other answers it. The noun is
*ʿŏnî*, affliction — the noun of the verb Sarai used on her, and the noun of Exodus 3:7, *"I have
surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry."*

Note also what she is *not* told: that she will be rescued, that Sarai will be punished, or that her
son will inherit anything. The name is a statement about God's hearing, not a promise about her
circumstances — and the boy will carry it around as a walking reminder for the rest of his life.

***"He will be like a wild donkey among men"*** (16:12). *Pereʾ ʾādām* — a wild-ass of a man. It
sounds like an insult in English and it is not obviously one in Hebrew, because the Bible's fullest
description of the animal is God's own boast at Job 39:5–8: *"Who has set the wild donkey free? … He
scorns the tumult of the city, neither does he hear the shouting of the driver."* The *pereʾ* is the
creature that cannot be enslaved. **That is the word given to the son of a slave**, four verses after
his mother is ordered back into slavery, and it is the best clue the chapter offers about what it
thinks of her situation.

*"His hand will be against every man, and every man's hand against him. He will live opposite all of
his brothers."* The last clause is *ʿal-pənê kol-ʾeḥāyw yiškōn* — *he will dwell over against / in
the face of all his brothers* — and it is genuinely ambiguous between **in defiance of** and
**alongside**. Genesis 25:18 repeats it word for word of his descendants, in a verse that is simply
describing where they live. The oracle is about an independent people on the desert margin of the
settled land, not a moral verdict on a boy, and the wiki flags that it has often been read as one,
in service of arguments Genesis is not making.

***"She called the name of Yahweh who spoke to her, 'You are a God who sees'"*** (16:13). ***ʾAttâ
ʾĒl Rŏʾî.*** **Hagar is the only person in the Bible who gives God a name.** Others build altars and
call *on* the name (4:26; 12:8), or name a place for what happened there (22:14; 28:19; 33:20); the
grammar here is different and unique in Scripture — *she called the name of Yahweh*, with God as the
direct object of the naming. A slave with no standing in the household, no husband of her own, and no
covenant, names God; and the name she gives him is about being looked at. See
[[Seeing and Being Seen]] and [[The Names of God]].

*"for she said, 'Have I even stayed alive after seeing him?'"* The Hebrew of 16:13b — *hă-gam hălōm
rāʾîtî ʾaḥărê rōʾî* — is one of the more corrupted-looking clauses in Genesis, and the standard
renderings pull in two directions: *have I really seen here the back of the one who sees me?* (taking
*ʾaḥărê* as *the back of*, as at Exodus 33:23), or the WEB's *have I even stayed alive after seeing
him?* (supplying the survival idiom of Exodus 33:20 and Judges 13:22). Both readings make the same
theological point from opposite ends — she has seen and lives — and no reconstruction is secure. The
wiki records the ambiguity and does not choose.

***"Therefore the well was called Beer Lahai Roi"*** (16:14). *Bəʾēr laḥay rōʾî*, **the well of the
Living One who sees me** — the name preserving hers, which is the one part of her that stays in the
land. Isaac will live at that well (24:62; 25:11): the son of the promise makes his home at the
spring named by the slave who was sent away.

*"Behold, it is between Kadesh and Bered."* A narrator's aside to a reader who knows the country.
Kadesh is the great oasis of the northern Sinai; Bered occurs nowhere else in the Bible and has never
been identified. See [[Beer Lahai Roi]].

### Panel C — the birth, and who names him

***"Hagar bore a son for Abram. Abram called the name of his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael"***
(16:15). The angel said ***you*** shall call his name (16:11); **Abram does the naming**, which means
he knows what to call the boy, which means she told him — the one act of communication between them
that the chapter implies and does not narrate.

**Sarai is not in these last two verses.** She proposed the plan, executed it, complained about it,
and vanishes from the chapter before the child arrives; the closing formula is *bore a son **for
Abram***, twice, and *his son*. The woman who said *perhaps I shall be built up
from her* is not named in the sentence where the building happens. She reappears at 17:15 with a
new name and a son of her own promised.

***"Abram was eighty-six years old"*** (16:16). The chapter ends on a number, like the registers of
chapters 5 and 11 — and the number is what makes the next thirteen years of the book legible. Ishmael
is thirteen at his circumcision (17:25), fourteen at Isaac's birth (21:5), and for those fourteen
years he is, as far as Abram knows, the son.

## People

- [[Hagar]] — an Egyptian slave, named seven times by the narrator and never once by either of the
  people who own her. She is given away, used, resented, afflicted, and driven out in six verses
  without speaking a word; then, alone in a desert, she is found by God, addressed by name, asked two
  questions, promised offspring beyond counting, and told her son's name — and she answers, and gives
  God a name of her own. She is the first person in Genesis to be met by the angel of Yahweh, the
  first woman since Eve God speaks to, the recipient of the Bible's first annunciation, and the only
  human being in Scripture who names God.
- [[Sarah]] — *Sarai*, who speaks the chapter's first words and its most brutal, and is absent from
  its last two verses. She reads 15:4's silence about her correctly and acts on it legally; she is the
  first person in the Bible to say that God has closed a womb; and when the plan works she blames her
  husband for it and takes it out on the woman. Genesis neither excuses her nor condemns her, and
  supplies instead a set of verbs from Genesis 3.
- [[Abraham]] — *Abram*, who listens, complies, and says one sentence in sixteen verses. He is not
  spoken to by God anywhere in this chapter — the only chapter between 12 and 22 of which that is
  true where God speaks at all — and he ends it by naming a son with a name he was not given, which
  is the chapter's quiet evidence that Hagar came back and talked to him.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — *malʾak YHWH*, first appearance in Scripture. He finds, asks, commands,
  and then promises in God's own first person; Hagar and the narrator both treat him as Yahweh
  himself.
- [[Ishmael]] — named before he is born, and for fourteen years after it Abram's only son. His name
  means *God hears*, his oracle makes him a free animal in a book of herdsmen, and the promise of an
  innumerable multitude reaches him through his mother rather than through his father.
- [[God]] — who does not speak to the covenant-bearer in this chapter and does speak, at length, to
  the slave. He hears an affliction nobody reported to him, gives the offspring promise to a woman,
  sends her back into the household that afflicted her, and is named by her for seeing.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — Hagar's country, named twice as her identity (*"an Egyptian"*, 16:1, 16:3) and never
  as a location. It is where the road to Shur goes, and it is where 12:16's *"female servants"* most
  likely came from: the wages Abram took for his wife in Egypt are, on the plainest reading, how an
  Egyptian slave girl came to be in a tent in Hebron. Genesis does not say so, and the sequence is
  hard to unsee.
- [[Canaan]] — the land Abram has now lived in ten years without a child (16:3), which is the whole
  reason the chapter happens.
- [[Shur]] — the region on the desert frontier between Canaan and Egypt; *"the way to Shur"* is the
  road home, and the direction of her flight is the chapter's unspoken answer to the question she
  refuses to answer.
- [[Beer Lahai Roi]] — the well she names, between Kadesh and Bered, and Isaac's address for the rest
  of his life.
- **Kadesh** and **Bered** — the two markers locating the well. Kadesh is the oasis already named at
  14:7 as *En Mishpat*, and it earns its own page when Numbers 13 does something with it; Bered
  occurs nowhere else in the Bible and is unidentified.

## Themes

- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — the theme's founding text. A *šipḥâ* is given as a wife, used as a
  womb, called *my servant* and *your maid* and never by name, placed *in your hand* with the
  discretion to do *whatever is good in your eyes*, afflicted with the exodus's own verb, and told by
  God to go back and submit. Genesis reports every step of it without a word of comment.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the chapter's own vocabulary. *Sarai saw* nothing; Hagar *saw that she
  had conceived*; her mistress was despised *in her eyes*; Abram tells Sarai to do what is good *in
  your eyes*; and it ends with *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, **the God who sees**, and a well named *the Living One who
  sees me*. The one person nobody in the household looks at names God for looking.
- [[Barrenness]] — the ten-year mark, the first attribution of a closed womb to God, and the human
  solution that works: a child is born, and he is the wrong child. The theme's most important
  demonstration that the obstacle is not a puzzle to be solved.
- [[Blessing]] — the offspring promise in its covenant grammar (*harbâ ʾarbeh*), given to a slave in
  a wilderness rather than to a patriarch under the stars.
- [[Marriage]] — Hagar is *given to Abram her husband to be his wife* (16:3), and the household that
  results is the Bible's first polygamous one outside the line of Cain. Genesis narrates the
  arrangement three times and shows what it does to the women each time.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the same Abram who ended a quarrel at 13:8–9 by giving away his own
  claim ends this one by giving away someone else.
- [[The Names of God]] — *ʾĒl Rŏʾî*, the only divine name in Scripture supplied by a human being, and
  the third *ʾĒl* title in four chapters after *ʾĒl ʿElyôn* (14:18) and before *ʾĒl Šadday* (17:1) —
  two of the three given by outsiders.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — the Bible's first birth annunciation, and its first oracle about an
  unborn child's character and descendants.
- [[Faith]] — the counterweight to 15:6. The man credited with righteousness for believing a promise
  about his own body spends the next chapter acting on his wife's plan without a word, and the text
  places the two chapters back to back without comment. Genesis's account of faith has room for this.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — ten years in a land that is not his and no heir in it; and a slave who
  runs toward the country the covenant will later call the house of bondage.
- [[The Nations]] — Ishmael is the first of the several nations Genesis will derive from Abraham
  outside the covenant line (25:12–18; 25:1–4; 36), and the only one promised in the covenant's own
  formula.

- Exodus 1:11–12; 2:15; 3:7 — affliction, flight, and a God who sees the affliction and hears the
  cry.
- Exodus 33:20–23 — seeing God and living, and *"you shall see my back"*: both halves of 16:13's
  ambiguity.
- Judges 13:2–24 — the angel of Yahweh, an annunciation to a woman, and *"we shall surely die,
  because we have seen God"*.
- 1 Samuel 1:11 — Hannah asking God to *look on the affliction of your servant*, in Hagar's
  vocabulary.
- Job 39:5–8 — the *pereʾ*, the wild donkey, as God describes it: free, city-scorning, and
  unbroken.
- Isaiah 60:6–7; Jeremiah 49:28–33 — Kedar and Nebaioth, Ishmael's sons, as nations in the prophets.
- Galatians 4:21–31 — Hagar, Sinai, and the two covenants.

## Notes

- **The legal background is well documented and it matters for reading Sarai.** Surrogacy through a
  slave is attested across the ancient Near East: the Code of Hammurabi §§144–147 legislates the case
  of a wife who gives her husband a slave girl, and §146 covers precisely the collision of Genesis
  16:4–6 — if the slave girl then *"claims equality with her mistress"*, the mistress may not sell
  her, but may return her to slave status among the household. Old Babylonian and Nuzi marriage
  contracts contain the same provisions. Sarai's proposal, her grievance, and her retaliation are
  each recognizable moves within a legal system Genesis's first readers knew. That does not make them
  right; it does mean the chapter is not narrating an eccentric cruelty but a normal institution
  producing its normal result.
- **Genesis withholds every verdict, and the withholding is the technique.** Nobody is cursed, nobody
  is rebuked, God criticizes no one, and the narrator offers not one evaluative word — in a book
  perfectly willing to say that *"Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked in Yahweh's sight"* (38:7). What
  the chapter supplies instead is vocabulary imported from other scenes: Eden's four verbs, the flood's
  *ḥāmās*, the exodus's *ʿānâ*. A reader who knows the rest of Genesis is handed the judgment and
  never told it.
- **Source criticism reads 16 and 21:9–21 as a doublet, and the observation survives whatever you
  make of it.** The two accounts share a structure (Sarah's grievance, Abraham's compliance, Hagar in
  the wilderness, a divine word, a well, a promise of a great nation) and differ at almost every
  detail: *Yahweh* against *God*, a flight against an expulsion, a pregnant woman against a mother
  with a child, an angel who finds her against one who calls from the sky. Classical analysis assigns
  16 mainly to J and 21 to E. The narrative as it stands makes the second an echo of the first that
  goes further — she does not have to go back. See [[The Two Flights of Hagar]].
- **What the chapter costs later, in the book's own terms.** Ishmael is Abram's son for fourteen years
  and is circumcised into the covenant at 17:23–26 before being told at 17:19–21 that the covenant is
  Isaac's; the household divides at 21:9; the two brothers bury their father together at 25:9; and
  Ishmael's descendants take Joseph to Egypt at 37:25–28. Genesis keeps him in the story and keeps
  him blessed (17:20; 21:13, 20) and outside the covenant at the same time, which is the pattern it
  will use again for Esau.
- **On the later religious traditions, briefly and as reception.** In Jewish midrash Hagar is
  variously an Egyptian princess given to Abram by Pharaoh, and is identified with Keturah (Genesis
  25:1) in *Genesis Rabbah* 61:4 and by Rashi. In Islam, Hājar and Ismāʿīl are central: the well of
  16:14 is identified with Zamzam at Mecca, her search for water between the hills of Ṣafā and Marwa
  is re-enacted in the *saʿy* of the hajj, and Ismāʿīl is traditionally the son of the sacrifice of
  Qurʾān 37:99–113, where the Qurʾān does not name him. None of this is in Genesis, and all of it is
  what a large part of the world means by these two names; the wiki records it as reception history
  and does not adjudicate it.
- **A note on how this page reads Genesis 16:9.** The command to return is the passage's hardest
  point and it is not made easier by anything in the chapter. This wiki states it plainly, notes what
  accompanies it, notes that Genesis 21 undoes it, and declines to convert it into a general
  instruction — because Genesis itself never generalizes it, and because the same book's own
  vocabulary for what she was returning to is the vocabulary it uses for the slavery God later breaks
  Egypt to end.
