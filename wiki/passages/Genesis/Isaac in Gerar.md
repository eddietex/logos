# Isaac in Gerar

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 26:1–35

## Summary

**This is the only chapter in the Bible that belongs to Isaac, and almost everything in it has
happened before.**

A famine, and the narrator says so outright: *"besides the first famine that was in the days of
Abraham."* A descent toward Egypt, halted. A wife called a sister. A king called Abimelech who
finds out and delivers the rebuke. A quarrel about wells. A treaty sworn at Beersheba with the same
king and the same army commander. An altar, and calling on the name of Yahweh. A place-name explained
by an oath. **Every one of those is a thing Abraham did, and the book puts them all in one chapter
and gives them to his son.**

**So the first question the chapter raises is what it is for**, and the answer it gives is in the two
places where it does *not* repeat: **26:2–5**, where God speaks to Isaac for the first time in his
life and grounds the promise on his father's obedience in vocabulary that belongs to Sinai; and
**26:12**, where the man plants a crop. **Isaac is the only patriarch in Genesis reported farming**,
and he does it during a famine, in soil he does not own, and reaps a hundredfold. The chapter's
theology is that the promise runs in the second generation without being renegotiated — and its
narrative is that the second generation gets rich in a foreign king's territory and is thrown out
for it.

**Then the last two verses turn on the family.** Esau, at forty, the age at which his father was
married by proxy to a woman fetched four hundred miles under oath, marries two Hittite women of the
neighbourhood on his own initiative, and *"They grieved Isaac's and Rebekah's spirits."* It is the
only sentence in the whole cycle in which Isaac and Rebekah are recorded feeling the same thing, and
it is the fuse for chapter 27.

### 26:1 — a famine the narrator will not let you take for new

> **1** There was a famine in the land, besides the first famine that was in the days of Abraham.
> Isaac went to Abimelech king of the Philistines, to Gerar.

***Milləbad hārāʿāb hāriʾšôn*** — **besides the first famine.** Genesis almost never cross-references
itself, and here it does it in the opening clause. **The book knows the reader has read 12:10** — *"a
famine in the land … Abram went down into Egypt to live as a foreigner there, for the famine was
severe in the land"* — and it says so before anything else happens. Whatever the relation between
this chapter and the Abraham material, **it is not one Genesis is trying to hide.** See
[[The Wife-Sister Episodes]] and [[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].

**The land that was promised keeps failing.** This is the second of the three famines that drive the
patriarchs off it: 12:10 sends Abram to Egypt, this one sends Isaac to Gerar, and 41–47 will send the
whole family to Egypt for four hundred years. **In each case the promised land cannot feed the
promised family**, and in each case the family survives by going where the food is. See
[[Land and Sojourning]].

***"Abimelech king of the Philistines."*** Genesis 20 called him simply *king of Gerar*; this verse
adds the gentilic, which 21:32 and 21:34 had already attached to the region. **The Philistines as an
archaeologically visible people arrive on that coast around 1200 BC**, centuries after any date
proposed for the patriarchs, and there is nothing Philistine about this kingdom: no pentapolis, no
*seren*, no Dagon, no iron, no army in the field. The wiki records it as an anachronism of the kind
that calls a town *Dan* at 14:14, and does not argue it away. See [[The Land of the Philistines]] and
[[Gerar]].

**On whether he is the same Abimelech.** Genesis does not say. He is a king of Gerar with the same
name, the same army commander's name, the same well, and the same habit of being right; the wiki
gives him a page qualified by the patriarch he appears with, exactly as it does for
[[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]], and does not decide between one long-lived man, a hereditary office,
and one tradition told twice. See [[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]].

### 26:2–5 — God speaks to Isaac, and the ground he gives is his father

> **2** Yahweh appeared to him, and said, “Don’t go down into Egypt. Live in the land I will tell you
> about.
> **3** Live in this land, and I will be with you, and will bless you. For I will give to you, and to
> your offspring, all these lands, and I will establish the oath which I swore to Abraham your
> father.
> **4** I will multiply your offspring as the stars of the sky, and will give all these lands to your
> offspring. In your offspring will all the nations of the earth be blessed,
> **5** because Abraham obeyed my voice, and kept my requirements, my commandments, my statutes, and
> my laws.”

**He is sixty years past the promise of him and this is the first time God has ever spoken to him.**

Named before conception (17:19), circumcised before consent (21:4), bound on a mountain without a
word (22:9), married by errand (24), blessed in four words after his father's funeral (25:11) — and
addressed, for the first time, at Gerar, in a famine, when he is on his way somewhere he has been
forbidden to go. **His wife heard from God a chapter earlier** (25:23), about their sons, and Genesis
never comments on the order. See [[Isaac]] and [[Rebekah]].

***"Don't go down into Egypt."*** *ʾAl-tērēd Miṣraymâ.* **This is the only prohibition in the
patriarchal promises**, and it is the divine form of the clause Abraham had already made his servant
swear twice — *"you don't bring my son there again"* (24:6, 8), of Mesopotamia. **Isaac is the one
patriarch who never leaves the land, and this verse and that oath are jointly why.** Abram went down
in the first famine and came out rich and rebuked; Jacob will be told at the same well, in almost the
same grammar, the exact opposite — *"Don't be afraid to go down into Egypt"* (46:3). **The same God
forbids the descent to the father's son and commands it to the grandson**, and the difference between
the two verses is the whole difference between a sojourn and an exodus. See [[Egypt]].

***"Live in the land I will tell you about."*** *Gûr bāʾāreṣ ʾăšer ʾōmar ʾēlêkā* — the syntax of
12:1's *"to the land that I will show you"*, reused. **But the verb is *gûr*, to sojourn as a
resident alien**, and it governs the next two clauses too. **God tells the heir of the land promise
to live in the land as a foreigner**, and the chapter proceeds to show exactly what that costs: he
farms ground he does not own, his wells are filled in, and a king evicts him from a country he has
been told is his. See [[Land and Sojourning]].

**The promise is restated whole, and every element of it is quoted from somewhere.** *I will be with
you* (from 21:22's outsider's verdict, now first-person); *bless you* (12:2); *all these lands* to you
and your offspring (13:15; 15:18); *the oath which I swore to Abraham* (22:16, the only place Genesis
says God swore by himself); *offspring as the stars of the sky* (15:5; 22:17); *in your offspring
will all the nations of the earth be blessed* (12:3; 18:18; 22:18). **Nothing here is new, and that
is the point** — the covenant is not renegotiated with the second generation, it is handed over
intact. See [[Covenant]], [[Dust, Stars, and Sand - The Offspring Promises]], and
[[All the Families of the Earth - Genesis 12.3 in the New Testament]].

***"because Abraham obeyed my voice, and kept my requirements, my commandments, my statutes, and my
laws."*** **This is the strangest verse in the chapter and one of the most argued-over in Genesis.**

***ʿĒqeb ʾăšer šāmaʿ ʾAbrāhām bəqōlî*** — *because Abraham listened to my voice* — is the language of
22:18, where the same clause follows the binding. What is added here is a list, and **the list is
Torah vocabulary**: *mišmartî* (my charge or requirement), *miṣwōtay* (my commandments), *ḥuqqôtay*
(my statutes), *wətôrōtāy* (my laws — the plural of *tôrâ*). **Those four words together are how
Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomistic History describe keeping the Sinai law**, and Sinai is four
hundred years and one book away. The readings, none adopted here:

- **A Deuteronomic hand.** The clause is written in the idiom of a later period and inserted to make
  Abraham the model of Torah obedience. This is the standard critical account and it rests on the
  vocabulary alone, which is genuinely distinctive.
- **Retrospective idiom, not anachronism.** Genesis says elsewhere that Abraham was told to *"walk
  before me, and be blameless"* (17:1) and that he *"will command his children and his household after
  him, that they may keep the way of Yahweh, to do righteousness and justice"* (18:19). On this
  reading 26:5 summarizes a life the book has already described, in the only vocabulary the tradition
  had for obedience.
- **The rabbinic reading**, that Abraham kept the whole law before it was given, takes the verse at
  maximum strength and generates a substantial literature.

**And the theological problem is separate from the dating problem.** Everywhere else in Genesis the
promise is unilateral: at 15 God passes between the pieces alone, and Abram undertakes nothing. Here
the promise to the second generation is given *because* the first generation obeyed. **Genesis puts
both statements in the book and does not reconcile them**, and Paul's argument in Galatians 3 and
Romans 4 that the promise came by faith rather than by law has to work with this verse in it. See
[[Because Abraham Obeyed My Voice - Law Before Sinai]] and
[[Credited to Him for Righteousness - Genesis 15.6 in Paul and James]].

**Note what the verse does not say: it does not say Isaac has done anything.** He is given the entire
covenant on his father's record, in the same chapter in which he will lie about his wife. See
[[Call and Election]].

### 26:6–11 — the ruse, the window, and a pun on his own name

> **6** Isaac lived in Gerar.
> **7** The men of the place asked him about his wife. He said, “She is my sister,” for he was afraid
> to say, “My wife”, lest, he thought, “the men of the place might kill me for Rebekah, because she
> is beautiful to look at.”

**The third wife-sister episode, and the first one in which the lie is not even half true.** Sarah
was Abraham's half-sister (20:12), which is the fact the other two turn on; **Rebekah is his cousin's
daughter, and Isaac has no defence available.** He also has no scene of instruction — Abram tells
Sarai what to say at 12:13, and admits at 20:13 that it was a standing policy from the start of the
journey; **here nobody is asked, and Rebekah does not speak in this chapter at all.**

***"Beautiful to look at"*** — *ṭôbat marʾeh*, all but the phrase used of Sarai at 12:11
(*yəpat-marʾeh*) and of Rebekah herself at 24:16. **The motive is quoted verbatim from his father's
mouth**: *"the men of the place might kill me for Rebekah"* against *"they will kill me, but they
will save you alive"* (12:12) and *"They will kill me for my wife's sake"* (20:11). See
[[The Wife-Sister Episodes]] and [[Deception]].

> **8** When he had been there a long time, Abimelech king of the Philistines looked out at a window,
> and saw, and, behold, Isaac was caressing Rebekah, his wife.

**The verb is his own name.** ***Yiṣḥāq məṣaḥēq ʾēt Ribqâ*** — *Isaac was Isaac-ing Rebekah.* The
participle is the intensive of *ṣāḥaq*, **to laugh**, the root the whole family is built on: Abraham
laughs (17:17), Sarah laughs and denies it (18:12–15), Sarah laughs again at the birth (21:6), and
the boy is named *he laughs*. **And the same intensive participle is the word that gets Ishmael
expelled** — *"Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham, mocking"*
(21:9), *məṣaḥēq*, where the WEB and most versions supply a hostile sense the Hebrew does not
require.

**Here the sense is unmistakably conjugal**, because it is what tells the king they are married, and
the WEB's *caressing* is as close as English gets. **So the word that names him is the word that
exposes him**, and it is the same word that put his brother in a desert. Genesis does not point any
of this out. See [[Laughter and the Name Isaac]].

***"Looked out at a window."*** *Wayyašqēp bəʿad haḥallôn.* **The discovery is domestic and
accidental** — no dream, no plague, no closed wombs, no divine speech at all. Genesis 12 had God
strike Pharaoh's house; Genesis 20 had God appear to Abimelech at night and negotiate. **Here the
king simply looks out of a window**, which is where kings and queens in the Hebrew Bible are usually
standing when something goes wrong (Judges 5:28; 2 Samuel 6:16; 2 Kings 9:30–32). **God does not act
in this episode**, and the man who catches the lie catches it by eye.

> **9** Abimelech called Isaac, and said, “Behold, surely she is your wife. Why did you say, ‘She is
> my sister?’” Isaac said to him, “Because I said, ‘Lest I die because of her.’”
> **10** Abimelech said, “What is this you have done to us? One of the people might easily have lain
> with your wife, and you would have brought guilt on us!”
> **11** Abimelech commanded all the people, saying, “He who touches this man or his wife will surely
> be put to death.”

***"What is this you have done to us?"*** — ***mah-zōʾt ʿāśîtā lānû*** — is the third occurrence of
the same interrogative rebuke from a foreign king: 12:18 to Abram, 20:9 to Abraham, 26:10 to Isaac.
**Three times the moral vocabulary in these stories belongs to the outsider**, and the wiki has noted
it at each occurrence.

**But the charge is different this time, and sharper.** Pharaoh and the first Abimelech complained
about what *had* happened; this one complains about what **nearly** did. *"One of the people might
easily have lain with your wife, and you would have brought guilt on us"* — *ʾāšām*, **guilt** or
**guilt-offering**, the collective liability the later law provides sacrifices for. **He is
prosecuting a risk**, and he is right: nothing happened, nobody was taken, and the exposure was real
for as long as the lie held, which the text says was *a long time*.

**And his answer to it is a capital edict.** *"He who touches this man or his wife will surely be put
to death"* — *môt yûmāt*, the formula of the law codes. **It is the first royal decree in Scripture,
and its content is protection for a resident alien and his wife**, issued by a Philistine king about
a man who has just lied to him. Compare his predecessor's *"Behold, my land is before you. Dwell
where it pleases you"* (20:15). **The sequence across the three episodes is a steady improvement in
the foreigner's behaviour and no improvement at all in the patriarch's.** See
[[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]].

### 26:12–17 — a hundredfold, and an eviction

> **12** Isaac sowed in that land, and reaped in the same year one hundred times what he planted.
> Yahweh blessed him.
> **13** The man grew great, and grew more and more until he became very great.
> **14** He had possessions of flocks, possessions of herds, and a great household. The Philistines
> envied him.

***"Isaac sowed in that land."*** ***Wayyizraʿ Yiṣḥāq bāʾāreṣ hahîʾ*** — **the only patriarch in
Genesis who is reported planting a crop.** Abraham and Jacob are herdsmen with tents and flocks;
Isaac puts seed in ground, which is a settled man's act, requires staying through a season, and is
the one thing in his life he is recorded doing on his own initiative besides praying for his wife.
**He does it in a famine, in a country he has been told to live in as a foreigner, on land that is
not his.** See [[Work]].

***"One hundred times what he planted."*** *Mēʾâ šəʿārîm* — literally *a hundred measures*, in a use
of *šaʿar* found nowhere else. **It is the only agricultural yield figure in Genesis**, and the number
is the one Jesus puts at the top of the parable of the sower's three returns (Matthew 13:8, 23; Mark
4:8, 20; Luke 8:8). **A hundredfold is not an ordinary harvest** — dryland barley in this country
returns in single or low double figures — and the narrator attaches the explanation to the same
sentence: ***"Yahweh blessed him."***

**The blessing lands two verses after the lie is exposed.** Genesis has done this in all three
wife-sister episodes: Abram leaves Egypt with livestock, silver and gold (12:16; 13:2); Abraham
leaves Gerar with sheep, cattle, servants, and a thousand pieces of silver (20:14–16); **Isaac is not
even required to leave, and reaps a hundredfold.** The book records the enrichment and passes no
verdict on it, three times running. See [[Blessing]] and [[Favor and Grace]].

***"Grew great, and grew more and more until he became very great."*** *Wayyigdal … wayyēlek hālôk
wəgādēl ʿad kî-gādal məʾōd* — **the root *gādal* four times in one verse**, an intensifying
construction the WEB reproduces closely. **It is the language of 12:2's *"I will make of you a great
nation"* applied to a man's estate.**

***"The Philistines envied him."*** ***Wayqanʾû ʾōtô Pəlištîm*** — **the first occurrence of *qānāʾ*
in the Bible.** The root will run through Rachel envying her sister (30:1) and Joseph's brothers
envying him (37:11) — the two envies that drive the rest of Genesis — before it turns and is applied
to God himself: *ʾĒl qannāʾ*, **a jealous God** (Exodus 20:5). **It enters Scripture here, as what
prosperous foreignness provokes.** See [[Envy]].

> **15** Now all the wells which his father’s servants had dug in the days of Abraham his father, the
> Philistines had stopped, and filled with earth.
> **16** Abimelech said to Isaac, “Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we.”
> **17** Isaac departed from there, encamped in the valley of Gerar, and lived there.

**Filling a well in is not theft; it is eviction.** Seizing a well, which is what happened to Abraham
at 21:25, transfers an asset. **Stopping one with earth destroys it**, and destroys it for whoever
comes next as well. In country where the water table is the only reason a household can stand at all,
it is the complete removal of the possibility of living there. See
[[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]].

***"Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we."*** ***ʿĀṣamtā mimmennû məʾōd.*** **A king
expels a foreign household from his territory because it has grown too numerous and too strong**, and
the Hebrew is within a word of what a later king will say about this man's descendants: *"Behold, the
people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we"* (Exodus 1:9), *rab wəʿāṣûm mimmennû*
— **the sentence that opens the bondage.** Genesis 26 has the same fear, the same verb, the same
comparative, and an eviction instead of a labour draft. See
[[Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt]].

**And Isaac goes.** No protest, no appeal to the promise he was given at 26:3 that *all these lands*
would be his, no reminder of the edict of 26:11. **He is told to leave the country God has just given
him and he leaves**, and this is the first of the three withdrawals that make up the rest of the
chapter.

### 26:18–22 — three wells, and a method

> **18** Isaac dug again the wells of water, which they had dug in the days of Abraham his father.
> For the Philistines had stopped them after the death of Abraham. He called their names after the
> names by which his father had called them.

**The verse is the chapter's own statement of what it is doing.** *"He called their names after the
names by which his father had called them"* — **restoring the names is the act**, more than the
digging is. It is either the redactional seam that stitches two well traditions together or the whole
point of the episode, and **the wiki records both readings without choosing**, on the same principle
it applies to the two creation accounts.

> **19** Isaac’s servants dug in the valley, and found there a well of springing water.
> **20** The herdsmen of Gerar argued with Isaac’s herdsmen, saying, “The water is ours.” He called
> the name of the well Esek, because they contended with him.
> **21** They dug another well, and they argued over that, also. He called its name Sitnah.
> **22** He left that place, and dug another well. They didn’t argue over that one. He called it
> Rehoboth. He said, “For now Yahweh has made room for us, and we will be fruitful in the land.”

***Bəʾēr mayim ḥayyîm*** — **a well of living water**, the Hebrew idiom for a spring rather than a
cistern: water that moves. **It is the phrase the prophets and the Gospels take over** (Jeremiah
2:13; 17:13; Zechariah 14:8; John 4:10–14; 7:38), and its first occurrence in Scripture is a hole in
the ground that two sets of herdsmen immediately fight over.

**Three wells, three names, and the names are a narrative in themselves.**

| well | Hebrew | sense | what happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Esek** | *ʿēśeq*, from *hitʿaśśəqû* | **contention** | dug, disputed, given up |
| **Sitnah** | *śiṭnâ*, from *śāṭan* | **hostility, accusation** | dug, disputed, given up |
| **Rehoboth** | *rəḥōbôt*, from *hirḥîb* | **room, broad places** | dug, unopposed, kept |

***Śiṭnâ* is the noun behind *śāṭān*, the accuser** — the word that stands as a common noun for an
adversary throughout the historical books and as a title in Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3. **Its first
occurrence in the Bible is the name of a well.** See [[Job of Uz]] and [[The Witness in Heaven]] for
where the word goes.

***"Yahweh has made room for us."*** *Kî ʿattâ hirḥîb YHWH lānû* — the verb behind *Rehoboth*, and
the only thing Isaac says in the whole sequence. **He does not argue, does not appeal to his father's
priority, does not invoke the treaty of chapter 21, and does not fight.** He digs, loses it, digs,
loses it, moves further out, and credits the room to God.

**It is the same method Abraham used with Lot** — *"Please separate yourself from me. If you go to
the left hand, then I will go to the right"* (13:9) — and it is the closest thing to a virtue Genesis
credits Isaac with. **Two wells conceded and a third kept is not a defeat**; it is what the book
elsewhere calls making peace, and it costs him twice before it works. See
[[Strife and Peacemaking]] and [[Abram and Lot Separate]].

### 26:23–25 — the one altar of his life

> **23** He went up from there to Beersheba.
> **24** Yahweh appeared to him the same night, and said, “I am the God of Abraham your father. Don’t
> be afraid, for I am with you, and will bless you, and multiply your offspring for my servant
> Abraham’s sake.”

***"I am the God of Abraham your father."*** ***ʾĀnōkî ʾĕlōhê ʾAbrāhām ʾābîkā*** — **the first time
in the Bible that God identifies himself to anyone by the name of an ancestor.** Abraham's servant
had prayed to *"Yahweh, the God of my master Abraham"* (24:12), but a human being saying it about God
is a different thing from God saying it about himself. **From here the formula becomes the standard
one**: to Jacob at Bethel (28:13), to Jacob again at 31:42 and 46:3, and out of a bush to Moses —
*"I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob"* (Exodus
3:6), the verse Jesus uses to argue the resurrection (Matthew 22:32). **God's name in the Hebrew
Bible acquires a genealogy, and this is where it starts.** See [[The Names of God]].

***"Don't be afraid."*** *ʾAl-tîrāʾ* — the second occurrence of the divine reassurance whose first was
to Abram at 15:1, and it is offered to a man who has just been evicted, who lost two wells without a
fight, and who lied about his wife because he was afraid.

***"For my servant Abraham's sake."*** *Baʿăbûr ʾAbrāhām ʿabdî.* **The second time in twenty-two
verses that Isaac's standing is grounded in his father** — 26:5 said *because Abraham obeyed*, this
says *for Abraham's sake* — and **it is the first use of *ʿebed*, servant, as a title of honour in
Scripture**, the word that will become the standing designation of Moses, David, the prophets, and
the figure of Isaiah 40–55.

> **25** He built an altar there, and called on Yahweh’s name, and pitched his tent there. There
> Isaac’s servants dug a well.

**This is the only altar Isaac builds in the Bible.** Abraham builds four — Shechem, Bethel, Hebron,
Moriah (12:7, 12:8, 13:18, 22:9); Jacob builds two and a pillar. **Isaac builds one, on the one
occasion God speaks to him at his own address**, and Genesis records no sacrifice on it.

***"Called on Yahweh's name."*** *Wayyiqrāʾ bəšēm YHWH* — the thread that begins at 4:26 in the days
of Enosh, is Abram's act at Bethel (12:8) and again on his return from Egypt (13:4), and is picked up
by Joel and by Paul as the definition of being saved. **Isaac's one occurrence of it is here.** See
[[Calling on the Name of Yahweh]] and [[Sacrifice and Offering]].

**And the verse's four verbs are a settlement in order**: altar, name, tent, well. **Worship first,
water last** — the reverse of the order the chapter has been living in for twenty verses, and the
only place in it where he does something before anybody has forced him to.

### 26:26–33 — the treaty, and a place name explained for the second time

> **26** Then Abimelech went to him from Gerar, and Ahuzzath his friend, and Phicol the captain of his
> army.
> **27** Isaac said to them, “Why have you come to me, since you hate me, and have sent me away from
> you?”

**The delegation is the one from Genesis 21 with a third man added.** *Abimelech*, *Phicol the captain
of his army* — the same two names and the same title, one patriarch later, at the same well, for the
same kind of agreement. **[[Ahuzzath]] is new, and *"his friend"* is not a remark about their
relationship**: *mērēʿēhû*, from *rēaʿ*, is the ancient Near Eastern court title later borne by
Hushai (2 Samuel 15:37) and Zabud son of Nathan (1 Kings 4:5), *the king's friend* — a standing
office, roughly a chief adviser. **The party is king, counsellor, and general**, which is a full
diplomatic embassy sent to a man they threw out.

***"Why have you come to me, since you hate me?"*** **It is the only sharp thing Isaac says in the
chapter**, and it is addressed to the party that holds all the power. *Śānēʾ*, **hate**, is the legal
register's word for a broken relationship as much as an emotional one — the word for repudiating a
wife or a covenant partner. **He is asking what the visit is for**, and the answer is that they are
frightened of him.

> **28** They said, “We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you. We said, ‘Let there now be an oath
> between us, even between us and you, and let us make a covenant with you,
> **29** that you will do us no harm, as we have not touched you, and as we have done to you nothing
> but good, and have sent you away in peace.’ You are now the blessed of Yahweh.”

***"We saw plainly that Yahweh was with you."*** *Rāʾô rāʾînû kî-hāyâ YHWH ʿimmāk* — the infinitive
absolute, *seeing we have seen*. **It is 21:22 restated by the same court** — *"God is with you in
all that you do"* — and it is the identical political judgment: **a man God is with is a man you sign
with rather than a man you evict**, which is the conclusion they reach one chapter after doing the
evicting. And it is what God had said to Isaac at 26:3 and 26:24, *I will be with you*, arriving back
as an observation from outside.

**Their account of their own conduct is generous to themselves and not quite false.** *"We have not
touched you"* is true in the narrow sense the edict of 26:11 made it true — nobody laid a hand on him
— **and it passes over the wells filled in with earth, the two disputes, and the expulsion.** Isaac
does not contest it.

***"You are now the blessed of Yahweh."*** *ʾAttâ ʿattâ bərûk YHWH.* **Foreigners have now said this
about the family three times** — Melchizedek over Abram (14:19), Laban's household over Rebekah's
escort (24:31), and Abimelech's embassy here — and each time the blessing formula of 12:2–3 comes
back to the family from the outside. See [[Blessing]] and [[The Nations]].

> **30** He made them a feast, and they ate and drank.
> **31** They rose up some time in the morning, and swore an oath to one another. Isaac sent them
> away, and they departed from him in peace.

**A meal, and then an oath at dawn.** Abraham's version had been livestock, seven ewe lambs, and a
question about what the lambs meant (21:27–30); **Isaac's is a banquet and a handshake**, with no
consideration paid, no witness object, and no complaint entered about the wells. *Wayyišbəʿû ʾîš
ləʾāḥîw* — **they swore, each man to his brother.** See [[Oaths and Vows]] and [[Covenant]].

***"They departed from him in peace."*** *Wayyēlkû mēʾittô bəšālôm* — and the whole chapter's method
is in that phrase. **Three wells and a country conceded without a blow, and a general goes home
having done nothing.** See [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

> **32** The same day, Isaac’s servants came, and told him concerning the well which they had dug, and
> said to him, “We have found water.”
> **33** He called it Shibah. Therefore the name of the city is Beersheba to this day.

***"The same day."*** **The timing is the comment.** The well of 26:25, dug on the day he arrived,
comes in with water on the day the oath is sworn — **and Genesis lets the coincidence carry the
theology it declines to state.**

***Šibʿâ*** is **seven**, and *šābaʿ*, **to swear**, is the same three consonants; the reflexive used
of oath-taking in Hebrew means something like *to seven oneself*. **So Genesis explains the name
Beersheba twice**: at 21:31 *"because they both swore an oath there"*, after seven ewe lambs were set
out, and here, where an oath is sworn and a well is found on one day and the well is called *Shibah*.
**The book does not notice that it has done it twice.** See [[Beersheba]].

***"To this day."*** *ʿAd hayyôm hazzeh* — **the narrator stepping out of the story to address his own
present**, and the verse is also the only place Genesis calls Beersheba a *city*.

### 26:34–35 — two verses that set chapter 27 going

> **34** When Esau was forty years old, he took as wife Judith, the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and
> Basemath, the daughter of Elon the Hittite.
> **35** They grieved Isaac’s and Rebekah’s spirits.

**Forty is his father's age at marriage** (25:20), and the two marriages could not be less alike.
**Isaac's wife was fetched four hundred miles from his father's kin by a servant under an oath sworn
on the explicit condition *"you shall not take a wife for my son of the daughters of the Canaanites"*
(24:3).** Esau takes two women of the neighbourhood, on his own initiative, and Genesis reports it in
one sentence with no negotiation, no journey, no well, no prayer, and no consent scene. See
[[Not from the Daughters of the Canaanites]] and [[Marriage]].

***"They grieved Isaac's and Rebekah's spirits."*** ***Wattihyênā mōrat rûaḥ lə-Yiṣḥāq
û-lə-Ribqâ*** — literally **they were a bitterness of spirit to Isaac and to Rebekah.** *Mōrâ* is
from *mārar*, to be bitter, the root behind *Marah* (Exodus 15:23) and Naomi's *"call me Mara"* (Ruth
1:20).

**It is the only sentence in the entire cycle in which Isaac and Rebekah are recorded feeling the
same thing.** The household has been divided since 25:28 — he loves Esau, she loves Jacob — and the
one thing that unites them is their elder son's wives. **And it is the fuse.** Rebekah's stated reason
for sending Jacob away at 27:46 is exactly this: *"I am weary of my life because of the daughters of
Heth. If Jacob takes a wife of the daughters of Heth, such as these, of the daughters of the land,
what good will my life do me?"* — spoken immediately after the blessing has been stolen, and it works
on Isaac at once (28:1–2). **The two verses that look like an appendix to Isaac's chapter are the
pretext for the next one.**

**And the Hittites are the people the family's one piece of property came from.**
[[Ephron the Hittite]] sold Abraham the cave at Machpelah in the most courteous transaction in
Genesis (23:10–18), and the assembly there is *"the children of Heth."* **The family owns one field, bought from Hittites,
and its first Hittite marriages are a grief.** See [[The Children of Heth]] and [[Esau]].

**On the names.** *Yəhûdît* is the feminine of *Yəhûdî* — **Judith, which is to say *Jewess* — and the
first woman to bear the name in Scripture is a Hittite**, four chapters before Judah is born.
*Bāśəmat*, from *bōśem*, is **spice** or **balsam**. *Bəʾērî* is **my well** or **of the well**, in the
chapter about wells. *ʾÊlôn* is **oak** or **terebinth**. Genesis draws attention to none of it.

**And the list does not match the one Genesis gives later.** At 36:2–3 Esau's wives are *Adah the
daughter of Elon the Hittite*, *Oholibamah the daughter of Anah*, and *Basemath Ishmael's daughter* —
**a different name attached to Elon's daughter, and the name *Basemath* attached to a different
father.** The wiki flags this rather than harmonizing it. See
[[The Names of Esau's Wives - Genesis 26 and Genesis 36]].

### What the chapter is doing

**It transfers a covenant without changing a word of it, and shows what carrying it looks like from
underneath.** God says everything to Isaac that he said to Abraham, adds a prohibition, and grounds
the whole thing on the father. Then the chapter spends twenty-eight verses on famine, a lie, a good
harvest, envy, filled wells, an eviction, two conceded springs, and a treaty with the people who
evicted him. **Nothing in it is a test, a trial, or a great deed. It is a man being blessed and being
pushed around, and the two are not separable.**

**And it is the last time the book will move at this speed.** From 27:1 the Jacob cycle takes over —
a blind father, a disguised son, a stolen blessing, a flight, twenty years with Laban, and a night at
a river — and Isaac will never again have a chapter of his own. **The two verses about Esau's wives
are the hinge**, and they are placed where they are because the grief they cause is what Rebekah will
use.

## People

- [[Isaac]] — **the only chapter that is his**, and he spends it being spoken to, caught out,
  blessed, envied, evicted, and asked for an oath. God addresses him for the first time in sixty
  years and grounds the covenant on his father twice over (26:5, 26:24). He is the only patriarch
  Genesis reports sowing a crop, the only one told not to go to Egypt, and the builder of exactly one
  altar. He concedes two wells rather than fight for them, says one sharp sentence in thirty-five
  verses, and never once invokes the promise he has just been given.
- [[Rebekah]] — **named four times and silent throughout.** She is called his sister to strangers
  without being consulted, is the reason a king issues a death penalty, and is not in the chapter
  again until its last word, where she and her husband grieve together for the only time in the
  cycle. Unlike Sarah in both earlier episodes, **she is never taken.**
- [[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]] — **king of Gerar, and the third foreign ruler in this book to hold
  the moral vocabulary.** He discovers the lie by looking out of a window, prosecutes a risk rather
  than an injury, protects the couple by capital edict, then evicts the household for growing too
  strong and comes back with his court to ask for an oath he has no leverage to demand.
- [[Ahuzzath]] — *"his friend"*, which is a court office and not a remark; the third man of the
  embassy, silent, and named only here in the Bible.
- [[Phicol]] — the captain of the army, present at both treaties a generation apart, silent at both,
  and there so that everyone can see what the alternative to agreeing would have been.
- [[God]] — **speaks twice, and both speeches are about Abraham.** The first is the longest thing he
  ever says to Isaac and contains the covenant entire, one prohibition, and a clause of Torah
  vocabulary four hundred years early; the second is *"I am the God of Abraham your father"*, the
  first time in Scripture God names himself by an ancestor. **He does not act in the wife-sister
  episode at all** — no dream, no plague, no closed wombs — and the exposure happens by eye.
- [[Abraham]] — dead, and named nine times: his famine, his oath, his obedience, his wells, his
  servants' digging, his names for them, his God, and his standing as *my servant*. **The chapter is
  held together by a man who is not in it.**
- [[Esau]] — forty, and the last two verses are his: two Hittite wives taken without reference to
  anyone, and a grief. His father was married at forty to a woman fetched from four hundred miles
  away under an oath that excluded exactly this.
- [[Judith]] — daughter of Beeri the Hittite, Esau's first wife, named once; the first Judith in the
  Bible, and a Hittite.
- [[Basemath (daughter of Elon)]] — Esau's second wife, named here and, with a different name and a
  different father, in the register of Genesis 36.
- [[Beeri the Hittite]] — Judith's father, named once; his name means *my well*, in the chapter about
  wells.
- [[Elon the Hittite]] — Basemath's father, and the one name in 26:34 that Genesis 36 keeps.

## Places

- [[Gerar]] — where he is sent instead of Egypt, where he farms, where he is envied, and where he is
  told to leave; the only town in the Bible in which the wife-sister ruse is performed twice.
- [[The Land of the Philistines]] — the country Genesis assigns Gerar to, by the name of a people who
  arrive on that coast centuries later; its king has a Semitic name, a counsellor, a general,
  herdsmen, and a well dispute, and none of the things the Philistines are known for.
- [[Egypt]] — the one place named in the chapter that nobody goes to. *"Don't go down into Egypt"* is
  the only prohibition in the patriarchal promises, and it is the reason Isaac never leaves the land.
- [[Esek]] — *contention*; the first well, dug in the valley of Gerar and given up.
- [[Sitnah]] — *hostility*; the second, given up on the same terms, and the first occurrence in the
  Bible of the noun behind *śāṭān*.
- [[Rehoboth]] — *room*; the third, unopposed, and the only place in the chapter Isaac names for
  something God did rather than for something that was done to him.
- [[Beersheba]] — where he goes up to, is met the same night, builds his one altar, and swears the
  second of the two treaties that explain the town's name; Genesis calls it a *city* only here.
- [[Canaan]] — the land *all these lands* means, promised in 26:3–4 and not named once in the
  chapter.

## Themes

- [[Covenant]] — the whole Abrahamic promise restated to the second generation without a single new
  term, and then grounded on the first generation's obedience.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — *gûr*: God tells the heir of the land promise to live in it as a resident
  alien, and the chapter itemizes what that costs.
- [[Blessing]] — a hundredfold harvest two verses after the lie is exposed, and *"You are now the
  blessed of Yahweh"* said by the people who threw him out.
- [[Deception]] — the third wife-sister ruse, inherited entire and with no half-truth left to stand
  on; nobody in the chapter condemns it and God does not intervene.
- [[Envy]] — *"The Philistines envied him"*, the Bible's first *qānāʾ*, and the root that will drive
  Rachel and Joseph's brothers before it becomes a name for God.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — two wells conceded and a third kept, and a treaty concluded with a
  feast and no consideration; the closest thing to a virtue Genesis credits Isaac with.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — *"they swore an oath to one another"*, the second treaty oath at Beersheba,
  and the well named for the swearing for the second time.
- [[Work]] — the only crop any patriarch plants, sown in a famine on ground he does not own.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — the one altar of his life, with no sacrifice recorded on it.
- [[The Nations]] — a foreign king who protects a foreigner by edict, expels him for prosperity, and
  then comes to sign with him; and the promise that *all the nations of the earth* will be blessed in
  his offspring, restated to a man living among them.
- [[Marriage]] — Esau at forty, taking two wives of the land, against the oath his grandfather made a
  servant swear at 24:3.
- [[The Names of God]] — *"I am the God of Abraham your father"*, the first time God gives himself an
  ancestor's name, and the beginning of the formula that reaches the burning bush.
- [[Call and Election]] — the covenant given to a man on his father's record, in the chapter where he
  lies about his wife.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's box read *Genesis 26:1–35* and **it stands as drawn;
  no unchecked boxes were redrawn this pass.** Two alternatives were considered and rejected.
  Splitting at 26:33 to hold 26:34–35 back for the chapter 27 unit is defensible — those two verses
  are Esau's, not Isaac's — **but the Masoretic and chapter divisions both keep them here, 27:1 opens
  with a fresh time marker (*"When Isaac was old"*), and the grief of 26:35 reads as the closing note
  of the chapter it is in rather than the opening of the next.** Splitting at 26:11 to separate the
  wife-sister episode from the wells was rejected because 26:12's *"Yahweh blessed him"* is the
  chapter's answer to 26:7 and depends on standing next to it.
- **On the title.** The plan's *Isaac in Gerar* was kept although a third of the chapter happens at
  Beersheba. **The alternative, *Isaac at Gerar and Beersheba*, is more accurate and less useful**:
  Gerar is where the chapter's problem is set, Beersheba is where the same treaty as chapter 21 is
  sworn, and [[The Treaty at Beersheba]] already holds that title's natural half. Recorded so that a
  later pass can revisit it rather than rediscover it.
- **On 26:5, unresolved.** *Mišmartî, miṣwōtay, ḥuqqôtay, wətôrōtāy* is Deuteronomic vocabulary in a
  patriarchal narrative, and it is the only place in Genesis where the promise to the next generation
  is given a reason in the previous one's obedience. **Three readings are set out above and none is
  adopted.** What is checkable and recorded: the four terms are the standard Deuteronomic set; the
  *because Abraham obeyed my voice* half of the clause is quoted from 22:18, where the binding
  supplies the referent; and Genesis 15:17 has God pass between the pieces alone, which the wiki keeps
  standing beside 26:5 rather than under it.
- **On chronology, unresolved and flagged.** Genesis gives no age for Isaac anywhere in 26:1–33, and
  the ruse at 26:7 is easier to picture if there were no twins in the household — which would put the
  Gerar material before 25:19–26 in time. **26:34 then jumps to Esau at forty, when Isaac is a
  hundred.** The chapter reads as a block of Isaac tradition placed whole rather than as a sequence,
  and the wiki records that as an observation about the arrangement, **not as a correction to the
  text and not as a claim that any event is out of order.** Nothing in the chapter dates itself.
- **On the relation to Genesis 12, 20, and 21.** Every substantial element of this chapter has an
  Abraham counterpart, and [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]] and
  [[The Wells of Abraham and the Wells of Isaac]] already set out the three standing accounts — one
  tradition told of two patriarchs, two real incidents in a family that kept using the same wells, and
  a deliberate literary rhyme. **The wiki adopts none of them**, and this pericope adds one datum to
  the file: **26:1 and 26:18 are the book's own cross-references to the Abraham material**, which is
  either the seam or the point.
- **On *məṣaḥēq* at 26:8.** The participle is the same form as 21:9, where it is what Sarah sees
  Ishmael doing and where the WEB supplies *mocking*. **Here the sense is conjugal and the context
  fixes it**, which is itself an argument that the 21:9 rendering is a choice rather than a reading.
  The wiki notes the two occurrences together and does not use one to settle the other.
- **Which new pages were made and which were not.** [[Abimelech (of Isaac's time)]] was created per
  the note already standing on [[Abimelech (of Abraham's time)]], which reserved it. [[Ahuzzath]],
  [[Judith]], [[Basemath (daughter of Elon)]], [[Beeri the Hittite]], and [[Elon the Hittite]] were
  created on the wiki's existing practice for named-once figures ([[Iscah]], [[Reumah]], [[Aner]]).
  [[Esek]], [[Sitnah]], and [[Rehoboth]] were created as places, following [[Beer Lahai Roi]];
  **Shibah deliberately did not get a page** and is folded into [[Beersheba]], because 26:33 is an
  etiology of that town's name rather than a separate site. **The valley of Gerar** was folded into
  [[Gerar]] for the same reason.
- **On *Basemath*.** Genesis 36:3 names a second Basemath, Ishmael's daughter, so the page created
  here is qualified from the start as *Basemath (daughter of Elon)* rather than left bare to be
  renamed when Genesis 36 arrives. **The collision is certain, it is documented inside this pericope's
  own cross-references, no inbound links existed to break, and the failure mode the wiki's
  disambiguation rule exists for is silent at both ends.** The counterpart page, when it is created,
  is *Basemath (daughter of Ishmael)*.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** The stolen blessing (27:1–45); Rebekah's use of 26:35 as her
  pretext and Esau's third marriage (27:46–28:9); Jacob at this well being told the opposite of 26:2
  (46:1–5); and Esau's *toledot* with its divergent wife list (36:1–8). All are referenced and none is
  developed. **No dangling links were left.**
