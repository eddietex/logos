# Abram in Egypt

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 12:10–20

## Summary

The promise is four verses old, and the land it was made in cannot feed him.

*"There was a famine in the land"* (12:10). Genesis puts it in five Hebrew words, with no
transition, immediately after Yahweh has said *"I will give this land to your offspring"* and Abram
has crossed the whole country building altars. The land of promise turns out to be a land you can
starve in. Nothing in the text marks this as a test, a punishment, or a surprise; it is simply the
next sentence, and it sends the man who has just arrived back out again.

**Every verb in the first half is a descent.** *Wayyēred*, *he went down* — the standing verb for
travel to Egypt in Hebrew, geographically true (the Nile delta is lower than the Judean hills) and
theologically loaded for the rest of the canon: Jacob goes down (46:3), the brothers go down (42:3),
and Israel *comes up* out of it. He goes *lāgûr*, **to sojourn**, the verb of resident-alien status
that [[Land and Sojourning]] is built on. He was a *gēr* in the land promised to him; now he is a
*gēr* in the land it isn't.

**He does not consult Yahweh, and Yahweh does not speak.** This is the first pericope of the Abraham
cycle with no divine speech in it at all — no command, no promise, no appearance, no altar, and no
calling on the name that he had proclaimed at [[Bethel]] one verse earlier. God is present in
exactly one clause of this story (12:17), and in it he acts without saying anything. The silence is
the pericope's shape: the man who was defined by obedience to a voice is now improvising, and the
narrator declines to tell us what God thinks of the improvisation.

**Abram's first recorded words in Scripture are an instruction to his wife to lie.** He has been
spoken to by God, promised five things, and moved four hundred miles without one line of reported
speech. Then:

> **11** *"See now, I know that you are a beautiful woman to look at.*
> **12** *It will happen, when the Egyptians see you, that they will say, ‘This is his wife.’ They
> will kill me, but they will save you alive.*
> **13** *Please say that you are my sister, that it may be well with me for your sake, and that my
> soul may live because of you."*

Three verses: an observation, a prediction, and a request, with the motive stated twice and both
times about himself — *that it may be well with **me***, *that **my** soul may live*. What he does
not say is what becomes of her once the Egyptians believe it. The plan has no step in which she is
protected; it works precisely because she is taken. He has calculated that a husband is a killable
obstacle and a brother is a man to negotiate with, and he is right about the Egyptians and silent
about the price.

**And it is not quite a lie.** Genesis 20:12, in the second of these episodes, has Abraham explain
to Abimelech: *"she is indeed my sister, the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my
mother."* On the plain sense she is [[Terah]]'s daughter by another wife, so *say that you are my
sister* is a true sentence deployed to produce a false belief. Genesis lets him say so eight
chapters later and never says it here, which leaves the reader of chapter 12 with what looks like a
flat lie and the reader of the whole book with something worse and more familiar. See
[[Deception]].

**Sarai says nothing, and the grammar keeps her an object.** She is spoken to (12:11), looked at
(12:14), praised (12:15), and *taken* — *wattuqqaḥ hāʾiššâ bêt parʿōh*, *"the woman was taken into
Pharaoh's house"*, a passive with no agent named. She does not answer her husband, does not speak to
Pharaoh, and is not asked. In eleven verses about her, she has no verb of her own. The one voice
raised on her behalf is not hers and not her husband's: it is the narrator's, at 12:17, insisting on
the word Abram had suppressed — *"because of Sarai, **Abram's wife**."*

**Abram is paid.** *"He dealt well with Abram for her sake. He had sheep, cattle, male donkeys, male
servants, female servants, female donkeys, and camels"* (12:16) — a bride-price, and the text lets
it sit in the middle of the story without a word of comment. This is the pericope's most
uncomfortable verse and its most easily skipped: the promise of blessing (12:2) is fulfilled in
livestock, on the wrong terms, by the wrong king, in the wrong country, and Genesis 13:2 will
describe the result as *"Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold."* He leaves Egypt
better off than he entered it, and the reason is his wife.

**Then God moves, once.** *"Yahweh afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of
Sarai, Abram's wife"* (12:17). Hebrew *wayənaggaʿ … nəgāʿîm gədōlîm*, a cognate accusative —
*plagued him with great plaguings*, the noun that Leviticus 13 will use for the marks of skin
disease. Genesis does not say what the plagues were, how Pharaoh worked out the cause, or whether
Yahweh told him. What it does establish is the direction of the rescue: not Abram's courage, not
Sarai's resistance, not a negotiation, but an unexplained affliction landing on a household that had
been told the woman was available. The promise defends itself, over the head of the man holding it.

**The rebuke comes from the pagan.** *"What is this that you have done to me? Why didn't you tell me
that she was your wife? Why did you say, 'She is my sister,' so that I took her to be my wife?"*
(12:18–19). Three questions in the interrogative form Genesis has so far reserved for God —
*"Where are you?"*, *"What have you done?"* (3:9, 13; 4:10) — and here they are in the mouth of an
Egyptian king, aimed at the man God chose. **Abram does not answer.** Not one word. He obeyed
without speaking at 12:4, he spoke to arrange the ruse at 12:13, and he is silent under the
accusation. Abimelech asks almost the same question at 20:9 (*"What have you done to us?"*) and
again of Isaac at 26:10 (*"What is this you have done to us?"*), and in all three the outsider is
the one who talks about right and wrong.

**The last verse is a deportation.** *"Pharaoh commanded men concerning him, and they escorted him
away with his wife and all that he had"* (12:20). Hebrew *šillaḥ*, *sent away* — the verb of
*"let my people go"* (Exodus 5:1) — with an armed escort attached. He is not punished, not robbed,
and not permitted to stay.

### The pattern this sets, in one table

The episode is Genesis's first sketch of the Exodus, and the correspondences are close enough that
Ramban made a principle out of them: *maʿăśēh ʾābôt sîmān lə-bānîm*, the deeds of the fathers are a
sign to the sons.

| | Genesis 12:10–20 | Exodus 1–14 |
|---|---|---|
| Why they go | famine in the land (12:10) | famine in the land (Genesis 47:4) |
| The verb | *went down* into Egypt, *to sojourn* | *went down*; *sojourned* (Deuteronomy 26:5) |
| The danger | *"they will kill me, but they will save you alive"* | *"every son … you shall cast into the river, and every daughter you shall save alive"* (Exodus 1:22) |
| God's act | *"Yahweh afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues"* | the ten plagues on Pharaoh and his house |
| Pharaoh's response | *"take her, and go your way"* | *"rise up, go out from among my people"* (Exodus 12:31) |
| The departure | escorted out (12:20) | driven out in haste (Exodus 12:33) |
| What they leave with | *"very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold"* (13:2) | silver, gold, and clothing from the Egyptians (Exodus 12:35–36) |
| Where they go | back up to the altar between Bethel and Ai (13:3–4) | to the mountain, to worship |

See [[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

## Key verses

> **10** There was a famine in the land. Abram went down into Egypt to live as a foreigner there,
> for the famine was severe in the land.

> **11** When he had come near to enter Egypt, he said to Sarai his wife, “See now, I know that you
> are a beautiful woman to look at.

> **12** It will happen, when the Egyptians see you, that they will say, ‘This is his wife.’ They
> will kill me, but they will save you alive.

> **13** Please say that you are my sister, that it may be well with me for your sake, and that my
> soul may live because of you.”

> **14** When Abram had come into Egypt, Egyptians saw that the woman was very beautiful.

> **15** The princes of Pharaoh saw her, and praised her to Pharaoh; and the woman was taken into
> Pharaoh’s house.

> **16** He dealt well with Abram for her sake. He had sheep, cattle, male donkeys, male servants,
> female servants, female donkeys, and camels.

> **17** Yahweh afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai, Abram’s wife.

> **18** Pharaoh called Abram and said, “What is this that you have done to me? Why didn’t you tell
> me that she was your wife?

> **19** Why did you say, ‘She is my sister,’ so that I took her to be my wife? Now therefore, see
> your wife, take her, and go your way.”

> **20** Pharaoh commanded men concerning him, and they escorted him away with his wife and all that
> he had.

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *Abram*. He speaks for the first time in Scripture, and what he says is *"Please say
  that you are my sister."* He leaves the land he has just been promised, does not consult the God
  who promised it, trades on his wife's safety for his own, accepts the payment, is rebuked by a
  foreigner, and answers nothing. Genesis passes no verdict on any of it. The same man is the New
  Testament's pattern of faith, and both facts are on the page.
- [[Sarah]] — *Sarai*, and the entire cause of the action without a word or a choice in it. She is
  sixty-five on the arithmetic of 17:17, and the Egyptians find her *"very beautiful"*; she is taken
  into a king's house and returned by a plague. Genesis records no reaction from her, then or after.
  What she does get is the narrator's phrase at 12:17 — *Abram's wife* — supplied at the moment her
  husband is profiting from having denied it. See [[Marriage]].
- [[God]] — *Yahweh*, present in one clause and silent in all the others. He does not warn Abram
  before the descent, forbid it, comment on the ruse, or speak to Pharaoh (contrast 20:3, where he
  addresses Abimelech in a dream and calls Abraham a prophet). He afflicts, and the promise survives
  by force rather than by anyone's virtue — which is the pericope's actual theology and the reason
  it is in the book.
- [[Pharaoh (of Abram's time)]] — unnamed, as every Pharaoh in Genesis and Exodus is. He takes the
  woman on the information he was given, is struck for it, works out the cause, and delivers the only
  moral speech in the passage. He returns the wife, keeps nothing, and expels the household under
  escort.
- **The princes of Pharaoh** — *śārê parʿōh*, the courtiers who see Sarai and *"praised her to
  Pharaoh"*. Their function is to make the taking official: this is not a seizure in the street but a
  court procedure, which is what makes Abram's silence at 12:18 so hard to read as anything but
  acquiescence.
- **The Egyptians** — a collective, and the only thing said about them is that they look. Abram's
  prediction of their behaviour (*"they will kill me"*) is never tested; nobody in Egypt does
  anything violent in this passage, and the one killing threatened in it is hypothetical.
- **Hagar, possibly** — not named until 16:1, where she is *"a servant, an Egyptian, whose name was
  Hagar"*. The *"female
  servants"* of 12:16 are the obvious place for her to have come from, and an old midrash (Genesis
  Rabbah 45:1) makes her Pharaoh's own daughter, given to Abram out of this episode. Genesis never
  connects the two verses. The wiki records the inference as an inference; see the notes.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — its first appearance as a place where anything happens, rather than as a name in
  [[The Table of Nations]]. Everything the rest of the Bible does with Egypt is prefigured here in
  eleven verses: the refuge that feeds you in a famine, the power that takes what it wants, the house
  God strikes, and the country you are escorted out of richer than you came.
- [[Canaan]] — offstage and failing. The land promised at 12:7 produces a famine at 12:10, and the
  text draws no line between the two. It is the first of a series: Isaac faces one (26:1) and is
  forbidden to go down; Jacob's sons face one (41:57–42:2) and go.
- [[The Negev]] — the road, unnamed here. He ends the previous pericope *"still going on toward the
  South"* (12:9) and the next one *"went up out of Egypt … into the South"* (13:1); the descent
  happens in the gap between those two verses.

## Themes

- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the theme's sharpest test. The promise of land is four verses old, and
  the land starves him out of itself. He becomes a *gēr* in Egypt while holding a title deed to
  Canaan that names his unborn offspring.
- [[Deception]] — the Bible's first human deceit worked by a chosen man, and the founding entry in a
  pattern that runs through Isaac, Rebekah, Jacob, Laban, and Joseph's brothers. It is a true
  statement used to produce a false belief, which is the form the rest of Genesis will prefer.
- [[Blessing]] — fulfilled, embarrassingly. *"I will bless you"* (12:2) turns into sheep, cattle,
  donkeys, servants, and camels, obtained by a ruse, from a king who will be plagued for accepting
  it. Genesis does not annotate the irony.
- [[Curse]] — *"I will curse him who treats you with contempt"* (12:3) operates for the first time,
  and it lands on a man who treated Abram generously on the strength of what Abram told him. The
  clause protects the promise, not the justice of the case.
- [[Judgment]] — God's one act here is an affliction, delivered without a word of explanation and
  aimed at a household rather than an individual. Compare the pattern of interrogation-then-sentence
  in Genesis 3 and 4: here the questions come from the human king and the sentence from God, in that
  order and with no overlap.
- [[Marriage]] — a wife handed over, taken into another man's house, and returned; and a marriage
  which is also a half-sibling relationship the Torah will later forbid (Leviticus 18:9). Genesis
  narrates the endangerment without a category for it.
- [[The Fear of God]] — by its absence, and the word is Abraham's own. At 20:11, explaining the same
  ruse to Abimelech, he says *"Surely the fear of God is not in this place. They will kill me for my
  wife's sake."* That is his stated reasoning, given retrospectively, and it turns out to be wrong
  about both kings.
- [[Call and Election]] — unaffected. Nothing in the pericope is conditional, revoked, or even
  mentioned. The chosen man behaves badly, and the choosing is not reopened.

## Notes

- **Genesis does not evaluate the episode, and readers have never been able to leave it alone.** The
  narrator supplies no adverb, no *and it was evil in the eyes of Yahweh*, no punishment, and no
  vindication. The strongest classical verdict against Abram is **Ramban's** (Nachmanides on 12:10):
  *"Abraham our father unintentionally committed a great sin"* by leaving the land in the famine and
  by exposing his wife, *"and because of this act the exile in the land of Egypt at the hand of
  Pharaoh was decreed for his children."* The strongest reading in the other direction takes the
  famine as a trial and the ruse as an ordinary precaution in a lawless world — the position implied
  by Abraham's own defence at 20:11 — with Yahweh's intervention as the covenant-keeping that the
  story is really about. The wiki takes neither. What is worth noting is the reason the question is
  open: Genesis routinely narrates the patriarchs' worst behaviour with the same flat voice it uses
  for their best, and any verdict a reader reaches has to be imported.
- **The chronology of Sarai's beauty.** Abram is seventy-five at 12:4, and 17:17 makes Sarai ten
  years younger, so she is about sixty-five when the Egyptians call her *"very beautiful"* and about
  ninety when she bears Isaac. The tension is real and has three standard answers: that the
  patriarchal lifespans stretch every stage of life proportionally (a woman of sixty-five in a
  hundred-and-seventy-five-year life); that the numbers belong to a different layer of the text than
  the narrative; and the frankly legendary route, taken by the **Genesis Apocryphon** from Qumran
  (1QapGen 20), which fills the gap with a courtier's poem cataloguing her face, hair, and hands and
  concluding that she surpasses all women. The wiki notes the difficulty and adopts none of the
  three.
- **The camels of 12:16 are the best-known anachronism crux in the patriarchal narratives.** Camels
  appear as domestic livestock at 12:16, 24:10–64, 30:43, 31:17, 32:7, and 37:25. The archaeozoology
  from the southern Levant — most influentially Sapir-Hen and Ben-Yosef's work on the Aravah copper
  smelting camps — puts the earliest concentrations of domesticated camel bone in the region no
  earlier than the late tenth or ninth century BC, which is a millennium after any conventional date
  for Abraham. The counter-case cites scattered earlier attestations elsewhere in the Near East and
  argues for limited, elite use before widespread caravan use. The two positions are not really about
  camels; they are about whether the patriarchal narratives preserve second-millennium memory or
  first-millennium composition. The wiki records the crux and takes no position on the date.
- **The bride-price is the verse most often passed over.** 12:16 is not a gift to a guest but a
  settlement on a woman's family, and the list is graded from sheep upward. Its consequence is
  material: 13:2 is a direct result, and the herds it names are what make Abram and Lot's herdsmen
  quarrel in the next pericope. The wealth that separates Abram from Lot, in other words, came out of
  Egypt with Sarai. Genesis says none of this; it simply reports the inventory in one verse and the
  quarrel in another.
- **How Pharaoh knew.** 12:17 gives the plague and 12:18 gives Pharaoh already in possession of the
  facts, with nothing in between. Genesis 20 fills the same gap with a dream and a divine speech;
  Genesis 12 leaves it blank. Rabbinic and patristic readers have supplied divination, a confession
  from Sarai, and an angel; the text supplies a break between two verses. It is worth noticing as a
  feature of the pericope rather than a defect: this is the version of the story in which God says
  nothing to anybody.
- **The three questions, and the silence after them.** *"What is this that you have done to me?"* is
  the same construction as God's *"What have you done?"* to Eve (3:13) and to Cain (4:10), and
  Abimelech will use it again at 20:9. In every other instance the person questioned answers —
  excuses, deflects, or protests. Abram, alone among them, says nothing at all, and the narrator does
  not tell us why. He will answer the same charge at 20:11–13, with an explanation that concedes the
  ruse was a standing policy: *"When God caused me to wander from my father's house, I said to her,
  'This is your kindness which you shall show to me. Everywhere that we go, say of me, "He is my
  brother."'"*
- **Whether the three wife-sister stories are one story.** Source criticism has read 12:10–20 (J),
  20:1–18 (E), and 26:6–11 (J) as three developments of a single traditional tale, pointing to the
  shared structure and the tidy progression from a taking with no explanation, to a taking prevented
  before consummation with a dream to explain it, to no taking at all. Literary readers reply that
  the differences are purposeful — the escalating moral cover given to the patriarch, the shifting
  amount of divine speech — and that a book that repeats a scene three times is usually doing
  something with the repetition. Both readings notice the same data. See
  [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].
- **No altar in Egypt.** Abram builds altars at Shechem (12:7), between Bethel and Ai (12:8), at
  Mamre (13:18), and again on the return at 13:4, where he *"called on Yahweh's name"*. In Egypt he
  builds nothing and calls on no one. It is the only stretch of his life in Genesis with a place, a
  duration, and no worship in it, and the narrator makes the point by omission — which is also how it
  makes the point about the promise, since the promise holds anyway.
