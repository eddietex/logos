# A Wife for Isaac

**Book:** [[Genesis]]
**Reference:** Genesis 24:1–67

## Summary

**This is the longest chapter in Genesis — sixty-seven verses — and God does not speak in it once.**

Yahweh is named nineteen times. He is sworn by, prayed to, thanked, credited, and blessed. Every
person in the chapter who opens their mouth attributes the outcome to him: the servant twice, Laban
and Bethuel jointly, the servant again. **And the narrator reports no divine word, no appearance, no
angel, and no intervention of any kind.** After twelve chapters in which God speaks to Abraham eight
times, tells Hagar her son's name in a desert, argues with a man about Sodom, warns a Philistine king
in a dream, and calls out of the sky to stop a knife — after all of that, the chapter in which the
covenant actually acquires its next generation is the one where he is silent from beginning to end.

**What replaces the divine speech is human speech.** Roughly two-thirds of the chapter is dialogue,
and one continuous block of it — the servant's speech at 24:34–49 — retells the first half of the
chapter back to the audience that needs convincing, at length, with changes. Genesis nowhere else
tells the same story twice in one chapter, and the differences between the two tellings are the most
carefully arranged thing in it.

### The shape

| verses | scene | who is driving |
|---|---|---|
| **1–9** | the oath at Abraham's tent: no Canaanite wife, and no going back | Abraham |
| **10–27** | the well outside the city of Nahor: a prayer, a sign, and Rebekah | the servant |
| **28–49** | the house: Laban's welcome, and the servant's retelling | the servant |
| **50–61** | the answer, the gifts, the delay, and the one question put to Rebekah | Laban, then Rebekah |
| **62–67** | the field at evening: Isaac | nobody speaks but the servant, in one clause |

**Abraham is in the first nine verses and never appears again in this chapter**, and he has one more
narrative appearance in Genesis: 25:1–11, his second family, his will, and his funeral. He speaks
nowhere in it. **His last recorded act is to make a servant swear**, and his last recorded words are a
statement of the promise. He does not meet Rebekah, does not attend the wedding, and is not told the
outcome on the page.

### 24:1–9 — the oath, and the two things that are not negotiable

> **2** Abraham said to his servant, the elder of his house, who ruled over all that he had, “Please
> put your hand under my thigh.
> **3** I will make you swear by Yahweh, the God of heaven and the God of the earth, that you shall
> not take a wife for my son of the daughters of the Canaanites, among whom I live.
> **4** But you shall go to my country, and to my relatives, and take a wife for my son Isaac.”

**The gesture is a euphemism and everybody has always known it.** *Śîm-nāʾ yādəkā taḥat yərēkî* —
*put your hand under my thigh* — where *yārēk* is the upper thigh and, in this construction, the
genitals; the same word supplies *"all the souls who came out of Jacob's thigh"* (Exodus 1:5). **It
occurs twice in the Bible and both times in Genesis**: here, and at 47:29, where Jacob makes Joseph
swear the same way about his burial. See [[Put Your Hand Under My Thigh]].

Why there. The obvious readings are that the oath is sworn on the organ of circumcision — the
covenant sign, cut into this exact flesh at 17:23–27 — or on the source of the offspring the oath
exists to secure, or both. Neither is stated. What is checkable is that **both occurrences concern a
promise that has to outlive the man swearing it**, and that both are about not being absorbed by the
country the family is living in: no Canaanite wife here, no Egyptian grave there.

**There are two prohibitions, and only one of them is about the bride.**

- *"you shall not take a wife for my son of the daughters of the Canaanites"* (24:3)
- *"Beware that you don't bring my son there again"* (24:6), and again — *"Only you shall not bring
  my son there again"* (24:8)

**The second is repeated, and it is the one Abraham will not release him from.** The servant asks the
sensible question — what if she will not come? — and gets a full discharge from the oath in that
case (24:8), so the marriage is conditional. **The land is not.** Isaac may end up unmarried; he may
not end up in Mesopotamia. *Lek-lekā* was one-way (12:1), and this is Abraham making sure it stays
one-way after he is dead. See [[Leave Your Country - The Two Lek-Lekha Commands]] and
[[Land and Sojourning]].

### 24:7 — the last thing Abraham says in the Bible

> **7** Yahweh, the God of heaven, who took me from my father’s house, and from the land of my birth,
> who spoke to me, and who swore to me, saying, ‘I will give this land to your offspring. He will
> send his angel before you, and you shall take a wife for my son from there.

**It is a summary of his own life in one sentence, and it is accurate.** *Who took me from my
father's house* — 12:1. *And from the land of my birth* — 11:31, 12:5. *Who spoke to me* — eight
times. *And who swore to me* — 22:16, the one oath God swears in Genesis, sworn nineteen verses after
the knife. Abraham cites the covenant's history as evidence for a proposition about the future, which
is what faith looks like when a man is stating it rather than being praised for it. See [[Faith]].

***"He will send his angel before you."*** **This is the first time in Scripture anyone says that
God will send an angel ahead of a journey**, and it is said by a man, not by God, and no angel
appears. Exodus will make the sentence a formula — *"Behold, I send an angel before you, to keep you
by the way"* (23:20; and 32:34; 33:2) — of the whole nation's march to the same land Abraham is
refusing to let his son leave. **Here it is a private prediction about a shopping trip for a wife, it
is never confirmed by the narrator, and the servant repeats it to Rebekah's family as though it had
been** (24:40). See [[The Angel of Yahweh]].

### 24:10–14 — the Bible's first prayer for guidance

> **12** He said, “Yahweh, the God of my master Abraham, please give me success today, and show
> kindness to my master Abraham.

**Nobody in the Bible has prayed like this before.** Abraham has argued with God about a city
(18:23–33) and interceded for a king (20:17), and both are conversations begun by God's own
appearing. Hagar was spoken to. Nobody so far has simply addressed God, unbidden, with a request for
help in a task, and got it. **The first person in Scripture to do so is a slave, he is unnamed, and
he does it standing next to a watering trough.** See [[Prayer]] and [[Slavery and Servitude]].

Three things about the prayer are worth separating.

**It is short and it is entirely about somebody else.** It opens by naming God as *the God of my
master Abraham*, and *my master* occurs three times in the three verses — eighteen times in the
chapter. He asks nothing for himself; the success he requests is his master's. The verb is *haqrēh-nāʾ*, *cause it to happen before me* —
from *qārâ*, the root behind *miqreh*, **chance**. He asks God to arrange a coincidence.

**The sign he proposes is not a coin-toss.** It is often filed with Gideon's fleece (Judges 6:36–40)
and Jonathan's password at Michmash (1 Samuel 14:8–10) as a fleece-type test, and the form is
identical. But look at what he actually specifies: *"Drink, and I will also give your camels a
drink."* **Ten camels at the end of a desert journey will drink until they stop, and a woman with one
pitcher will be at that trough for a long time.** The servant has not asked for an arbitrary token;
he has designed a test that can only be passed by someone with a specific and expensive kind of
generosity toward a stranger. **Whether that makes it wisdom or presumption, Genesis does not say** —
and the narrator's refusal to grade it is the same refusal it has kept for five pericopes running.

**And it is answered before it is finished.** *"Before he had finished speaking, behold, Rebekah came
out"* (24:15) — *ṭerem killâ lədabbēr*. The servant's own retelling makes the timing even tighter and
moves the prayer inward: *"Before I had finished speaking **in my heart**"* (24:45). Compare Isaiah
65:24, *"before they call, I will answer"*.

***Ḥesed*, four times, and the pair that becomes a creed.** The word the WEB renders *kindness* and
*loving kindness* is **ḥesed** — covenant loyalty, the faithfulness owed inside a relationship rather
than the favour shown outside one. It carries the chapter: 24:12 (*show kindness to my master*),
24:14 (*you have shown kindness*), 24:27 (*his loving kindness and his truth*), 24:49 (*deal kindly
and truly*). Twice it is paired with *ʾemet*, **truth** — ***ḥesed we-ʾemet*** — and that pair is
what God will say about himself at Exodus 34:6, in the sentence the rest of the Hebrew Bible quotes
more than any other. **A servant asking for a favour at a well is using the vocabulary God will later
use to define his own character.** See [[Favor and Grace]] and [[Covenant]].

### 24:15–27 — Rebekah, described entirely in verbs

> **18** She said, “Drink, my lord.” She hurried, and let down her pitcher on her hand, and gave him
> a drink.
> **19** When she had finished giving him a drink, she said, “I will also draw for your camels, until
> they have finished drinking.”
> **20** She hurried, and emptied her pitcher into the trough, and ran again to the well to draw, and
> drew for all his camels.

**Genesis gives her two adjectives and about a dozen verbs, and the adjectives are the least
interesting thing on the page.** *"Very beautiful to look at, a virgin"* (24:16) is the standard
notice; then: *came out, went down, filled, came up, said, hurried, let down, gave, finished, said,
hurried, emptied, ran, drew.* **She is the most physically active person in the Abraham cycle**, and
every verb in the sequence is work.

**She passes the sign and then exceeds it.** The servant asked God for a woman who would say a
sentence. Rebekah says the sentence — and then does the thing, unprompted, at length, for ten
animals, *"until they have finished drinking"*, which is the servant's own phrase given back to him
with a completion clause attached.

**And the man says nothing while she does it.** *"The man looked steadfastly at her, remaining
silent, to know whether Yahweh had made his journey prosperous or not"* (24:21) — *mištāʾēh*, a rare
verb, gazing in astonishment or suspense. He watches the entire watering without a word. It is the
one moment of interiority Genesis gives him, and it is a man withholding judgment on his own answered
prayer.

**Then the gold comes out before the question does.** *"As the camels had done drinking, the man took
a golden ring of half a shekel weight, and two bracelets … and said, 'Whose daughter are you?'"*
(24:22–23). He hands over substantial jewellery — a nose-ring, per 24:47 — to a woman whose family he
has not yet established. **His own retelling reverses the order** (24:47: *"I asked her … I put the
ring on her nose"*). One of the two is tidier than what happened; see below.

***"Blessed be Yahweh, the God of my master Abraham."*** (24:27) The bow, and the second recorded
human blessing of God in Genesis — Noah's *"Blessed be Yahweh, the God of Shem"* (9:26) is the first.
He credits the guidance retroactively: *"As for me, Yahweh has led me on the way."* *Bad-derek nāḥanî
YHWH* — **on the road he led me** — which is as near as this chapter comes to a report of divine
action, and it is a character's claim, not the narrator's.

### 24:28–33 — Laban sees the gold

> **30** When he saw the ring, and the bracelets on his sister’s hands, and when he heard the words
> of Rebekah his sister … he came to the man.
> **31** He said, “Come in, you blessed of Yahweh. Why do you stand outside? For I have prepared the
> house, and room for the camels.”

**Genesis introduces Laban with a causal clause, and the cause is jewellery.** *Wayhî kirʾōt et-hannezem*
— *and it happened, when he saw the ring* — then he ran. The verse gives the sight of the gold before
the hearing of the report, in that order, and only then the running. **The first thing the Bible ever
tells us about Laban is what he noticed**, and it will be doing the same thing to him for eight
chapters: the wage changed ten times (31:7, 41), the substituted bride (29:23–25), the household gods
he chases his daughter across a desert for (31:30–35). **He is a hospitable man throughout, and he is
never generous without an angle**, and the chapter that turns on a woman's unforced kindness at a
trough opens its portrait of her brother by telling you what he was looking at. See [[Laban]].

The hospitality itself is impeccable — unloading, straw and feed, water for feet, a meal — and it
follows [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] closely enough to be a deliberate echo. See
[[Hospitality]].

***"I will not eat until I have told my message."*** (24:33) The servant refuses food until the
business is done, which is the reverse of every hospitality scene so far in Genesis, where the meal
comes first and the announcement after it.

### 24:34–49 — the retelling, and its six edits

Fifteen verses restating what the reader has just read. **The interest is entirely in the
differences**, because a speech to Rebekah's family is a negotiation and the servant knows it.

| what happened | what he says | the effect |
|---|---|---|
| *"go to my country, and to my relatives"* (24:4) | *"go to my **father's house**, and to my relatives"* (24:38) | narrows the mandate onto this house specifically — flattery, and pressure |
| *"Beware that you don't bring my son there again"* — said twice (24:6, 8) | **omitted entirely** | the one clause that says *your daughter must emigrate and never see us again* is not mentioned |
| release if *"the woman isn't willing to follow you"* (24:8) | release if *"they don't give her to you"* (24:41) | moves the decision from the bride to the family — who are the people he is standing in front of |
| the prayer spoken aloud at the well (24:12) | *"Before I had finished speaking **in my heart**"* (24:45) | a silent prayer is harder to have staged |
| gifts given, **then** *"Whose daughter are you?"* (24:22–23) | *"I asked her … **then** I put the ring on her nose"* (24:47) | removes the appearance of buying an answer |
| nothing said about the estate | *"He has given all that he has to him"* (24:36) | the marriage settlement, volunteered, to a household that has just seen a shekel of gold |

**None of these is a lie and all of them are advocacy.** The servant is doing what a good envoy does,
and Genesis reports it in full without a syllable of comment — the same silence it keeps about the
price at Machpelah and about Abraham's two sister-stories. **It also, quietly, gives the family a
version of the arrangement in which their consent is the deciding factor and the exile clause has
gone missing**, and then has them consent.

**He also gets a relationship slightly wrong**, or loosely right: *"my master's brother's daughter"*
(24:48). Rebekah is Nahor's *grand*daughter. *Bat* and *ʾaḥ* both run loose in Hebrew kinship
language, and the servant's point — she is from the right house — is exactly true.

### 24:50–61 — the answer, the delay, and the one question anybody asks her

> **50** Then Laban and Bethuel answered, “The thing proceeds from Yahweh. We can’t speak to you bad
> or good.

**Laban is named before his father.** [[Bethuel]] is alive, is in the room, and answers only as the
second half of a compound subject; by 24:53 the gifts go *"to her brother and her mother"* with no
father in the sentence at all. The explanations offered — an aged or ill father, a household where a
brother held authority over a sister's marriage, a later insertion of the name — are all guesses, and
**Genesis does not say**.

***"We can't speak to you bad or good."*** *Lōʾ nûkal dabbēr ʾēleykā raʿ ʾô-ṭôb* — an idiom for
having no standing to answer either way. **The same family uses the same idiom about the same
matter a generation later**, when God warns Laban off Jacob: *"Be careful that you don't speak to
Jacob either good or bad"* (31:24, 29). Laban's first recorded position and his last are both that
he has nothing to say.

**Then he tries to keep her for a while, and it is the only friction in the chapter.** *"Let the
young lady stay with us a few days, at least ten"* (24:55) — the Hebrew, *yāmîm ʾô ʿāśôr*, *days or
ten*, is genuinely obscure and has been read as ten days, ten months, and a year. Whatever the
period, the servant refuses it: *"Don't hinder me, since Yahweh has prospered my way."*

> **57** They said, “We will call the young lady, and ask her.”
> **58** They called Rebekah, and said to her, “Will you go with this man?” She said, “I will go.”

**One word in Hebrew. *ʾĒlēk*. I will go.**

It is the only question put to a bride in Genesis, and it is asked to break a deadlock about a
travel date rather than to settle whether the marriage happens — the marriage was agreed at 24:51.
**That does not make the answer smaller.** She is the only matriarch who consents on the page, the
only one who travels to her marriage, and the only person in the patriarchal narratives besides
Abraham who leaves country, kindred, and father's house for a land she has not seen. **Abraham was
commanded to and was seventy-five. She is asked, and she is not promised anything.** See
[[Rebekah]] and [[Leave Your Country - The Two Lek-Lekha Commands]].

> **60** They blessed Rebekah, and said to her, “Our sister, may you be the mother of thousands of
> ten thousands, and let your offspring possess the gate of those who hate them.”

**This is the covenant promise, and the people saying it have never heard it.** Two chapters ago, on
a mountain, God swore to Abraham: *"Your offspring will possess the gate of his enemies"* (22:17) —
*wəyîraš zarʿăkā ʾēt šaʿar ʾōyəbāyw*. Here an Aramean household in Mesopotamia sends its daughter off
with *wəyîraš zarʿēk ʾēt šaʿar śōnəʾāyw*, **the same clause with the same verb, the same noun, and a
synonym for enemies**, spoken to a woman instead of a man. Nobody in the room was at Moriah. **The
oath sworn in secret to one man on a mountain comes back out of the mouths of strangers as a wedding
toast**, and the narrator does not mention the coincidence. See
[[Possess the Gate of Those Who Hate Them]] and [[Blessing]].

**And a nurse goes with her** (24:59), unnamed. Genesis will name her once, at her funeral: *"Deborah,
Rebekah's nurse, died, and she was buried below Bethel under the oak"* (35:8). A woman is attached to
Rebekah's household here and buried under a named tree eleven chapters later, and those are the only
two verses she has.

### 24:62–67 — the field at evening

> **63** Isaac went out to meditate in the field at the evening. He lifted up his eyes and looked.
> Behold, there were camels coming.
> **64** Rebekah lifted up her eyes, and when she saw Isaac, she got off the camel.

**Both of them lift up their eyes, in consecutive verses.** *Wayyiśśāʾ … ʿênāyw* is Genesis's formula
for the moment before something arrives — Abraham at Mamre (18:2), Abraham on the mountain (22:4,
13), Lot looking at the Jordan (13:10). Here it is used twice in a row, once for each of them, and it
is the only mutual one in the book.

***"To meditate"* is a guess.** *Lāśûaḥ* (24:63) occurs **nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible** in this
form, and every translation is a reconstruction: to meditate, to muse, to complain or lament, to
stroll, to relieve himself. The Septuagint has him going out *to exercise* — or, on another reading
of its verb, *to chatter*. **The most famous quiet moment in the patriarchal narratives rests on a
word nobody can define**, and the wiki says so rather than picking one.

**He is coming from Beer Lahai Roi** (24:62) — [[Hagar]]'s well, the place named by the slave who was
sent away, where 25:11 says he settles for good. His mother is dead, his father is at Beersheba or
Hebron, and **the son of the promise is living at the water hole named by the Egyptian**. Genesis
reports it in a subordinate clause. See [[Beer Lahai Roi]].

**She veils herself, and Genesis says why she does it and not what it means.** *"Who is the man who
is walking in the field to meet us?"* — she asks, is told, and covers. Readers have taken the veil as
bridal custom and as modesty before a stranger; **the chapter that has just gone to enormous lengths
to make her visible ends by covering her face**, and five chapters later a veil in this same family
will let one sister be married in another's place (29:23–25).

> **67** Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah’s tent, and took Rebekah, and she became his wife.
> He loved her. Isaac was comforted after his mother’s death.

**Four clauses, and the third one is a first.** *Wayyeʾĕhābehā* — **he loved her** — is **the first
time in the Bible a husband is said to love his wife**, and only the second occurrence of *ʾāhab* in
Scripture. The first was 22:2: *"your son, your only son, whom you love, even Isaac."* **Love enters
the Bible as the thing that makes a sacrifice cost something, and its second appearance is this.**

***"Isaac was comforted after his mother's death."*** *Wayyinnāḥēm* — the passive of the verb behind
[[Comfort from the Cursed Ground - Naming Noah]], and the same root Genesis used of God at 6:6.
**The chapter's last word is about a grief the book never showed him having.** He was not named at
her funeral, said nothing there, and is not recorded as mourning; the only evidence Genesis gives
that he grieved his mother at all is the sentence that says he stopped.

**And the tent is hers.** He brings Rebekah into *"his mother Sarah's tent"* — the tent Sarah laughed
in at 18:9–15, still standing, still called by her name, three years after her death on the book's own
arithmetic. **Genesis begins this chapter with an old man arranging the future and ends it with a
young woman walking into a dead woman's tent**, and the succession the whole chapter was arranged to
produce is reported in eleven words.

### What the chapter is doing

**Everything in it is attributed to God and nothing in it is done by God on the page.** The servant
prays and thanks; Laban and Bethuel concede the case to Yahweh; the servant refuses a delay because
*"Yahweh has prospered my way"*; Abraham predicts an angel. **The narrator confirms none of it.**
What the narrator reports is a man's plan, a servant's design, a woman's initiative at a well, a
brother's eye for gold, and a coincidence of timing — *before he had finished speaking* — that is
either the whole answer or nothing at all.

That is a different literary method from anything earlier in Genesis, and it is the method the book
uses for the rest of its length: **the Joseph narrative, twenty-plus chapters with almost no divine
speech, is written in exactly this register**, and its thesis sentence (*"you meant evil against me,
but God meant it for good"*, 50:20) is a character's interpretation of events the narrator declined to
interpret. **Genesis 24 is where the book learns to do that.**

## People

- [[Abraham]] — *"old, and well stricken in age"*, and blessed *"in all things"*; he administers an
  oath, states the promise in the fullest words he ever gives it, forbids his son's return to
  Mesopotamia twice, and then leaves the book. **This is his last scene and these are his last
  recorded words.**
- [[Abraham's Servant]] — the *elder of his house, who ruled over all that he had*: unnamed across
  sixty-seven verses, given more dialogue than any minor figure in Genesis, and **the first person in
  the Bible to pray a petition for guidance and be answered**. He designs the sign, watches in
  silence, bows twice, and argues the case with six careful edits.
- [[Rebekah]] — arrives before the prayer ends, passes a sign she cannot know is being applied,
  exceeds it by watering ten camels unasked, and answers the only question anybody puts to her with
  one word: *"I will go."* **The only matriarch who consents to her own marriage on the page.**
- [[Isaac]] — absent for sixty-one verses of the chapter about his marriage; comes in from
  [[Beer Lahai Roi]], goes out into a field at evening to do something the Hebrew cannot name, and is
  the first husband in Scripture said to love his wife.
- [[Laban]] — Rebekah's brother, introduced by the gold he saw; an exemplary host who runs the
  negotiation his father should be running, concedes the case to Yahweh, and then tries to keep his
  sister ten more days.
- [[Bethuel]] — alive, present, and named second; he speaks once, jointly with his son, and is absent
  from the verse that distributes the gifts.
- [[Sarah]] — dead, and named twice: as the mother who bore the heir *"when she was old"* (24:36) and
  as the owner of the tent Rebekah is brought into (24:67). Her tent outlives her by three years and
  keeps her name.
- [[Milcah]] — named in the narrator's introduction of Rebekah (24:15) and in Rebekah's own answer
  (24:24); the grandmother whose line supplies the matriarch.
- [[Nahor (brother of Abram)]] — the brother who stayed; his city is the destination, and 24:15
  identifies Rebekah by him rather than by her father.
- [[God]] — named nineteen times, sworn by, prayed to, thanked, credited, and blessed, and **silent
  from the first verse to the last**. The longest chapter in Genesis contains no divine speech, no
  appearance, and no reported act.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — promised twice by human speakers (24:7, 40), and never seen. The first
  *angel sent before you* in Scripture, in a prediction the narrator never confirms.
- [[Eliezer of Damascus]] — not in the chapter. The tradition that he is the servant is old,
  reasonable, and unsupported; the narrator withholds a name for sixty-seven verses.

## Places

- [[Aram Naharaim]] — *Mesopotamia*, and *the city of Nahor*: where the family that stayed behind
  lives, and where two of the three patriarchal marriages are sourced.
- [[Haran (city)]] — the crossroads the household settled at, and the standing candidate for *the
  city of Nahor*; Jacob will come back to the same house for both his wives.
- [[Canaan]] — named in the prohibition rather than as a setting: *"the daughters of the Canaanites,
  among whom I live"*, and the country Isaac may not leave under any circumstances.
- [[Beer Lahai Roi]] — Hagar's well, and where Isaac is coming from when the caravan arrives; 25:11
  will make it his address.
- [[The Negev]] — *the land of the South*, where Isaac is living while his wife is being fetched from
  four hundred miles away.
- [[Hebron]] and [[Beersheba]] — Abraham's two addresses in the preceding chapters, and neither is
  named here; the chapter never says where the oath is sworn or where the servant returns to.

## Themes

- [[Prayer]] — the theme's founding text: the first unbidden human petition in Scripture, offered by
  a slave, answered mid-sentence, and followed by the first thanksgiving that names what was asked
  for.
- [[Marriage]] — a marriage arranged by oath, negotiated by proxy, agreed by a brother, consented to
  by the bride, and ending in the Bible's first statement that a husband loved his wife.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the chapter opens on an oath with a gesture, a sanction, a release clause, and
  a clause with no release; *see* the second-person conditions at 24:8 and 24:41 and how they differ.
- [[Covenant]] — *ḥesed we-ʾemet*, four uses of *ḥesed*, and Abraham reciting the covenant's own
  history as the ground of a practical decision.
- [[Favor and Grace]] — the chapter's operative word is *ḥesed* rather than *ḥēn*: loyalty owed
  inside a relationship, asked for by a man with no standing to ask.
- [[Blessing]] — *"Yahweh had blessed Abraham in all things"* (24:1) is the chapter's first sentence
  and its premise, and an Aramean household unknowingly pronounces the Moriah oath over Rebekah at
  24:60.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the one non-negotiable term of the oath is that Isaac never go back. The
  bride may refuse; the land may not be left.
- [[The Nations]] — the covenant line marries out of Canaan and into Aram, twice; the Arameans behave
  impeccably and are the source of Israel's mothers.
- [[Hospitality]] — Laban's welcome is textbook and follows
  [[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]] closely; the servant breaks the pattern by
  refusing the meal until he has spoken.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — the chapter's protagonist is property. He runs the household, carries
  the estate's wealth on ten camels, swears a binding oath, prays the Bible's first prayer for
  guidance, and is never given a name.
- [[Faith]] — Abraham's statement at 24:7, which is faith as a man argues it rather than as a
  narrator credits it; and the servant's willingness to be told no (*"that I may turn to the right
  hand, or to the left"*).
- [[Walking with God]] — *"Yahweh, before whom I walk, will send his angel with you"* (24:40), the
  servant quoting Abraham's self-description as the warrant for the whole errand.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the servant *looked steadfastly*; Laban *saw the ring*; both Isaac and
  Rebekah *lifted up their eyes*; and she ends the chapter behind a veil.

## Notes

- **On the pericope's boundaries.** The plan's box read *Genesis 24:1–67*, the chapter is a single
  unbroken narrative with one cast and one errand, and **no unchecked boxes were redrawn**. The
  chapter is long enough to have been split — the oath (1–9), the well (10–27), the house (28–61),
  and the homecoming (62–67) are all self-contained — and it was kept whole because the retelling at
  24:34–49 only works as an effect if the first telling is on the same page. The next box,
  *The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael — Genesis 25:1–18*, stands as drawn.
- **On God's silence.** Stated above and worth repeating as a checkable fact rather than a reading:
  Yahweh is named nineteen times in Genesis 24 and speaks no words. Every theological claim in the
  chapter is made by a character. The narrator reports the timing of Rebekah's arrival (24:15) and
  nothing else that could be called an act of God. **The wiki records this as a feature of the
  narration, not as a claim about whether God acted.**
- **On the servant's identity.** Jewish tradition identifies him with [[Eliezer of Damascus]] (15:2)
  as a matter of course, and the identification fits the description at 24:2 and the arithmetic.
  Genesis withholds the name across sixty-seven verses in which it had every opportunity, which reads
  as a choice. **Recorded as tradition and not adopted**, consistent with the existing
  [[Eliezer of Damascus]] page. He is given a page here as **[[Abraham's Servant]]**, under the
  `AGENTS.md` rule that an unnamed figure known by a title is filed under the title and the patriarch
  he appears with — the same treatment as [[Lot's Wife]] and [[Job's Children]]. **If a later pass
  ever concludes the two are the same man, the fix is a redirect note on both pages, not a merge.**
- **On the camels.** Ten of them, drinking, is the mechanism of the whole sign. The domesticated
  camel's arrival in the southern Levant is dated by current archaeology — copper-smelting sites in
  the Aravah, and the absence of camel remains in earlier strata — to around the tenth century BC,
  well after any date proposed for Abraham, and the camels of Genesis 12:16, 24, 30:43, and 32:7 are
  the standard example in that argument. The counter-cases are that the evidence is negative and
  regional, that domesticated camels are attested earlier in Arabia and Mesopotamia, and that
  small-scale caravan use would leave little trace. **The wiki records the observation and adopts
  neither conclusion**, on the same footing as the Hittite-law proposal at
  [[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]].
- **On *lāśûaḥ* (24:63).** The form is unique in the Hebrew Bible and its meaning is unrecoverable.
  Proposals: *śîaḥ*, to meditate or muse (the traditional rendering, and the one the WEB takes);
  *śîaḥ*, to complain or lament, which would make it grief for Sarah; *šûaḥ*, to stroll or roam; and
  a euphemism for relieving himself, which the medieval commentators discuss and most moderns
  dismiss. The Septuagint's *adoleschēsai* can mean either exercising or chattering. **The wiki names
  the crux and does not choose.**
- **On whether the sign was proper.** It is formally identical to Gideon's fleece and Jonathan's sign,
  both of which Scripture reports without grading, and it is materially different from both in that
  the outcome depends on a person's character rather than on a random event. Readers have called it
  faith and called it presumption for two thousand years. **Genesis grades it not at all**, and
  neither does this wiki; what the chapter does report is that the servant refused to conclude
  anything until the watering was finished (24:21).
- **On the six edits in the retelling.** Set out in the table above. **They are advocacy, not
  falsehood**, and Genesis reports them without comment, in the same manner as the price at
  Machpelah. The load-bearing one is the omission of *"don't bring my son there again"*: the family
  agrees to send Rebekah without ever being told, on the page, that the arrangement is permanent and
  one-way. Whether they inferred it is not stated.
- **A contradiction flagged, not resolved: who is in charge of Bethuel's house.** 24:50 has *"Laban
  and Bethuel"* answering, in that order, with the son first; 24:53 gives the gifts to *"her brother
  and her mother"* with no father; 24:55 has *"her brother and her mother"* asking for the delay.
  Either Bethuel is present and marginal, or the name in 24:50 is intrusive. **Both readings are
  live and the wiki adopts neither**; the [[Bethuel]] page already records the question and this
  pericope does not settle it.
- **What was left for later pericopes.** Rebekah's nurse is not given a page: she is unnamed here and
  named only at her burial (35:8), and a page for her belongs to that pericope, where the name and
  the oak are. **If one is made it must be `Deborah (Rebekah's nurse)`**, because Deborah the
  prophetess and judge (Judges 4–5) will need the bare name or its own qualifier, and under
  `AGENTS.md` a silent basename collision is exactly what the qualifier rules exist to prevent. This
  note exists so the next agent does not create a bare `Deborah.md`.
- **Paddan Aram is not used here and is not a second page.** Genesis 24:10 says *Aram Naharaim*
  (WEB: *Mesopotamia*); 25:20, 28:2, and the rest of the Jacob cycle say *Paddan Aram*. They are the
  same region under two designations and are handled on one page, [[Aram Naharaim]], with the second
  name in its `also_known_as`. **A separate `Paddan Aram.md` should not be created later.**
