# Laws of Holiness and Compassion

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 22:16–31

## Summary

The ordinances turn from things to persons. The first case still has the shape of the property
laws: a man who entices an unbetrothed virgin and lies with her must pay the bride-price and take
her as his wife, and if her father refuses, he pays the bride-price of virgins anyway (22:16–17).
Then the casuistic *"If a man …"* gives way to short absolute sentences. Three offences carry death
or destruction: the sorceress is not to be allowed to live, whoever lies with an animal is to be put
to death, and whoever sacrifices to any god but Yahweh *"shall be utterly destroyed"* (22:18–20).

**At 22:21 God starts speaking in the first person about the people with no one to protect
them.** Israel must not wrong or oppress the alien, *"for you were aliens in the land of Egypt"*,
and must not take advantage of a widow or a fatherless child. If they cry to him, *"I will surely
hear their cry"*, and his wrath will make the oppressors' wives widows and their children fatherless
(22:21–24). A loan to a poor member of *"my people"* is to carry no interest; a cloak taken as a
pledge goes back before sunset, because it is all he has to sleep in, and *"when he cries to me … I
will hear, for I am gracious"* (22:25–27).

The unit closes on what is owed to God and to authority. Israel is not to blaspheme God or curse a
ruler of the people (22:28). They are not to delay the offering of the harvest and the presses;
the firstborn sons are given to God, and the firstborn of cattle and sheep likewise, after seven
days with the mother, *"on the eighth day"* (22:29–30). The last sentence names what all of it is
for: *"You shall be holy men to me"*, and so they must not eat flesh torn by animals in the field
but *"cast it to the dogs"* (22:31).

## Notes

### The boundary, and the verse numbering

The planned boundary is kept. **The seduction case of 22:16–17 could have gone with the property
laws**: it is a casuistic *"If a man …"* with a payment, and the payment goes to the father. It is
kept here because the plan put it here and because 22:15 closes the property cases on hire; it also
begins the unit's turn from things to persons. The unit ends at 22:31, where the holiness sentence
closes the run; 23:1 opens a new subject, false report and the courtroom.

As in the previous unit, **English and Hebrew number the chapter differently**: the WEB's 22:16–31
are Masoretic 22:15–30 (*Miqra according to the Masorah*, via Sefaria). This page uses the WEB's
numbering throughout.

### Seduction and the bride-price

*Yəpatteh*, *entices* (22:16; MAM 22:15), is a verb of persuasion, not force, and the remedy is
written with a doubled verb, *mōhar yimhārennâ*, literally *he shall surely pay the bride-price for
her*. The noun is *mōhar*, the word Shechem used when he told Jacob's sons, *"Ask me a great amount
for a dowry"* (Genesis 34:12, *mōhar ûmattān*). **Shechem offered exactly what this law would
require, and Dinah's brothers did not accept it.** The wiki has recorded that tension on
[[Dinah and the Massacre at Shechem]] without settling it, and this page does not settle it either;
Genesis 34 is before Sinai and its narrator does not apply the later law.

**The father keeps the right to refuse** (22:17), and the seducer pays *"according to the dowry of
virgins"* anyway. The law does not say what the woman herself wants, and it does not say how large
the sum is. Deuteronomy 22:28–29, a related case in which the man *"grabs her"*, fixes the sum at
fifty shekels of silver and adds that *"He may not put her away all his days"*. Its reason clause
uses the verb *ʿinnâ* — *"because he has humbled her"* (MAM) — the verb that Genesis 34:2 uses of
Shechem and that 22:22 will use for taking advantage of the widow. The relation between the two laws
is not argued here. The Septuagint (Rahlfs 1935) translates *mōhar* as *phernē*, a dowry, in both
22:16 and 22:17 (Greek 22:15–16).

### Three sentences of death

Verses 18–20 change form. They give no case and no remedy, only a sentence.

**The sorceress.** *Məkaššēpâ lōʾ təḥayyeh* (MAM 22:17): a feminine singular participle, and a
piel verb, *you shall not let live*. **Both words have already appeared in Exodus.** The root is
the one used for Pharaoh's *"sorcerers"*, *məkaššəpîm*, in 7:11, so a practice Egypt's court
employed is not to be tolerated in Israel's camp. And the verb is the one used of the midwives, who
*"saved the baby boys alive"*, *wattəḥayyênā* (1:17–18, MAM): they let live the boys the king had
condemned. **The text does not draw either connection**; both are recorded as shared Hebrew
vocabulary. Why the law names a woman is not explained. The Septuagint has the masculine plural
*pharmakous ou peripoiēsete*, *you shall not keep sorcerers alive* (Rahlfs 1935, Greek 22:17), and
the KJV has *"a witch"*. Later law widens the prohibition to men and women alike (Leviticus 20:27;
Deuteronomy 18:10–11).

**The animal.** *"Whoever has sex with an animal shall surely be put to death"* uses the
*môt yûmāt* formula of the capital cases in 21:12–17. Leviticus 18:23 restates it as a defilement.

**The other god.** *Zōbēaḥ lāʾĕlōhîm yoḥŏrām biltî layhwh ləbaddô* (MAM 22:19): sacrificing *to
gods*, plural in form, *except to Yahweh alone*, is punished by *ḥērem*, being *"utterly
destroyed"*. **The first commandment (20:3) is here given a penalty.** Whether this is the first
occurrence of the root *ḥrm* in Scripture was not checked.

### The alien, the widow, and the fatherless — Egypt's verbs

**The motive clause, *"for you were aliens in the land of Egypt"* (22:21), is backed by vocabulary:
the verbs Israel is forbidden to use on the weak are the verbs Egypt used on Israel.**

| 22:21–22 (MAM 22:20–21) | where Exodus and Genesis used it before |
|---|---|
| *gēr*, alien — *gērîm hĕyîtem*, *you were aliens* | *kî-gēr yihyeh zarʿăkā*, *your offspring will live as foreigners* (Genesis 15:13) |
| *lōʾ tilḥāṣennû*, *you shall not oppress him* | *hallaḥaṣ ʾăšer miṣrayim lōḥăṣîm ʾōtām*, *the oppression with which the Egyptians oppress them* (Exodus 3:9) |
| *lōʾ təʿannûn*, *you shall not take advantage of / afflict* | *ləmaʿan ʿannōtô*, *to afflict them* (1:11); *wəʿinnû ʾōtām*, *they will afflict them* (Genesis 15:13) |

All five verses were checked in the Hebrew. **Israel is told not to become Egypt.** The verb *ʿinnâ*
also describes what Shechem did to Dinah (Genesis 34:2) and is the reason clause of Deuteronomy
22:29, so the same unit uses it twice with different victims.

**The number changes inside the sentence.** In the Hebrew, 22:22 is plural (*təʿannûn*), 22:23 is
singular — *ʾim-ʿannēh təʿanneh ʾōtô*, *if you afflict him at all*, and *ṣaʿăqātô*, *his cry* — and
22:24 returns to the plural: *I will kill you*, *your wives*, *your children*. The WEB smooths the
middle verse to *"them … their cry"*. **The law addresses each Israelite and then the whole people,
and one victim's cry is enough.**

Verse 23 is built on three doubled verbs, each an infinitive absolute with its finite verb:
*ʿannēh təʿanneh … ṣāʿōq yiṣʿaq … šāmōaʿ ʾešmaʿ*, *if you afflict at all, if he cries at all, I
will surely hear*. **The noun is *ṣəʿāqâ*, the cry of Sodom's victims and of Israel under Egypt**
(Genesis 18:20–21; Exodus 3:7, 9). The penalty in 22:24 is measured on the offence: those who took
advantage of widows and orphans leave widows and orphans behind. See
[[A Great Cry - The Outcry of the Oppressed and the Cry of Egypt]] and [[Retribution]].

### The loan and the cloak

*ʾEt-ʿammî ʾet-heʿānî ʿimmāk* (MAM 22:24) reads literally *my people, the poor one with you*: the
poor borrower is called God's people before he is called poor. The lender is not to act toward him
*kənōšeh*, *as a creditor*, and is not to put *nešek*, *interest*, on him. That *nešek* comes from a
root meaning *to bite* is a common derivation; it was not checked against a lexicon in this pass.
Later law restates the rule (Leviticus 25:35–37; Deuteronomy 23:19–20, which allows interest
from a foreigner), and the Psalms and prophets make it a mark of the righteous (Psalm 15:5; Ezekiel
18:8). See [[Lending Without Interest - Exodus 22.25 and the Poor Brother]].

**The pledge law gives its reason in the form of a question.** The cloak, *śalmâ* / *śimlâ*, must
come back *"before the sun goes down"*, because *"that is his only covering, it is his garment for
his skin. What would he sleep in?"* (22:26–27). *Śimlâ* is the garment Shem and Japheth laid over
their father (Genesis 9:23). **The lender is allowed to hold the pledge only in daylight**, when the
man does not need it. Deuteronomy 24:10–13 adds that the lender may not enter the house to take it
and calls its return *"righteousness to you before Yahweh"*. Amos 2:8 accuses those who *"lay
themselves down beside every altar on clothes taken in pledge"*. In Job,
[[Eliphaz the Temanite]] accuses Job of taking such pledges (Job 22:6), and Job himself describes
the poor who go naked (Job 24:7–10); see [[Eliphaz's Third Speech]] and [[Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him]].

**The section ends on God's self-description: *"I will hear, for I am gracious"*, *kî-ḥannûn
ʾānî*.** The adjective returns in 34:6, *ʾēl raḥûm wəḥannûn*, *merciful and gracious* (MAM), when
God proclaims his name to Moses. **In the Book of the Covenant it is first said as the reason a
creditor must give back a coat.**

### Neither God nor a ruler

*ʾĔlōhîm lōʾ təqallēl wənāśîʾ bəʿamməkā lōʾ tāʾōr* (MAM 22:27). **The two verbs are the two verbs
of Genesis 12:3**, *ûməqallelkā ʾāʾōr*, where God promised Abram, *"I will curse him who"* treats
him with contempt (MAM; the WEB renders *qillēl* as *treats … with contempt* there and *blaspheme*
here). *Qillēl* is to make light of; *ʾārar* is the formal curse. Whether the echo is intended
cannot be shown.

***ʾĔlōhîm* is again open to two readings**, as it was at 21:6 and 22:8–9. The WEB has *"God"*;
the KJV has *"Thou shalt not revile the gods"*; the Septuagint has *theous ou kakologēseis*, *you
shall not speak evil of gods*, plural (Rahlfs 1935, Greek 22:27). This page records the readings and
does not choose.

*Nāśîʾ*, *ruler*, is the title the Hittites gave Abraham, *"a prince of God"* (Genesis 23:6,
*nəśîʾ ʾĕlōhîm*), and that the narrator gave Shechem, *"the prince of the land"* (Genesis 34:2).
The Septuagint's second clause, *archontas tou laou sou ou kakōs ereis*, *you shall not speak evil
of the rulers of your people*, is the sentence Paul quotes in the singular at Acts 23:5, *Archonta
tou laou sou ouk ereis kakōs* (SBLGNT). See
[[You Shall Not Speak Evil of a Ruler - Exodus 22.28 in Acts 23.5]].

### The first of everything, and the eighth day

*Məlēʾātəkā wədimʿăkā lōʾ təʾaḥēr* (MAM 22:28) is very compressed: literally *your fullness and
your tear you shall not delay*. The WEB's *"from your harvest and from the outflow of your presses"*
interprets the two nouns as grain and the juice of grapes or olives; the Septuagint does the same,
*aparchas halōnos kai lēnou sou*, *firstfruits of your threshing floor and your winepress* (Rahlfs
1935). **Neither *fullness* nor *tear* is a usual word for a crop; the meanings given here come from
the translations, not from the Hebrew alone.**

***"You shall give the firstborn of your sons to me"* is stated without the redemption clause** of
13:13, 15 (*"you shall redeem all the firstborn of man among your sons"*), and 34:20 will restore it.
**This page records the difference and does not harmonize it**; the wiki's fullest treatment of it,
including the readings that take 22:29 as shorthand for the full law and those that do not, is
[[Child Sacrifice and the Redemption of the Firstborn]].

The animal firstborn stays *"seven days … with its mother, then on the eighth day you shall give it
to me"* (22:30). **The eighth day is the day of circumcision** (Genesis 17:12; 21:4), and Leviticus
uses the same interval twice more, for circumcision (12:3) and for any newborn animal offered
(22:27, *seven days … with its mother; and from the eighth day*). See
[[The Eighth Day - Circumcision and the Firstborn Given to God]].

### Holy men, and the dogs

The closing sentence, *wəʾanšê-qōdeš tihyûn lî*, *you shall be men of holiness to me* (MAM 22:30),
puts in the law the promise of 19:6, *"a holy nation"* (*gôy qādôš*), and makes it a matter of
diet. The meat refused is ***ṭərēpâ***, *torn* — **the noun of Genesis 31:39**, where Jacob said he
never brought Laban *"that which was torn of animals"*, and of the same root as the torn animal in
22:13. **One chapter treats the torn animal as evidence in a keeping dispute (22:13) and as food a
holy people may not eat (22:31).** See
[[The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22]].

The carcass goes *"to the dogs"*, *lakkeleb*. **The last dog in Exodus was the one that would not
move its tongue against Israel on the night of the firstborn** (11:7). Whether that is meant as an
echo cannot be shown. Later law treats torn meat as making the eater unclean until evening
(Leviticus 17:15), allows the carcass to be given or sold to a foreigner (Deuteronomy 14:21), and
forbids it to priests (Ezekiel 44:31). Those texts are recorded here, not harmonized with this one.
