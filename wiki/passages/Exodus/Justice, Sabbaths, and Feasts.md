# Justice, Sabbaths, and Feasts

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 23:1–19

## Summary

The covenant ordinances turn to the administration of justice: false reports, malicious witnesses,
pressure from a crowd, favoritism, false charges, and bribes (23:1–8). The poor must neither receive
a favorable verdict because they are poor nor be denied the justice due them. Between the courtroom
commands come two instructions about an enemy's animals: return the wandering ox or donkey and help
the donkey fallen under its load (23:4–5). Hostility does not cancel an obligation to help.

The prohibition of oppressing an alien is grounded in Israel's experience in Egypt (23:9). That
concern for people without power continues into the calendar: the seventh year's uncultivated
produce feeds the poor and then the animals, and the seventh day's rest refreshes working animals,
the servant's son, and the alien (23:10–12). Exclusive allegiance to God frames the transition to
three annual feasts (23:13–17): Unleavened Bread in Abib recalls the departure from Egypt, while
Harvest and Ingathering mark the field's produce.

The unit closes with rules for offerings: no sacrificial blood with leavened bread, no festival fat
left until morning, the first of the first fruits brought to God's house, and no boiling a young
goat in its mother's milk (23:18–19). The last prohibition supplies no explanation. These are
commands for Israel's life and worship, not a report that the people have already kept them.

## People

- [[God]] — the speaker, who refuses to justify the wicked (23:7) and claims the feasts and
  offerings.
- [[Moses]] — receives the continuing address by context (20:22; 21:1); not named in this unit.
- Witnesses, the poor litigant, the enemy, the alien, the servant's son, and the male worshippers
  are legal or social categories, not newly introduced named figures.

## Places

- [[Egypt]] — remembered both as the place of alien status (23:9) and as the land left in Abib
  (23:15).
- [[Horeb]] — Sinai remains the narrative setting; the mountain is not named in these verses.
- The fields, vineyard, olive grove, and house of Yahweh anticipate settled life. No town or
  sanctuary site is identified here.

## Notes

### The boundary

The planned boundary is retained. Verses 1–9 concern justice and assistance, 10–12 order work and
rest, and 13–19 join exclusive worship to the calendar and offerings. Verse 20 begins the promise of
the angel sent ahead, ingested as [[The Angel Sent Ahead]]. The present unit has several subjects, but
keeps the practical ordinances together before that change of address.

### Justice for the poor without a purchased verdict

Verses 3 and 6 must be read together. The prohibition of favoring a poor litigant does not permit
denying that person's rights; the protection of the poor does not authorize false testimony in their
favor. A crowd cannot make a wrong verdict right (23:2), and a bribe corrupts people who otherwise
see and speak rightly (23:8). The danger is the distortion of judgment, not simply the appointment
of obviously wicked judges.

God's refusal to justify the wicked (23:7) grounds the warning against killing the innocent through
false charges. This is a statement about judicial wrong, not an assertion that every person
acquitted in court is morally faultless in every respect. Nor does the verse by itself settle the
later theological question of forgiveness.

### An enemy remains someone to help

Verses 4–5 interrupt the courtroom instructions with a wandering animal and an animal collapsed
under a load. The owner's hostility is expressly named and does not remove the duty. The command
requires action, not merely the absence of revenge. It does not say that the enemy has apologized or
that helping has already produced reconciliation. The later comparison is developed in
[[Help for an Enemy - Exodus 23, Proverbs 25, and Matthew 5]].

### Rest reaches those who cannot grant it to themselves

The seventh-year rule does more than suspend cultivation: it identifies eaters, first the poor and
then the animals (23:11). The seventh day likewise has named beneficiaries, including the servant's
son and the alien (23:12). This strengthens the social purpose already visible in Exodus 20:10; it
does not replace the creation rationale of 20:11.

The protections do not abolish the unequal household status presupposed by Exodus 21:1–11. Shared
rest and continuing servitude remain together in the legislation. The six-year service term of 21:2
and the cultivation cycle of 23:10–11 have a similar pattern, but these verses do not identify their
calendars or institute a cancellation of all debts.

### The calendar, the sanctuary, and what remains unexplained

Exodus 23:15 links Unleavened Bread to Abib and the departure; 23:16 names Harvest and Ingathering
by their agricultural setting. Verse 17 explicitly requires male appearance. It does not say that
only males may attend. Deuteronomy 16:11, 14 later includes women, servants, and vulnerable
residents in festival rejoicing; the distinction between required appearance and participants should
be kept.

The house of Yahweh in 23:19 anticipates a place for offerings but identifies neither a city nor a
completed building. It cannot establish Jerusalem as the location of this Sinai scene. The different
locational language of Exodus 20:24 and Deuteronomy 16:5–6 is retained in
[[Three Times a Year - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 16]].

The goat-and-milk prohibition is precise but unexplained. Neither a fertility rite nor a general
prohibition of all meat with all dairy is stated here. This page does not supply an unverified
historical rationale. See
[[A Young Goat in Its Mother's Milk - Exodus 23, Exodus 34, and Deuteronomy 14]].

### The covenant renewed — Exodus 34:1–28

The parallel laws now belong to [[The Covenant Renewed]] (34:18–26). They specify rest during plowing and
harvest, protection of the land during pilgrimage, and an overnight rule naming the Passover
sacrifice rather than 23:18's festival fat. The recurring calendar does not erase these
differences.
