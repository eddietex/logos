# The Altar and the Hebrew Slave

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 20:22–21:11

## Summary

Moses has gone near to the thick darkness, and Yahweh gives him words for Israel. The speech begins
with what the people have seen: God has talked with them from heaven. They are therefore not to make
gods of silver or gold alongside him (20:22–23). In place of an image, he prescribes an altar:
earth, or, if stone, stones that no tool has cut. Burnt offerings and peace offerings are to be
sacrificed on it, and he promises to come and bless *"in every place where I record my name"*
(20:24). No steps are to lead up to it, so that the worshipper's nakedness is not exposed
(20:25–26).

Then a heading: *"Now these are the ordinances which you shall set before them"* (21:1). The first
ordinance concerns a Hebrew servant who has been bought. He serves six years and goes out free in
the seventh *"without paying anything"*. If he came in married, his wife goes out with him. If his
master gave him a wife, she and her children remain the master's (21:2–4). If the servant declares
that he loves his master, his wife, and his children and will not go free, his master brings him to
God, to the door or doorpost, bores his ear with an awl, and he serves for ever (21:5–6). A daughter
sold by her father as a female servant does not go out as the men do. Instead the law sets limits on
the master who takes her: if she does not please him, she must be allowed to be redeemed and may not
be sold to foreigners. If she is given to his son, she is treated as a daughter. If her master takes
another wife, he may not reduce her food, clothing, or marital rights, and if he fails in these
three, she goes free without payment (21:7–11).

## Notes

### Where the speech begins, and why the boundary falls at 21:11

The boundary drawn when the plan was made is kept. Exodus 20:22 begins a new speech (*"Yahweh said
to Moses"*) after the people asked for a mediator, and 21:12 turns from servitude to capital crimes
(*"One who strikes a man so that he dies"*). The two halves of the unit are connected by more than
their position. The altar instructions govern Israel's approach to God; the first ordinance governs
a master's hold over a fellow Hebrew. **The ordinances begin with a bought servant, before murder,
theft, or property**, so the people just reminded that they were brought *"out of the house of
bondage"* (20:2) are first told how long they may keep a brother in service.

Exodus 24:7 calls what Moses reads to the people *"the book of the covenant"*. This page uses that
name for the collection beginning here only as a convention. Exodus does not mark where the book
begins or ends, and 24:4 says only that Moses *"wrote all Yahweh's words"*.

### You have seen that I talked with you from heaven

The argument against images is drawn from what the people saw: they heard a voice from heaven
(20:22), and so are not to make gods of silver or gold *"to be alongside me"* (20:23). The WLC has
*ʾittî*, *with me*. **The prohibition is aimed at companions for Yahweh as much as at rivals to
him**, and it states the second command (20:4–5) again from the other side. Exodus 32 will test the
point: there the calf of gold is presented as the gods *"which brought you up out of the land of
Egypt"* (32:4, checked in raw).

### An altar that human craft cannot improve

**The altar is defined by what is not done to it.** It may be of earth (*ʾădāmâ*, WLC), the noun of
*"the ground from which he was taken"* (Genesis 3:23, WLC). If it is of stone, the stones may not be
dressed (*gāzît*, *hewn*, which occurs nowhere else in Genesis–Deuteronomy by WLC lemma search). The
reason is stark: *"if you lift up your tool on it, you have polluted it"* (20:25). The WEB's *tool*
translates *ḥarbəkā*, which the WLC tags with the lemma of *ḥereb*, *sword*. The verb is *ḥālal*,
*profane*, the verb Jeremiah 34:16 uses when Judah takes back its freed servants and *"profaned my
name"* (WLC). This page reports both words without claiming to know what the sword-word implies
about the reason for the rule. It is commonly read as excluding human artistry or violence from the
altar, but Exodus states only the result: a tool makes the altar profane.

**The tension with the tabernacle altar must be stated.** Exodus 27:1–8 prescribes an altar of
acacia wood overlaid with bronze, made *"with hollow planks"*, and Exodus 28:42–43 prescribes linen
breeches for the priests *"when they come near to the altar"*. The earth-or-stone altar of 20:24–26,
set up *"in every place"*, and the single crafted altar of the sanctuary are different provisions in
the same book. One common reading takes the second as the altar of the tent and the first as the
provision for altars elsewhere, such as Joshua's on Mount Ebal. Another treats them as legislation
from different settings. **Exodus itself does not reconcile them**, and this page does not choose.

The prohibition of steps is grounded in exposure: *"that your nakedness may not be exposed to it"*
(20:26). The noun is *ʿerwâ* (WLC), the word for Noah's nakedness that Ham saw and his brothers
covered (Genesis 9:22–23). In the WLC it occurs nowhere else in Exodus except the breeches of
28:42. The two provisions solve the same problem differently, by the absence of steps here and by
covering the body there.

### In every place where I record my name

The promise *"In every place where I record my name I will come to you and I will bless you"*
(20:24) does not yet limit worship to one sanctuary. *Every place* allows more than one, and the
patriarchs' altars at Shechem, near Bethel, at Beersheba, and elsewhere already stand behind it
(Genesis 12:6–8; 26:25). Deuteronomy 12:5 speaks instead of *"the place which Yahweh your God shall
choose out of all your tribes, to put his name there"*. **The two laws describe different
arrangements, and the wiki records the difference rather than ironing it out.** It does not settle
whether Deuteronomy restricts Exodus, applies it, or reflects another period.

*ʾAzkîr*, *I record* or *I cause to be remembered*, is a form of *zākar*, the verb of *"Remember the
Sabbath day"* (20:8) and of God's remembering his covenant (2:24; 6:5), all checked by WLC lemma.
Human beings are told to remember the day; here **God causes his own name to be remembered**, and
the initiative in choosing the place is his.

### Six years, then free for nothing

*"If you buy a Hebrew servant, he shall serve six years and in the seventh he shall go out free
without paying anything"* (21:2). The WEB's *servant* is the WLC's *ʿebed*, the word of the *house
of bondage* (*bêt ʿăbādîm*, 20:2). The verb *yaʿăbōd*, *he shall serve*, is the root around which
the book is built. *Hebrew* here names the subject of a law for the first time: what Pharaoh's court
called the slaves (Exodus 1–10) becomes the word for the people whose servitude is limited. **The
service is bounded by the number of the Sabbath.** Six years of work and release in the seventh
repeat the week of 20:9–10 at the scale of years. The verse does not say whether the seventh year
counts from the purchase or is a fixed national year. Deuteronomy 15:12 and Jeremiah 34:14 likewise
speak of the servant's own six years of service.

*Ḥinnām*, *without paying anything*, occurs in the WLC Torah only here, in 21:11, in Numbers 11:5,
and in Laban's *"should you therefore serve me for nothing?"* (Genesis 29:15). **The word Laban used
of a worker he did not want to pay is used here of a servant who owes nothing for his release.**

### The wife, the children, and the man who stays

The provisions in 21:3–4 are where the text is hardest to read. A man who came in married leaves
with his wife, but if *"his master gives him a wife"*, she and the children remain the master's and
*"he shall go out by himself"*. **The release law can divide a family**, and Exodus states it
without softening. The comparison with Genesis is close and uncomfortable: Laban's *"The daughters
are my daughters, the children are my children"* (Genesis 31:43) is close to this law's claim.
Genesis does not grant it to him, since Leah and Rachel leave with Jacob.

Then a servant may refuse his freedom. His reason is love (*ʾāhabtî*, WLC), named in three
directions: *"my master, my wife, and my children"* (21:5). The text records it as his own plain
declaration and does not question the motive. **The love may be real, and the alternative was
leaving his wife and children.** The law offers him both and leaves the pressure in place.

His master brings him *"to God"* (21:6). The WLC has *hāʾĕlōhîm*, with the article, and the KJV
renders it *"unto the judges"*, checked at bible-api.com (`translation=kjv`). **The WEB takes it as
God; the KJV takes it as human judges.** The phrase permits both readings, and this page does not
decide between them. The ear is bored at *"the door or … the doorpost"*. *Məzûzâ* is the word of the
Passover doorposts that bore the blood (12:7, WLC), so the household that kept a servant is marked
at the same place as the house God passed over. The awl (*marṣēaʿ*) occurs in the Torah only here
and in Deuteronomy 15:17 (WLC lemma search). *Ləʿōlām*, *for ever*, is in **tension with Leviticus
25**, where an Israelite who sells himself serves *"until the Year of Jubilee"* and then goes out
with his children (25:40–41). The two texts are stated together on
[[The Hebrew Servant Goes Free - Exodus 21, Deuteronomy 15, and Jeremiah 34]].

### The daughter who does not go out as the men do

*"If a man sells his daughter to be a female servant, she shall not go out as the male servants do"*
(21:7). In context, the sale is into a household where she is expected to become a wife or
concubine, and the verses that follow protect her within that arrangement rather than ending it
after six years. **Two readings of 21:7 remain possible.** It may deprive her of the male servant's
release, or it may exempt her from release into a position worse than the one guaranteed to her.
Deuteronomy 15:12, 17 explicitly gives the Hebrew woman the same seventh-year release and the same
ear-boring as the man. Exodus 21:7 and Deuteronomy 15 therefore differ, and this page records the
**tension** rather than resolving it.

Three verbs constrain the master. If she displeases him, *"he shall let her be redeemed"*. The WLC
verb is *pādâ*, the verb of the firstborn's redemption (13:13, 15). He has no power to sell her to
*"a foreign people, since he has dealt deceitfully with her"* (21:8). *Bəbigdô*, from *bāgad*, *deal
treacherously*, occurs in the WLC Torah only here. **The law names the broken arrangement as the
master's faithlessness, not the woman's failure.**

The verse has a known textual variant. The WLC's written text (*ketiv*) has *lōʾ*, *not*, before the
verb, while the marginal reading (*qere*) is *lô*, *for himself*. The WEB's *"who has married her to
himself"* follows the qere. The ketiv would make the clause say that he has *not* taken her in the
way the WEB's *married* describes, and the sense of the clause changes with the reading. This page
makes no further claim about the verb's precise sense.

If she is given to his son, she is treated *"as a daughter"* (21:9). If her master takes another
wife, he may not reduce her *"food, her clothing, and her marital rights"* (21:10). The last noun,
*ʿōnâ*, occurs nowhere else in Genesis–Deuteronomy by WLC lemma search, so its meaning cannot be
settled from other uses in the Torah. The WEB's *marital rights* is one rendering among others, and
this page claims no more for it. **If the three are withheld, she goes out *ḥinnām*, *"without
paying any money"*** (21:11), the same release the man received at the beginning of the unit.

### What the law does and does not do

The ordinances do not abolish servitude. They assume it can be bought, inherited through a
master-given wife, made permanent, and entered by a father selling his daughter. What they do is
**limit a master's hold over a Hebrew** in time, in what he may do with a woman he has taken, and in
what he must provide her. They are spoken to a people just told they were brought out of the house
of bondage (20:2). The wiki states both facts, as [[Slavery and Servitude]] does throughout, and
does not let either cancel the other.
