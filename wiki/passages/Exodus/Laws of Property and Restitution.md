# Laws of Property and Restitution

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 22:1–15

## Summary

The ordinances move from bodily injury to loss of property. A thief who has slaughtered or sold
a stolen ox must repay five oxen, and four sheep for a sheep (22:1). A thief struck dead while
breaking in brings no guilt of bloodshed on the one who struck him, but once *"the sun has risen
on him"* the guilt returns; the thief must make restitution, and if he has nothing he is sold for
his theft (22:2–3). A stolen animal found alive in his hand is repaid double (22:4).

Two cases follow of damage without theft: livestock let loose to graze another man's field, and a
fire that spreads through thorns into grain. In both the one responsible makes restitution
(22:5–6). Then come things held for someone else. Money or goods deposited with a neighbor and
stolen are repaid double by the thief if he is found; if not, the householder comes *"near to God"*
so it can be determined whether he took them himself, and in every disputed claim the one God
condemns pays double (22:7–9). An animal in a neighbor's keeping that dies, is injured, or is driven
away with no one seeing is settled by *"the oath of Yahweh"*, with no restitution; one stolen from
the keeper is repaid; one torn by a wild animal is brought as evidence and not repaid (22:10–13).
A borrowed animal injured or killed in the owner's absence must be repaid; if the owner was with
it, it need not; and a hired thing *"came for its lease"* (22:14–15).

## Notes

### The boundary, and a verse-numbering difference

The planned boundary is kept. Verses 1–15 are about property and what is owed for it; at 22:16 the
cases turn to an unbetrothed virgin, then sorcery, idolatry, and the vulnerable, which belong
together in the next unit. Verse 15 closes with hire, the last kind of holding another person's
property.

**English and Hebrew number this chapter differently.** In the Masoretic text the WEB's 22:1 is
21:37, so the WEB's 22:2–15 are Hebrew 22:1–14 (checked against the *Miqra according to the
Masorah* edition via Sefaria). This page uses the WEB's English numbering throughout.

### Multiplied repayment, after a matching scale

One chapter earlier the injury cases set life against life and eye against eye (21:23–25).
**Theft is answered differently: not with matching harm but with more than was taken.** A stolen
animal still alive is repaid double (22:4); one already slaughtered or sold costs five oxen for an
ox and four sheep for a sheep (22:1). The text gives no reason for the higher rate when the animal
has been disposed of, or for the difference between ox and sheep. Readings that it reflects an ox's
working value, or that a sale or slaughter compounds the theft, are interpretations and not stated
in the verses. The thief who cannot pay is not let off but *"sold for his theft"* (22:3). That ties
this unit back to the servant law of 21:2, which has already regulated the sale of a Hebrew into
service. The text does not say here whether the six-year limit applies to such a sale.

### The thief at night and in daylight

Verses 2–3 are the passage's most compressed sentence. The WEB reads *"there shall be no guilt of
bloodshed for him"* when a thief breaking in is struck dead, and *"If the sun has risen on him,
guilt of bloodshed shall be for him"*. The Masoretic text has the plural noun *dāmîm*, *bloods*, in
both clauses: *ʾên lô dāmîm*, and then *dāmîm lô*. The KJV renders it *"there shall no blood be
shed for him"*.

**The usual reading is a day-and-night distinction**: in the dark the householder cannot tell
what the intruder intends and is not guilty for killing him, but in daylight killing a thief who
can be seen, identified, and made to pay is a homicide. The text names only sunrise; the reasons
are interpretation. The phrase *"on him"* also leaves it unclear whether the sunrise marks the
time of the break-in or the time of the killing. **The next clause, *"he shall make restitution"*,
also changes subject without saying so.** In context it is the thief who repays, since the
following sentence says he can be sold; the WEB keeps the Hebrew's unmarked pronoun. This
continues the concern of 21:12–14 with intent: the law lets a person defend their home without
licensing the killing of a thief once there is another remedy.

The Hebrew noun for *breaking in* is *maḥteret* (MAM, 22:1 Hebrew). It is often connected with a
verb meaning *dig* or *dig through*, picturing a thief digging through a mud-brick wall; that
derivation is recorded here as a common interpretation, not verified against a lexicon in this pass.

### Damage without theft

Grazing and fire are cases of **loss without dishonesty.** The grazing case requires restitution
*"from the best of his own field, and from the best of his own vineyard"* (22:5): payment is
measured by the quality of what is given, not just its quantity. In the fire case liability falls
on *"he who kindled the fire"* (22:6) even though the thorns, not he, carried it into the grain.
Neither case requires intent, which matches the pit and the bull in 21:33–36. **What is owed for
loss does not depend on malice.**

### Deposit, oath, and the God who condemns

The deposit cases rely on an oath because they have no witness: *"no man seeing it"* (22:10).
Where the thief is caught he pays double (22:7). Where no thief is found, suspicion falls on the
keeper, who *"shall come near to God, to find out if he hasn't put his hand to his neighbor's
goods"* (22:8). The
WEB's *goods* is *məlʾeket rēʿēhû* (MAM, 22:7 Hebrew), the noun for the *"work"* God finished on
the seventh day (Genesis 2:2) and the *"work"* the Sabbath commandment sets aside (20:9–10), and it
recurs in the keeper's oath at 22:11. **One noun covers both a person's work and the goods it
produces**; the text does not comment on the choice. In verse 9 *every* disputed claim — ox, donkey, sheep, clothing, *"any kind of lost
thing"* — goes *"before God"*, and *"He whom God condemns shall pay double"*.

**Translations divide here.** The WEB has *"God"* in 22:8–9; the KJV has *"the judges"* in all
three places, as it did at 21:6. The Hebrew in each place is *hāʾĕlōhîm* or *ʾĕlōhîm*, and in
22:9 the verb *condemns* is plural, *yaršîʿun* (MAM, 22:8 Hebrew). A plural verb with *ʾĕlōhîm* is
one reason some readers see human judges here. The plural ending is verified in the edition named;
**whether it settles the referent is not**, because *ʾĕlōhîm* is grammatically plural in form even
where it names Israel's God, and this page does not choose between sanctuary oath, oracle, and
court. The punishment for a false claim is the thief's double payment: **perjury costs what the
theft would have.**

The animal-keeping case names the procedure as *"the oath of Yahweh"* (22:11, *šəbuʿat YHWH*,
MAM): the keeper swears he did not take what was entrusted to him, and the owner *"shall accept
it"*. The law accepts a sworn statement as final because the matter has no witness but God.

### What the keeper owes

Verses 10–13 make a keeper liable for what he could have prevented and exempt him from what he
could not. Death, injury, or a raid seen by no one: an oath, and no payment. Theft *"from him"*:
payment, apparently because a watched animal should not have been stolen. A kill by a wild animal:
***"let him bring it for evidence. He shall not make good that which was torn"*** (22:13). The noun
the WEB renders *evidence* is *ʿēd* (MAM), the word used in *ʿēd šāqer*, the commandment's *"false
testimony"* (20:16; Hebrew 20:13), and in the *witness* of the heap Laban and Jacob raised
(Genesis 31:44). The torn carcass is the keeper's witness. The intensive *ṭārōp yiṭṭārēp*, *torn,
it is torn*, uses the same root in the same doubled construction as Jacob's *ṭārōp ṭōrap Yôsēp* over
Joseph's coat (Genesis 37:33, MAM).

Genesis has already shown both halves. Jacob told Laban he never brought the torn animal and bore
the loss, *"whether stolen by day or stolen by night"* (Genesis 31:39) — the exemption this law
grants, which he says he never used. And Joseph's brothers brought a torn and bloodied coat to their
father as evidence of a wild animal's kill that never happened (Genesis 37:31–33). **The law trusts
the brought evidence; Genesis has shown that evidence can be faked.** Neither text refers to the
other. See [[The Shepherd's Liability - Genesis 31.39 and the Law of Exodus 22]] and
[[A Garment Used as Evidence - The Coat and the Cloak]].

The loan cases turn on whether the owner was present: a borrower pays for loss in the owner's
absence, but not when the owner was with the animal (22:14–15). The last clause, *"If it is a
leased thing, it came for its lease"*, is terse in the WEB and the Hebrew alike (*ʾim-śākîr hûʾ bāʾ
biśkārô*). It is commonly read to mean the hire price covers the risk, or that a hired worker or
animal is the owner's own responsibility. This page leaves both readings open.
