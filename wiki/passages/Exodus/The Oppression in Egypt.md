# The Oppression in Egypt

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 1:1–22

## Summary

**Exodus opens with a sentence from Genesis and a blessing fulfilled, and then spends sixteen
verses on a king trying to undo it.**

The chapter has two halves and a hinge. **1:1–7 is the end of Genesis, written again**: the sons of
Israel who came into Egypt, named by their mothers' order; the seventy souls; Joseph and his whole
generation dead; and the people *"fruitful"* until *"the land was filled with them"*. **1:8 is the
hinge**: *"Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who didn't know Joseph."* **1:9–22 is three
measures, each harsher than the last and each failing**: forced labour on the store-cities of
[[Pithom]] and [[Rameses]] (1:9–14), a secret order to two midwives to kill the boys at birth
(1:15–21), and a public decree to the whole nation to throw every newborn son into the river (1:22).
The chapter ends with the third measure in force and its failure not yet told. That is chapter 2.

**The king fights a blessing, and the narrator reports each round in the blessing's own words.**
1:7 stacks the verbs of Genesis 1:28 and 9:1–7 — *fruitful, increased abundantly, multiplied, grew
exceedingly mighty, filled*. Pharaoh's stated aim is *"lest they multiply"* (1:10). The first
measure's result is *"the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied"* (1:12); the second's
is *"the people multiplied, and grew very mighty"* (1:20). The reader is never told that God is
behind the increase — **God is not the subject of a single verb in the chapter until 1:20** — but
the vocabulary has already said it. See
[[They Got Possessions and Multiplied - Genesis 47.27 and Exodus 1.7]].

**The powerful are anonymous and the powerless are named.** The king of Egypt has no name in this
chapter or anywhere in the book. The two midwives do: [[Shiphrah and Puah]] (1:15). They are the
first people Exodus names after the twelve sons, the only people in the chapter who make a choice,
and the only ones God is said to do anything for. See [[Names and Naming]].

### The shape of it

| | verses | the king's measure | the result |
|---|---|---|---|
| prologue | 1:1–7 | — | *"the land was filled with them"* |
| hinge | 1:8 | *"a new king … who didn't know Joseph"* | |
| 1 | 1:9–14 | taskmasters, store-cities, brick and mortar | *"the more they multiplied"* |
| 2 | 1:15–21 | the midwives told to kill the sons at birth | *"the people multiplied, and grew very mighty"* |
| 3 | 1:22 | *"all his people"* told to cast every son into the river | *(chapter 2)* |

Each measure widens the circle of the guilty — first taskmasters, then two women in private, then
**the whole Egyptian nation** — and moves from exploiting the Hebrews to killing them.

## Key verses

### These are the names (1:1–7)

> **1** Now these are the names of the sons of Israel, who came into Egypt (every man and his
> household came with Jacob):
> **2** Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah,
> **3** Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin,
> **4** Dan and Naphtali, Gad and Asher.
> **5** All the souls who came out of Jacob's body were seventy souls, and Joseph was in Egypt
> already.
> **6** Joseph died, as did all his brothers, and all that generation.
> **7** The children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and grew
> exceedingly mighty; and the land was filled with them.

***Wəʾēlleh šəmôt***, ***"and these are the names"***, **is the book's Hebrew title (*Shemot*) and
the sentence Genesis 46:8 began with**: *"These are the names of the children of Israel, who came
into Egypt, Jacob and his sons"* — see [[Jacob's Family Settles in Egypt]]. The opening *and*
makes Exodus a continuation, not a new beginning. Genesis 46 needed seventy names to reach seventy;
**Exodus gives the eleven who came, says Joseph was already there, and gives the total.** See
[[Seventy Souls - The Count of Jacob's House in Genesis, Exodus and Acts]].

**The order is by mother**, and it is Genesis 35:23–26's order, not Genesis 46's: Leah's six
([[Reuben]], [[Simeon]], [[Levi]], [[Judah]], [[Issachar]], [[Zebulun]]); Rachel's one, since
[[Joseph]] is not among those who *came* ([[Benjamin]]); then Bilhah's ([[Dan (person)]],
[[Naphtali]]) and Zilpah's ([[Gad]], [[Asher]]). Genesis 46 puts Zilpah's sons before Rachel's. See
[[The Twelve Sons and the Lists That Reorder Them]].

*"Every man and his household"* — ***ʾîš ûbêtô*** — **introduces the word the chapter will end on.**
The midwives are rewarded with *bāttîm*, households (1:21). Pharaoh's three measures aim to stop
households being made; God's one act in the chapter is to make two.

*"All the souls who came out of Jacob's body"* — literally *of Jacob's thigh*, ***yōṣəʾê
yerek-yaʿăqōb***, the euphemism of the oath in [[A Wife for Isaac]]. See
[[Put Your Hand Under My Thigh]].

**1:6 closes Genesis a second time.** *"Joseph died, as did all his brothers, and all that
generation"* — Genesis 50:26 gave Joseph's death; this gives everyone's. **The generation that
knew Joseph is gone before the king who does not know him arrives**, and the book will use the
same sentence-shape again, five books later, for the generation that knew Joshua (Judges 2:8–10).
See [[Another Generation Arose - Exodus 1.6-8 and Judges 2.8-10]].

**1:7 is five verbs and a result**: *pārû* (were fruitful), *wayyišrəṣû* (swarmed — the WEB's
*increased abundantly*, the verb of the water-creatures in Genesis 1:20–21 and of 9:7), *wayyirbû*
(multiplied), *wayyaʿaṣmû bimʾōd məʾōd* (grew mighty *very very*), *wattimmālēʾ hāʾāreṣ* (and the
land was filled). **Fill is Genesis 1:28's verb, and *the land* is Egypt.** The creation mandate
is fulfilled for the first time by one family, and outside the land of promise. Genesis 47:27 said
it in three verbs; this says it in five. See [[Blessing]] and [[Covenant]].

### A new king (1:8–10)

> **8** Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who didn't know Joseph.
> **9** He said to his people, "Behold, the people of the children of Israel are more and mightier
> than we.
> **10** Come, let us deal wisely with them, lest they multiply, and it happen that when any war
> breaks out, they also join themselves to our enemies, and fight against us, and escape out of
> the land."

***"Who didn't know Joseph"*** **is the whole of the explanation, and it is not ignorance.** A king
of Egypt could hardly be unaware of the vizier who bought the country for the crown (Genesis
47:13–26). *Yādaʿ* in Hebrew covers acknowledgment and obligation — the debt one house owes
another. **This king does not acknowledge the debt.** The verb will come back from the next
Pharaoh as the book's thesis in negative: *"Who is Yahweh, that I should listen to his voice …? I
don't know Yahweh"* (5:2). God's answer is the refrain of the plagues: *"The Egyptians shall know
that I am Yahweh"* (7:5). **Exodus 1:8 starts the argument about knowing that the plagues finish.**
See [[Pharaoh (of Joseph's time)]], [[Pharaoh (of the oppression)]], and
[[Kingship and Empire]].

Who he was is not recoverable from the text, and the wiki does not decide it. Stephen calls him
*"a different king"* (Acts 7:18), and *new* is often read as *new dynasty* — the native Egyptian
kings who expelled the Asiatic Hyksos in the sixteenth century BC are one candidate, the
Ramesside kings of the thirteenth (because of the city name *Raamses*, 1:11) another. The dating
argument belongs to [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].

***"The people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we"*** — *rab wəʿāṣûm mimmennû*
— **is Abimelech's sentence to Isaac** (*"you are much mightier than we"*, Genesis 26:16), and
*ʿāṣûm* is the verb of 1:7's *grew mighty*. **Pharaoh reads the blessing as a threat**, in its own
words. Abimelech's answer was expulsion; Pharaoh's is to keep them and use them. See
[[Mightier Than We - Isaac in Gerar and Israel in Egypt]] and [[Envy]].

**It is the king, not the narrator and not God, who first calls them *a people***, *ʿam* — and he
sets them over against *his* people, *ʿammô*, in the same breath. Exodus will spend its plagues
contesting whose people they are: *"Let my people go"*.

***"Come, let us deal wisely with them"*** — ***hābâ nitḥakkəmâ lô*** — **is Babel's opening
word.** *Hābâ*, *come*, is what the builders said at Genesis 11:3 (*"Come, let's make bricks"*) and
11:4 (*"Come, let's build ourselves a city"*), and Babel too gives its reason with *pen*, *lest*
(*"lest we be scattered"*; here *"lest they multiply"*). And Pharaoh's answer is brick: 1:14's
*"mortar and brick"*, *ḥōmer ûləbēnîm*, are Babel's materials (Genesis 11:3). **The first empire to
say *come, let us* was building a name for itself; this one builds store-cities with someone else's
labour.** See [[Come, Let Us - Babel's Bricks and Pharaoh's]] and [[The Tower of Babel]].

*Deal wisely* is ***ḥākam***, the root of *wisdom*, used here of cunning. **Egypt was the place where Joseph's wisdom saved the country** (*"there is no one so
discreet and wise as you"*, Genesis 41:39); **its first act after forgetting him is to be wise
against his family.** See [[Wisdom]].

***"And escape out of the land"*** — ***wəʿālâ min-hāʾāreṣ*** — **is literally *and go up from the
land***, and it is the verb Joseph died on: *"God will surely visit you, and bring you up out of this
land"* (Genesis 50:24). **Pharaoh's worst case is Joseph's promise.** The king fears war, alliance
with an enemy, and departure; the last is what happens, and the first is how (chapter 14). See
[[Carry Up My Bones - Joseph's Oath and Its Keeping]] and
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]].

### Taskmasters and store-cities (1:11–14)

> **11** Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens. They built
> storage cities for Pharaoh: Pithom and Raamses.
> **12** But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and the more they spread out.
> They were grieved because of the children of Israel.
> **13** The Egyptians ruthlessly made the children of Israel serve,
> **14** and they made their lives bitter with hard service, in mortar and in brick, and in all
> kinds of service in the field, all their service, in which they ruthlessly made them serve.

**Two words from Genesis 15:13 arrive here together.** Abram was told his offspring would *"serve"*
a foreign nation who *"will afflict them four hundred years"*. 1:11 is *ʿannōtô*, *to afflict
him* — the same *ʿānâ* — and 1:13–14 uses *ʿābad*, *serve*, five times in two verses (*made …
serve*, *service*, *service*, *service*, *made them serve*). **The prophecy is being kept, word for
word, by the people it condemns** — and Genesis 15:14 had already said what comes next: *"I will
also judge that nation, whom they will serve."* See
[[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]] and
[[Affliction and Flight - Hagar and the Exodus]], where *ʿānâ* is what Sarai did to Hagar.

***Śārê missîm***, ***taskmasters*** (literally *officers of the levy*), **is the corvée**: state
labour drafted from a subject population. Israel's own history will reproduce it. Jacob's blessing
foresaw Issachar *"a servant doing forced labor"* (Genesis 49:15, *mas-ʿōbēd*), and Solomon
conscripts the remaining Canaanite peoples into *"a levy of bondservants"* to build, among other
things, *"all the storage cities that Solomon had"* (1 Kings 9:19–21) — **the same *ʿārê
miskənôt*, *storage cities*, as Pithom and Raamses.** The narrator says nothing about the echo in
either direction. See [[Slavery and Servitude]], [[Issachar]], and [[Jacob Blesses His Sons]].

***Siblōtām***, ***their burdens***, **is the book's word for the bondage**: Moses sees *"their
burdens"* (2:11), Pharaoh orders *"Get back to your burdens!"* (5:4), and God promises *"I will
bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians"* (6:6).

**[[Pithom]] and [[Rameses]].** *Pithom* is generally taken as Egyptian *Per-Atum*, *house of
Atum*, in the Wadi Tumilat east of the Delta; *Raamses* as *Pi-Ramesse*, the Delta residence of the
Ramesside kings. Genesis 47:11 already called Jacob's settlement *"the land of Rameses"* — the
place where Joseph gave them *"the best of the land"* is the place where they now make bricks.
See [[Goshen]] and [[Egypt]].

***"But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and the more they spread out"*** —
*kēn yirbeh wəkēn yiprōṣ* — **reports the first measure failing in Genesis's vocabulary.** *Rābâ*
is the blessing's *multiply*; *pāraṣ*, *burst out*, is what God promised Jacob at Bethel (*"you
will spread abroad to the west, and to the east"*, Genesis 28:14) and what Jacob did under Laban —
*"The man increased exceedingly"* (30:43). See [[Served, Released, Pursued - The Laban Years as Proto-Exodus]].

***"They were grieved"*** — ***wayyāquṣû*** — **is dread with revulsion in it**, the word of
Rebekah's *"I am weary of my life"* (Genesis 27:46; see [[Isaac Sends Jacob to Paddan Aram]]) and of
Moab's distress at the multitude of Israel (Numbers 22:3). Pharaoh feared; now the Egyptians feel
sick at the sight of them.

***Bəpārek***, ***ruthlessly***, **appears in the Torah only here (1:13, 14) and in Leviticus 25**,
where the law of the Hebrew servant forbids it: *"You shall not rule over him with harshness, but
shall fear your God"* (Leviticus 25:43; again 25:46, 53). **The law takes the oppressor's adverb
and the midwives' virtue from this one chapter and sets them against each other** — *harshness* is
what Egypt did, and *fear God* is what stopped it. See
[[With Harshness - Exodus 1.13-14 and Leviticus 25]] and [[The Fear of God]].

***"They made their lives bitter"*** — *wayyəmārərû* — gives the root *mārar* that Israel will eat
at the Passover as bitter herbs (12:8) and drink at Marah (15:23). See [[Work]] and [[Suffering]].

### The midwives (1:15–21)

> **15** The king of Egypt spoke to the Hebrew midwives, of whom the name of the one was Shiphrah,
> and the name of the other Puah,
> **16** and he said, "When you perform the duty of a midwife to the Hebrew women, and see them on
> the birth stool; if it is a son, then you shall kill him; but if it is a daughter, then she shall
> live."
> **17** But the midwives feared God, and didn't do what the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved
> the baby boys alive.
> **18** The king of Egypt called for the midwives, and said to them, "Why have you done this thing,
> and have saved the boys alive?"
> **19** The midwives said to Pharaoh, "Because the Hebrew women aren't like the Egyptian women; for
> they are vigorous, and give birth before the midwife comes to them."
> **20** God dealt well with the midwives, and the people multiplied, and grew very mighty.
> **21** Because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.

***Lamyalldōt hāʿibriyyōt*** **can mean *the Hebrew midwives* or *the midwives of the Hebrews*.**
The WEB takes the first. The second — Egyptian women serving Hebrew mothers — is an old reading
(the Septuagint's phrasing allows it, and Josephus is usually cited as taking them for
Egyptians), and it would make the chapter's two righteous figures Gentiles who fear God, like Job and the sailors of Jonah. **The
names favour the WEB**: *Shiphrah* is a Semitic name (*šāpar*, *to be beautiful*), and *Puah* is
usually explained from Semitic too, though less securely. The wiki follows the WEB and records the
alternative. See [[Shiphrah and Puah]].

Two midwives for a people that *"filled the land"* is not a census; **they are best read as the
heads of their trade**, the women the king could summon by name. The text does not say so.

***"On the birth stool"*** — ***ʿal-hāʾobnāyim***, *on the two stones* — names a woman's birthing
seat; the only other use of the word is the potter's wheel of Jeremiah 18:3.

**The king's first error is about who is dangerous.** He fears men of fighting age (1:10) and so
kills sons and spares daughters. **Every move that defeats him in chapters 1–2 is made by a woman**:
two midwives, Moses' mother, his sister, and Pharaoh's own daughter. The order *"if it is a
daughter, then she shall live"* lets live everyone who will undo it. See
[[Down to Egypt and Up Again - Abram's Descent as Proto-Exodus]], where the same arithmetic —
*"they will kill me, but they will save you alive"* (Genesis 12:12) — is Abram's fear of Egypt.

***"But the midwives feared God"*** — ***wattîrêʾnā hamyalldōt ʾet-hāʾĕlōhîm*** — **is the
chapter's turning point and its only reason given for anything.** The fear of God is what Abraham
assumed was absent in Gerar (*"Surely the fear of God is not in this place"*, Genesis 20:11), what
Joseph claimed for himself before his brothers (*"I fear God"*, 42:18), and the first thing said of
Job (Job 1:1). **Here it is a reason for civil disobedience**: they *"didn't do what the king of
Egypt commanded them"*. The fear of God is not reverence in the abstract; it is the fear that
outranks the fear of the king. Compare Acts 5:29: *"We must obey God rather than men."* See
[[The Fear of God]].

***"Saved the baby boys alive"*** — *wattəḥayyênā ʾet-hayəlādîm* — **is Joseph's last verb.**
*"God meant it for good, to bring to pass, as it is today, to save many people alive"* (Genesis
50:20) uses the same root *ḥāyâ*, causative. **Joseph's providence was worked by a vizier with the
granaries of Egypt; this one is worked by two women who disobey Egypt's king.** See
[[You Meant Evil Against Me - The Two Intentions at Genesis 50.20]] and [[Providence]].

**The verb *ḥāyâ*, *to live*, runs the whole scene.** The king says *"she shall live"* (*wāḥāyâ*,
1:16); the midwives *let live* (1:17, 18); and their excuse is that Hebrew women are ***ḥāyôt***,
*vigorous* — lively, full of life, and a word that elsewhere means *animals* (1:19). **Pharaoh
issued a sentence of death and got back a pun on life.** See [[Life and Death]].

**Their answer is a lie, or at best a half-truth, and the text does not comment on it.** It
reports the excuse, then that God dealt well with them, then — repeating the cause — *"Because the
midwives feared God, he gave them families"*. **What the narrator gives as the reason for the
reward is the fear, not the answer.** The answer stands without praise or blame. Three readings
have a long history, and the wiki records them without choosing:

1. **The deception was right.** A murderous order forfeits the truth owed to its giver; the
   midwives' words are of a piece with their deed, and both are rewarded. Rahab hiding the spies
   from the king of Jericho (Joshua 2:1–7) is the standard companion case, and Hebrews 11:31 and
   James 2:25 commend her act without mentioning the lie.
2. **The fear was rewarded and the lie was forgiven.** God rewarded the mercy, not the falsehood —
   the reading usually associated with Augustine, who would not allow that any lie is righteous.
3. **It was not a lie.** The excuse may have been partly true, or the midwives may simply have come
   late on purpose, so that what they said was literally the case.

See [[The Midwives and Rahab - Deceiving the King to Save Life]] and [[Deception]], where the
midwives stand beside Rebekah, Jacob, Laban, Tamar, and Joseph — the first case in the Bible in
which the deceived party is a king trying to kill children.

***"God dealt well with the midwives"*** — ***wayyêṭeb ʾĕlōhîm*** — **is God's first act in
Exodus.** The God of this chapter is *ʾĕlōhîm*; the name *Yahweh* does not appear in Exodus until
3:2. **He acts in the chapter only for the two women who feared him, and he gives them *houses***
(*bāttîm*, 1:21) — households, families, descendants: the thing the king was trying to stop, given
to the women who would not stop it. See [[God]] and [[Blessing]].

### Into the river (1:22)

> **22** Pharaoh commanded all his people, saying, "You shall cast every son who is born into the
> river, and every daughter you shall save alive."

**The order is now public and national.** The first measure used officers, the second two women in
private; the third is given to *"all his people"*. **The genocide becomes the duty of every
Egyptian.** That is what the tenth plague answers: a death in *every* Egyptian house (12:30).

*"Every son who is born"* has no qualifier in the Hebrew; the Septuagint and the Samaritan
Pentateuch add *to the Hebrews*, which is plainly what is meant.

***"The river"*** — ***hayəʾōrâ*** — **is the Nile, the source of Egypt's life, made into its
grave for Hebrew boys.** Exodus will reverse the instrument at every step. The river is where Moses
is laid in a basket and drawn out (2:3–10); the river is the first water turned to blood (7:20);
and the sea is where *"Pharaoh's chariots and his army"* are cast (15:4). **What Egypt did to
Israel's sons in water, water does to Egypt's army.** See [[The Nile]] and [[Retribution]].

**The chapter ends without a resolution.** The decree is in force and nothing is said to have
stopped it. The next verse is *"A man of the house of Levi went and took a daughter of Levi as his
wife"* (2:1) — another household — and the next birth is a son. See [[The Birth of Moses]].

## The king, the people, and God

**What God does in this chapter is almost nothing, and what is done to Israel is almost everything.**
He is not mentioned in 1:1–16. He does not speak in the whole chapter. He is never said to see,
hear, or remember — that is 2:23–25, after a generation's suffering, and its delay is the point.
**What the chapter shows instead is the blessing working without him visibly at work**: the people
multiply in the passive voice, twice under pressure, and the two women who fear him are the only
channel through which he acts.

**Psalm 105 tells the same story with God as the subject of the oppression as well as the growth**:
*"He increased his people greatly, and made them stronger than their adversaries. He turned their
heart to hate his people, to conspire against his servants"* (Psalm 105:24–25). **Exodus 1 does not
say that.** It gives the king his own reasons — fear of numbers, fear of war, fear of departure —
and keeps God out of the hatred. The psalm's reading anticipates the hardening of Pharaoh's heart
in chapters 4–14, where both the king and God are said to harden it. This is a difference of
emphasis between two biblical accounts, not a contradiction between wiki pages, and it is recorded
here rather than resolved. See [[Providence]] and [[Judgment]].

**Deuteronomy's creed remembers the chapter in three verbs**: *"The Egyptians mistreated us,
afflicted us, and imposed hard labor on us"* (Deuteronomy 26:6) — after *"He went down into Egypt,
and lived there, few in number. There he became a great, mighty, and populous nation"* (26:5).
**Israel's liturgy keeps both halves of Exodus 1: the growth and the affliction, in that order.**
See [[Remembrance]].

**Stephen's retelling** follows Exodus 1 closely — *"the people grew and multiplied in Egypt, until
there arose a different king, who didn't know Joseph"* — and then compresses the three measures
into one: *"The same took advantage of our race, and mistreated our fathers, and forced them to
throw out their babies, so that they wouldn't stay alive"* (Acts 7:17–19). He also gives the
seventy as *"seventy-five souls"* (7:14), with the Septuagint. See
[[Seventy Souls - The Count of Jacob's House in Genesis, Exodus and Acts]].

**Matthew's infancy narrative is built on this chapter**: a king who fears a child, a massacre of
the boys of a town (Matthew 2:16), and a child who escapes — by going *into* Egypt. See
[[Every Son Who Is Born - Pharaoh's Decree and Herod's]].

## Notes

- **The king is given his own page as `Pharaoh (of the oppression)`**, not `Pharaoh (of the
  Exodus)`. Exodus 2:23 says *"the king of Egypt died"* before Moses is sent back, so the king of
  chapters 1–2 and the king of the plagues are two men. The schema's narrative-keyed qualifier rule
  is applied to each; the Exodus king gets his page when he first appears.
- **The Hebrew notes on this page** (*šāraṣ*, *hābâ*, *ʿālâ*, *ʿānâ*, *bəpārek*, *ʾobnāyim*,
  *ḥāyôt*) are standard lexical observations; none depends on an emendation.
- **Pithom's and Raamses' identifications** are the usual ones, given as such. The Brooklyn Papyrus
  and other Egyptian lists are sometimes cited for a name like *Shiphrah* among Asiatic servants;
  the wiki has not checked that report and does not rely on it.
