# The Ten Commandments

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 20:1–21

## Summary

God speaks to the assembly at Sinai, identifying himself as the God who brought Israel out of Egypt
before giving the commands. Exclusive worship, the prohibition of idols, reverence for his name, and
the holy Sabbath stand beside duties toward parents and neighbors. The commands protect life,
marriage, possessions, and truthful testimony, and reach into the desire to possess what belongs to
another (20:1–17).

The people tremble at the thunder, lightning, trumpet, and smoking mountain. They ask Moses to speak
in God's place because they fear that hearing God will kill them. Moses explains the encounter as a
test intended to produce a fear of God that restrains sin. The people remain at a distance while
Moses approaches the thick darkness where God is (20:18–21). The public speech and the request for
mediation belong to one scene; no tablets are handed over within this pericope.

## Notes

### The deliverer gives the commands

The opening identity and rescue precede the prohibitions (20:2–3). Israel is not told to earn the
departure from Egypt by obeying; the departure grounds the present claim. Nor does this make Sinai
the first divine command: the garden, the Passover, Marah, and the manna already contain
instructions. The chapter advances the covenant scene of [[Israel at Mount Sinai]] without yet
narrating the blood ceremony of Exodus 24 ([[The Blood of the Covenant]]).

The unit is traditionally called the Ten Commandments. Exodus 20:1 introduces God's words without
numbering them within this speech; Exodus 34:28 in the raw WEB uses the designation “ten
commandments.” This page describes the commands by reference rather than imposing a particular
numbering. It preserves the planned 20:1–21 boundary: the people's reaction and Moses' approach
complete the public encounter before the altar instructions of 20:22–26, now ingested as
[[The Altar and the Hebrew Slave]].

### Worship, generations, and the name

The prohibition covers other gods, making idols or images, and bowing to or serving them (20:3–5).
The references to heaven, earth, and water give it a comprehensive scope. The clause must be read
with its worship setting; this page does not turn it into an unqualified judgment on every kind of
art. Later sanctuary instructions themselves prescribe cherubim (Exodus 25:18–20, checked in raw; see
[[The Ark, the Table, and the Lampstand]] and [[Cherubim]]), a distinction the [[Idolatry]] page retains.

God's jealousy accompanies his demand for exclusive allegiance. The warning of judgment on the third
and fourth generation of those who hate him is paired with loving kindness to thousands of those who
love him and keep his commands (20:5–6). The WEB says “thousands,” without adding “generations” in
verse 6. **The tension with individual responsibility remains:** Ezekiel 18:20 says that a son does
not bear his father's iniquity, while Job 21:19–21 objects to punishment stored up for children.
Continuing the parents' hatred and distinguishing consequences from personal guilt are possible ways
to relate the texts; neither is a complete explanation expressly supplied by Exodus 20.

The command about God's name attaches guilt to its misuse (20:7). False religious oaths are a
relevant application, but the verse does not confine itself to a list of oath formulas, casual
exclamations, or forbidden pronunciations. Its stated concern is accountable use of the name, not a
new divine name being revealed.

### Rest is required of an entire household

The Sabbath is addressed to someone responsible for others: sons, daughters, male and female
servants, livestock, and a stranger within the gates must all cease work (20:8–11). The command
therefore regulates the hearer's power to demand labor as well as the hearer's own activity. It
grounds the weekly pattern in the creator's work, rest, blessing, and sanctification, taking up
[[The Creation of the World]] and extending the practice already narrated in [[Manna and Quail]].

**The protection does not abolish the household hierarchy.** Servants rest in 20:10 and are still
included among the neighbor's household in 20:17. The rescue from bondage in 20:2 and the continuing
institution of servitude must both remain visible. Deuteronomy 5:14–15 makes shared rest and
remembered bondage its explicit rationale; Exodus 20:11 gives creation as its rationale.

### Parents, neighbors, and desire

Honoring father and mother is linked to long life in the land God gives (20:12). Both parents
receive honor, and the command is addressed within the assembly rather than introduced as a rule
only for small children. It does not supply a case-by-case explanation of every early death.

The short prohibitions in 20:13–16 protect the neighbor's life, marriage, possessions, and standing
under testimony. The WEB renders 20:13 “murder”; this page makes no independent claim about the full
lexical range of the underlying Hebrew. False testimony has a concrete victim: the neighbor. The
prohibition of coveting then names house, wife, servants, and animals before extending to anything
the neighbor has (20:17). **The law already addresses desire before Matthew 5 or Romans 7 interprets
it.** Its household list is not a declaration of equal social standing among those named.

### Fear that withdraws and fear that restrains sin

Moses' reassurance and his call to fear God occur in the same sentence (20:20). In context, he
distinguishes the terror that expects immediate death from an awareness of God intended to prevent
sin. He does not deny the seriousness of the encounter: the people remain far off and only Moses
approaches (20:21). The darkness marks God's presence here, not God's absence or an evil rival to
him.

The request for mediation is not described as a refusal to obey: the people say they will listen to
Moses (20:19). Deuteronomy 5:28 explicitly approves their request while 5:29 expresses a desire for
lasting fear and obedience. Hebrews 12 later uses the scene in its contrast of Sinai and Zion; that
later warning should not be retrojected as an accusation of rebellion in this paragraph.

**Correction to the existing [[Testing]] synthesis:** its assertion that the tested are never told
the purpose while the test is occurring is contradicted by Moses' explanation here. Genesis 22 and
Job 1–2 do withhold the reader's knowledge from their protagonists; that pattern is not universal.
