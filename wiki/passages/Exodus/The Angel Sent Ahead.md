# The Angel Sent Ahead

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 23:20–33

## Summary

The Book of the Covenant ends not with another ordinance but with a promise about the road. God
says he is sending an angel before Israel to keep it on the way and bring it to *"the place which I
have prepared"* (23:20). The angel is to be heeded and not provoked, because *"he will not pardon
your disobedience, for my name is in him"* (23:21). If Israel listens to his voice and does all God
speaks, God will be an enemy to its enemies (23:22), and the angel will bring it to six named peoples
whom God will *"cut off"* (23:23).

The middle of the speech is a choice between masters. Israel is not to bow to the peoples' gods or
serve them, but to demolish their pillars (23:24), and to serve Yahweh, who will bless bread and
water, take sickness away, prevent miscarriage and barrenness, and fill out the number of their days
(23:25–26). God then describes how the land will change hands: his terror and confusion sent ahead,
the hornet driving out three of the peoples, and a deliberately slow dispossession — *"little by
little"* — so that the land does not go wild before Israel can fill it (23:27–30). A border is drawn
from the Red Sea to the sea of the Philistines and from the wilderness to the River (23:31).

The speech ends where it began, with the danger of the peoples' gods: no covenant with them or their
gods, no residence for them in the land, *"for if you serve their gods, it will surely be a snare to
you"* (23:32–33). As with the laws before it, this is a promise and a command addressed to a people
still at Sinai. Nothing in these verses reports that any of it has happened yet.

## People

- [[God]] — the speaker throughout, who sends the angel, places his name in him, and promises to
  fight, bless, heal, and drive out.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — *"an angel"* (23:20), then *"my angel"* (23:23). He is to be heard and
  not provoked, and he does not pardon. He is promised here and does not appear or speak in this
  unit.
- [[Moses]] — the hearer of the continuing address by context (20:22; 21:1); not named in these
  verses.
- The Amorite, Hittite, Perizzite, Canaanite, Hivite, and Jebusite (23:23, 28) are peoples, not
  individuals; no person page is created for them here. See [[The Nations]] and [[Canaan]].

## Places

- [[Canaan]] — the *"place which I have prepared"* (23:20) and *"your land"* (23:26, 33), described
  by its inhabitants rather than named.
- [[The Red Sea]] — the first limit of the border, *yam-sûp* (23:31; MAM), the sea already crossed.
- [[The Land of the Philistines]] — *"the sea of the Philistines"* (23:31), the Mediterranean coast
  as a boundary.
- [[The Euphrates]] — *"the River"*, *hannāhār* (23:31; MAM). The verse does not name it, but the
  definite *the River* is the usual short form for the Euphrates, and Genesis 15:18 names it in full.
- [[Horeb]] — Sinai remains the narrative setting; the mountain is not named here.

## Notes

### The boundary

The planned boundary is kept. Verse 20 changes from ordinances to promise (*"Behold, I send"*), and
verse 33 closes the speech that began at 20:22. Chapter 24 opens with a new instruction to Moses
(24:1) and is the next pericope, [[The Blood of the Covenant]]. Read together, 20:22–23:33 starts and ends on the same subject:
it opens with no gods of silver or gold beside Yahweh (20:23), and it closes with the peoples' gods
as a snare.

### Who the angel is

The unit says three things about the angel that set him apart from an ordinary envoy. He is to be
heeded as one heeds God (*"listen to his voice"*, 23:21–22, followed at once by *"all that I
speak"*). He *"will not pardon your disobedience"* — a power to forgive or withhold that the Old
Testament usually reserves to God. And *"my name is in him"* (23:21). The Hebrew says *šəmî
bəqirbô*, literally *my name is in his midst* (MAM). The same *qereb*, *midst*, comes back four
verses later, when God promises to take sickness away *miqqirbekā*, *from your midst* (23:25; MAM).

The wiki does not settle the identity question. [[The Angel of Yahweh]] sets out the three standing
readings: an envoy speaking in his sender's voice, a mode of God's own presence, and a distinct
divine figure. Verse 21 is the strongest single support for the second and third readings: an angel
who carries the divine name and does not pardon. Verse 20's *"I send an angel"* — sender and sent
plainly distinct — is the support for the first. The text holds both and explains neither.

**Why it matters later in Exodus.** The *qereb* language becomes the crisis after the golden calf. At
33:2–3 God again promises to *"send an angel before you"*, but adds *"I will not go up among you"* —
*lōʾ ʾeʿĕleh bəqirbəkā*, literally *in your midst* (MAM; WEB 33:3, *not yet ingested*). At 34:9 Moses
asks *"please let the Lord go among us"* — *bəqirbēnû* (MAM). So the question chapter 23 leaves
open, whether God himself goes when his angel goes, becomes the question Moses argues about after
the calf. See [[An Angel Before You - Genesis 24, Exodus 23, and Exodus 33]].

**"Will not pardon."** The verb is *nāśāʾ*, *lift* or *bear* (*lōʾ yiśśāʾ ləpišʿăkem*, 23:21; MAM),
the same verb behind Cain's *"greater than I can bear"* (*minnəśōʾ*, Genesis 4:13; MAM). Moses' plea at 34:9 uses a
different verb, *sālaḥ* (MAM). That contrast concerns wording; the wiki does not argue from it that
the angel is less merciful than God, which the text does not say.

### Serve them, or serve Yahweh

The book's key verb comes back three times in ten verses, and it defines the choice. *"You shall not
bow down to their gods, nor serve them"* (*wəlōʾ tāʿobdēm*, 23:24); *"You shall serve Yahweh your
God"* (*waʿăbadtem*, 23:25); *"if you serve their gods, it will surely be a snare"* (*taʿăbōd*, 23:33;
all MAM). [[Exodus]] is the story of a people taken out of Egypt's service into God's. At the edge of
the land, this speech says the danger is a new master, not a return to the old one.

The blessings attached to serving Yahweh (23:25–26) are all bodily and domestic: bread and water, no
sickness, no miscarriage or barrenness, a full number of days. Marah has already joined water to
the removal of Egypt's diseases (15:25–26), and the wiki reads 23:25 as a fuller form of that
promise. See [[Bitter Water at Marah]] and [[Healing]]. Job 21:10 later describes the wicked enjoying
the fertility promised here; see [[Why Do the Wicked Live]].

### How the land changes hands

Verses 27–31 attribute the dispossession almost entirely to God: his terror (*ʾêmātî*, 23:27; MAM)
sent ahead, the peoples *confused* (*wəhammōtî*, the verb of the panic at the sea, 14:24; MAM), their
backs turned, and *the hornet* (*haṣṣirʿâ*, 23:28; MAM). Israel's own part comes only at the end of
verse 31: *"you shall drive them out before you."*

**The hornet** is not explained. Readings include a literal insect plague, a figure for panic, and a
symbol for an outside military power (Egypt is often proposed). The text does not decide between
them, and the wiki records them as reported options, not checked against any particular commentary.
Deuteronomy 7:20 and Joshua 24:12 repeat the hornet. Joshua 24:12 adds *"not with your sword, nor
with your bow"* (WEB).

**"Little by little."** *Məʿaṭ məʿaṭ* (23:30; MAM). The reason given is ecological: a land emptied all
at once would become *"desolate"* and the wild animals would multiply (23:29). The delay is presented
as care for the land and for Israel, not as failure. Judges 2:1–3 later gives a different reason for
peoples left in the land: Israel's disobedience. Both explanations are in Scripture, and the wiki
does not merge them. See [[Little by Little - The Hornet, the Snare, and the Land Not Taken]].

### Six peoples, then three

Verse 23 lists six peoples, the same six named at the bush (3:8) but in a different order; verse 28
names three of them. Deuteronomy 7:1 adds the Girgashite to make seven, and Genesis 15:19–21 has
ten. The lists measure the land by its inhabitants and do not need to agree in count. See
[[From the River of Egypt to the Euphrates]].

### The border

*"From the Red Sea even to the sea of the Philistines, and from the wilderness to the River"* (23:31).
This is the second border drawn in the Torah after Genesis 15:18, and it is stated from the
standpoint of a people coming out of the southern desert: Red Sea in the south, Mediterranean in the
west, wilderness, and the River in the north-east. Like Genesis 15:18, it is far larger than the land
the tribes ever held. The wiki follows the identification of *"the River"* with the Euphrates and
notes that the verse itself does not name it.

### The snare

*Môqēš* (23:33; MAM) is the word Pharaoh's servants used of Moses — *"How long will this man be a
snare to us?"* (10:7). Exodus 34:12 and Deuteronomy 7:16 apply it again to the peoples or their gods,
and Judges 2:3 records it coming true. See [[Locusts and Darkness]].

### Not a license read off the page

These verses order the destruction of cult objects (pillars) and forbid covenants and coexistence
with named peoples in a specific land at a specific time. They contain no general rule for other
peoples or periods, and the wiki does not extend them into one. Their stated motive is the danger of
the peoples' gods (23:24, 33), not ethnic hostility. The moral questions conquest texts raise are
real. They belong to the Deuteronomy and Joshua passages where the ban is commanded and narrated,
and this page leaves them there.
