# Laws of Violence and Injury

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 21:12–36

## Summary

The ordinances turn from the terms of servitude to killing, bodily injury, and responsibility for
harm. A fatal assault brings death, but an unintentional killing receives a place of flight;
planning a neighbor's murder defeats even a claim to shelter at God's altar (21:12–14). Attacking
or cursing a parent and kidnapping a person are also capital offenses (21:15–17).

The following cases distinguish outcomes and relationships. An injured man who recovers is owed
lost time and healing costs (21:18–19). A servant killed under a master's rod must be punished,
but the next verse exempts the master in a case the WEB describes as getting up after a day or
two, explicitly because the servant is his property (21:20–21). A fight that injures a pregnant
woman introduces a fine where no harm follows and the matching scale of life, eye, tooth, and
wound where harm follows (21:22–25). A master who destroys a servant's eye or knocks out a tooth
must release that servant (21:26–27).

A fatal goring requires the bull's death. The owner's prior knowledge and failure to restrain a
dangerous bull can make the owner liable to death as well, with provision for a ransom; the death
of a servant instead carries a specified payment to the master (21:28–32). An uncovered pit and
one bull killing another lead to compensation rules that again distinguish an unanticipated loss
from a danger the owner knew about (21:33–36).

## Notes

### A set of cases, not one undifferentiated penalty

The planned boundary is retained. Verse 12 opens the homicide provisions; verses 33–36 already
bridge bodily injury and property loss, but finish the sequence about dangerous animals. The next
unit begins at 22:1 with theft. Here the differences between cases are the point: intent matters
in 21:13–14, recovery in 21:18–21, a victim's status in 21:20–27 and 32, and an owner's prior
warning in 21:29 and 36. Treating the whole unit as only *eye for eye* conceals most of its work.

### Refuge does not protect planned murder

In the WEB, an unintentional killing is something *"God allows … to happen"* (21:13, abbreviated).
This is the verse's account of the event, not a declaration that every death is blameless or that
intention never matters. The next verse makes a scheming killer liable even at *"my altar"*.
The altar just prescribed in 20:24–26 cannot cancel this obligation. The location and procedure of
refuge remain unspecified here; Numbers 35 supplies cities, judgment, witnesses, and the avenger.

### Parents, kidnapping, and an injured neighbor

The death clauses for attacking and cursing parents do not state that a parent has died
(21:15, 17). The severity therefore cannot be explained as nothing more than a rule against
homicide. They stand beside the command to honor father and mother in 20:12. The kidnapping clause
covers sale or continued possession (21:16); the text has not abolished every permitted purchase
of a servant, since 21:2 has just regulated one. It forbids seizing a person into that situation.

In 21:18–19 the recovering victim's ability to walk with his staff clears the assailant of the
fatal outcome at issue, but not of all obligations. Lost time and the costs of healing still
belong to the person who caused the injury. Healing here is a material duty owed by a human being,
not only an act requested of God.

### Protection and unequal status

**The tension must remain visible.** A master cannot kill a servant under the rod with impunity
(21:20), and a destroyed eye or knocked-out tooth brings freedom (21:26–27). Yet 21:21 grounds an
exemption in ownership, and 21:32 directs the thirty shekels for a servant's death to the master.
These protections do not establish equality with the other victims or abolish slavery.

The WEB says *"if he gets up after a day or two"* (21:21). This page preserves that wording rather
than replacing it with an unverified claim about eventual death or the exact duration of survival.
Likewise *"he shall surely be punished"* (21:20) specifies no method or measure of punishment in
this translation; one must not silently supply execution. Men and women are both expressly named
in the injury-release provisions and the goring cases, while social status still changes remedies.

### The pregnant woman and the limits of an English rendering

The WEB renders 21:22 as premature birth followed by no harm, for which the husband's demand is
subject to the judges' allowance. If harm follows, 21:23–25 gives the matching scale of penalties.
The harm clause does not explicitly name its recipient in the English text. **This page follows
the WEB wording without claiming that it settles the debated question of miscarriage versus
premature live birth, whose harm is meant, or the passage's application to other pregnancy cases.**
No claim about the exact Hebrew wording is being made. The KJV comparison checked for this pass
uses *"her fruit depart from her"* and *"no mischief follow"* (21:22); that wording alone also does
not settle the interpretation.

### Matching harm and the other remedies beside it

The eye-for-eye sequence places harm against corresponding harm rather than an escalating
multiplier. Calling this a limit on retaliation is a reasonable reading of its proportionality;
it does not by itself establish how courts historically applied every penalty. The neighboring
cases explicitly name payments, healing costs, and emancipation. The text does not say that each
item in 21:23–25 must instead be a cash payment, nor does it narrate an actual court carrying out
bodily punishment. Leviticus 24 repeats the matching rule; Matthew 5:38–42 quotes it and directs
its hearers toward nonretaliation. The connection page keeps that difference of instruction clear.

### What an owner knew

The bull whose dangerous habit had been reported makes the negligent owner liable along with the
animal (21:29). Ransom is conditional: *"If a ransom is laid on him"* (21:30), not a general right
to buy exemption from any killing. Numbers 35:31 refuses ransom for a murderer; 35:32 also refuses
payment to shorten the refuge resident's required stay. Those are distinct legal cases.

The uncovered pit and the previously dangerous bull extend responsibility to preventable property
loss (21:33–36). Where no prior dangerous habit is specified, the two owners share the live bull's
sale price and the carcass; where prior danger was known and ignored, full replacement is owed.
The text places human life, bodily injury, and animal property in one legal sequence but does not
assign all of them one remedy.
