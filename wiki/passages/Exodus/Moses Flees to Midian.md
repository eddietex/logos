# Moses Flees to Midian

**Book:** [[Exodus]]
**Reference:** Exodus 2:11–25

## Summary

**Three interventions, one flight, one marriage, one son — and then, for the first time in Exodus,
God.** [[Moses]] grows up and goes out to *"his brothers"*. He sees an Egyptian striking a Hebrew,
looks both ways, kills the Egyptian, and buries him in the sand. The next day he tries to stop two
Hebrews fighting and is answered with the question that will follow him for forty years: *"Who made
you a prince and a judge over us? Do you plan to kill me, as you killed the Egyptian?"* (2:14). He is
afraid; Pharaoh hears and seeks his life; he flees to [[Midian]] and sits down by a well. There he
drives off shepherds who are bullying seven sisters at the troughs and waters their flock. Their
father, the priest of Midian — called **Reuel** here and **Jethro** from 3:1 — takes him in and gives
him his daughter [[Zipporah]]. She bears a son, and Moses names him [[Gershom]]: *"I have lived as a
foreigner in a foreign land"* (2:22).

**Then the camera pulls back to Egypt, and the chapter ends in a different register altogether.**
*"In the course of those many days, the king of Egypt died"*; Israel groans and cries out under the
bondage, and their cry *"came up to God"*. Four verbs follow, each with *God* as its named subject:
**he heard, he remembered, he saw, he knew** (2:24–25). After a chapter and a half in which God acts
once (1:20–21) and is otherwise absent, the narrator names him five times in three verses.

**The passage is built as a hinge.** Its first half is Moses trying to deliver Israel on his own
initiative, by his own hand, and failing — rejected by the people he acts for and hunted by the king
he acts against. Its last three verses are God turning toward Israel on his own initiative and by his
own covenant. **Between the two is a man sitting by a well in a foreign country**, watering someone
else's sheep, naming his son for his exile. **Stephen's reading of the scene is exactly this**: *"He
supposed that his brothers understood that God, by his hand, was giving them deliverance; but they
didn't understand"* (Acts 7:25).

### The shape of it

| | verses | where | what Moses does | what comes of it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2:11–12 | Egypt | sees an Egyptian strike a Hebrew; strikes the Egyptian | a body hidden in the sand |
| 2 | 2:13–14 | Egypt | rebukes a Hebrew striking a Hebrew | *"Who made you a prince and a judge?"* |
| 3 | 2:15 | Egypt → Midian | flees from Pharaoh's face | sits down by a well |
| 4 | 2:16–19 | Midian | drives off shepherds, waters the sisters' flock | *"An Egyptian delivered us"* |
| 5 | 2:20–22 | Midian | dwells with Reuel; marries; names a son | *"a foreigner in a foreign land"* |
| 6 | 2:23–25 | Egypt | — | God hears, remembers, sees, knows |

**Three interventions on behalf of the wronged, in escalating distance from his own people**: an
Egyptian against a Hebrew, a Hebrew against a Hebrew, and Midianite shepherds against Midianite
women. **The first two fail; the third, done for strangers, is the one that is rewarded.**

## Key verses

### The first intervention (2:11–12)

> **11** In those days, when Moses had grown up, he went out to his brothers, and looked at their
> burdens. He saw an Egyptian striking a Hebrew, one of his brothers.
> **12** He looked this way and that way, and when he saw that there was no one, he killed the
> Egyptian, and hid him in the sand.

***"When Moses had grown up"*** — ***wayyigdal Mōšeh*** — **is the second time the chapter has said
he grew** (2:10, 11), and it skips everything Stephen and later tradition filled in: his education,
his standing at court, his age. Acts 7:23 supplies *"forty years old"*; Exodus does not, and gives
only his age at the confrontation with Pharaoh, eighty (7:7). See [[The Birth of Moses]].

***"He went out to his brothers"*** — **the word is said twice in one verse**: *ʾel-ʾeḥāyw*, *to his
brothers*, and *mēʾeḥāyw*, *one of his brothers*. **The narrator settles in the first sentence of
Moses' adult life which house he belongs to.** He was raised as the son of Pharaoh's daughter (2:10);
he goes out to the Hebrews as kin. Hebrews 11:24–25 makes this the act of faith: he *"refused to be
called the son of Pharaoh's daughter, choosing rather to share ill treatment with God's people"*.
Exodus records the going out and draws no conclusion about his motive. See [[Brotherhood]].

***"And looked at their burdens"*** — ***siblōtām*** — **is the word of 1:11**, *"to afflict them
with their burdens"*. Moses *sees* (*rāʾâ*) twice in verse 11 and once more in verse 12. **His first
recorded acts as a man are acts of seeing**, which is what God will do at 2:25, in the same verb.
See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

***"An Egyptian striking a Hebrew"*** and ***"he killed the Egyptian"*** — ***makkeh*** and
***wayyak*** — **are the same verb, *nākâ*, to strike.** The WEB renders the second *killed*, which is
what it amounted to, but the Hebrew lets Moses answer the Egyptian with the Egyptian's own act. The
next verse uses it a third time, of a Hebrew striking his fellow (*takkeh*, 2:13), and God will take it
up in 3:20: *"I will reach out my hand and strike Egypt"*. **The verb that begins with an Egyptian
overseer's beating ends as the plagues.** See [[Retribution]].

***"He looked this way and that way, and when he saw that there was no one"*** — ***wayyiren kōh
wākōh wayyarʾ kî ʾên ʾîš*** — **is the narrator's comment on what kind of act this is.** A man who is
acting as a judge does not check for witnesses. Moses looks around because he knows the killing will
not be accepted as justice — by Egypt certainly, and as it turns out by the Hebrews either — and he
hides the body (*ṭāman*, the verb Achan uses of hiding his plunder in the ground, Joshua 7:21). **The
text neither condemns nor approves him.** It records the precaution, the concealment, and the fact
that both failed.

***"No one"*** — ***ʾên ʾîš***, *there was no man* — **has a later echo the wiki records as an
allusion, not a citation.** Isaiah describes Yahweh looking at a people with no one to defend them:
*"He saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor. Therefore his own arm
brought salvation to him"* (Isaiah 59:16). **Moses looked and saw no man, so his hand did it in
secret; Yahweh looks and sees no man, so his own arm does it openly.** Whether Isaiah intends Exodus 2
is not demonstrable; the inversion is what the pairing shows.

**Was the killing right?** The passage does not say, and the rest of Scripture does not agree on how
to read it.

- **Stephen reads it as rescue**: Moses *"defended him, and avenged him who was oppressed, striking
  the Egyptian"* (Acts 7:24) — *ekdikēsin*, vindication — and the fault Stephen finds is in the
  Hebrews who *"didn't understand"* (7:25).
- **Exodus reads it with a silence and a hiding.** There is no word from God before the act and none
  after; Moses checks for witnesses; the act has to be concealed; it makes Moses a fugitive. **God's
  deliverance, when it comes, will be done in front of everyone** (14:30–31).
- **Later Jewish tradition supplies what the text leaves out** — a midrash (Exodus Rabbah 1:29) has
  Moses look *"this way and that"* into the Egyptian's future and see that no one worth sparing would
  come from him, which turns the glance for witnesses into a judicial inquiry.

The wiki records all three and adopts none. See [[Vengeance]] and [[Guilt and Conscience]].

### The second intervention (2:13–14)

> **13** He went out the second day, and behold, two men of the Hebrews were fighting with each
> other. He said to him who did the wrong, "Why do you strike your fellow?"
> **14** He said, "Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you plan to kill me, as you killed
> the Egyptian?" Moses was afraid, and said, "Surely this thing is known."

***"Him who did the wrong"*** — ***lārāšāʿ***, ***the guilty one***, *the wicked* — **is a verdict.**
Moses' first words in Scripture are a judge's question to the party he has already found at fault:
*lāmmâ takkeh rēʿekā*, *why do you strike your neighbour?* **He is doing exactly what the man accuses
him of doing** — acting as a judge — and the man's objection is not that the verdict is wrong but that
no one gave Moses the office. See [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

***"Who made you a prince and a judge over us?"*** — ***mî śāmkā ləʾîš śar wəšōpēṭ ʿālênû*** — **is
the deliverer refused by the delivered**, and Scripture comes back to the sentence more than once.

- **Joseph's brothers** asked the same kind of question of the dreaming brother: *"Will you indeed
  reign over us? Or will you indeed have dominion over us?"* (Genesis 37:8). **Both times, the man
  rejected by his brothers is the one through whom God will save them**, and both times the rejection
  sends him out of the land. See [[Joseph Sold into Slavery]].
- **Stephen quotes it twice** (Acts 7:27, 35), the second time to turn it: *"This Moses, whom they
  refused, saying, 'Who made you a ruler and a judge?'—God has sent him as both a ruler and a
  deliverer"* (7:35). **The speech is building toward its charge against its hearers**, who have done
  the same to Jesus.
- **Jesus asks it of himself, in the negative**: *"Man, who made me a judge or an arbitrator over
  you?"* (Luke 12:14), declining to settle an inheritance dispute.

See [[Who Made You a Prince and a Judge - The Rejected Deliverer]] *(new)* and [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

***"Do you plan to kill me, as you killed the Egyptian?"*** — **the killing was not hidden after
all.** The one witness Moses failed to account for was the man he rescued, or someone the man told.
The secret act has become common knowledge among the Hebrews before it reaches Pharaoh.

***"Moses was afraid"*** — ***wayyîrāʾ Mōšeh*** — **and here Exodus and Hebrews need setting side by
side.** Hebrews 11:27: *"By faith, he left Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king; for he endured,
as seeing him who is invisible."* Exodus 2:14–15 has him afraid and fleeing *"from the face of
Pharaoh"*. **The two are reconciled in two ways, and the wiki records both without choosing:**

1. **Hebrews 11:27 is about the exodus itself**, not the flight to Midian. The verse sits between
   Moses' choice to suffer with God's people (11:24–26) and the Passover (11:28), and *"left Egypt"*
   then means leading Israel out under Pharaoh's wrath — when he is indeed not afraid (10:28–29).
   Many commentators take this view.
2. **Hebrews 11:27 is about the flight**, which the ordering supports (the flight comes before the
   Passover), and *"not fearing the wrath of the king"* is a statement about the deeper motive under a
   fear Exodus does record — Moses left because he would not go back to being Pharaoh's son, not
   because he was afraid of him.

**Either way, Exodus's own word is *afraid*, and the fear in this passage is the fear of a man, which
the midwives of chapter 1 were praised for not having** (1:17). See [[The Fear of God]] and
[[Faith]].

### The flight (2:15)

> **15** Now when Pharaoh heard this thing, he sought to kill Moses. But Moses fled from the face of
> Pharaoh, and lived in the land of Midian, and he sat down by a well.

***"He sought to kill Moses"*** — **the king who ordered every Hebrew son drowned now seeks the life
of the one who escaped, and does not find him.** It is the last thing [[Pharaoh (of the oppression)]]
does in Scripture. Whether he knew the man was a Hebrew is not said; the killing of an Egyptian by a
member of his own household would have been a capital crime either way.

***"Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh"*** — ***wayyibraḥ Mōšeh mippənê parʿōh*** — **is Hagar's
sentence.** *"Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled from her face"* (Genesis 16:6) is
*wattibraḥ mippāneyhā*: the same verb, the same *from the face of*. **Both are fugitives from an
oppressor's household, both go into the wilderness, both stop at water, and both meet the angel of
Yahweh there** (Genesis 16:7; Exodus 3:2). See [[Affliction and Flight - Hagar and the Exodus]] and
[[The Two Flights of Hagar]].

**It is Jacob's flight too**: *"flee to Laban, my brother"* (Genesis 27:43) — *bərāḥ* — a son fleeing
a brother's threat to kill him, who goes east, comes to a well, waters a flock, and marries the
daughter of the house. **The man who will lead Israel out of Egypt repeats, step for step, the exile
of the man Israel is named after.** See [[Jacob]] and [[Jacob, Leah, and Rachel]].

***"And lived in the land of Midian, and he sat down by a well"*** — **the verb is *yāšab* both
times**: *wayyēšeb bəʾereṣ-midyān wayyēšeb ʿal-habbəʾēr*, *he dwelt in the land of Midian and he sat
by the well*. It comes back a third time at 2:21 (*lāšebet*, *to dwell*). **The fugitive's whole
story is told as sitting down**: the prince of Egypt has stopped moving. The definite article, *the
well*, is the storyteller's: a reader who knows Genesis knows what happens at *the* well.

[[Midian]] is kin. Midian was a son of [[Abraham]] by [[Keturah]] (Genesis 25:2), sent east with
gifts; Midianite traders carried Joseph into Egypt (Genesis 37:28, 36). **Moses flees Egypt to the
descendants of the people who brought Joseph there.** See
[[Ishmaelites or Midianites - The Caravan That Bought Joseph]].

### The third intervention (2:16–19)

> **16** Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters. They came and drew water, and filled the
> troughs to water their father's flock.
> **17** The shepherds came and drove them away; but Moses stood up and helped them, and watered
> their flock.
> **18** When they came to Reuel, their father, he said, "How is it that you have returned so early
> today?"
> **19** They said, "An Egyptian delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds, and moreover he drew
> water for us, and watered the flock."

**This is the third telling of the betrothal at the well**, after the servant and Rebekah (Genesis 24)
and Jacob and Rachel (Genesis 29). The stranger far from home, the well, the girls who come to draw,
the deed of strength, the running home, the father's invitation, the marriage — every element is in
place, and in the established order. **What Exodus changes is that the family is not kin and the
deed is not for a chosen bride**: Moses does not know which of the seven he will marry, and he waters
the flock of a foreign priest because the shepherds were wrong. See [[The Betrothal at the Well]].

***"The shepherds came and drove them away"*** — ***wayəgārəšûm*** — **is *gāraš*, to drive out**, a
heavy verb for bullying at a trough. It is the verb of the expulsion from Eden (*"So he drove out the
man"*, Genesis 3:24) and of Sarah's demand about Hagar (*"Cast out this servant and her son"*,
Genesis 21:10); and **in Exodus it is the verb of the end**: Pharaoh *"shall drive them out of his
land"* (6:1), *"he will surely thrust you out altogether"* (11:1), *"they were thrust out of Egypt"*
(12:39). **The book's first *gāraš* is shepherds driving women from water, and Moses undoes it.**

***"But Moses stood up and helped them"*** — ***wayyōšīʿān*** — **is *yāšaʿ*, to save**, the verb of
*salvation* and of the name *Joshua*. It is the verb the narrator will use over the sea: *"Thus
Yahweh saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians"* (14:30). **The first person *saved* in
Exodus is seven Midianite women, at a well, by a fugitive.**

***"An Egyptian delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds"*** — ***hiṣṣîlānû miyyad hārōʿîm*** —
**is the other verb of rescue, *nāṣal*, and the other half of the exodus's formula.** *"I have come
down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians"* (3:8) — *ləhaṣṣîlô miyyad miṣrayim* — **is
this sentence with the nouns exchanged.** The daughters say *out of the hand of the shepherds*; God
will say *out of the hand of the Egyptians*. **And the deliverer the daughters describe is *an
Egyptian*.** That is what Moses looks like to everyone outside his family: his clothes, his speech,
his bearing are Egyptian. To the Hebrews he is a man with no right to judge them; to the Midianites
he is an Egyptian. See [[Names and Naming]].

***"Moreover he drew water for us"*** — ***wəgam-dālōh dālâ lānû***, the infinitive doubled for
emphasis: *he even drew and drew for us*. **The drawing-of-water verb is *dālâ*, not *māšâ*, the
*draw out* of 2:10** — the narrator does not repeat the name's pun here, and the wiki does not
supply it.

***"Reuel, their father"*** — ***Rəʿûʾēl***, ***friend of God*** — **is a priest of a people outside
the covenant line, and the narrative gives him no name for his god.** He is the third such figure:
[[Melchizedek]], *"priest of God Most High"* (Genesis 14:18), and [[Potiphera]], *"priest of On"*
(Genesis 41:45), both fathers or blessers of the line from outside it. He will bless Yahweh and
offer sacrifice in Exodus 18. See [[Jethro]] *(new)* and [[Priesthood]].

### Reuel's welcome and the marriage (2:20–22)

> **20** He said to his daughters, "Where is he? Why is it that you have left the man? Call him, that
> he may eat bread."
> **21** Moses was content to dwell with the man. He gave Moses Zipporah, his daughter.
> **22** She bore a son, and he named him Gershom, for he said, "I have lived as a foreigner in a
> foreign land."

***"Why is it that you have left the man?"*** — **the father's first words are a rebuke for
inhospitality**, three quick questions and an order. *"Call him, that he may eat bread"* is the
formula of Abraham at Mamre (*"I will get a morsel of bread"*, Genesis 18:5) and of Laban's welcome
(Genesis 24:31–33; 29:13–14). **The fugitive from Pharaoh's house is fed by a stranger's.** See
[[Hospitality]].

***"Moses was content to dwell with the man"*** — ***wayyôʾel Mōšeh lāšebet*** — **is *yāʾal*, to
agree, to be willing, to consent**, a word used of settling a bargain. **No courtship, no bride-price,
no seven years**: the third verb of *dwelling* in seven verses, and then a gift. *"He gave Moses
Zipporah, his daughter"* — **the woman who will save his life at a lodging place on the way back to
Egypt** (4:24–26), and who is sent home before the exodus and brought back by her father (18:2). See
[[Zipporah]] *(new)* and [[Marriage]].

***Ṣippōrâ*** means ***bird*** (*ṣippôr*). The narrator does not comment on it.

***"He named him Gershom, for he said, 'I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land'"*** —
***gēr hāyîtî bəʾereṣ nokriyyâ*** — **the name is heard as *gēr šām*, *a sojourner there*.**
Exodus 18:3 repeats it word for word, with a second son, Eliezer, named for the escape:
*"My father's God was my help and delivered me from Pharaoh's sword"* (18:4).

**The naming speech says three things the passage has not said aloud.**

- ***Gēr*** is the word of Genesis 15:13 — *"your offspring will live as **foreigners** in a land that
  is not theirs"*. **Moses is the first of Abraham's offspring to call himself what God said they
  would be — and he says it in Midian, not in Egypt.** The foreign land he means is the one he is
  standing in; his family is the one in Egypt. Or the reverse: the Hebrew allows either, and readers
  have taken both. See [[Four Hundred Years - The Sojourn and the Exodus]].
- **It sets Moses beside [[Joseph]], who also named his sons for his exile.** Manasseh: *"God has made
  me forget all my toil, and all my father's house"*; Ephraim: *"God has made me fruitful in the land
  of my affliction"* (Genesis 41:51–52). **Joseph's names speak of forgetting home and flourishing
  abroad; Moses' first son's name speaks only of not belonging.** And **Joseph names God in both;
  Moses names God in neither of the names Exodus 2 gives** — the God-name is held back to Eliezer,
  in chapter 18, after the deliverance.
- **It becomes the law.** *"You shall not oppress an alien, for you know the heart of an alien, since
  you were aliens in the land of Egypt"* (Exodus 23:9; cf. 22:21) — *gēr*, every time. Leviticus
  19:34 and Deuteronomy 10:19 make it a command to *love* the foreigner, on the same ground. **The
  lawgiver had a son whose name was the reason for the law.**

See [[A Foreigner in a Foreign Land - Gershom and the Law of the Stranger]] *(new)*,
[[A Stranger and a Foreigner - The Sojourner's Confession]], [[Gershom]] *(new)*, and
[[Land and Sojourning]].

**Stephen says Moses *"became the father of two sons"* in Midian** (Acts 7:29); Exodus 2 names one,
and 4:20 (*"his sons"*) and 18:3–4 supply the second. Gershom's line reappears in two places that do
not flatter it: a grandson of Gershom serving as priest at Dan's idol (Judges 18:30), and the plain
listing of *"Shebuel the chief"* among the Levites (1 Chronicles 23:16). The first is the subject of a
famous scribal problem, recorded on [[Gershom]].

### God hears, remembers, sees, and knows (2:23–25)

> **23** In the course of those many days, the king of Egypt died, and the children of Israel sighed
> because of the bondage, and they cried, and their cry came up to God because of the bondage.
> **24** God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with
> Jacob.
> **25** God saw the children of Israel, and God was concerned about them.

***"In the course of those many days"*** — **the narrator lets Moses' entire Midian life pass in a
clause.** Stephen counts forty years (Acts 7:30); Exodus 7:7 gives Moses eighty at the confrontation.
The king who sought his life dies; **Israel's condition does not change with him**, which is the
point of reporting the death here: the oppression was never one man's policy. Exodus 4:19 picks it
up: *"all the men who sought your life are dead"*. See [[Pharaoh (of the oppression)]].

***"Because of the bondage"*** — ***min-hāʿăbōdâ***, said twice in one verse — **is the *ʿăbōdâ*,
the *service*, of 1:13–14.** The book's governing root returns at the moment God turns toward it. See
[[Slavery and Servitude]].

***"They cried, and their cry came up to God"*** — ***wayyizʿāqû wattaʿal šawʿātām
ʾel-hāʾĕlōhîm*** — **the cry is not addressed to God.** Israel groans and cries out *because of the
bondage*; it is the cry that goes up, not a prayer that is sent. **No one in Exodus 1–2 prays.** The
narrator gives God the hearing of a cry nobody directed at him, which is the shape of Abel's blood
crying from the ground (Genesis 4:10) and of *"the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah"* that reached him
(Genesis 18:20–21) — **and, closest of all, of Hagar's boy, whose voice God heard when it was his
mother, not the boy, who was weeping** (Genesis 21:16–17). See [[Prayer]] and [[Suffering]].

**The four verbs:**

| | the verb | what it answers |
|---|---|---|
| ***wayyišmaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*** | *God heard* their groaning (*naʾăqātām*) | the cry that went up (2:23) |
| ***wayyizkōr ʾĕlōhîm*** | *God remembered* his covenant | the king who *"didn't know Joseph"* (1:8) |
| ***wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm*** | *God saw* the children of Israel | Moses, who *saw* their burdens (2:11) |
| ***wayyēdaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*** | *God knew* — the WEB's *was concerned* | the Pharaoh who did not *know* (1:8) |

***"God heard"*** — ***šāmaʿ*** — **is Ishmael's name** (*God hears*, Genesis 16:11) and Simeon's
(*"Yahweh has heard"*, Genesis 29:33); the God of the exodus hears in the same verb he heard the
slave woman in. See [[Affliction and Flight - Hagar and the Exodus]].

***"God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob"*** — **the first time the
narrator names all three patriarchs together as the covenant's parties.** Joseph had done it in his
dying words — *"the land which he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob"* (Genesis 50:24) — and
Exodus 3:6 will put the triad in God's own mouth. ***Zākar*** in Scripture is not recollection after forgetting but action on a
commitment: *"God remembered Noah"* and the waters fell (Genesis 8:1); *"God remembered Abraham, and
sent Lot out"* (Genesis 19:29); *"God remembered Rachel"* and opened her womb (Genesis 30:22). **Each
time, the remembering is the hinge of a rescue, and here it is the hinge of the book.** The covenant
remembered is the one that put the four hundred years on a schedule (Genesis 15:13–14). God repeats
it to Moses at 6:5 — *"I have heard the groaning of the children of Israel … and I have remembered my
covenant"* — and Zechariah's song still calls it the purpose of salvation: *"to remember his holy
covenant, the oath which he swore to Abraham, our father"* (Luke 1:72–73). See
[[God Remembered - Noah, Abraham, Rachel, and Israel]] *(new)*, [[Remembrance]], and [[Covenant]].

***"God was concerned about them"*** — ***wayyēdaʿ ʾĕlōhîm***, literally ***and God knew*** — **ends
the chapter on the verb that began the oppression.** *"A new king arose over Egypt, who didn't know
Joseph"* (1:8): Egypt's forgetting started the bondage, and God's knowing ends the account of it. The
verb has no object in the Hebrew — *God knew* — and the translations supply one (*took notice*, *was
concerned*, *had regard for them*). **The bare form is the stronger: the narrator does not say what
God knew, because it is everything Exodus 1 described.** Exodus 3:7 completes it: *"I know their
sorrows"*. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

## The deliverer who fails and the God who turns

**Read whole, the passage refuses the reader the story it seems to be starting.** A Hebrew raised in
Pharaoh's house, who sees the suffering of his people and strikes their oppressor, is the hero of a
national liberation story. **Exodus tells that story and then shows it collapsing in a day**: the
oppressor's death has to be hidden, the oppressed want no judge, the king hears, and the would-be
liberator is a shepherd in Arabia for the next forty years. **The deliverance begins only when God
hears a cry no one sent him, remembers a covenant made before Moses' family existed, and sees.**

| | Moses (2:11–15) | God (2:23–25; 3:7–10) |
|---|---|---|
| sees | *"looked at their burdens"* | *"God saw the children of Israel"* |
| initiative | his own; no word from God | his own; no prayer from Israel |
| method | strikes one Egyptian in secret | *"I will … strike Egypt"* (3:20) in public |
| received by Israel | *"Who made you a prince?"* | *"the people believed"* (4:31) |
| outcome | flight | the exodus |

**And yet the fugitive is the one God sends.** Stephen's conclusion is the passage's: the man the
people refused, God sent *"as both a ruler and a deliverer"* (Acts 7:35). The sequence Exodus sets
up — the deliverer rejected by his own, exiled, married among foreigners, then sent back — is the
pattern of [[Joseph]] before him, and the New Testament reads it forward. See
[[Who Made You a Prince and a Judge - The Rejected Deliverer]].

## Notes

- **Reuel and Jethro.** The priest of Midian is *Reuel* at 2:18 and *Jethro* at 3:1; 4:18; 18:1–12.
  Numbers 10:29 has *"Hobab, the son of Reuel the Midianite, Moses' father-in-law"*, which can be
  parsed to make either Hobab or Reuel the father-in-law; Judges 4:11 calls Hobab *ḥōtēn Mōšeh*, which
  the WEB renders *"Moses' brother-in-law"* and other translations *father-in-law*. **The standard
  explanations are that one man had two names (or a name and a title), that *Reuel* is the clan
  father and *Jethro* the head of household, or that the traditions differ.** The wiki files the man
  under **[[Jethro]]**, the name Exodus uses for him most often, and records the others there. The
  bare-name collision with [[Reuel (son of Esau)]] is therefore avoided, and that page's forward
  reference is updated.
- **Hebrews 11:27 and *"Moses was afraid"*** — recorded as an apparent tension with two standard
  readings; neither adopted.
- **Whether the killing was justified** is not stated in Exodus; Stephen's approving reading, the
  narrative's own reticence, and later tradition are recorded side by side.
- **The Isaiah 59:16 echo** of *ʾên ʾîš* is an allusion the wiki notes, not a demonstrated citation.
- **The *ʾên ʾîš* / *yāšaʿ* / *nāṣal* / *gāraš* / *šāmaʿ* / *zākar* / *yādaʿ* observations** are
  lexical and checked against the Hebrew forms given; the WEB's English differs in places (*killed*
  for *nākâ*, *helped* for *yāšaʿ*, *was concerned* for *yādaʿ*), and the page notes each.
