# The Twelve Stones from the Jordan

**Book:** [[Joshua]]
**Reference:** Joshua 4:1–24

## Summary

After the nation crosses, Yahweh tells Joshua to have twelve men, one per tribe, carry stones
from the priests’ standing place in the Jordan to the night’s camp. Joshua explains that the
stones will prompt future children to ask about the crossing. The men obey. Verse 9 also
records Joshua setting twelve stones within the Jordan where the priests stood.

The priests remain until the commanded work is finished. Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh
cross armed as Moses instructed, about forty thousand ready for battle. Yahweh establishes
Joshua before Israel. At his command the priests come out, and the river returns over its
banks. Israel camps at Gilgal on Jericho’s east border on the tenth day of the first month.
Joshua erects the carried stones there and gives the answer for future children: Yahweh
dried the Jordan as he dried the Red Sea, so all peoples may know his power and Israel may
fear him forever.

## Notes

**Two locations for stones.** Verses 3–8 and 20 describe stones removed from the river and set
at Gilgal. Verse 9 describes Joshua setting twelve stones within the river. The stored WEB
therefore presents two placements; it does not explain the river stones’ purpose or whether
they remained visible after the water returned. The account’s tension should not be erased
by relocating verse 9’s stones to Gilgal. Their remaining to the narrator’s day is not a
modern archaeological identification.

**A recapitulated crossing.** Verse 1 opens with the nation across; verses 10–13 return to the
crossing and the ark’s movement. Verses 15–18 detail the priests’ departure. Reading every
sentence as a new crossing would multiply events the text does not require. The chapter
completes the preceding unit and interprets it through remembrance; the boundary is retained.

**Authority and solidarity.** Verse 14 fulfills 3:7’s promised magnifying of Joshua. Moses’
commands still govern the action. The eastern tribes are descendants of the named patriarchs,
not those men personally; the forty thousand are a combined battle contingent, not a census
of all Israel. The twelve representatives and priests are unnamed and receive no invented
individual biographies.

**An explained sign.** Children must be told what the stones mean. The memorial is not
called an altar or object of worship. Teaching reaches backward to the sea and outward to all
peoples; Israel’s lasting fear of Yahweh is the intended response. The tenth day dates arrival;
the Passover at Gilgal is not yet narrated here.

**Sources and limits.** Quotations are copied from stored WEB Joshua 4. The children’s question
comparison uses stored WEB Exodus 12:26–27; 13:8–16 and Deuteronomy 6:20–25.
No new Hebrew or Greek claim, unstored-book quotation, modern site identification, or historical
date is asserted. Gilgal’s relationship to Deuteronomy 11:30 remains unresolved.
