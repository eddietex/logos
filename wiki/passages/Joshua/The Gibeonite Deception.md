# The Gibeonite Deception

**Book:** [[Joshua]]
**Reference:** Joshua 9:1–27

## Summary

Other kings gather to fight Israel, but Gibeon stages an embassy from a supposedly distant country.
Worn clothes and moldy supplies support the story. Joshua questions the envoys, yet the men do not
seek Yahweh’s counsel. Joshua makes peace and the princes swear to preserve their lives.

Three days later Israel learns that its treaty partners are neighbors belonging to four cities. The
congregation complains, but the princes insist that an oath sworn by Yahweh cannot be broken without
risking wrath. Joshua confronts the deception. The envoys admit that fear of the destruction
commanded through Moses drove their action. He saves them from being killed and assigns enduring
service as wood cutters and water drawers for the congregation and Yahweh’s altar.

## Key verses

> **9:14** The men sampled their provisions, and didn’t ask counsel from Yahweh’s mouth.

> **9:15** Joshua made peace with them, and made a covenant with them, to let them live. The princes of the congregation swore to them.

> **9:20** We will do this to them, and let them live; lest wrath be on us, because of the oath which we swore to them.”

> **9:23** Now therefore you are cursed, and some of you will never fail to be slaves, both wood cutters and drawers of water for the house of my God.”

> **9:27** That day Joshua made them wood cutters and drawers of water for the congregation and for Yahweh’s altar to this day, in the place which he should choose.

## Notes

The unchanged boundary includes both the ruse and its settlement. The opening coalition (1–2) sets
Gibeon’s response against the choice to fight. The chapter’s criticism is specific: evidence is
examined but Yahweh is not consulted (14). It does not say that questioning strangers or examining
provisions is itself wrong.

**Legal tension:** Deuteronomy 7:1–2 prohibits a covenant with these peoples, and 20:15–18
distinguishes them from distant cities. Joshua 9:7 recognizes precisely this issue. The oath then
prevents killing (18–20). Both claims must remain visible; the narrative does not report a divine
decree retrospectively approving the treaty.

**Survival and coercion:** The Gibeonites explain their fear rather than deny the lie (24–25).
Joshua rescues them, yet imposes slave labor (23–27). Their service to the altar should not be
recast as voluntary priesthood or an unqualified grant of equal status.

The final notice associates their work with the place Yahweh would choose (27), without naming
Jerusalem or dating the narrator’s present. No exact Hebrew claim, later-book quotation, modern site
identification, or identification with later temple personnel is needed for this ingest. All quoted
verses above are copied from stored WEB Joshua 9.

The consequence appears in [[The Sun Stands Still at Gibeon]] (Joshua 10:1–15): the treaty
partners call for defense, and Joshua comes with Yahweh’s assurance of victory.
