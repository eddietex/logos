# Rahab and the Spies

**Book:** [[Joshua]]
**Reference:** Joshua 2:1–24

## Summary

Joshua sends two spies secretly from Shittim to Jericho. Rahab shelters them in her house,
hides them under flax on the roof, and sends the king’s pursuers toward the Jordan with a false
account of their departure. Before the spies sleep, she explains her choice: news of the sea
crossing and the defeats of Sihon and Og has terrified the city, and she acknowledges Yahweh
as God in heaven and on earth.

She asks an oath protecting her family in return for her protection of the spies. They pledge
reciprocal kindness, conditional on secrecy, a scarlet line at the window, and the family staying
inside her house. She lowers them out and directs them to hide in the mountain for three days.
Their report to Joshua repeats her confidence that Yahweh has given Israel the land. No conquest
or rescue of the household is yet narrated.

## Key verses

> **2:9** She said to the men, “I know that Yahweh has given you the land, and that the fear of you has fallen upon us, and that all the inhabitants of the land melt away before you.

> **2:11** As soon as we had heard it, our hearts melted, and there wasn’t any more spirit in any man, because of you: for Yahweh your God, he is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath.

> **2:14** The men said to her, “Our life for yours, if you don’t talk about this business of ours; and it shall be, when Yahweh gives us the land, that we will deal kindly and truly with you.”

> **2:19** It shall be that whoever goes out of the doors of your house into the street, his blood will be on his head, and we will be guiltless. Whoever is with you in the house, his blood shall be on our head, if any hand is on him.

> **2:24** They said to Joshua, “Truly Yahweh has delivered all the land into our hands. Moreover, all the inhabitants of the land melt away before us.”

## Notes

**The testimony is the intelligence.** The chapter gives little military survey beyond the city
wall, gate, and escape route. Its decisive information is what Rahab says about the inhabitants’
fear. The spies’ final report (24) repeats her testimony (9), reversing the discouraging report
in Numbers 13. This is a literary comparison, not an explanation the narrator supplies for why
Joshua chose two men.

**Confession and action.** Her confession reaches beyond a local contest between peoples: Yahweh
rules heaven and earth (11). She acts on it before the city falls. Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25
later commend her reception and sending of the messengers in discussions of faith and works
(paraphrased here; no New Testament wording is quoted).

**Deception and protection.** Verses 4–6 expressly contrast what Rahab tells the messengers with
where she has put the men. The protective act is inseparable from this deception in the plot,
but the chapter gives no general moral permission to lie. The comparison with the midwives must
also retain a difference: Exodus 1 explicitly states God’s favorable treatment of them; Joshua 2
records a human promise whose fulfillment is still ahead.

**An oath with a boundary.** The scarlet line identifies the house; staying inside and keeping
silent define the pledge’s scope. Verse 19 assigns responsibility for blood according to that
boundary. The text does not identify scarlet as sacrificial blood or explicitly interpret the
sign as a type of Christ. Such a reading would require a separately identified interpretive
tradition, not an assertion about this chapter’s stated meaning.

**Timing remains unresolved.** Joshua 1:11 announces a crossing within three days; the spies hide
three days in 2:22, and 3:2 introduces another three-day interval. The narration does not explain
how these notices overlap. A retrospective arrangement or inclusive counting may be proposed,
but neither is established here. The existing tension is retained rather than silently harmonized.

**Scope.** The stored WEB supplies every quotation above. No Hebrew or Greek lexical claim is
made. The king remains unnamed, the spies remain unnamed, the mountain is not assigned a modern
location, and Rahab’s occupation does not establish a sexual encounter with her guests.
