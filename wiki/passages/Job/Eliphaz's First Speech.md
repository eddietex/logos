# Eliphaz's First Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 4:1–5:27

## Summary

Seven days of silence end, Job curses the day of his birth, and the first man to answer him is the
kindest of the three. This is the speech the rest of the dialogue is measured against: every later
speech by a friend is shorter on courtesy, shorter on hope, and longer on the wicked. It is also
the speech in which the doctrine that will govern twenty-two chapters is stated for the first time
in the book — not by an enemy, but by a man who crossed a desert to sit in the ashes for a week.

It runs in five movements.

**1. The courtesy (4:2–6).** Eliphaz begins by asking leave to speak — *"If someone ventures to
talk with you, will you be grieved?"* — and then remembers Job's own past ministry: he has
*"instructed many"*, *"strengthened the weak hands"*, *"made firm the feeble knees"*. The pivot is
gentle and it is already the whole argument: *"But now it has come to you, and you faint."* And
then 4:6, the question on which the speech turns: ***"Isn't your piety your confidence? Isn't the
integrity of your ways your hope?"***

That verse uses the prologue's vocabulary without knowing it. *Piety* is *yirʾâ*, fear — the fear
of God the narrator credited to Job at 1:1 and that God repeated at 1:8 and 2:3. *Integrity* is
*tōm*, from the root of *tām*, blameless, and of the *tummâ* God said Job *"still maintains"* at
2:3. **Eliphaz has, without having been in the room, reached for the exact two words the heavenly
court argued over.** And he asks of them the accuser's question turned inside out. The accuser
asked *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* (1:9) — implying the fear was bought. Eliphaz asks
*isn't your fear your confidence?* — assuming it should pay, and urging Job to keep cashing it.
Both men hold the same theory of the transaction; one uses it to doubt Job, the other to console
him. The book's whole design is to find out whether Job's fear is anything other than a confidence
or a hope, and Eliphaz's first piece of pastoral advice is that it should be exactly that.

**2. The doctrine (4:7–11).** *"Remember, now, whoever perished, being innocent? Or where were the
upright cut off?"* This is the doctrine of [[Retribution]] entering the book's dialogue, and it
enters as an empirical claim — *"According to what I have seen"* (4:8). Those who plow iniquity
and sow *ʿāmāl*, trouble, reap it. The reader has just watched ten children die under a house in
a wind (1:19) and a household of servants die by sword and fire, and was told in advance that the
man they belonged to was blameless. The question *who ever perished, being innocent?* has an answer
on the page before it is asked. Verses 10–11 then crush a pride of lions in five different Hebrew
words for *lion* — the roaring one, the fierce one, the young lions, the old lion, the lioness's
cubs — the most crowded zoological couplet in the Old Testament, and an image of the strong and
predatory broken. It is meant to describe the wicked. It describes a household.

**3. The vision (4:12–21).** Eliphaz then claims something no one else in the dialogue claims: a
revelation. A word *"secretly brought to me"*, a whisper, *"visions of the night, when deep sleep
falls on men"*, fear and trembling until his bones shook, *"a spirit passed before my face. The
hair of my flesh stood up"*, a form he could not discern, *"Silence, then I heard a voice."* It is
the most atmospheric theophany in the wisdom books, and every detail is chosen to authenticate
what follows.

What follows is a commonplace. *"Shall mortal man be more just than God? Shall a man be more pure
than his Maker?"* God *"puts no trust in his servants. He charges his angels with error"*; how much
less those who live *"in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust, who are crushed before
the moth"*. This is the first of the book's three *what is man* passages (with 15:14–16 and
25:4–6), treated in full at [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]]. Two things in it are new to
this wiki. **First, 4:18's *"he puts no trust in his servants"* collides with the only time the
reader has heard God speak of a servant:** *"Have you considered my servant Job?"* (1:8, 2:3). The
voice in the night tells Eliphaz that God trusts none of his servants; God in the council has
staked his own credit on one. **Second, the visitor is a *rûaḥ*** — a spirit, a breath, a wind —
and the last *rûaḥ* in the book was the *"great wind"* that came from the wilderness and brought the
house down on Job's children (1:19). Eliphaz's authority for the doctrine arrives in the same word as
the disaster the doctrine will be used to explain.

**4. The fool (5:1–7).** *"Call now; is there any who will answer you? To which of the holy ones
will you turn?"* There is no one to appeal to. Then a short exemplum, told in the first person: *"I
have seen the foolish taking root, but suddenly I cursed his habitation. His children are far from
safety. They are crushed in the gate."* Eliphaz is describing a type, and he does not say *you*. He
does not have to: Job's children were crushed, and Eliphaz has sat for seven days beside a man
whose habitation fell. The section ends in the line everyone knows — *"man is born to trouble, as
the sparks fly upward"* (5:7) — whose *trouble* is again *ʿāmāl*, the word of 4:8 and of Job's own
3:10, and whose *sparks* are literally *bənê-rešep*, *sons of Resheph*, the name of a Canaanite god
of plague and fire. The proverb is fatalistic, and it sits awkwardly with the verse before it,
which insists trouble *does not* spring from the ground — it has a cause. Eliphaz wants both: that
suffering is universal, and that it is earned.

**5. The counsel and the promise (5:8–27).** *"But as for me, I would seek God. I would commit my
cause to God"* — and then a doxology to the God who does *"great things that can't be fathomed,
marvelous things without number"*, who sends rain, lifts the lowly, frustrates the crafty, and
saves the needy, *"So the poor has hope, and injustice shuts her mouth."* Verse 13 is the line Paul
will quote: *"He takes the wise in their own craftiness."*

Then the speech's turn to hope, and its most famous words: ***"Behold, happy is the man whom God
corrects. Therefore do not despise the chastening of the Almighty. For he wounds, and binds up. He
injures, and his hands make whole."*** From 5:19 to 5:26 Eliphaz promises, in the second person,
everything Job has lost: deliverance *"in six troubles; yes, in seven"*, redemption from death in
famine and from the sword in war, a covenant with the stones of the field, peace in the tent, a
fold with nothing missing, offspring *"as the grass of the earth"*, and a grave *"in a full age,
like a shock of grain comes in its season."* He closes on his authority, and it is plural: *"Look
this, we have searched it, so it is. Hear it, and know it for your good."*

**The speech's tragedy is that most of it is true and all of it is misapplied.** God does frustrate
the crafty; Paul says so. God does discipline those he loves; Proverbs and Hebrews say so. God does
wound and bind up; Deuteronomy 32:39 and Hosea 6:1 say so. And the promise of 5:19–26 is, item for
item, what Job will receive in 42:10–17. What is false is the premise that makes the speech
pastoral: that Job is being *corrected*. The prologue has ruled that out. God called it *ḥinnām*,
*without cause* (2:3), and correction without a fault to correct is not correction. Eliphaz's
counsel is the right medicine for a disease Job does not have, and by offering it he has told Job
what he thinks the disease is. The forty chapters that follow are Job refusing the diagnosis, which
means refusing the cure — and he gets the cure anyway, by the other road. See
[[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]].

## People

- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — speaking for the first time, and at his most generous. Everything the
  wiki has said of his trajectory — courtesy, then insinuation, then fabrication — starts here, and
  this is the courtesy. But the insinuation is already present in 4:7 and 5:3–5, and the speech
  never once considers that Job might be what the narrator and God both said he was.
- [[Job (person)]] — addressed throughout and never named. Eliphaz's picture of him in 4:3–4 — a
  teacher, a strengthener of weak hands and feeble knees — is the first description of Job's
  character from anyone who knew him, and it agrees with the narrator. Job will expand it himself
  in chapter 29.
- [[God]] — named as *ʾĕlôah*, *ʾēl*, and *Šadday*, never *Yahweh*. Eliphaz's God has three faces in
  two chapters: the breath of anger that consumes the wicked (4:9), the Maker before whom no one is
  pure and who trusts no servant (4:17–18), and the healer who wounds in order to bind up (5:18).
  The reader knows a fourth that Eliphaz does not: the God who said *"my servant Job"*.
- [[Job's Children]] — not named, and present in the speech twice. 4:7's *"who ever perished, being
  innocent?"* is asked over their graves, and 5:4's fool whose *"children are far from safety. They
  are crushed in the gate"* is as close as anyone in the first cycle comes to saying they deserved
  it. Bildad will say it outright at 8:4.
- [[The Sons of God]] — 4:18's *"servants"* and *"angels"* (*malʾākāyw*), and 5:1's *"holy ones"*
  (*qədōšîm*): the heavenly court described from the outside by a man who has never seen it, in
  terms that contradict what the reader saw there.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — silent, but inside the *"we"* of 5:27:
  *"we have searched it, so it is."* Eliphaz speaks for the three.

## Places

- [[Teman]] — in Eliphaz's gentilic at 4:1. The town was proverbial for wisdom (Jeremiah 49:7;
  Obadiah 8–9), and this is the speech in which its wise man gives his credentials: observation
  (*"According to what I have seen"*, 4:8; *"I have seen the foolish taking root"*, 5:3), revelation
  (4:12–21), and the consensus of the learned (*"we have searched it"*, 5:27).

No other place is named. The speech's landscape is all rhetorical: *"houses of clay, whose
foundation is in the dust"* (4:19), the city *"gate"* where the fool's children are crushed (5:4),
the *"thorns"* the hungry harvest from (5:5), and *"the stones of the field"* (5:23).

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — stated for the first time in the dialogue, at 4:7–8, as something Eliphaz has
  seen. Everything that follows in the friends' speeches is a development of these two verses.
- [[The Fear of God]] — 4:6's *yirʾâ*, the fear the accuser asked the price of, now offered to Job
  as his *confidence*. Eliphaz unknowingly gives the accuser's answer: that fear is held for what it
  secures.
- [[Integrity]] — 4:6's *tōm*, the prologue's word. Eliphaz treats Job's integrity as an asset that
  should be producing returns, not as the thing being tested.
- [[Hope]] — *tiqwâ* twice: 4:6 (*"isn't the integrity of your ways your hope?"*) and 5:16 (*"So the
  poor has hope"*). These are the noun's first two occurrences in the book. Job will reply in the
  next chapter, *"What is my strength, that I should wait?"* (6:11), and by 17:15 will be asking
  where his hope is at all.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — 4:12–21, the only revelation claimed by any human speaker in Job, and
  the only night vision narrated in the wisdom books. Its vocabulary is borrowed from the narratives —
  *tardēmâ*, *deep sleep*, is what fell on Adam (Genesis 2:21) and on Abram before the covenant
  (15:12) — and its content is a maxim.
- [[Sin]] — 4:17–19, the first of the three *what is man* statements: no mortal is pure before his
  Maker.
- [[Righteousness]] — 4:17's verb *yiṣdāq*, *be just*, the question Job will take up at 9:2 —
  *"how can man be just with God?"* — and turn from a rebuke into a legal problem.
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — 5:1, *"To which of the holy ones will you turn?"*: the possibility
  of a heavenly advocate raised in order to be dismissed, eight chapters before Job asks for an
  umpire (9:33), and forty-one before Eliphaz himself is told to go to Job for one (42:8).
- [[Suffering]] — given its first account in the book by someone other than the sufferer: as
  correction (5:17), with a promise attached. This is the theme's *discipline* account, and Elihu
  will build four chapters on it.
- [[Wisdom]] — Eliphaz's three warrants for knowledge, observation, revelation, and consensus, all
  used in one speech; and 5:13, the verse Paul cites in 1 Corinthians to make wisdom foolish.

## Notes

- **Why the first speech matters more than its length.** The dialogue is often read as three
  rounds of the same argument, and on that reading the first speech of the first round is merely
  the opening statement. It is more than that, because it is the only speech in which a friend
  offers Job a future. Every promise the friends make is made here; Bildad's at 8:5–7 and Zophar's
  at 11:13–19 are shorter restatements, and from chapter 15 there are none. Read the speech as
  what the friends had to give, and the later speeches as what was left once Job declined it.

- **The vision and its content.** The wiki has noted on [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] that the night
  vision's apparatus is enormous and its message a commonplace. Two further points. The first is
  that the vision has been read by some interpreters as genuinely demonic — a spirit Eliphaz *"couldn't
  discern"*, bringing a message that makes God untrusting and men worthless, which is the accuser's
  position in a quieter voice. The text does not say so and the wiki does not adopt the reading,
  but it is worth recording that the book never confirms the vision either: God's verdict at 42:7
  covers what Eliphaz said, including what he said the spirit said. The second is that the message,
  however obtained, is close to what the canon says elsewhere (Psalm 143:2) — which is exactly the
  problem [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]] describes. A true doctrine delivered with the
  authority of revelation becomes harder to refuse, not easier to apply.

- **"Silence, then I heard a voice" (4:16).** *Dəmāmâ wāqôl* — literally *silence and a voice*. The
  same two words, in the other order, are Elijah's *qôl dəmāmâ daqqâ* at Horeb (1 Kings 19:12),
  usually rendered *"a still small voice"* or *"a sound of thin silence"*. The coincidence is real
  and the contexts are opposite: Elijah's voice comes after a wind, an earthquake and a fire in
  which God was *not*; Eliphaz's comes after a spirit and a shudder, and he takes it for God's. Both
  are stories about a man in crisis being told something by a voice out of quiet. Only one of them
  is endorsed by its book.

- **Sparks, or the sons of Resheph (5:7).** *Rešep* is a deity attested across the Levant — a god of
  plague and of the fire of the bow, equated in Egypt and at Ugarit with warrior gods — and the
  word also appears in the Hebrew Bible as a common noun for *flame*, *pestilence* or *bolt*
  (Deuteronomy 32:24; Habakkuk 3:5; Psalm 78:48; Song of Songs 8:6). *Bənê-rešep* here is most
  likely a poetic name for sparks or birds of prey, with the old divine name surviving as an image.
  The WEB's *sparks* is the standard rendering. What it loses is a faint echo: the plague-god's
  sons fly upward, and Job's body is covered with a plague from sole to crown (2:7).

- **5:9 in Job's mouth (9:10).** Job repeats Eliphaz's doxology almost word for word — *"great
  things past finding out; yes, marvelous things without number"* — and changes its use completely.
  Eliphaz praises the God who does great things in order to commend appealing to him. Job says the
  same thing in a chapter about a God who *"goes by me, and I don't see him"* (9:11) and whom no one
  can summon to court. **The same doxology, as encouragement and as despair.** It is one of several
  places where Job does not dispute what the friends say about God, only what follows from it.

- **Hebrews 12 and the two halves of this speech.** It is a coincidence of the canon, not an
  allusion Hebrews makes to Job, but it is worth recording: Hebrews 12:5–6 quotes Proverbs 3:11–12,
  the parallel to Job 5:17, and six verses later Hebrews 12:12 quotes Isaiah 35:3 — *"lift up the
  hands that hang down and the feeble knees"* — which is the language of Job 4:3–4. **The two images
  that frame Eliphaz's speech, the strengthened knees and the discipline of God, sit together in
  one New Testament paragraph about enduring suffering.** The difference is that Hebrews addresses
  people whose suffering it calls discipline from a Father; Eliphaz addresses a man whose suffering
  God called *without cause*. See
  [[Do Not Despise the Chastening - Job 5.17, Proverbs 3.11-12, and Hebrews 12]].

- **5:13 is not the only New Testament quotation of Job.** The wiki's page on
  [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] previously called it *the only direct New Testament quotation of the
  book of Job*. Romans 11:35 — *"Or who has first given to him, and it will be repaid to him
  again?"* — is generally counted as a quotation of Job 41:11 (Hebrew 41:3), and like 1 Corinthians
  3:19 it follows the Hebrew rather than the Septuagint. The claim has been corrected there to *one
  of two*, and the second was checked at the ingest of chapter 41 — see
  [[Who Has First Given to Me - Job 41.11 in Romans 11.35]], which records the one textual uncertainty
  (a near-identical line in some Septuagint manuscripts of Isaiah 40). What remains true is that
  **5:13 is the only one taken from a speech God rebukes** — Romans 11:35 quotes God.

- **The plural of 5:27.** *"We have searched it, so it is."* *Ḥăqarnûhā* is the verb of research
  and investigation — the same root as *"things that can't be fathomed"* (*ʾên ḥēqer*) at 5:9. The
  God of 5:9 cannot be searched out; the doctrine of 5:27 has been. Eliphaz does not notice the
  tension, and it is precisely the tension God's speeches will exploit: *"Have you entered into
  the springs of the sea? Or have you walked in the recesses (ḥēqer) of the deep?"* (38:16).
