# I Know That My Redeemer Lives

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 19:1–29

## Summary

Chapter 19 is the most desolate chapter in the book and it contains the most confident sentence in
the book, and the confident sentence comes *after* the desolation rather than instead of it. The
structure matters more here than anywhere else in Job, because the famous verse is unreadable
detached from what precedes it: twelve verses of God's assault, eight verses of total human
abandonment, a plea for pity that nobody answers, a wish for a permanent record, and then, out of
that, *"But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives."*

**The reply to Bildad (19:1–6) turns his own image on him.** *"How long will you torment me, and
crush me with words?"* answers [[Bildad's Second Speech]] at its opening word: Bildad asked *"How
long will you hunt for words?"* (18:2), and Job hands the phrase back. *"You have reproached me ten
times"* is a round number for *repeatedly*, on the idiom of Genesis 31:7 and Numbers 14:22.

Then verse 4, which is easy to read past and is the sharpest thing in the movement: *"If it is true
that I have erred, my error remains with myself."* Job is not confessing. He is refusing the
friends' standing: even granting, for argument, that he has gone wrong, the error is between him
and God and is none of their business. It is a claim about jurisdiction, and it is the closest the
book comes to saying out loud that the friends have no office.

And verse 6 takes Bildad's six-fold snare of 18:8–10 and reassigns it. *"Know now that God has
subverted me, and has surrounded me with his net."* Same image, same vocabulary, opposite agent.
Bildad's wicked man walks into a net *"by his own feet"*; Job says the net was thrown.

**The assault (19:7–12) is Job's second full description of God as his attacker**, after 16:9–14,
and it has a different shape. Where chapter 16 was an animal and an archer, chapter 19 is a
besieging army and a legal void. It opens with the failure that generates everything else:
*"Behold, I cry out of wrong, but I am not heard. I cry for help, but there is no justice."* Then
the road walled up and darkness set in the paths (compare Lamentations 3:7–9, which is the closest
thing in Scripture to this passage); the stripping of glory and the crown taken from his head; the
breaking down *"on every side"*; and — the line that will matter in ten verses — *"My hope he has
plucked up like a tree."* Bildad had just said of the wicked man that *"his roots shall be dried up
beneath"* (18:16). Job accepts the image, applies it to himself, and names the gardener. The
movement ends with siege works: *"His troops come on together, build a siege ramp against me, and
encamp around my tent."* One man, and God brings an army.

**The abandonment (19:13–20) is the passage's real horror, and it is the part nobody quotes.**
Eight verses that walk outward through every ring of a man's household and find no one: brothers
put far away, acquaintances estranged, relatives gone, familiar friends forgotten him, his own
house-servants treating him as a stranger, a servant who will not answer when called, *"My breath
is offensive to my wife. I am loathsome to the children of my own mother."* Then the bottom, which
is the children: *"Even young children despise me. If I arise, they speak against me."* And the
summary, verse 19: *"All my familiar friends abhor me. They whom I loved have turned against me."*

Verse 13 attributes the whole catalogue to God — *"He has put my brothers far from me"* — which is
what keeps the chapter from being a complaint about people. The social death is part of the
assault. And verse 20 gives the physical version of the same fact in a phrase that has outlived its
context by four hundred years in English: *"My bones stick to my skin and to my flesh. I have
escaped by the skin of my teeth."*

**The plea (19:21–22) is the only one of its kind in the book.** *"Have pity on me, have pity on me,
you my friends; for the hand of God has touched me."* Job has demanded, accused, argued, and mocked;
this is the one place he asks the three men for something, and the repetition is the sound of a man
who has stopped strategising. It is not granted. Bildad has just finished describing him as a man
whose name will be erased, and [[Zophar's Second Speech]] follows immediately with twenty-nine
verses on the wicked vomiting up his wealth.

The second half of verse 22 is a charge with a precise shape: *"Why do you persecute me as God, and
are not satisfied with my flesh?"* The friends are doing to him what God is doing to him — and the
accusation of cannibalism (*eating a man's flesh* is the Semitic idiom for slander, Daniel 3:8,
Psalm 27:2) says what he thinks their theology is for.

**The wish (19:23–24), and why it is placed here.** *"Oh that my words were now written! Oh that
they were inscribed in a book! That with an iron pen and lead they were engraved in the rock
forever!"* Job expects to die uncleared, and what he wants is not to be remembered fondly but for
the *testimony* to survive him — a record that outlasts both his body and the people currently
misreporting him. It is an escalation through three durabilities: written, inscribed in a scroll,
and cut into rock with an iron stylus and lead run into the grooves. And it is the answer to Bildad
one chapter earlier: *"His memory shall perish from the earth. He shall have no name in the
street"* (18:17). The reader holds the outcome of that argument in their hands.

**The Redeemer (19:25–27).** *"But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives. In the end, he will
stand upon the earth. After my skin is destroyed, then in my flesh shall I see God, whom I, even I,
shall see on my side. My eyes shall see, and not as a stranger."* Four things are being claimed,
and the Hebrew is damaged in almost every clause after the first (see the Notes, which set out what
is certain and what is not).

The one clause that is not in doubt is the one that carries the weight. *Gōʾălî ḥay* — **my
Redeemer lives**. A *gōʾēl* is not an abstraction: he is the nearest kinsman on whom the law lays
the duty to act for a relative who cannot act for himself — buying back sold land, buying a man out
of slavery, marrying a widow to raise a name for the dead, and hunting down a murderer. It is a
family office, and Job has just spent eight verses establishing that his family is gone. He asserts
a kinsman in the place where he has proved he has none, and the verb is *lives*, which is what he
has stopped expecting of himself.

Whether Job means he will see God after death or before it, in the body or out of it, is genuinely
open. What is not open is that this is the same reach as 9:33's umpire and 16:19's witness, one
stage further on and stated for the first time as knowledge rather than desire — *I know* — and
that it is stated by a man who, three verses earlier, was begging his friends for pity and, in the
next breath, says *"My heart is consumed within me."* The confidence does not arrive as a
resolution. It arrives as an outburst inside a collapse, exactly as 16:19 did.

**The warning (19:28–29) closes the speech by turning the friends' own doctrine on them.** *"Be
afraid of the sword, for wrath brings the punishments of the sword, that you may know there is a
judgment."* Job's final word to the three is a retribution argument, which is either an
inconsistency or the point: he has never denied that God judges, only that the friends can read the
verdict off a man's skin. And it is the one prediction in the dialogue that comes true — at 42:7 the
wrath is kindled, and against them.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — his sixth speech, and the one in which the two poles of his character are not
  forty verses apart but two. He is stripped of family, household, wife, servants, and children in
  verses 13–20; he begs for pity in verse 21; and he asserts a living kinsman-redeemer in verse 25.
  This is also the passage where he says the most precise thing he ever says about the friends'
  standing: *"my error remains with myself"* (19:4).
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — answered directly. 19:2 turns 18:2's *"How long"* back on him, 19:6
  takes his net, 19:10 takes his uprooted tree, and 19:23–24 answers his erased name.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — included throughout in the plural
  (*"you"* in verses 2–5 and 28–29 is plural in Hebrew), and warned by name of nothing but the
  sword.
- [[God]] — in this chapter he is three things at once again, and the distance between them is
  shorter than in Job 16. He is the besieger of verses 7–12, the one who has driven off every human
  being Job has (19:13), and the God whom Job expects to *see* — *"whom I, even I, shall see on my
  side"* (19:27). Whether the Redeemer of 19:25 is God or a third party is the passage's central
  unresolved question; see the Notes and [[The Witness in Heaven]].
- **Job's wife** — 19:17, her only appearance outside 2:9, and she appears as a woman who cannot
  stand the smell of him. The book gives her one line and one recoil.
- [[Job's Children]] — dead since chapter 1, which is what makes 19:17's *"the children of my own
  mother"* the nearest kin he has left to be loathsome to, and what makes the *gōʾēl* of 19:25 a
  claim with nobody behind it.

## Places

No geography, again, and the absence is now a pattern in the dialogue. The locations in this
chapter are a walled-up road (19:8), a besieged tent (19:12), a house full of people who treat their
master as a stranger (19:15), and a rock face where Job wants words cut (19:24).

- [[Sheol]] — not named. This is significant rather than incidental: Job's previous speech ended at
  the gates of Sheol with his hope going down into the dust (17:16), and this one ends with a
  Redeemer standing *"upon the earth"* — literally *upon dust*, the same word. Whether 19:25 puts
  the Redeemer over Job's grave or simply on the ground of the living is one of the passage's
  unresolved cruxes.

## Themes

- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — the theme's high point. *Gōʾēl* is the fourth and last of the legal
  offices Job reaches for (umpire, 9:33; witness and voucher, 16:19; surety, 17:3; redeemer, 19:25),
  and it is the only one drawn from family law rather than the courtroom, which is why it is the one
  that stuck.
- [[Hope]] — 19:10 says God *"plucked up"* his hope like a tree, and fifteen verses later he says
  *I know*. The chapter does not reconcile these, and the wiki's reading is that it is not supposed
  to: the confidence of 19:25 is not the recovery of the hope of 19:10 but something asserted in its
  place.
- [[Suffering]] — the chapter supplies the Bible's fullest account of suffering's *social*
  dimension. Verses 13–20 are not about pain; they are about being unbearable to everyone who used
  to love you, and about that too being God's doing.
- [[Righteousness]] — held without proof and without support. 19:4's *"my error remains with
  myself"* is the claim in its most careful form: not *I have not sinned*, but *whatever I am, I am
  not answerable to you for it*.
- [[Judgment]] — Job asks for one (19:7's *"there is no justice"*), predicts one (19:29's *"that
  you may know there is a judgment"*), and is proved right about the second at 42:7.
- [[Vengeance]] — carried inside the word *gōʾēl*, which is also the title of the avenger of blood
  (*gōʾēl haddām*, Numbers 35:19). Read with 16:18's *"Earth, don't cover my blood"*, Job is
  invoking the officer whose job is to take up the case of a man who has been killed. He is not dead
  yet, which is the point of the strangeness.
- [[Life and Death]] — 19:26's *"after my skin is destroyed"* is the book's most contested clause on
  what, if anything, lies past death; see [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].
- [[Remembrance]] — 19:23–24's engraved rock, set directly against 18:17's erased name. Job wants a
  record, not a memorial, and the distinction is that a record can still be used as evidence.
- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine is not attacked here (chapter 21 does that); it is simply
  bypassed. Job's answer to a description of the wicked man's ruin is not *that is not what happens*
  but *look at me and then tell me what my ruin proves*.
- [[Blood]] — through the *gōʾēl haddām* sense of 19:25 and its link back to 16:18.

## Notes

- **What *gōʾēl* means (19:25).** The root *gāʾal* is family law, not courtroom law. The *gōʾēl* is
  the nearest able kinsman, and the duty falls on him by relationship rather than by contract. Four
  offices are attested: **buying back** land a relative has had to sell (Leviticus 25:25),
  **redeeming** a relative who has sold himself into slavery (Leviticus 25:47–49), **marrying** a
  childless widow to raise up the dead man's name (the whole plot of Ruth), and **avenging** a
  relative's blood (*gōʾēl haddām*, Numbers 35:19). Every one of them is an act performed *for
  someone who cannot act for himself*, and three of the four are performed for someone who has lost
  everything or is dead. That is why the word is the right one here and why the four preceding legal
  metaphors were not quite: an umpire, a witness, and a guarantor all require a functioning court,
  and the *gōʾēl* requires only a relative who shows up. See
  [[The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood]].

- **What is certain and what is not in 19:25–27.** Certain: *gōʾălî ḥay*, *my Redeemer lives*, and
  that Job says he *knows* it. Certain: he expects to *see God* and to see him *for himself*
  (*lî*), with his own eyes, *"and not as a stranger"* — the Hebrew is *wəlōʾ-zār*, *and not
  another*, i.e. these eyes and no one else's. Everything between those two is contested.
  **(a) *ʾaḥărôn ʿal-ʿāpār yāqûm*** — *"In the end, he will stand upon the earth"*: *ʾaḥărôn* is an
  adjective, *the last one*, and it may describe the Redeemer (*he, the last, will arise*) or the
  time (*afterward*); *ʿāpār* is *dust*, which in this book means a grave as often as it means
  ground (17:16, 42:6). *Stand over the dust* can be a witness rising to speak in court, a champion
  standing on a battlefield, or a kinsman standing at a grave. **(b) 19:26a** — *wəʾaḥar ʿôrî
  niqqəpû-zōʾt*, roughly *and after my skin they have struck off — this*, is broken: the verb is
  plural with no subject and *zōʾt*, *this*, has no antecedent. Every English rendering here is a
  repair. **(c) *ûmibbəśārî ʾeḥĕzeh ʾĕlôah*** — *"then in my flesh shall I see God"*: the
  preposition *min* means both *from within* and *away from*. *From my flesh I shall see God* can
  mean *in the body* or *stripped of it*, and the whole afterlife question in this passage turns on
  a two-letter prefix.

- **Three readings of 19:25–27, and what each costs.** **(1) Vindication after death, in the body**
  — the resurrection reading, standard since the Vulgate (*in novissimo die de terra surrecturus
  sum*) and fixed in English by the KJV and Handel. Its strength is that it takes *"after my skin is
  destroyed"* at face value. Its cost is Job's own testimony elsewhere: 7:9's *"He who goes down to
  Sheol shall come up no more"* and 14:12's *"they shall not awake, nor be roused out of their
  sleep"* are flat denials of exactly this, and nothing in the book retracts them.
  **(2) Vindication before death, in this life** — Job expects to be reduced almost to nothing
  (*after my skin is destroyed* meaning *wasted as I am*) and then to see God clear his name while
  he still breathes. Its strength is consistency with the rest of Job and with the epilogue, where
  this is more or less what happens. Its cost is that it makes 19:26a's *after* do very little work.
  **(3) Posthumous vindication that Job will not witness** — the Redeemer clears his name over his
  grave; the engraved record of 19:23–24 is what survives to be vindicated. Its strength is the
  tight link to the preceding verses. Its cost is 19:27's *"my eyes shall see"*, which is hard to
  neutralise. The wiki records all three, prefers (2) on the evidence of the book as a whole, and
  holds that the passage's real force does not depend on the choice: in every reading Job asserts
  that someone with a family duty toward him is alive and will act, at a moment when the entire
  visible world has told him otherwise.

- **"The hand of God has touched me" (19:21).** *Nāgəʿâ bî yad-ʾĕlôah*. The verb *nāgaʿ* is the
  accuser's word: *"stretch out your hand now, and touch* (*ûgaʿ*) *all that he has"* (1:11), and
  *"touch* (*wəgaʿ*) *his bone and his flesh"* (2:5). Job never heard either speech. He is
  attributing the blow to the right hand in the sense the book cares about — God said *"all that he
  has is in your power"*, so the permission is God's — and to the wrong one in the sense he means,
  since the hand that moved was not God's own. This is the sharpest instance of the book's central
  irony: the reader knows the mechanism, and the man who has to live inside it does not, and his
  complaint is substantially correct anyway.

- **Who recoils at 19:17?** *"My breath is offensive to my wife. I am loathsome to the children of
  my own mother."* The second clause is *bənê biṭnî*, literally *the sons of my belly*, and the
  possessive is the difficulty. **Job's brothers** — *the sons of the womb that bore me*, taking
  *biṭnî* loosely for the mother's womb; this is the WEB's rendering, and it agrees with verse 13's
  *"He has put my brothers far from me"*. **Job's own children** — the plain sense of the pronoun,
  which would make the line a lament for the dead of chapter 1; against it, the whole catalogue is
  about living people turning away, and the dead cannot find a man loathsome. **His grandchildren or
  wider clan** — an attempt to keep the pronoun and supply living referents, with nothing else in
  the book behind it. The wiki takes the first and flags that a translation reading *"the children
  of my own mother"* has silently made the choice for the reader. See [[Job's Children]].

- **"By the skin of my teeth" (19:20).** The Hebrew is as odd as the English: *bəʿôr šinnāy*, *with
  the skin of my teeth*. Teeth have no skin, and the two standard explanations are that it means the
  gums, or that it is deliberately absurd — *I have escaped with nothing at all*. The phrase entered
  English through the Geneva Bible (1560), and the modern idiom, meaning *only just*, is a slight
  softening: Job is not saying he narrowly escaped, he is saying that what he escaped with is
  nothing.

- **Verses 23–24 and the book that exists.** The wish is for three levels of permanence — writing,
  a scroll (*sēper*), and letters cut into rock with an iron stylus and the grooves filled with
  lead. It is worth stating plainly what the reader is doing while they read it. Bildad has just
  guaranteed that the wicked man's *"memory shall perish from the earth"* and that *"he shall have
  no name in the street"* (18:17). Job's name is one of the most widely known personal names on
  earth, and Bildad's is known only because it appears in the record of what Job said. The book
  answers its own characters, and it does so by existing.

- **19:28–29 and Job's consistency.** Job closes by threatening the friends with judgment, which
  looks like the doctrine he spends the book resisting. It is not, quite. Job has never denied that
  God punishes wrongdoing; his position is that the punishment cannot be run backwards into a
  diagnosis. Warning men who are wronging him that there is a judgment is compatible with that, and
  the epilogue treats the warning as accurate. The verse also contains a textual difficulty worth
  noting: *"because the root of the matter is found in me"* follows the Masoretic *bî*, *in me*,
  where many manuscripts and versions read *bô*, *in him*, which would make it the friends saying
  the fault lies in Job. The sense of the warning is unaffected.
