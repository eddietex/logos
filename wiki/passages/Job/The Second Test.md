# The Second Test

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 2:1–10

## Summary

Ten verses, and six of them are chapter 1 said again. That is the point. **The prologue's second
half is built as a near-copy of its first, so that everything the author changed is visible against
a fixed background** — and what he changed is four things: a sentence God adds, a claim the accuser
substitutes, a limit moved one notch inward, and a voice that has not spoken before.

**The repetition is exact enough to be measured.** Verse 1 is 1:6 with one addition; verse 2 is 1:7
word for word; verse 3a is 1:8 word for word. Then the copying stops. *"He still maintains his
integrity, although you incited me against him, to ruin him without cause"* has no counterpart in
chapter 1, because in chapter 1 there was nothing yet to say it about.

**God's addition is the hardest sentence in the book, and it has three parts.** *He still maintains
his integrity* — God declares the first test passed, which means **the verdict on Job's piety is
already in before the second test is proposed**, and the second test is therefore not needed to
settle anything. *You incited me against him* — the verb is ***hēsît***, to incite, entice, stir up
against, a verb whose other objects in Scripture are almost uniformly evil: the wife or friend who
entices to serve other gods (Deuteronomy 13:6), the Assyrian field-commander enticing Jerusalem to
surrender (2 Kings 18:32), and — in the single closest parallel — ***śāṭān*** who *"stood up against
Israel and incited David"* at 1 Chronicles 21:1, against Yahweh's anger doing the identical job at
2 Samuel 24:1. **God uses of himself the verb the Bible uses of seduction.** And *to ruin him
without cause* — ***ḥinnām***, the accuser's own word from 1:9, handed back across the same room.
See [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]] for the first half of that exchange.

**God concedes the incitement and does not disown the act.** The whole sentence says: you moved me,
it was mine, and it was for nothing. Scripture nowhere else has God describe his own action in these
terms, and the book makes no attempt to soften it — not here, not in the whirlwind, and not in the
epilogue, which drops the accuser entirely and calls the whole thing *"all the evil that Yahweh had
brought on him"* (42:11).

**The accuser does not concede, and does not repeat himself either.** His claim in chapter 1 was
that Job's fear of God was a purchase — *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* His claim here is
different: *"Skin for skin. Yes, all that a man has he will give for his life."* **That is not the
same argument, and it is a smaller one.** The first said the category of disinterested love of God
is empty. The second says only that a man values his own body above his possessions — a
proposition nobody would dispute, and one that concedes, in passing, that Job did not break when
his possessions went. **The accuser loses the first round and never says so; he substitutes a
second claim and proceeds as if it had been the claim all along.** It is the move of a prosecutor
who has lost a count and amends the indictment, and the book lets it pass without comment.

**The limit moves in, using the same words both times.** At 1:12 everything Job has was *"in your
power"* — in your hand — with the man excepted. At 2:6 the man is *"in your hand"*, with his *nepeš*
excepted. The accuser's request also escalates in the body's own vocabulary: *"touch his bone and
his flesh"* (2:5). ***ʿEṣem ûbāśār***, bone and flesh, is elsewhere the idiom of kinship — Adam's
*"bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh"* at Genesis 2:23, Laban's *"you are my bone and my flesh"*
at Genesis 29:14. **Used of a man's own body it names the last thing that is his**, and that is
exactly what the hedge now encloses: not the herds, not the children, not the skin, but the breath.

**The execution takes one verse.** Chapter 1 gave ten verses and four messengers to the first
disaster; the second gets *"So Satan went out from the presence of Yahweh, and struck Job with
painful sores from the sole of his foot to his head"* and nothing else. There are no agents this
time — no Sabeans, no fire, no Chaldeans, no wind — and no messenger, because there is nobody to be
told. **The accuser strikes with his own hand, and then walks out of the Bible's oldest treatment of
him and is never mentioned again by anyone in the book.**

**The disease is a covenant curse, and that is the whole engine of the following forty chapters.**
*Šəḥîn rāʿ*, a malignant boil, *"from the sole of his foot to his head"* — and Deuteronomy 28:35
threatens the disobedient with *"a sore boil, of which you can't be healed, from the sole of your
foot to the crown of your head."* **The same disease, the same two anatomical limits, the same
phrase.** *Šəḥîn* is also the sixth plague on Egypt (Exodus 9:8–11) and the eruption the priest
inspects for uncleanness at Leviticus 13:18–23. **Job is struck with the visible, diagnosable
signature of a man under judgment**, which is precisely why three educated friends will spend
twenty-five chapters constructing the sin it must be evidence of. They will read the symptom
correctly by every rule they have and arrive at a false verdict. See
[[The Boil of Deuteronomy 28 on a Blameless Man]].

**And then the potsherd and the ashes.** *"He took for himself a potsherd to scrape himself with,
and he sat among the ashes"* (2:8). A *ḥereś* is a shard of broken pot — the cheapest object in the
ancient world, what is left when a vessel is finished. **The greatest of all the children of the
east is reduced, in one clause, to the tool a beggar would use.** The ashes are *ʾēper*, the
standing substance of public mourning, and they will be the last word Job speaks in the book:
*"I repent in dust and ashes"* (42:6). **He begins his suffering in the same material he ends it in,
and the meaning is not the same twice.** See [[Sitting in Dust and Ashes]].

**His wife gets one sentence, and it is the most carefully constructed sentence in the prologue.**
*"Do you still maintain your integrity? Renounce God, and die."* Two things are happening in it at
once, and both are quotations.

She is quoting **God**: *ʿōdəkā maḥăzîq bətummātekā* — *you still maintain your integrity* — is
2:3's *ʿōdennû maḥăzîq bətummātô* with the pronoun changed from third person to second. **The only
two speakers in the book who use this phrase are Yahweh and Job's wife**, and what God says as
commendation she says as an objection. And she is completing **the accuser's** sentence: he
predicted twice, at 1:11 and 2:5, that Job would *bārak* God to his face; she says *bārēk ʾĕlōhîm*,
the imperative of that verb, the scribal euphemism for its opposite discussed at [[Job of Uz]].
**The accuser's prediction is spoken aloud in the story, in his own vocabulary, by the last person
left who loves Job — and the accuser has already left and will never know.** See
[[Renounce God and Die - The Euphemism and the Accuser's Prediction]].

**Job's answer refuses the speech and not the woman, and the distinction is in the Hebrew.** He does
not say *you are a foolish woman*. He says *you speak as one of the foolish women would speak* —
*kədabbēr ʾaḥat hannəbālôt* — putting the whole weight on the utterance. ***Nābāl*** is not
stupidity; it is moral senselessness, the word of the *nəbālâ* done to [[Dinah]] at Genesis 34:7,
of Nabal at 1 Samuel 25, of the fool who says in his heart there is no God at Psalm 14:1. **He
convicts the sentence of it and stops there.**

**And then he answers her in the first person plural.** *"Shall **we** receive good at the hand of
God, and shall **we** not receive evil?"* She has lost the same ten children he has; his theology is
offered as jointly held, not handed down. The verb ***qibbēl*** — to receive, to accept — is a rare,
late, Aramaizing word in Biblical Hebrew, and it is one of the small pieces of evidence in the long
argument about when this book was written. The substance of the line is the book's spine:
**both good and *raʿ*, calamity, come from one hand**, which is what Isaiah 45:7, Lamentations 3:38
and Amos 3:6 will say flatly and what most religion exists to avoid saying. See
[[Shall We Receive Good and Not Evil - Weal and Woe from One Hand]].

**The narrator's verdict comes back one degree narrower.** At 1:22 it was *"Job did not sin, nor
charge God with wrongdoing."* Here it is *"In all this Job didn't sin **with his lips**."*
*Biśpātāyw.* Whether the qualifier is prose variation or a deliberate reservation is one of the
oldest live questions in the book's interpretation, and the Targum to Job settles it in one
direction by adding a gloss the Hebrew does not have — *but in his thoughts he already harboured
words*. **Chapter 3 begins eleven verses later.**

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — the last thing he says before chapter 3, and the position he never afterwards
  abandons even while saying the opposite kind of thing for twenty-eight chapters. **Note what the
  scene takes away that chapter 1 left him.** In 1:20 he could stand, tear his robe, and worship; a
  full mourning rite requires a body that works. Here he can scrape, and sit. The book has moved him
  from a man performing grief to a man who is a symptom.
- [[Job's Wife]] — unnamed, four Hebrew words, and the entire Christian and Jewish interpretive
  tradition built on them. She is the only human being in the book who addresses Job before the
  friends arrive, the only one who tells him to stop, and the only other person who lost all ten
  children. Her page sets out the hostile reading (Augustine's *diaboli adiutrix*), the sympathetic
  reading, and the Septuagint's six-verse expansion that changes her character completely.
- [[Satan]] — *haśśāṭān*, in his **last appearance in the book and in the Hebrew Bible's earliest
  extended treatment of him**. He arrives, is asked the same question, gives the same answer, hears
  that he was wrong, changes his claim without conceding, is granted a second permission, strikes
  the man himself, and leaves at the end of verse 7. **No character ever learns he existed**, and
  the epilogue that rebukes the friends and vindicates Job does not mention him.
- [[God]] — who volunteers Job's name a second time, announces that Job has held, says he was
  *incited*, calls the ruin *ḥinnām*, and then authorises the next round in the following breath.
  **This is the most exposed God in the Old Testament**: he claims the act, refuses the excuse
  available to him, and does it in a scene no one on earth will ever be shown.
- [[The Sons of God]] — *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, present at 2:1 as at 1:6 and silent again. Their second
  and last appearance in the prose frame; they turn up once more at 38:7, shouting for joy at the
  founding of the earth.
- [[Job's Children]] — dead before this scene opens and not mentioned in it, which is itself worth
  noticing: the loss that the book will keep returning to is not what the wife's line is about, or
  not obviously, and Job's *"shall we receive"* includes her in a grief the text otherwise records
  only from his side.

## Places

- [[Uz]] — unnamed here, as in 1:13–22. The ashes Job sits *among* are the only physical detail the
  scene gives, and the Septuagint alters even that, placing him *"on the dung-heap outside the
  city"* — the source of the dunghill of Christian iconography, and of a civic exclusion the Hebrew
  does not describe.
- **Heaven** — no page, as at 1:6. It is *"the presence of Yahweh"*, a place one can come *"among"*
  others in and *"go out from"*, and the text supplies nothing else. See [[The Divine Council]].

## Themes

- [[Integrity]] — the theme's defining verse, twice. ***Tummâ*** appears at 2:3 in God's mouth and
  at 2:9 in his wife's, and Job will make it his oath-word at 27:5: *"until I die I will not put
  away my integrity from me."* **It is not moral perfection; it is the wholeness of a thing that is
  all of one piece** — the same root as the *tām* of Job 1:1 and the *tāmîm* of Noah at Genesis 6:9.
  What the second test attacks is precisely the seam between what Job says and what he has left.
- [[Testing]] — the second instalment, and the one that exposes the structure. **A test whose result
  is announced at 2:3 and then run again at 2:6 is not gathering evidence in any ordinary sense**,
  and the book does not explain why the first result was insufficient. What changes is only the
  claim being tested, and the accuser changed that himself.
- [[Suffering]] — the wiki's central case moves from loss to pain. Everything taken in chapter 1 was
  *outside* Job; from 2:7 the affliction is in his body, permanent, visible, and — under the only
  diagnostic anyone in the book has — legible as guilt.
- [[The Fear of God]] — still the thing at issue, though the accuser has quietly stopped asking
  about it. 1:9 asked what Job's fear of God was *for*; 2:4 asks only what a man will trade. **The
  second question is answerable and the first one is not, which is why the first one is the book.**
- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine arrives in this passage as a *disease*. The friends have not yet
  spoken and their case is already made for them by Deuteronomy 28's curse list, written on a man
  God has three times called blameless. See
  [[The Boil of Deuteronomy 28 on a Blameless Man]].
- [[Righteousness]] — certified a third and fourth time: 2:3 repeats 1:8 word for word and then adds
  that the verdict has now been *demonstrated*. **Four certifications, none of them audible to
  anyone on earth.**
- [[Providence]] — *"shall we not receive evil?"* is the flattest statement in Scripture that *raʿ*
  comes from God's hand, and the narrator certifies it as not a sin in the next clause. Beside it
  stands God's own *"you incited me"*, which complicates the agency without relieving him of it.
  **The passage holds both and reconciles neither.**
- [[Marriage]] — the Bible's bleakest marital exchange, and the only conversation between a husband
  and wife in the book. He refuses her counsel and answers in the plural; she is not mentioned again
  until 19:17, where *"my breath is offensive to my wife"*, and is absent from the restoration at
  42:10–17, which gives Job ten more children and does not say by whom.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the passage turns twice on the verb *bārak* meaning its opposite, once
  on the difference between calling a woman foolish and calling her speech foolish, and finally on
  the narrator's decision to acquit Job's *lips* specifically.
- [[Sin]] — 2:10's verdict, narrowed. The wiki notes the narrowing and does not resolve it, because
  the two readings — prose variation, or a door left open for chapter 3 — are both defensible and
  the text decides neither.
- [[Life and Death]] — *nepeš* at 2:6, the thing the hedge now protects, and *wāmūt* at 2:9, the
  thing Job's wife recommends. **In four words she offers him the one exit the accuser is forbidden
  to take**, and the book does not say whether she knows that is what she is doing.
- [[Judgment]] — the court reconvenes with the same composition as before: a bench, a prosecutor,
  and an absent defendant with no counsel. [[The Witness in Heaven]] traces what Job starts asking
  for once he is allowed to speak.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — *šəḥîn* is the eruption of Leviticus 13:18–23, which the priest examines
  and may declare unclean. **The book of Job has no priest, no law, and no Israel**, so the
  contamination Job carries is social rather than cultic — and the friends, arriving at 2:12, will
  not recognise him.
- [[Blessing]] — the fourth and fifth *bārak* of the prologue, both euphemisms: the accuser's
  prediction at 2:5 and the wife's imperative at 2:9. The word has now meant its opposite three
  times and itself twice, inside two chapters.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — *"all that a man has he will give for his life"* is a valuation, and
  it is the accuser's answer to 1:21. He grants that Job surrendered the inventory and argues that
  the inventory was never the price.

## Notes

- **Why a second test at all?** The strongest reading is that the accuser's first claim has failed
  and he knows it. **His second claim is not a restatement but a retreat**, and the retreat is
  disguised by a proverb. *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* is a claim about whether disinterested
  piety exists; *"all that a man has he will give for his life"* is a claim about self-preservation
  that almost any reader would grant. God's answer is to allow the smaller test anyway, which has
  the effect of making the demonstration exhaustive: **there is nothing left to take that would not
  kill him**, and the hedge at 2:6 is placed exactly there. After 2:7 the accuser has no further
  proposal available, and he never makes one.

- **"You incited me against him" (2:3).** Four things follow from this clause and they should be
  kept apart. (1) **God says the initiative came from the accuser** — which is at odds with 1:8,
  where God raises Job's name first, and the text does not reconcile them. (2) **God does not say
  the accuser did it.** The verb of ruin is passive to the incitement but the actor is God: *you
  incited me… to ruin him*. (3) **God calls it ruin.** *Ballaʿ*, to swallow up, engulf — not
  *discipline*, not *refine*, not *test*. (4) **God calls it *ḥinnām*.** The one word that would
  have justified it — *cause* — is the word God explicitly withholds from his own action. Readers
  who want the book to supply a theodicy have to get past a sentence in which God declines to offer
  one about himself.

- **"Skin for skin" (2:4).** *ʿÔr bəʿad-ʿôr*, and nobody knows what it means. Three proposals:
  a trader's formula for an even exchange, hide for hide; a proverb about surrendering someone
  else's skin to save one's own (on which reading the accuser is saying Job traded his children for
  his own safety, which is vicious and which nothing in the text supports); and a physical image,
  one layer of hide given up to shield the next, as an arm is raised to protect the head. **The
  gloss in the same verse runs the same way under all three** — *"all that a man has he will give
  for his life"* — so the argument does not turn on the idiom. What the idiom does supply is tone:
  it is a market saying, and the accuser uses it about a man's dead children.

- **The boil, and why it matters more than the herds.** *Šəḥîn rāʿ* is a specific, named, diagnosable
  affliction with a place in Israel's law and in its curse formulae. Chapter 1's disasters were
  catastrophes; chapter 2's is a **verdict written on a body**, and the difference is what makes the
  dialogue possible. A man who has lost his livestock can be pitied. A man covered in the boil of
  Deuteronomy 28 must be explained, and every explanation available to the men who arrive at 2:11
  requires a sin. **The friends do not invent their doctrine; they read it off his skin.**

- **The potsherd.** *Ḥereś*. The same word as the shards of Isaiah 30:14 and Psalm 22:15's *"my
  strength is dried up like a potsherd"*. It is debris, and Job's use of it is the only self-care in
  the book. It is worth resisting the pious reading here: **scraping a suppurating skin disease with
  a piece of broken pot is not a symbol, it is what a man with no ointment and no servants does.**

- **"Among the ashes" — or on the ash-heap?** The Hebrew *bətôk-hāʾēper* means among or in the midst
  of the ashes, and *ʾēper* is the substance of mourning (Esther 4:1–3, Jeremiah 6:26, Ezekiel
  27:30, Jonah 3:6). The Septuagint reads *epi tēs koprias exō tēs poleōs* — **on the dung-heap
  outside the city** — which is a different picture: not a mourner in ashes but an outcast at the
  village midden. Christian art, the *Testament of Job*, and the English idiom all descend from the
  Greek. **The wiki follows the Hebrew and records the Greek**, and notes that the Greek's reading
  is what makes the friends' failure to recognise him at 2:12 a matter of location as well as
  disfigurement.

- **Job's wife: what the text says and what has been said about her.** The text gives her one
  sentence and never records a reply from Job beyond 2:10, never names her, and never brings her
  back except at 19:17. Around that silence sits a very large tradition, almost all of it hostile:
  Augustine's *diaboli adiutrix*, the devil's helper; Chrysostom's view that the accuser spared her
  deliberately as his most effective instrument; Calvin's *organum Satanae*. The **Septuagint gives
  her a six-verse speech** in place of the Hebrew's one line — she describes wandering from house to
  house as a servant, waiting for the sun to set, and her children *"the fruits of my womb, for whom
  I toiled with hardships in vain"* — which transforms her from an instrument into a second victim
  and is very likely a translator's attempt to make her intelligible. **The Hebrew supports two
  readings and closes neither.** She is voicing the accuser's line, in the accuser's vocabulary; and
  she is the only other person alive who buried those ten children, watching a man she cannot help
  hold on to a *tummâ* that is doing nothing for him except keeping him in agony. The wiki states
  both and declines to adjudicate; see [[Job's Wife]].

- **What Job does not say to her.** He does not deny the loss, does not defend God's justice, does
  not tell her she is wrong about the facts, and does not tell her to be quiet. He says her sentence
  belongs to a class of sentence, asks one question, and stops. **It is also the only time in the
  book that Job answers an argument without heat**, which is worth remembering when chapters 3–31
  are read as evidence of his temperament.

- **"Didn't sin with his lips" (2:10).** Three positions. (1) **Stylistic** — the narrator varies
  1:22's fuller formula and means the same thing; Hebrew prose does this constantly. (2) **A
  deliberate reservation** — the narrator acquits the speech and says nothing about the man,
  preparing for chapter 3, where Job says a great deal he will later be asked about. The **Targum to
  Job** takes this line and makes it explicit, glossing *but in his thoughts he already harboured
  words*. (3) **A judgment about the whole prose frame** — that the narrator is marking the end of
  the *patient* Job of tradition, the Job of James 5:11, and that from chapter 3 the book is doing
  something else entirely. **The wiki does not choose**, but notes that whichever is right, 42:7
  commends the man who speaks in chapters 3–31 and not the one who speaks here.

- **Where the prologue has got to.** Two councils, two permissions, two limits, two certifications,
  and one man on an ash-heap. **Every fact necessary to settle the argument of the next forty
  chapters is now in the reader's hands, and none of it is in anyone else's.** The next three men to
  arrive will do the best work available to honest people reasoning from evidence, and they will be
  wrong, and God will tell them so. That gap — between what the reader was shown at 1:6 and 2:1 and
  what the characters can possibly know — **is not a flaw in the book's construction; it is the
  book.**
