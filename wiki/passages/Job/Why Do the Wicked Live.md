# Why Do the Wicked Live

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 21:1–34

## Summary

The second cycle ends with the only speech in the dialogue in which Job argues the friends' subject
instead of his own. For three chapters [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] (15:20–35),
[[Bildad the Shuhite]] (18:5–21) and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] (20:4–29) have painted the same
picture — the wicked man terrified, childless, burned out, choking on his gain — and none of them
has answered a word of what Job said in chapters 16, 17 or 19. Here Job stops talking about
himself, takes their picture, and checks it against the world. **It is the first speech of the
book that is an argument in the ordinary sense**: a thesis, evidence, an objection anticipated and
met, a witness called, and a conclusion. And it is spoken to the friends from the first verse to
the last — like chapter 19 it contains no prayer, and unlike chapter 19 it contains no hope. Job
does not address God once.

**The opening (21:2–6) asks for the one consolation they have not offered: listening.** *"Listen
diligently to my speech. Let this be your consolation."* The word is *tanḥûmōtêkem*, *your
consolations*, and it is pointed. Eliphaz opened the cycle asking *"Are the consolations
(tanḥumôt) of God too small for you?"* (15:11); the friends came, in the first place, *"to comfort
him"* (2:11). Job now redefines the word: their silence would be the comfort. The speech ends on the
same root — *"So how can you comfort me (tənaḥămûnî) with nonsense?"* (21:34) — so the whole chapter
is bracketed by the thing the comforters were sent to do and have not done. See
[[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].

*"After I have spoken, mock on"* (21:3) is singular in the Hebrew, *talʿîg*, and the likeliest
target is Zophar, who had accused Job of mocking (*wattilʿag*, 11:3) and has just finished speaking.
*"Is my complaint to man?"* (21:4) — his quarrel is not with them, which is why he can afford to be
impatient with them. Then **a gesture he will make himself at the end of the book**: *"Look at me,
and be astonished. Lay your hand on your mouth"* (21:5). The friends were silent for seven days
once (2:13); Job asks them to resume. At 40:4, before the whirlwind, it is Job who says *"I lay my
hand on my mouth"*. See [[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].

And verse 6 tells us this is not a debating point for him: *"When I remember, I am troubled. Horror
takes hold of my flesh."* What he is about to say frightens the man saying it. A world in which the
wicked do well is not a gotcha against the friends; it is a world Job has to live in.

**The thesis (21:7–13) is the friends' three portraits, inverted line by line.**

| the friends | Job 21 |
|---|---|
| *"the triumphing of the wicked is short"* (20:5) | *"Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow mighty in power?"* (21:7) |
| *"neither son nor grandson among his people"* (18:19); *"his children shall seek the favor of the poor"* (20:10) | *"Their child is established with them in their sight, their offspring before their eyes"* (21:8) |
| *"A sound of terrors is in his ears"* (15:21); *"Terrors shall make him afraid on every side"* (18:11) | *"Their houses are safe from fear"* (21:9) |
| *"fire shall consume … that which is left in his tent"* (20:26); *"brimstone shall be scattered on his habitation"* (18:15) | *"neither is the rod of God upon them"* (21:9) |
| the joy of the godless *"but for a moment"* — *ʿădê-rāgaʿ* (20:5) | *"In an instant (bəregaʿ) they go down to Sheol"* (21:13) |

The last row is the sharpest. Zophar granted the wicked their joy *"for a moment"*; Job takes his
word and moves it. The moment is not the joy, it is the dying — a quick, painless descent into
[[Sheol]] at the end of a long good life. That is precisely what Job, who is dying slowly, has been
denied.

And the evidence Job chooses is **his own former life, handed to the wicked.** Their child
*established in their sight* (21:8) — his ten were killed together (1:18–19). Their *rod of God*
absent (21:9) — he begged God to *"take his rod away from me"* (9:34). *"Their bulls breed without
fail. Their cows calve, and don't miscarry"* (21:10) — the verb is *šākal*, and the sentence is the
covenant blessing of Exodus 23:26, *"No one will miscarry or be barren in your land"*, enjoyed by
men who have refused the covenant's God; Job's herds were driven off and burned (1:14–17). *"They
send out their little ones like a flock. Their children dance"* (21:11) — the *little ones*,
*ʿăwîlîm*, are the word for the children who despised Job two chapters ago (19:18), and Job's own
children died at a feast (1:13, 18). *"They sing to the tambourine and harp, and rejoice at the
sound of the pipe"* (21:12) — and at 30:31 he will say *"my harp has turned to mourning, and my pipe
into the voice of those who weep."*

The *harp and pipe*, *kinnôr* and *ʿûgāb*, are the pair of Genesis 4:21, where [[Jubal]] is *"the
father of all who handle the harp and pipe"* — in the line of Cain. Job does not make the
allusion and probably does not intend it, but the reader of the canon hears it: the music of the
prosperous godless is the music of Cain's city, and nothing has silenced it. See
[[The Line of Cain and the Birth of Seth]].

**Then the wicked speak (21:14–16), and what they say is the accuser's question.** *"They tell God,
'Depart from us, for we don't want to know about your ways. What is the Almighty, that we should
serve him? What profit should we have, if we pray to him?'"* The *profit* question (*mah-nnôʿîl*) is
the one the accuser asked in heaven: *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* (1:9). The accuser assumed
piety is paid; the wicked of chapter 21 have done the sum and concluded it is not worth it — and
they are thriving. Malachi 3:14 hears the same sentence in Israel: *"It is vain to serve God. What
profit is it?"*

There is a darker irony under *"Depart from us"*. Job has asked God to go away more than once — *"Let
me alone"* (7:16), *"Cease then. Leave me alone"* (10:20), *"Look away from him, that he may rest"*
(14:6) — and God has not gone. The wicked say it and are left in peace. The prayer Job could not get
answered is answered for them.

**16b is a disclaimer, and it will be stolen.** *"The counsel of the wicked is far from me."* Job
describes them; he does not join them. At 22:18 Eliphaz will quote the whole clause back word for
word — *waʿăṣat rəšāʿîm rāḥăqâ mennî* — after quoting 21:14's *"Depart from us"* at 22:17. The next
speech in the book is built partly out of this one.

**The count (21:17–18) takes Bildad's proverb and asks for its frequency.** *"How often is it that
the lamp of the wicked is put out, that their calamity comes on them?"* Every phrase is Bildad's:
*"the light of the wicked shall be put out … his lamp above him shall be put out"* (18:5–6, the same
verb, *dāʿak*), *"calamity shall be ready at his side"* (18:12, the same *ʾêd*). The stubble before
the wind and the chaff the storm carries away (21:18) are Psalm 1:4's portion for the wicked. Job is
not denying that the proverbs exist. He is asking how often the world bears them out, and the answer
the question expects is *not often enough to read a man's life by*. See
[[The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out]].

**The objection (21:19–21) is the friends' last refuge, and Job answers it as a moral claim.** *"You
say, 'God lays up his iniquity for his children.'"* This is where the doctrine retreats when the
wicked man himself dies at peace: the bill is passed to his sons — and the friends have each
gestured at it (5:4; 18:19; 20:10). Job will not have it. *"Let him recompense it to himself, that he
may know it. Let his own eyes see his destruction. Let him drink of the wrath of the Almighty. For
what does he care for his house after him, when the number of his months is cut off?"* A dead man
does not feel what happens to his heirs — Job said so of every man at 14:21 — so punishing the
children punishes the wrong person and does not touch the right one. **It is Ezekiel 18's argument,
from the mouth of a man whose children were killed as part of a test of their father.** See
[[Visiting the Iniquity of the Fathers - Job 21.19, Jeremiah 31, and Ezekiel 18]], and for the cup of
21:20, [[The Vine and the Cup]].

**21:22 anticipates the reply.** *"Shall any teach God knowledge, since he judges those who are
high?"* The friends will say that Job is presuming to instruct God. Job turns it: it is their
doctrine that dictates to God what he must do and when, and it is the world God actually governs
that contradicts them. The question is Isaiah 40:14's — *"Who taught him knowledge?"* — and Paul's at
Romans 11:34, one verse before he quotes Job 41:11.

**The two deaths (21:23–26) are the speech's center, and Job is the second man.** *"One dies in his
full strength, being wholly at ease and quiet. His pails are full of milk. The marrow of his bones is
moistened. Another dies in bitterness of soul, and never tastes of good."* *Bitterness of soul*,
*nepeš mārâ*, is Job's own phrase for himself, three times over (3:20; 7:11; 10:1). And *"in his
full strength"* is ***bəʿeṣem tummô***, *in the very bone of his completeness* — *tōm*, the root of
*tām*, *blameless*, the first word the book says about Job (1:1) and the root of the *integrity* his
wife told him to let go of (2:9). The wicked man dies *whole*; the whole man is dying in bitterness.
The two are then buried together: *"They lie down alike in the dust. The worm covers them."* Death
does not distinguish them, and it is the only thing in the speech that treats them equally.

**The witnesses (21:27–33) are travellers.** *"Behold, I know your thoughts"* — Job knows what the
portraits were for. *"For you say, 'Where is the house of the prince? Where is the tent in which the
wicked lived?'"* — Bildad's closing *"the tent of the wicked shall be no more"* (8:22) and *"such are
the dwellings of the unrighteous"* (18:21), with the *prince*, *nādîb*, as a sneer at their
real target: Job, the greatest man of the east, whose house is gone. Against the friends'
witnesses — the fathers, the ancients, the *"gray-headed and the very aged"* (8:8; 15:10, 18; 20:4)
— Job calls the *"wayfaring men"*, *ʿôbrê dārek*, people who have been somewhere. The friends'
authority is depth of time; Job's is breadth of observation.

What the travellers report (21:30) is contested; see the Notes. What follows is not: *"Who shall
declare his way to his face?"* (21:31). Nobody tells the powerful man the truth about himself, and
the phrase is Job's own from 13:15 — *"I will maintain my ways before him"*, *dərākay ʾel-pānāyw*.
Job is begging to have his ways argued to God's face; the wicked man never has his said to his own.

**And then the funeral (21:32–33).** *"Yet he will be borne to the grave. Men shall keep watch over
the tomb. The clods of the valley shall be sweet to him. All men shall draw after him, as there were
innumerable before him."* The word for the tomb is ***gādîš***, and the reader of the first cycle
has seen it before. It is Eliphaz's word at 5:26, his promise of the good death to a Job who would
accept correction: *"You shall come to your grave in a full age, like a shock of grain (gādîš) comes
in its season."* The shock of grain that Eliphaz promised the righteous is the grave-mound over the
wicked, honoured with a vigil, followed by a crowd. See
[[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]].

**The verdict (21:34)**: *"So how can you comfort me with nonsense, because in your answers there
remains only falsehood?"* *Nonsense* is ***hebel***, breath — Ecclesiastes' word — and *falsehood*
is ***maʿal***, the word for a breach of faith: the trespass against holy things of Leviticus 5:15,
Achan's sin at Jericho (Joshua 7:1). Job is not saying the friends are mistaken. He is saying their
answers are an act of unfaithfulness — to him, and, as 13:7–10 already warned and 42:7 will confirm,
to God.

Nobody answers chapter 21. Eliphaz's third speech opens on a different subject entirely — whether
Job's righteousness profits God (22:2–3) — and then simply invents the crimes the doctrine needs
(22:6–9). Bildad's third is six verses on the greatness of God. Zophar never speaks again.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — arguing, for once, about someone other than himself, and still describing
  himself throughout: everything the wicked enjoy in 21:7–13 is something he has lost, and the man
  who *"dies in bitterness of soul"* (21:25) speaks in his own phrase. He does not address God in
  this speech at all; of his replies so far, only chapter 19 is as wholly turned to the friends.
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the speaker Job answers first: the singular *"mock on"* (21:3) turns
  Zophar's accusation of 11:3 back on him, 21:7 denies 20:5's *"short"*, and 21:13's *"in an
  instant"* takes Zophar's own word for the moment of the wicked's joy. Zophar never replies.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — whose lamp (18:5–6), calamity (18:12), childlessness (18:19) and tent
  (8:22; 18:21) are each quoted and put to a count.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — whose *"consolations of God"* (15:11) are redefined at 21:2, whose
  terrors (15:21) do not arrive, and whose *gādîš* of 5:26 becomes the wicked man's tomb at 21:32. He
  answers in chapter 22 by quoting 21:14 and 21:16 back.
- [[God]] — spoken about and never spoken to. He is the one whose rod is not on the wicked (21:9),
  whom the wicked tell to depart (21:14), who is said to *distribute sorrows in his anger* less often
  than the friends claim (21:17), and who *"judges those who are high"* (21:22). The question of why
  he lets the wicked live is put to the friends rather than to him.
- [[Job's Children]] — not named, and present in every line of 21:8–12: the children *established
  in their sight*, the little ones sent out like a flock, the dancing at a feast.
- [[Jubal]] — not named; his *harp and pipe* (Genesis 4:21) are the instruments of 21:12.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — *"In an instant they go down to Sheol"* (21:13). For Job the descent has been a long
  approach — the gates of Sheol in 17:16, the pit called *father* in 17:14 — and for the wicked it is
  a step taken without pain at the end of a full life.

The *"clods of the valley"* (21:33), *rigbê naḥal*, put the grave in a wadi bottom — the soft earth of
a valley floor where a burial could actually be dug. No named place.

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — attacked frontally, and for the first time on its own ground. Job does not argue
  that he is innocent here; he argues that the doctrine's predictions fail on the wicked, which is
  testable, where its predictions on the righteous were not.
- [[Suffering]] — by contrast: the speech is a portrait of the life Job has lost, assigned to men
  who renounced God.
- [[Life and Death]] — two deaths side by side (21:23–26), one whole and one bitter, and then the
  same dust and the same worm. Death is the only thing in the chapter that treats the two men alike.
- [[Burial and the Grave]] — the wicked man's honoured burial: carried, watched, followed by a
  procession, the clods sweet to him (21:32–33). The good funeral is the detail the friends' doctrine
  cannot absorb.
- [[Judgment]] — *"Shall any teach God knowledge, since he judges those who are high?"* (21:22), and
  the disputed 21:30, where judgment is either *reserved for* the wicked or something they are
  *spared from*.
- [[Wisdom]] — two epistemologies set against each other: the friends' tradition from the ancients,
  Job's report from the *"wayfaring men"* (21:29).
- [[The Fear of God]] — its negation spoken aloud: *"What is the Almighty, that we should serve him?
  What profit should we have?"* (21:15), the accuser's *"for nothing"* (1:9) answered by men who
  decided it was not worth it.
- [[Prayer]] — *"if we pray to him"* (21:15): prayer measured by its yield and judged unprofitable;
  and *"Depart from us"* (21:14), the prayer Job has prayed (7:16; 10:20; 14:6) and the wicked are
  granted.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — herds that breed without loss, houses safe, pails full of milk: the
  covenant blessings of Exodus 23:26 and Deuteronomy 28:4 enjoyed outside the covenant.
- [[Blessing]] — misdirected, apparently: the blessings Deuteronomy promises to obedience on the men
  of 21:14.
- [[Sin]] — and its inheritance: whether *"God lays up his iniquity for his children"* (21:19).
- [[Integrity]] — *bəʿeṣem tummô*, *in his very completeness* (21:23): the root of Job's *tām* and
  *tummâ*, used of a wicked man's bodily wholeness at death.
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — music that will turn to mourning for Job (21:12; 30:31), and a funeral
  for the wicked that is attended (21:33) where Job's was held over a living man (2:12–13).
- [[Language and Speech]] — the hand laid on the mouth (21:5), the opponents' words quoted back
  (21:19, 28), and the friends' answers named *hebel* and *maʿal* (21:34).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"in their sight … before their eyes"* (21:8); *"Let his own eyes see
  his destruction"* (21:20); *"Look at me, and be astonished"* (21:5).
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the lamp of the wicked, and how seldom it goes out (21:17).

## Notes

- **The reading of 21:30 — *reserved for* or *spared from*.** The WEB follows the KJV: *"the evil man
  is reserved to the day of calamity, that they are led out to the day of wrath."* Read that way, the
  travellers testify that judgment is postponed, not cancelled — a concession to the friends, and a
  sentence 2 Peter 2:9 could have been written from (*"to keep the unrighteous under punishment for
  the day of judgment"*). Most modern translations (RSV, NIV, ESV, NJPS) read the opposite: *"the evil
  man is spared in the day of calamity, he is rescued in the day of wrath."* The Hebrew can bear
  both. *Ḥāśak* means *to hold back*, and *ləyôm ʾêd* can mean *for* the day or *in* the day; Job
  38:23 uses the same verb for hail God has *"reserved against the time of trouble"*, while 33:18 uses
  it for a soul *kept back from* the pit. **This page reads it as *spared***, on the strength of
  context: 21:29 calls the travellers as Job's witnesses, and 21:31–33 describe a wicked man nobody
  confronts and everybody mourns. A witness who confirmed the friends' doctrine would be a strange
  witness for Job to call. The WEB's reading is recorded, not overruled, and it is the one that later
  Christian reading made most of.

- **The speech's address.** Every second-person form in chapter 21 is plural, except 21:3b's
  *talʿîg* — and the plural is the friends, not God. That makes it, with chapter 19, the least
  God-directed of Job's speeches, and the reason is structural rather than devotional: this is the one
  place Job meets the friends on their own question. Every earlier reply turned from them to God
  because their question was not the one he was asking. When he finally takes up their question, he
  has no reason to turn.

- **What Job does *not* claim.** He does not say the wicked are never punished. *"How often"*
  (21:17) is a question about frequency, and it concedes that the lamp sometimes goes out. Nor does
  he say God is unjust here, as he did at 9:22–24. The claim is narrower and more damaging: **the
  world does not sort people visibly enough for anyone to read a man's standing from his
  circumstances.** That is all Job needs. The friends' case against him was never that God judges —
  Job agrees God judges — but that his ruin *shows* his guilt, and chapter 21 removes the inference.

- **Ezekiel 18, and why 21:19 matters for Job in particular.** The friends had used Job's dead
  children against him from the start (8:4; 18:19; 20:10 by implication), and 21:19 is Job's answer
  to the generalised form of the argument: if the wicked man dies at peace and his sons pay, the
  sons are punished for what they did not do and the father feels nothing. That is a moral objection
  to transgenerational punishment as such, made by the one man in Scripture whose children's deaths
  the reader *knows* were not a punishment for anything. The prologue said Job offered burnt
  offerings for sins his children *may* have committed (1:5); the friends assumed they had; and now
  Job argues that even if a father sinned, his children's deaths would not be just payment for it.
  Deuteronomy 24:16 says the same as law, Jeremiah 31:29–30 and Ezekiel 18 as prophecy, and Jesus at
  John 9:2–3 rejects both halves of the disciples' question about the man born blind.

- **"The marrow of his bones is moistened" (21:24).** The first half of the verse is uncertain:
  *ʿăṭînāyw*, which the WEB renders *pails*, occurs nowhere else, and translations vary between
  *pails*, *loins*, *body*, and *sides*. The image is either a farmer's prosperity (pails full of milk)
  or a body still sleek with fat. The second half is clearer: bones moist with marrow are the
  opposite of the dry bones of grief and age (Proverbs 17:22, *"a crushed spirit dries up the
  bones"*), and of Job's own *"my bones are pierced in me"* (30:17). The wicked man goes to his grave
  well fed.
