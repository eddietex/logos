# Job of Uz

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 1:1–5

## Summary

Five verses of plain prose, and every one of them is a load-bearing wall. The book that will spend
forty chapters arguing about whether Job's suffering is deserved opens by settling the question
before a single character speaks — and then hands the answer to the reader alone.

**The verdict (1:1) is the narrator's, not a character's.** *"That man was blameless and upright,
and one who feared God, and turned away from evil."* Four descriptions, and the voice giving them
is the omniscient one that tells us what God said in heaven and what Job said in his tent. This is
not Job's self-assessment, which the friends could dismiss, and not yet God's, which comes at 1:8
and 2:3. It is the narrative frame itself, the same authority that tells us there was a man at
all. **Everything that goes wrong in the following forty chapters goes wrong because no one inside
the story has heard this sentence.** Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar, and Elihu argue from the evidence on
the ground; Job argues from his own conscience, which the friends treat as an interested witness.
The reader argues from verse 1, and the reader is the only one who can.

**The location (1:1) puts the book outside Israel on purpose.** Uz is east or south-east of Canaan,
in the Edomite or north-Arabian orbit; verse 3 calls Job *"the greatest of all the children of the
east"*. There is no covenant here, no law, no Sinai, no tabernacle, no priesthood, and no
Israelite ancestry. The problem the book is about is posed as a human problem before it is a
covenantal one, and it is posed in a setting that looks patriarchal — a man who is his own priest,
whose wealth is livestock, whose lifespan will run to 140 years after the story ends (42:16).
Whether that setting is historical memory or deliberate archaism, its effect is the same: nothing
in Job's case can be answered by appeal to Israel's particular history.

**The household (1:2) and the wealth (1:3) are counted in numbers that mean completeness.** Seven
sons and three daughters make ten; seven thousand sheep and three thousand camels make ten
thousand; five hundred yoke of oxen and five hundred donkeys make a thousand. Nothing is short.
The list is also a portrait of a specific kind of prosperity — camels in the thousands means
caravan trade as well as pasture, and *"a very great household"* means the servants who will be
killed alongside the animals in verse 15. When the four messengers arrive in a single afternoon,
they will work down this inventory item by item in the order it is given here.

And this is where the trap closes. **Every line of verse 3 is exactly what the doctrine of
[[Retribution]] reads as evidence.** Wealth is the visible index of God's approval; therefore its
removal is the visible index of God's disapproval; therefore the man stripped of it has been
found out. The friends will not have to invent that inference — it is the ordinary wisdom of the
world the book is set in, and much of the Old Testament says something close to it. The prologue
supplies the premises so that the poem can break the conclusion.

**The practice (1:4–5) is the most revealing thing in the unit.** Job's children hold a rotating
cycle of feasts, each in his own house, and they send for their three sisters to join them — a
detail that argues against the old reading of these feasts as debauchery, since carousing brothers
do not invite their sisters. The text says nothing critical of them at all. What it shows instead
is their father: when the round of feasting has run its course, Job *"sent and sanctified them, and
rose up early in the morning, and offered burnt offerings according to the number of them all."*

Three features of that ritual matter. It is **preemptive** — offered for sins that may not have
been committed, on the hypothesis *"It may be that my sons have sinned."* It is **for sins of the
heart** — *"and renounced God in their hearts"*, an interior offence that no observer could detect
and no ritual system is designed to catch. And it is **habitual**: *"Job did so continually"*, which
in the Hebrew is *all the days*. This is scrupulous piety at its outer limit, a man covering
offences that are hypothetical, invisible, and not his own.

Which is precisely what makes it usable. **The accuser's question in the next scene is aimed at
this verse.** *"Does Job fear God for nothing? Haven't you made a hedge around him?"* (1:9–10). A
man this careful can always be described as a man who has been well paid for his care, and the
prologue has just spent verse 3 documenting the payment. The narrator's certification and the
accuser's insinuation are drawn from the same five verses; the difference is that one of them has
been told what is in Job's heart and the other is guessing.

There is one more echo, and it is the sharpest thing in the passage. Job's fear is that his
children may have *renounced God in their hearts* — inwardly, privately, without saying it. The
accuser's wager is that Job will *"renounce you to your face"* (1:11). The same verb, twice in
seven verses, moved from the heart to the face. The father spends his mornings insuring his
children against the very act he is about to be tested for, and the test will be the public
version of the private sin he feared.

## Key verses

> **1** There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job. That man was blameless and upright,
> and one who feared God, and turned away from evil.

> **2** There were born to him seven sons and three daughters.

> **3** His possessions also were seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred yoke of
> oxen, five hundred female donkeys, and a very great household; so that this man was the greatest
> of all the children of the east.

> **4** His sons went and held a feast in the house of each one on his birthday; and they sent and
> called for their three sisters to eat and to drink with them.

> **5** It was so, when the days of their feasting had run their course, that Job sent and
> sanctified them, and rose up early in the morning, and offered burnt offerings according to the
> number of them all. For Job said, “It may be that my sons have sinned, and renounced God in their
> hearts.” Job did so continually.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — introduced here, and introduced by verdict rather than by genealogy, which is
  unusual: Genesis presents its men by descent (*"this is the history of the generations of…"*),
  and Job is presented by character. He does not speak to another human being in this unit; the one
  line of his that we get is spoken to himself (*"For Job said…"*), and it is an anxiety about his
  children's hearts. That is the whole portrait — a man whose piety runs ahead of any known
  offence.
- [[Job's Children]] — seven sons and three daughters, unnamed, appearing here as a feasting
  cycle and a reason for their father's ritual. They are the object of the first thing Job is shown
  doing and they will be the last item the messengers report in 1:18–19. The text does not accuse
  them of anything.
- [[God]] — present in this unit only as the object of Job's fear and of the hypothetical
  renunciation, and named *ʾĕlōhîm*, the generic word, rather than *Yahweh*. The covenant name
  arrives in verse 6 with the heavenly council and holds through the prose frame; the poetry will
  then almost entirely abandon it. Nothing here is addressed to God and nothing is said by him.

## Places

- [[Uz]] — the land Job comes from, named in the first clause and never described. It is outside
  Israel, and the book keeps it there.

Verse 3's *"the children of the east"* (*bənê-qedem*) is a region rather than a place: the
collective term for the tribes east of the Jordan and into the Syrian and Arabian deserts, used
elsewhere of the Midianites and Amalekites (Judges 6:3), of Solomon's rivals in wisdom (1 Kings
4:30), and of Kedar and Hazor (Jeremiah 49:28). Placing Job at the head of them is a claim about
stature, not a map reference — and, given 1 Kings 4:30, a hint that this is wisdom's home
territory.

## Themes

- [[The Fear of God]] — introduced here in its first Joban form, as one of the four terms of the
  narrator's verdict, and immediately made the book's central question: the accuser will not ask
  *whether* Job fears God but *why*. Verse 5 shows what the fear looks like in practice, which is
  what makes verse 9 answerable at all.
- [[Righteousness]] — *tām wə-yāšār*, blameless and upright, declared of a man before he is shown
  doing a single thing. This is the same narrative move Genesis 6:9 makes with Noah, and it is the
  move the friends will refuse to accept when they meet its consequences.
- [[Blessing]] — verse 3 is the fullest inventory of material blessing in the wiki so far, and the
  book is going to take all of it away and then, at 42:12, give back double. What blessing *means*
  when it can be withdrawn from a blameless man is the question the inventory exists to raise.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — the burnt offering (*ʿōlâ*) of 1:5, the wiki's second after Noah's
  altar at Genesis 8:20, and again with no command, no priest, and no law behind it. Job's is the
  first offering in Scripture made for a sin that may not have happened.
- [[Sin]] — hypothesised rather than observed, located in the heart, and named with a verb that
  means the opposite of what it does (see the note on *bārak* below).
- [[Retribution]] — not stated in this unit and structurally everywhere in it. The passage
  assembles, in five verses, exactly the correlation between righteousness and prosperity that the
  rest of the book will pull apart.
- [[Wisdom]] — *"the greatest of all the children of the east"* places Job in the territory the Old
  Testament associates with international wisdom (1 Kings 4:30), which is where a book that audits
  the wisdom tradition would want its test case to live.

## Notes

- **The four terms of 1:1.** *Tām* (blameless, whole, of a piece), *yāšār* (upright, straight),
  *yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm* (God-fearing), *sār mērāʿ* (turning away from evil). The first two are about what
  the man is, the second two about what he does and what he refuses. Note that *tām* is not *tāmîm*,
  the sacrificial word used of Noah at Genesis 6:9 and of the unblemished animal in Leviticus,
  though they are the same root and the sense is close; Job's word is the one Genesis 25:27 uses of
  Jacob as *"a quiet man"*, dwelling in tents. **None of the four means sinless.** They describe
  integrity — a life without a gap between its parts — and that distinction is what Job himself
  will insist on for thirty chapters: not that he has never sinned, but that nothing in his life
  accounts for this.

- **"Renounced God" is the verb *bless* (1:5).** The Hebrew is *bērak*, *to bless*, used here and
  at 1:11, 2:5, and 2:9 where the sense is plainly the opposite. The usual explanation is a
  scribal euphemism: the phrase *curse God* was too dangerous to write, so the antonym was
  substituted, exactly as later Jewish texts substitute *bless* for *curse* in similar contexts.
  Some argue instead for a genuine idiom of taking leave of someone — blessing as parting — which
  would give *"said goodbye to God"*. Either way the effect on the reader of the Hebrew is a
  deliberate strangeness, and it lands on the book's four hinge moments: what Job fears his
  children did, what the accuser predicts twice, and what Job's wife tells him to do. English
  versions render it *curse* (KJV, ESV, NIV) or *renounce* (WEB, NRSV); the WEB's choice is the
  softer one and slightly blunts 2:9.

- **"On his birthday" (1:4).** The Hebrew is *yôm*, *his day*, and the WEB's *"birthday"* is one
  reading of it — the same phrase is unambiguously a birthday at 3:1, where Job curses *"his day"*.
  The other reading, taken by most modern versions, is a rotation: each son hosting on his
  appointed day, so that the feasting cycles through the seven houses and comes round again, which
  fits *"when the days of their feasting had run their course"* in verse 5 better than seven
  scattered birthdays would. Nothing in the argument turns on it, but the rotation reading explains
  why Job's sacrifice is periodic rather than annual.

- **Were the children doing anything wrong?** A long interpretive tradition reads the feasts as
  dissipation and Job's sacrifices as well-founded, and Bildad will state the harshest version of
  it to Job's face at 8:4: *"If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into
  the hand of their disobedience."* The text gives no support for it. The narrator reports the
  feasts without a word of criticism, notes that the sisters were included, and frames Job's
  concern as a hypothesis (*"It may be"*) about the heart, not a response to anything seen. Read
  that way, verse 5 characterises the father rather than the children — and Bildad's use of it in
  chapter 8 becomes one more instance of the friends manufacturing a sin to fit a calamity.

- **Job as his own priest.** No altar is mentioned, no sanctuary, no officiant but Job, and no
  command from God authorising any of it. He *sanctifies* his children (*qādaš*, the verb of ritual
  preparation) and offers a whole burnt offering for each. This is the patriarchal pattern —
  Noah at Genesis 8:20, Abraham, Jacob — and one of the strongest internal arguments that the book
  is set, or means to appear set, before Sinai. It also sets up the epilogue's symmetry: the man
  who intercedes here for children who may have sinned is told at 42:8 to intercede for three
  friends who certainly have, and *"Yahweh accepted Job."* See
  [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

- **The numbers, and the doubling at 42:12.** Seven and three, seven thousand and three thousand,
  five hundred and five hundred: the inventory is stylised, and its point is sufficiency rather
  than arithmetic. At 42:12 the livestock is returned exactly doubled — fourteen thousand sheep,
  six thousand camels, a thousand yoke of oxen, a thousand donkeys — while the children are
  replaced in the original numbers, seven sons and three daughters, not fourteen and six. The
  asymmetry is usually read one of two ways: either as a quiet refusal to treat children as
  replaceable property, or, in the rabbinic reading, as a doubling after all, on the grounds that
  the first ten are not lost but waiting. Both readings agree the text is not careless here.

- **Where is Uz?** See [[Uz]]. The short answer is that the book does not say, that the two
  genealogical Uzzes point in different directions (Aramean and Edomite), and that Lamentations
  4:21 puts Edom *"in the land of Uz"*, which together with Eliphaz of Teman is why the Edomite
  location is usually preferred.

- **Prose and poetry.** Job 1:1–2:13 and 42:7–17 are prose; everything between is verse. The
  vocabulary differs too — *Yahweh* belongs almost entirely to the frame, and the poetry uses *ʾĒl*,
  *ʾĔlôah*, and *Šadday*. This has led to a long-standing proposal that an older folk tale about a
  righteous sufferer has been split open and a poetic dialogue set inside it. The wiki records that
  as a compositional theory, not a settled result; whatever its history, the book as it stands
  depends on the seam, because the prologue exists precisely to give the reader information the
  poem then withholds from everyone in it.
