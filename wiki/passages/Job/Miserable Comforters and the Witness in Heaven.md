# Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 16:1–17:16

## Summary

Job's answer to [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] contains the highest thing he has yet said and the
lowest, forty verses apart, with no reconciliation attempted between them. At 16:19 he declares
that his witness is in heaven. At 17:16 he asks whether his hope will go down with him to the
gates of Sheol. Both are in the same speech, and the speech does not resolve which is true. That
refusal is the passage's subject.

**The reply to the friends (16:1–5) is brief and contemptuous, and it turns their own claim inside
out.** *"I have heard many such things. You are all miserable comforters!"* — in Hebrew, *comforters
of trouble*, which reverses the reason they came: 2:11 says the three set out *"to sympathize with
him and to comfort him"*. They have accomplished the opposite of their stated purpose, and Job
names it. Then verse 4, which is the most quietly devastating sentence in the chapter: *"I also
could speak as you do. If your soul were in my soul's place, I could join words together against
you, and shake my head at you."* He is not claiming their arguments are unanswerable. He is
claiming they are easy — that fluent theology about someone else's catastrophe is a thing anyone
can produce, and that the only reason he is not producing it is that he would choose not to. The
head-shake he mentions is a public gesture of derision, the one made at the sufferer of Psalm 22:7
and at the ruined city of Lamentations 2:15, and the one the passers-by will make at the cross
(Matthew 27:39).

**The account of the assault (16:6–17) is the most violent portrayal of God in the Hebrew Bible,
and it is spoken by the man the book calls blameless.** The metaphors do not settle; they pile up.
God is a wild animal — *"He has torn me in his wrath, and persecuted me. He has gnashed on me with
his teeth"*. God is a wrestler who takes him by the neck and dashes him to pieces. God is an archer
who *"set me up for his target"*, whose *"archers surround me"*, who *"splits my kidneys apart, and
does not spare"* and *"pours out my gall on the ground"*. God is a besieging army breaking *"me with
breach on breach"* and a warrior who *"runs on me like a giant"*. There is no petition in any of it
and no request for mercy; it is a description of what is being done to him by someone he cannot
resist and will not stop addressing.

Two details in this movement carry the argument. The first is verse 8: *"You have shriveled me up.
This is a witness against me. My leanness rises up against me. It testifies to my face."* Job has
grasped exactly what Eliphaz did to him in 15:6 — his condition is being read as testimony — and
he attributes the manufacture of that testimony to God. His wasted body is a false witness, and God
made it. The second is verse 17, which is the ground the whole speech stands on: *"Although there
is no violence in my hands, and my prayer is pure."* The reader knows this is true. Job 1:1, 1:8,
and 2:3 have already said so, twice in God's own voice. Everything Job says about God's violence is
said by a man whose innocence the book has certified in advance, which is why the passage cannot be
dismissed as the distortion of a guilty conscience.

**Then the turn (16:18–21), and it happens in the space of a single verse.** *"Earth, don't cover
my blood. Let my cry have no place to rest."* This is a direct reach back to [[Cain and Abel]]:
Genesis 4:10–11 has Abel's blood crying from the ground, and the ground opening its mouth to
receive it. Blood absorbed by the earth stops crying; blood left exposed goes on demanding an
answer. Job, still alive, addresses the earth and forbids it to bury the evidence — and in the
next breath, without transition, he finds someone to hear the cry: *"Even now, behold, my witness
is in heaven. He who vouches for me is on high."*

The words are legal. *ʿĒd* is a courtroom witness; *śāhēd* is an Aramaic loanword for the same
office; and verse 21's request is that this figure *"would maintain the right of a man with God"* —
argue the case of a man *against* God. Job has been feeling for this figure since 9:33, where he
complained that *"there is no umpire between us, that might lay his hand on us both"*; he will name
him a living Redeemer at 19:25; Elihu will offer a candidate at 33:23. What is extraordinary here
is the direction of the appeal. The God of verses 9–14 is the assailant. The witness of verse 19 is
the one who will testify against the assailant. Job is appealing from God to God, and the book does
not tell him — or us — whether those are two beings or one.

**Chapter 17 turns the plea into a legal demand and then watches it fail.** Verse 3 is the boldest
formulation in the speech: *"Now give a pledge, be collateral for me with yourself. Who is there who
will strike hands with me?"* The vocabulary is commercial — standing surety, the handshake that
seals a guarantee (Proverbs 6:1, 17:18, 22:26). Job is asking God to post bond on Job's behalf with
God, because no one else will: *"Who is there who will strike hands with me?"* is a question with
no answer in the room. And verse 4 explains why the room is empty — *"you have hidden their heart
from understanding"* — which makes the friends' obtuseness God's doing too.

From there the speech descends. He is *"a byword of the people"* and they spit in his face (17:6);
his days are past and his plans broken off (17:11); the friends' assurances are men *"saying 'The
light is near' in the presence of darkness"* (17:12). And the last four verses take the descent to
its end, in a set of grim adoptions: if Sheol is my house, if I have made my bed in the darkness,
if I have said to corruption *"You are my father"* and to the worm *"My mother"* and *"my sister"* —
*"where then is my hope?"* The final image is of hope itself going down through the gates of Sheol
and settling into the dust with him.

**The two halves do not fit, and the book means them not to.** A witness in heaven and a hope in
the dust are not stages of an argument, one correcting the other; they are a fortnight apart in the
same man's mouth. The friends are consistent and wrong. Job is inconsistent and, at 42:7, the one
God says spoke rightly. Whatever the book is recommending, it is not tidiness — and it is not the
kind of faith that has stopped registering the evidence.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — speaking for the fifth time, and further out than he has been. This is the
  speech in which he stops arguing with the friends after five verses and spends the remaining
  thirty-five on God: accusing him in 16:7–14, appealing past him in 16:19–21, demanding surety
  from him in 17:3, and blaming him for the friends' incomprehension in 17:4.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — the speaker being answered. 17:10's *"I shall not find a wise man
  among you"* replies directly to the *"gray-headed and the very aged"* of 15:10, and 16:2's
  *"vain words"* echoes back the *"vain knowledge"* of 15:2.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — Job answers all three together. Every
  address to the friends in this speech is plural (*"You are all"*, 16:2; *"as for you all"*,
  17:10), which is why the reply belongs to the cycle and not only to Eliphaz.
- [[God]] — present in three incompatible roles inside forty verses, and this is the passage where
  that fact becomes the wiki's problem as well as Job's. He is the attacker of 16:7–14, the witness
  and voucher in heaven of 16:19, and the surety Job demands at 17:3. He is also addressed in the
  second person (*"you have surely worn me out"*, 16:7) and described in the third (*"He has torn
  me"*, 16:9) within two verses, which is a feature of the Hebrew and not a slip.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — named twice, at 17:13 and 17:16, and it closes the speech. Job's Sheol is not a place
  of punishment; it is the common destination, dark, dusty, and out of reach of the argument he is
  trying to have. *"The gates of Sheol"* gives it a city's architecture, and the adoption formula of
  17:14 gives it a family.

No geography otherwise. Job's whole world in this speech is his own body, the friends around him,
the earth under him, and heaven above.

## Themes

- [[Suffering]] — the passage supplies the theme's most extreme vocabulary in Scripture. What
  distinguishes it from lament elsewhere is the identification of the agent: not enemies, not
  chance, not the absence of God, but God's own hand, described in the language of assault.
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — 16:19–21 and 17:3 are the theme's founding texts. Witness, voucher,
  the one who maintains a man's right against God, and the guarantor who strikes hands: four legal
  offices sought in eight verses, by a man who has concluded that his own testimony will not be
  believed.
- [[Hope]] — 17:15–16 asks the question the book will not answer for another twenty-five chapters.
  Note the grammar: not *I have no hope* but *where then is my hope? … who shall see it?* The hope
  is not denied, it is misplaced, and the speech ends looking for it.
- [[Retribution]] — attacked from the other side than usual. Job does not argue the doctrine here;
  he simply reports what it looks like from inside a case it has misdiagnosed, and 16:8 names the
  mechanism precisely — a ruined body taken as testimony, and so a verdict manufactured out of the
  punishment.
- [[Righteousness]] — 16:17's *"there is no violence in my hands, and my prayer is pure"* is the
  claim the friends call blasphemy and the narrator has already validated. 17:9's *"He who has clean
  hands shall grow stronger and stronger"* is the same word, in a verse that sounds startlingly
  like the friends (see Notes).
- [[Judgment]] — the passage is saturated with courtroom procedure — witness, testimony, pledge,
  the maintaining of a right — and the whole difficulty is that the judge is also the defendant and
  also, Job hopes, the witness for the plaintiff.
- [[Life and Death]] — 16:22's *"the way of no return"* and the whole of 17:13–16. Job expects to
  die uncleared, and the urgency of the appeal comes from that expectation, not from a fear of
  death itself.
- [[Wisdom]] — 17:10's flat verdict on the friends, which is Job's answer to Eliphaz's credentials.

## Notes

- **"Miserable comforters" (16:2).** *Mənaḥămê ʿāmāl* — *comforters of trouble*, or *troublesome
  comforters*. The root *nāḥam* is the wiki's recurring one: it is the verb of Lamech's hope at
  Genesis 5:29 and of God's grief at Genesis 6:6, and it is the verb of 2:11, where the three
  friends set out to do the thing Job here says they have failed at. The genitive is doing the
  insult: they are not comforters who happen to be bad at it, they are dispensers of *ʿāmāl*, the
  word for toil, misery, and mischief — the same word that will describe what the wicked conceive
  at 15:35, four verses earlier in the dialogue. Job hands Eliphaz's own noun back to him.

- **"If your soul were in my soul's place" (16:4).** The verse is often read as Job saying he would
  behave better in their position, and verse 5 does say that: *"I would strengthen you with my
  mouth."* But the sting of verse 4 is the first half. He is granting that he *could* produce their
  speeches — that the fluency they take for wisdom is a function of standing outside the fire. The
  claim is about the epistemic position of the comforter, not about the truth of the doctrine, and
  it is the oldest and best statement of an objection that has never been improved on.

- **Is the God of 16:9–14 a picture of God or of the accuser?** The Hebrew of verse 9 uses *ṣārî*,
  *my adversary*, which is the same root as *śāṭān*, and a minority of readers take verses 9–14 as
  Job unknowingly describing the figure of the prologue rather than God. The reading is attractive
  because it lets the reader hold the violence at arm's length, and it should be resisted on the
  text's own grounds. Verse 7 addresses God directly in the second person — *"But now, God, you have
  surely worn me out"* — and the third-person verbs that follow have no other antecedent. More
  decisively, the theology of the book does not permit the distinction: the prologue is explicit
  that the accuser acts only by permission (1:12, 2:6), and Job, who has not read the prologue, is
  right in substance to lay it at God's door. The wiki takes 16:9–14 as spoken of God, and takes
  its violence as intended.

- **The identity of the witness (16:19).** Three readings, all with real support, and the passage
  does not adjudicate. **(1) God himself.** Job appeals from the God he is experiencing to the God
  he has known — from God-as-he-seems to God-as-he-is. This is the majority view among modern
  commentators, it fits the strict monotheism of the book, and it explains why the figure is *in
  heaven* and *on high* without further introduction. Its difficulty is verse 21, where the witness
  *"maintain[s] the right of a man with God"*, which reads more naturally of a third party than of
  a litigant taking both sides. **(2) A distinct heavenly advocate** — an angel or intercessor,
  understood as the office Elihu describes at 33:23–24. This suits the legal language, suits the
  progression from 9:33's umpire to 19:25's redeemer, and suits a book that has already shown us a
  populated heavenly court. Its difficulty is that Job elsewhere insists no such figure exists
  (9:33 is a complaint, not a claim), and that the book never confirms one. **(3) Job's own cry,
  personified.** Verse 18 has just asked that his cry *"have no place to rest"* — that it keep
  travelling — and verse 19 may be saying that the cry has arrived and now stands as his witness.
  This reading is elegant, follows the verse order exactly, and is the least popular; against it,
  *"he who vouches for me"* is personal and the Hebrew participle is masculine singular.

  What can be said without choosing: Job wants an opposite number to the prosecution, and the
  irony the reader carries is that the heavenly court really does contain a figure taking an
  interest in his case — the accuser of chapters 1 and 2 — and that Job is asking, blind, for that
  figure's counterpart. Christian reading has taken 16:19–21 with 19:25 and 9:33 as a reach toward
  the mediator of 1 Timothy 2:5 and the advocate of 1 John 2:1, and that reading is set out with
  its limits on [[The Witness in Heaven]]. What must not be lost in it is that Job is asking for an
  advocate *against God*, which is not what the New Testament texts describe.

- **"Be collateral for me with yourself" (17:3).** The verb is *ʿārab*, to go surety, and the idiom
  in the second half — *"strike hands"* — is the Proverbs gesture of guaranteeing another man's
  debt. The request is that God guarantee Job's case in a court where God is the opposing party.
  Nothing else in the Hebrew Bible asks this. It is the same logic as the witness in heaven pressed
  one step further, into procedure, and it is the point at which Job's legal metaphor visibly
  exceeds what a legal metaphor can hold. Some read this as the book showing the metaphor break —
  Job needs something law cannot supply — and the whirlwind speeches, which answer him in a
  completely different register, give that reading some support.

- **Job 17:9, and whether Job means it.** *"Yet shall the righteous hold on his way. He who has
  clean hands shall grow stronger and stronger."* In isolation this is a Proverbs verse, and it is
  in the mouth of a man two verses from calling the worm his mother. Three readings. **Irony or
  quotation** — Job repeating the friends' slogan back at them, which suits the surrounding
  contempt and the *"as for you all"* of the next verse. **A genuine flash of confidence** — the
  same sudden upward break as 16:19, and evidence that these breaks are the speech's real pattern
  rather than an aberration. **Textual disturbance** — a number of commentators have thought 17:8–9
  intrusive or displaced, and the chapter is one of the more difficult in the book. The wiki
  records all three and prefers the second, on the ground that the passage has already made one
  unprepared upward turn at 16:19 and the reader has been taught to expect them.

- **Sheol in Job.** *Šəʾôl* here is the undifferentiated realm of the dead: dark, dusty, and shared
  by the righteous and the wicked alike. There is no judgment in it, no fire, and no reward — the
  developed afterlife of later Judaism and of the New Testament is not present in this book, and
  reading it back into 17:13–16 flattens the problem the chapter is posing. This matters for Job's
  argument. Because Sheol offers no correction of earthly verdicts, vindication must happen *before*
  he dies or not at all, which is exactly why 16:22 (*"the way of no return"*) makes the appeal of
  16:19 urgent rather than patient. The one place where Job appears to reach past death for
  vindication is 19:25–27, and that passage's Hebrew is famously broken. See [[Sheol]].

- **The adoption formula (17:14).** Saying *"You are my father"* to corruption and *"My mother"* to
  the worm uses the language of legal kinship declaration, the form behind Psalm 2:7 and Psalm
  89:26. Job is not describing decay; he is being received into a family. Read against the prologue
  — seven sons and three daughters, all dead in the first chapter — the choice of metaphor is not
  an accident.

- **Where the chapter division falls.** The speech runs 16:1–17:16 without a break; the chapter
  boundary between 16:22 and 17:1 cuts a continuous movement about death and is a medieval
  convenience, not a unit of the text. This wiki ingests the speech whole for that reason, which is
  also why the second half of the Key verses section carries explicit chapter numbers.
