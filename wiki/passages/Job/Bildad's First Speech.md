# Bildad's First Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 8:1–22

## Summary

Job has just finished a speech whose last fifteen verses were addressed to God: *"Oh remember that
my life is a breath"* (7:7), *"what is man, that you should… test him every moment?"* (7:17–18),
*"Why do you not pardon my disobedience?"* (7:21). [[Bildad the Shuhite]] answers none of it to God
and very little of it to Job. **His speech is twenty-two verses long and makes one argument, which
it states in its third verse and restates in its twentieth:** God does not pervert justice, so what
has happened to Job's house is just, and what happens next is up to Job. Everything between those
two verses is illustration — an appeal to tradition, and two plants.

It runs in four movements.

**1. The wind and the question (8:2–3).** *"How long will you speak these things? Shall the words of
your mouth be a mighty wind?"* *Rûaḥ kabbîr*. Job had said it of himself first — *"the speeches of
one who is desperate are as wind"* (6:26) — and asked the friends not to reprove words for being
wind. Bildad reproves them for exactly that. It is the fourth *rûaḥ* of the book, and the reader is
the only person present who knows what the first one did: **a *"great wind from the wilderness"*
struck the four corners of the house and killed the children** (1:19). Two verses later Bildad
explains that wind's work as a sentence.

Then the question the speech exists to ask: ***"Does God pervert justice? Or does the Almighty
pervert righteousness?"*** (8:3). The verb is *ʿāwat*, to bend or make crooked, used twice, with
*mišpāṭ* and *ṣedeq* as its objects — justice and righteousness, the pair Abraham invoked at Sodom.
It is not a bad question; it is the right one asked the wrong way round. Abraham asked *"Shouldn't
the Judge of all the earth do right?"* (Genesis 18:25) to hold God to justice in a case where the
innocent were at risk; **Bildad asks it to close a case in which the innocent has already
suffered.** For Abraham the question is a lever. For Bildad it is a lid. See
[[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].

Job will answer it in two stages. At 9:2 he grants it — *"Truly I know that it is so"* — and asks
the question underneath it instead: *"how can man be just with God?"* And at 19:6 he answers it
outright, with Bildad's own verb: ***"know now that God has subverted me"*** — *ʿiwwətānî*, God has
*bent* me. Bildad asked whether God bends justice, expecting no; Job says God has bent *him*.
Elihu, much later, will repeat Bildad's sentence nearly word for word (34:12).

**2. The children, and the conditional (8:4–7).** ***"If your children have sinned against him, he
has delivered them into the hand of their disobedience."*** This is the sentence for which Bildad is
remembered, and it is worth reading exactly. It is grammatically conditional, and some readers have
heard tact in the *if*. But the *if* is not doubtful about the conclusion, only about the premise,
and the conclusion is written in the past tense: they are dead, so — if the doctrine holds — they
sinned. The phrase *"into the hand of their disobedience"* (*bəyad-pišʿām*) makes their own
transgression the agent that killed them, as though the wind had been their sin come home. **Ten
people died in a single afternoon, and the first time a friend names them it is as evidence.**
Eliphaz had come within one step of it (5:4); Bildad takes the step. See [[Job's Children]].

And the cruelty serves the speech's one kindness, which follows at once. The children are past
help; Job is not. *"If you want to seek God diligently, make your supplication to the Almighty. If
you were pure and upright, surely now he would awaken for you"* (8:5–6). Three *ifs* in three
verses: if they sinned, if you seek, if you were pure. **The logic of 8:4–6 is a contrast between
the dead and the living** — your children died in their sin, but you are still alive, so you still
have time. It is the offer of restoration chapter 18 will withdraw.

Two words in the offer are Job's own, and Bildad uses both against him without seeming to notice.
The verb *seek diligently* is *šāḥar*, to seek early, at dawn — **the very word Job had just spoken
to God as the last line of his speech: *"You will seek me diligently, but I shall not be"*** (7:21).
Job imagined God coming to look for him one morning and finding nobody there. Bildad reverses the
direction: it is Job who should be up early looking for God. And *"pure and upright"* —
*yāšār* — is the narrator's word for Job in the book's first verse, *"blameless and upright"*
(1:1), and God's word for him twice in the council (1:8; 2:3). Bildad sets as a condition the one
thing the reader has been told is already true.

Then the promise: ***"Though your beginning was small, yet your latter end would greatly
increase"*** (8:7). **This is the most exactly fulfilled prediction in the book, and the man who
makes it is rebuked by God.** The epilogue says: *"So Yahweh blessed the latter end of Job more than
his beginning"* (42:12) — the same pair of nouns, *rēʾšît* and *ʾaḥărît*, in the same order and the
same sense. Bildad's ending happens. His reason for it does not: the latter end comes after Job is
declared to have spoken *"the thing that is right"* (42:7), and after Job has prayed for Bildad.
See [[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]].

**3. The fathers (8:8–10).** *"Please inquire of past generations. Find out about the learning of
their fathers. (For we are but of yesterday, and know nothing, because our days on earth are a
shadow.) Shall they not teach you, tell you, and utter words out of their heart?"* Eliphaz's
authority was a vision in the night (4:12–16). Bildad's is the accumulated memory of the ancestors,
and he is candid about why it is needed: **a single human lifetime is too short to see the pattern
work out.** The fathers saw more of it than we can.

The premise is Job's. *"My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle"* (7:6), *"my life is a breath"*
(7:7); the shadow will return in Job's mouth at 14:2. **Job and Bildad agree that life is brief,
and draw opposite conclusions from it.** For Bildad, brevity means a man must defer to what the
generations before him learned. For Job, brevity is a reason for God to relent before it is too late
— *"Oh remember that my life is a breath."* Deuteronomy makes Bildad's move in a different cause
(*"Ask your father, and he will show you"*, 32:7), and David makes Job's in a prayer (*"our days on
the earth are as a shadow"*, 1 Chronicles 29:15). Job's sharpest reply is at 12:2–3: *"No doubt, but
you are the people, and wisdom shall die with you. But I have understanding as well as you."*

The parenthesis of 8:9 contains the speech's one unintended truth: *"we are but of yesterday, and
know nothing."* It is offered as humility. It is, as the reader knows from the council of 1:6–12,
simply accurate.

**4. Two plants (8:11–19).** What the fathers teach turns out to be a nature lesson. ***"Can the
papyrus grow up without mire? Can the rushes grow without water? While it is yet in its greenness,
not cut down, it withers before any other reed"*** (8:11–12). The *gōmeʾ* and the *ʾāḥû* are plants
of the Nile marsh — *ʾāḥû* is the *"marsh grass"* Pharaoh's cattle graze in (Genesis 41:2, 18), and
*gōmeʾ* is what the basket of the infant Moses was woven from (Exodus 2:3). They grow fast and look
magnificent; cut off their water and they are the first thing in the marsh to die, before they are
even harvested. *"So are the paths of all who forget God. The hope of the godless man shall
perish"* (8:13).

Then the image changes register: *"Whose confidence shall break apart, whose trust is a spider's
web. He shall lean on his house, but it shall not stand"* (8:14–15). The Hebrew for *spider's web* is
*bêt ʿakkābîš*, **a spider's *house*** — so the godless man leans on his house, and his house is a
spider's. The reader cannot not think of Job's eldest son's house, which fell (1:19). Bildad does not
say so. He does not need to.

The second plant (8:16–19) is harder. It is green in the sun, its shoots spread through the garden,
its roots grip the stones — and then it is torn out of its place, and the place itself disowns it:
*"I have not seen you."* *"Behold, this is the joy of his way: out of the earth, others shall
spring."* Most readers take it as the godless man again, now a vigorous plant rather than a fragile
one, and the *joy* of 8:19 as acid irony. A minority read it as a contrast — the righteous plant
that survives uprooting and springs up again. The wiki takes the first reading as the plain sense and
records the second; see the Notes. Either way, **the image Job answers is the first one.** At
14:7–9 he says there is more hope for a felled tree than for a man: *"Though its root grows old in
the earth… yet through the scent of water it will bud."* Bildad's papyrus dies without water; Job's
tree comes back at the smell of it, and Job's point is that a man does not. See
[[The Papyrus Without Water - Bildad's Plants, Psalm 1, and Jeremiah 17]].

**5. The verdict and the promise (8:20–22).** ***"Behold, God will not cast away a blameless man,
neither will he uphold the evildoers."*** *Tām*, blameless — the first word of the narrator's verdict
on Job (1:1), and God's (1:8; 2:3). And *māʾas*, to cast away or reject — **the verb Job had used of
himself a few verses before Bildad began: *"I loathe my life"*** (7:16). Bildad's sentence is
designed to comfort: if Job is *tām*, God will not reject him. Its effect is a syllogism Job finishes
in the next chapter, using both of Bildad's words: ***"I am blameless (tām). I don't respect myself.
I despise (ʾemʾas) my life… he destroys the blameless and the wicked"*** (9:21–22). If God does not
reject the blameless, and Job is blameless, and Job is rejected, then the premise is wrong.

The close is the gentlest passage any friend speaks in the dialogue: *"He will still fill your mouth
with laughter, your lips with shouting. Those who hate you shall be clothed with shame. The tent of
the wicked shall be no more"* (8:21–22). Laughter and the shout of joy, *śəḥôq* and *tərûʿâ*, are
the vocabulary of Psalm 126:2. **And the last line is the speech's final irony.** At the end of the
book, the men who must be put to shame and covered by an intercessor are not Job's enemies: they are
Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar (42:7–9). Bildad has described his own rebuke.

## People

- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — his first speech, and the first time he speaks at all after seven silent
  days on the ground (2:13). Twenty-one verses of speech against Eliphaz's forty-seven, and the most
  compressed statement of the doctrine in the book: one question (8:3), one application
  (8:4), one offer (8:5–7), one authority (8:8–10), one illustration (8:11–19), one verdict (8:20).
- [[Job (person)]] — addressed in the second person throughout, which Bildad's second speech will not
  do. Nothing he said in chapters 6–7 is engaged on its merits, but two of his words are taken and
  turned — *seek diligently* (7:21 → 8:5) and *reject* (7:16 → 8:20) — and his request to be taught
  his error (6:24) is answered with a reading list: the fathers.
- [[Job's Children]] — 8:4. The first time anyone in the dialogue mentions them, and they are
  mentioned as proof. Bildad will do it again, anonymously, at 18:19.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — not addressed, but the speech is his argument in a harder voice.
  Eliphaz asked *"who ever perished, being innocent?"* (4:7) and stopped short of the children;
  Bildad names them. Eliphaz's authority was a vision; Bildad's is the fathers. And Eliphaz opens his
  next speech on Bildad's image: *"Should a wise man… fill himself with the east wind?"* (15:2).
- [[God]] — called *ʾēl* and *šadday* (8:3, 5), and described throughout as a mechanism that cannot
  malfunction. In the whole speech he is never addressed and never quoted.

## Places

No place is named. The images are borrowed from a geography, though: the papyrus and marsh reed of
8:11 are Nile plants, and *ʾāḥû* is a loanword from Egyptian. A man from [[Shuah]], on any of its
proposed locations, would know them as a traveller's or a proverb's knowledge, not a farmer's — which
fits a speech whose authority is what the fathers said rather than what Bildad saw.

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — stated as a single syllogism: God does not pervert justice (8:3), therefore the
  dead children sinned (8:4), therefore a pure man who seeks God will be restored (8:5–7). Every
  premise of the book's argument is here in five verses.
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣedeq* and *mišpāṭ* at 8:3, and *"the habitation of your righteousness"* at
  8:6: righteousness as a condition God is bound to reward.
- [[Integrity]] — *tām* at 8:20 and *yāšār* at 8:6: the narrator's two words for Job in 1:1, offered
  back to him as a condition he has yet to meet.
- [[Wisdom]] — wisdom as inheritance (8:8–10), justified by the brevity of a human life, and the one
  thing in the speech that is accidentally true: *"we… know nothing."*
- [[Hope]] — *"The hope of the godless man shall perish"* (8:13): the noun Job had said was spent
  (7:6), now made a mark of the godless. Answered at 14:7: *"there is hope for a tree."*
- [[Prayer]] — 8:5, the friends' first instruction to pray, given to a man who had begun praying at
  7:7 and whom Bildad has just heard do it.
- [[Sin]] — named at 8:4 of the children and nowhere specified; *"their disobedience"* is inferred
  from their deaths.
- [[Suffering]] — read backwards from its outcome: the dead must have sinned, and the living sufferer
  is being given time.

## Notes

- **Is the *if* of 8:4 tactful?** The verse is a conditional, and some commentators hear Bildad
  softening the blow — *if* your children sinned. The wiki does not think the grammar can carry that.
  The protasis is the only uncertain part: the apodosis, *"he has delivered them"*, is in the past
  and is a fact, and the doctrine runs from fact to premise. A conditional whose result is already in
  is not a hypothesis but an inference. What the *if* does do is set up the contrast of 8:4–6, three
  conditionals in a row, which the wiki reads as Bildad's real meaning: the dead are beyond help and
  the living are not.

- **The second plant (8:16–19).** The verses can be read two ways and the Hebrew does not settle it.
  **(a) Continuation** — the godless man again, this time as a vigorous garden plant rather than a
  fragile reed; uprooted, disowned by its place, and replaced by others, with *"this is the joy of
  his way"* as sarcasm. This is the majority reading, and it is the one that makes the speech one
  illustration in two images. **(b) Contrast** — a second, hardy plant whose roots grip the rock,
  which, even when torn out, springs up again from the earth: the righteous man's resilience, setting
  up 8:20. This reading takes *"out of the earth, others shall spring"* as renewal rather than
  replacement and has some support in older commentary. The wiki adopts (a) and records (b), and notes
  that on either reading Bildad's argument is the same and only its tone changes.

- **Egyptian plants in an Edomite or Arabian mouth.** *ʾĀḥû* is borrowed from Egyptian (*ꜣḫ*, a
  marsh plant), and *gōmeʾ* is papyrus, which does not grow wild in Edom or Arabia. Neither fact
  locates Bildad. Both fit the book's wider horizon, which knows Egypt's reeds, Sheba's caravans, and
  Tema's wells, and which repeatedly gives its speakers more of the world than any one homeland would
  supply. Job's own reply reaches for *"the ships of reed"* — the papyrus skiffs, *ʾŏniyyôt ʾēbeh* —
  at 9:26.

- **"He would awaken for you" (8:6).** *Yāʿîr ʿāleykā*. God roused as if from sleep is the language
  of the lament psalms — *"Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?"* (Psalm 44:23); *"Wake up! Rise up to
  defend me"* (Psalm 35:23). Bildad uses a sufferer's prayer as a promise: pray, and God will wake.
  He does not seem to notice that it concedes the thing Job is complaining of — that from where Job
  sits, God is not attending to justice. Job's complaint at 7:17–20 is the opposite in any case: not
  that God sleeps but that he will not stop watching.

- **A speech without the name Yahweh.** None of the friends uses the name *Yahweh* anywhere in
  the dialogue; Bildad's *ʾēl* and *šadday* are the patriarchal names, consistent with a setting
  outside Israel. See [[The Names of God]].
