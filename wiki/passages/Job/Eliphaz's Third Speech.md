# Eliphaz's Third Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 22:1–30

## Summary

The third cycle opens with the last speech Eliphaz makes, and it is the one where the friends'
argument finally finishes itself. For two cycles the doctrine of retribution has been applied to
Job by implication — a portrait of the wicked man that happened to match his losses. Here the
implication is dropped. **Eliphaz names crimes, in the second person, and none of them happened.**
Then, in the same speech, he makes the most beautiful offer of restoration any friend makes, and
ends on a sentence that the epilogue will fulfil with Eliphaz himself as its object.

The speech has four movements: a question about whether God gains anything from a man's
righteousness (22:2–4); the charge sheet (22:5–11); a misquotation of Job and a quotation of him
(22:12–20); and the call to return (22:21–30).

**1. What does God get out of it? (22:2–4).** *"Can a man be profitable to God? Surely he who is
wise is profitable to himself. Is it any pleasure to the Almighty, that you are righteous? Or does it
benefit him, that you make your ways perfect?"* It is a new subject — Job's chapter 21 is not
answered at all — and it is a real theological claim: God is self-sufficient, human goodness adds
nothing to him, and therefore his dealings with a man cannot be motivated by anything except that
man's deserts. Eliphaz means it as the premise of a syllogism. If God gains nothing either way, he
has no reason to afflict a righteous man, so the affliction must be for wickedness.

The reader knows why the premise fails. **The prologue is a scene in which God has a stake in Job's
righteousness.** *"Have you considered my servant Job? For there is no one like him in the earth"*
(1:8; 2:3) is a boast, and the accuser's challenge is aimed at it: *"Does Job fear God for
nothing?"* (1:9). The whole book happens because Job's integrity matters in heaven. Eliphaz's verb
for *make perfect* in 22:3 is *tattēm*, from *t-m-m* — the root of *tām*, *blameless*, and of
*tummâ*, *integrity*, the very thing God said Job *"still maintains"* (2:3) — so the question he
asks, *does it benefit God that your ways are blameless?*, is the question the council was convened
to answer, and the answer it reached was yes. See
[[Can a Man Be Profitable to God - Job 22.2-3, Job 35.6-8, and Luke 17.10]].

Verse 4 makes the irony total: *"Is it for your piety that he reproves you, that he enters with
you into judgment?"* The word is ***yirʾâ***, the fear of God — Job's word in the narrator's first
sentence (*"one who feared God"*, 1:1) and Eliphaz's own at 4:6 (*"Isn't your piety your
confidence?"*). **It is the most exactly wrong question in the dialogue.** It was for his piety.
That is what the accuser disputed and what the test was set up to find out.

**2. The charge sheet (22:5–11).** *"Isn't your wickedness great? Neither is there any end to your
iniquities."* Then six counts:

| 22:6–9 | what it would mean | what the book says |
|---|---|---|
| *"you have taken pledges from your brother for nothing"* | seizing collateral from a kinsman without a real debt — the garment that Exodus 22:26–27 says must go back before sunset | Job: *"I was a father to the needy"* (29:16) |
| *"and stripped the naked of their clothing"* | taking the poor man's cloak as the pledge | Job: *"if I have seen any perish for want of clothing … if he hasn't been warmed with my sheep's fleece"* (31:19–20) |
| *"You haven't given water to the weary to drink"* | refusing the most basic hospitality | Job: *"have eaten my morsel alone"* — denied under oath (31:17) |
| *"you have withheld bread from the hungry"* | the same, and the first work of mercy in every biblical list | Job: *"If I have withheld the poor from their desire"* (31:16) |
| *"You have sent widows away empty"* | the widow turned away from a powerful man's door | Job: *"I caused the widow's heart to sing for joy"* (29:13) |
| *"the arms of the fatherless have been broken"* | the orphan's strength — his means of defending himself — crushed | Job: *"then let my shoulder fall from the shoulder blade, and my arm be broken from the bone"* (31:22) |

The charges are not random. They are **the inverse of the classical list of righteousness** — the
list Ezekiel 18:7 gives (restores the pledge, gives bread to the hungry, covers the naked), Isaiah
58:7 gives (bread to the hungry, the naked covered), and Jesus gives at Matthew 25:35–36 (hungry and
fed, thirsty and given drink, naked and clothed). Eliphaz has taken the canonical description of a
righteous man and negated every clause. See
[[Bread to the Hungry and Clothing to the Naked - Job 22.6-9, Job 31, and Matthew 25]].

And they come from nowhere. **Eliphaz has no evidence for any of them and does not claim any.**
There is no report, no witness, no *I have seen*. The *"Therefore"* of 22:10 — *"Therefore snares
are around you. Sudden fear troubles you"* — runs the argument backwards and shows how it works:
the snares are the evidence, the sins are the inference, and the inference has been stated as fact.
Zophar had drafted the charge in the third person (*"he has oppressed and forsaken the poor"*,
20:19); Eliphaz attaches Job's name to it. This is what [[Eliphaz's Second Speech]] called the
trajectory from courtesy to insinuation to fabrication, completed.

**It also contradicts Eliphaz.** His first words to Job were *"Behold, you have instructed many,
you have strengthened the weak hands. Your words have supported him who was falling"* (4:3–4).
Here the same man is accused of breaking the arms of the fatherless. Eliphaz has not learned
anything about Job between chapter 4 and chapter 22. What has changed is the pressure of the
doctrine: the suffering has continued, Job has refused to confess, and a sin of the necessary size
has to be found. The wiki records the contradiction as Eliphaz's own, between 4:3–4 and 22:6–9; the
narrator (1:1) and God (1:8; 2:3) support the first, and nothing in the book supports the second.

There is one word in the charge sheet that the reader cannot hear innocently. *"You have taken
pledges from your brother **for nothing**"* — ***ḥinnām***. It is the accuser's word: *"Does Job
fear God **for nothing**?"* (1:9). It is God's word: *"you incited me against him, to ruin him
**without cause**"* (2:3). And it is Job's: *"he … multiplies my wounds **without cause**"* (9:17).
Everywhere else in the book *ḥinnām* names the gratuitousness of what is being done *to* Job.
Eliphaz, who cannot know any of this, transfers it to something Job is supposed to have done.

Verses 10–11 finish the count with consequences: snares, sudden fear, *"darkness, so that you can
not see, and floods of waters cover you"*. The snares are Bildad's (18:8–10). The last clause —
***wəšipʿat-mayim təkassekkā*** — will be heard once more, word for word, in God's own mouth: *"Can
you lift up your voice to the clouds, that abundance of waters may cover you?"* (38:34). Eliphaz's
flood is a threat; God's is a question about which of them commands the weather.

**3. What Job said, and did not say (22:12–20).** *"Isn't God in the heights of heaven? See the
height of the stars, how high they are! You say, 'What does God know? Can he judge through the thick
darkness? Thick clouds are a covering to him, so that he doesn't see. He walks on the vault of the
sky.'"* **Job never said this.** It is the speech of the practical atheist of the Psalms — *"How does
God know?"* (Psalm 73:11), *"God has forgotten. He hides his face"* (Psalm 10:11), *"Yah will not
see"* (Psalm 94:7) — and Eliphaz has put it in Job's mouth. What Job actually said is the opposite.
His complaint has been that God sees **too much**: *"you watcher of men"* (7:20), *"Do you have eyes
of flesh? … that you inquire after my iniquity, and search after my sin?"* (10:4–6), *"now you count
my steps. Don't you watch over my sin?"* (14:16). Job's God is a surveillant who will not look away;
Eliphaz's Job is a man who thinks God cannot see through the cloud. The *thick darkness* is
***ʿărāpel***, the cloud God was in at Sinai (Exodus 20:21) and that Solomon says he dwells in
(1 Kings 8:12) — the darkness of his presence, not of his absence. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

The next movement does quote Job, and accurately. 22:15–16 points to *"the old way, which wicked men
have trodden, who were snatched away before their time, whose foundation was poured out as a
stream"* — most naturally the generation of the Flood, swept away with the ground under them (see
the note below). And then:

> **17** who said to God, 'Depart from us;' and, 'What can the Almighty do for us?'
> **18** Yet he filled their houses with good things, but the counsel of the wicked is far from me.

*"Depart from us"* is ***sûr mimmennû***, Job's quotation of the wicked at 21:14. *"What can the
Almighty do for us?"* is 21:15 compressed. *"He filled their houses with good things"* concedes, for
one clause, the whole of Job's chapter 21 — the wicked *do* prosper. And then the clause that closes
verse 18 is Job's own disclaimer, letter for letter: ***ʿăṣat rəšāʿîm rāḥăqâ mennî***, *"The counsel
of the wicked is far from me"* (21:16). Job said it to keep himself apart from the men he was
describing. Eliphaz says it to keep himself apart from Job. It is the plainest case in the dialogue
of a friend quoting Job verbatim, and he does it to take Job's position away from him: the line
Job used to say *I am not one of them* becomes Eliphaz's way of saying *you are*.

Verses 19–20 add the spectators: *"The righteous see it, and are glad. The innocent ridicule them,
saying, 'Surely those who rose up against us are cut off. The fire has consumed their remnant.'"*
*Yirʾû ṣaddîqîm wəyiśmāḥû* is close to the line Psalm 107:42 will use (*"The upright will see it,
and be glad"*), two verses after the line Job's hymn of 12:21–24 shares with the same psalm. The
verb *cut off*, *kāḥad*, is the one Eliphaz used in his first question to Job: *"where were the
upright cut off (nikḥādû)?"* (4:7). And *"the fire has consumed"* cannot be read in this book without
hearing *"The fire of God has fallen from the sky"* (1:16). Eliphaz is describing the righteous
laughing at the destruction of the wicked, in the terms of Job's own disaster, to a man who is
sitting in the ashes of it.

**4. Return, and be restored (22:21–30).** And then, with no transition, the warmest passage in all
three friends' speeches:

*"Acquaint yourself with him, now, and be at peace."* The verb is ***hasken***, from the same root,
*s-k-n*, as *"Can a man be profitable to God?"* (22:2). The speech is built on the root's two senses:
a man cannot be *of use* to God, but he can be *used to* him — familiar with him, at home with him.
*"Please receive instruction from his mouth"* — the word is ***tôrâ***, its only occurrence in the
book. *"If you return to the Almighty, you shall be built up"* — ***šûb***, the prophets' verb for
repentance, and the one the friends never stop urging. *"Lay your treasure in the dust, the gold of
Ophir among the stones of the brooks. The Almighty will be your treasure, and precious silver to
you"* — a pun, *beṣer* (ore) and *bəṣûr* (among the rocks) and *bəṣārêkā* (your treasure), and a
charge: Eliphaz thinks Job's wealth was his god. Job will answer it under oath at 31:24–25: *"If I
have made gold my hope, and have said to the fine gold, 'You are my confidence'"*. See [[Ophir]].

Then the promises, one per verse. *"You shall delight yourself in the Almighty, and shall lift up
your face to God"* (22:26) — Zophar's *"lift up your face"* (11:15), and the idiom God uses of Job
at 42:8, *"I will accept him"*, literally *I will lift his face*. *"You shall make your prayer to
him, and he will hear you. You shall pay your vows"* (22:27). *"Light shall shine on your ways"*
(22:28). *"When they cast down, you shall say, 'be lifted up.' He will save the humble person"*
(22:29). And the last:

> **30** He will even deliver him who is not innocent. Yes, he shall be delivered through the
> cleanness of your hands.

**This is the last thing Eliphaz says in the book, and it comes true about Eliphaz.** At 42:7–8 God
tells him *"My wrath is kindled against you … my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept
him, that I not deal with you according to your folly"*. Eliphaz is the man who is not innocent. He
is delivered through the cleanness of Job's hands — Job's prayer, Job's offering — and 42:10 makes
Job's own restoration follow on that act: *"Yahweh turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for
his friends."* **Every promise in 22:26–30 is fulfilled, and the condition attached to them in
22:23 is not.** Job prays and is heard; his face is lifted; the one who is not innocent is
delivered by his hands. He never confessed the sins of 22:6–9, because he never committed them.

So the speech's two halves do not fit together, and they are not meant to. The first half invents a
guilty man; the second describes, in exact detail, what the innocent man will do for the guilty
one. Eliphaz has written the epilogue and cast himself in the wrong role. See
[[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]] and
[[Mediation and Advocacy]].

Job does not answer the charges in the next chapter. He answers by turning away from the friends to
the absent God — *"Oh that I knew where I might find him!"* (23:3) — and then, in chapter 24,
describes the exact crimes Eliphaz listed being committed by other people, in the open, unpunished:
*"they take the widow's ox for a pledge … There are those who pluck the fatherless from the breast,
and take a pledge of the poor, so that they go around naked without clothing. Being hungry, they
carry the sheaves"* (24:3, 9–10). The pledges, the naked, the hungry, the widow, the fatherless:
every count of 22:6–9 is there, with a real defendant, and *"God doesn't regard the folly"* (24:12).
The formal rebuttal waits until chapters 29 and 31, where Job takes every charge in turn and swears
it is false.

## People

- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — speaking for the third and last time. The speech is the endpoint of
  his trajectory and the clearest single text for the wiki's reading of him: the charges of 22:6–9
  are fabricated, they contradict his own praise of Job at 4:3–4, and they are followed without a
  pause by the most generous offer in the dialogue. **His last sentence, 22:30, describes his own
  rescue at 42:8.**
- [[Job (person)]] — silent, and addressed in the second person throughout; the first speech in
  which a friend names his crimes. He is quoted twice: falsely at 22:13–14, and word for word at
  22:17–18.
- [[God]] — *ʾēl*, *ʾĕlôah* and *šadday* (the Almighty, five times in the chapter), never Yahweh.
  Here the God who gains nothing from human righteousness (22:2–3), who sits above the stars
  (22:12), whose *ʿărāpel* does not hide him (22:13), whose *tôrâ* is to be received (22:22), and
  who hears the prayer of the man who returns (22:27).
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — silent. Bildad's snares (18:8–10) and
  Zophar's charge of oppressing the poor (20:19) are both absorbed into this speech. Bildad will
  speak six more verses (25:1–6); Zophar has spoken his last.

## Places

- [[Ophir]] — the gold country, named for the first time in Job at 22:24: *"the gold of Ophir among
  the stones of the brooks."* It is a byword here, not a location: the finest gold there is, to be
  thrown into a wadi bed. The book names it once more, in the hymn to wisdom (28:16), as
  one of the things that cannot buy it — [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]].
- [[Teman]] — in Eliphaz's gentilic at 22:1, for the last time until 42:7, 9.

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine's last full statement by a friend, and its most revealing: 22:10's
  *therefore* shows the inference running from suffering to sin, and 22:5–9 shows what it does when
  no sin can be found.
- [[Righteousness]] — 22:3's question whether Job's righteousness pleases God, which the prologue
  has answered; and 22:6–9's list, the inverse of the canonical description of the righteous man.
- [[Integrity]] — *tattēm dərākêkā* (22:3), the *t-m-m* root used to ask whether Job's blamelessness
  benefits God.
- [[The Fear of God]] — 22:4's *yirʾâ*: *"Is it for your piety that he reproves you?"* — the one
  question in the dialogue whose true answer is yes.
- [[Sin]] — the first specific sins attributed to Job by anyone, and all of them invented.
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — the widow, the fatherless, the hungry, the thirsty and the naked: the
  classes Scripture assigns to the righteous man's care, named here as the ones Job neglected.
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — *"stripped the naked of their clothing"* (22:6), the pledged cloak,
  against Job's 31:19–20.
- [[Hospitality]] — water to the weary and bread to the hungry (22:7), the most elementary duty of
  the house, and the one Job will swear he never refused (31:17, 32).
- [[Brotherhood]] — *"pledges from your brother"* (22:6): the law's special protection of the
  *ʾāḥ* in lending (Deuteronomy 15:7–11; 24:10–13), charged as broken.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — 22:24–25: gold of Ophir into the wadi, and God as the treasure;
  answered at 31:24–25.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — 22:12–14, a God supposedly blind behind the cloud, put in the mouth of
  a man whose complaint has been that God never stops watching.
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the darkness that keeps the wicked from seeing (22:11), the *ʿărāpel* God
  is said to hide in (22:13), and the light promised on the penitent's ways (22:28).
- [[Prayer]] — 22:27, *"You shall make your prayer to him, and he will hear you"*, fulfilled at
  42:8–10 in a prayer for Eliphaz.
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — *"You shall pay your vows"* (22:27).
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — 22:30, the innocent man's hands delivering the guilty one; the
  friends' only statement of intercession, made by the man who will need it.
- [[Judgment]] — *"that he enters with you into judgment"* (22:4), the courtroom Job has been
  demanding since chapter 9, offered back as a sentence; and the Flood generation of 22:15–16.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the misquotation of 22:13–14 and the exact quotation of 22:17–18.

## Notes

- **Why the speech is the friends' argument completed.** The step from Zophar's 20:19 to Eliphaz's
  22:6 is short and it is the whole case. The friends began with a principle (*the innocent do not
  perish*, 4:7), inferred from it that Job's suffering had a cause, described the cause generically
  (chapters 15, 18, 20), and here specify it. Nothing new has been learned about Job at any stage.
  The specifics are generated by the doctrine, which is why they are the most generic sins in the
  ancient Near Eastern catalogue — the sins of the rich man against the poor, the sins a wealthy
  sheikh of the east would most obviously have the opportunity to commit. Eliphaz has not
  investigated Job; he has asked what a man in Job's position would have done wrong.

- **The pledge.** Lending at interest to a fellow Israelite was forbidden (Exodus 22:25), and a
  pledge taken for a loan was hedged with restrictions: no millstone (Deuteronomy 24:6), no entering
  the house to take it (24:10–11), the cloak returned at nightfall because *"it is his only
  covering"* (Exodus 22:26–27), and no widow's garment at all (Deuteronomy 24:17). Amos 2:8 indicts
  those who *"lay themselves down beside every altar on clothes taken in pledge"*. Eliphaz's
  *"pledges from your brother for nothing"* describes the abuse the law most worried about: the
  security taken when nothing was owed, so that the poor man loses his cloak for a debt that does
  not exist. Job lives outside Israel and outside Sinai, but the book assumes the same moral law.

- **22:8.** *"But as for the mighty man, he had the earth. The honorable man, he lived in it."* The
  verse is obscure and the WEB's past tense is one reading of it. The *mighty man* is ***ʾîš
  zərôaʿ***, *the man of arm*, and the next verse breaks *the arms* (*zərōʿôt*) of the fatherless:
  the powerful man got the land, the weak man's arm was broken. Most interpreters read 22:8 as a
  further charge — Job favoured the great — or as a sarcastic description of Job himself. The *arm*
  returns twice: in Job's oath, *"let … my arm be broken from the bone"* (31:22), and in God's speech,
  *"From the wicked, their light is withheld. The high arm is broken"* (38:15).

- **The Flood in 22:15–16.** *"The old way … whose foundation was poured out as a stream"* names no
  event, and the wiki does not treat the allusion as certain. But *ʿôlām*, *of old*, the wicked
  *"snatched away before their time"*, a foundation *poured out* by a *nāhār*, and a speaker who
  has just threatened Job with *"floods of waters"* (22:11) together make the generation of
  Genesis 6–7 the likeliest referent, and it is the reading of most commentators ancient and modern.
  If so, Eliphaz is using the Flood as an exemplum of judgment — the use 2 Peter 2:5 and Jesus'
  *"as the days of Noah"* will make of it. See
  [[Noah and the Flood]] and [[As It Was in the Days of Noah]].

- **"Him who is not innocent" (22:30).** The Hebrew is ***ʾî-nāqî***, with *ʾî* as a negative —
  a rare use, found also in the name *Ichabod*, *ʾî-kābôd*, *no glory* (1 Samuel 4:21). The KJV took
  *ʾî* as the common noun *island* and printed *"He shall deliver the island of the innocent"*, and
  some modern versions emend to *the innocent man*, which removes the irony by removing the guilty
  party. The WEB, RSV, NIV and ESV keep the negative. The second half, *bəbōr kappêkā*, uses *bōr*,
  *cleanness* — the same noun as the *lye* of 9:30, where Job said that washing his hands with it
  would not help, *"yet you will plunge me in the ditch"*. Eliphaz promises that the cleanness Job
  says he cannot achieve will be enough to save someone else.

- **"What does God know?" (22:13).** It is worth being precise about the misquotation, because it is
  the one place the friends misrepresent Job's words rather than his deeds. Job has said that God
  does not *answer* (9:16; 19:7), that God *hides his face* (13:24), and in chapter 21 that God does
  not *punish* the wicked as the friends claim. He has never said God does not *see* or *know*. His
  word for God at 7:20 is *nōṣēr hāʾādām*, the watcher of men. Eliphaz has heard *God does not
  punish the wicked* and reported it as *Job thinks God cannot see the wicked*, which is the creed of
  the fool in the Psalms, and which turns Job's complaint about God's justice into a denial of God's
  knowledge. The difference is the difference between a lament and an apostasy.

- **The profit question has a long future.** Elihu repeats 22:2–3 almost exactly at 35:6–8 — *"If
  you are righteous, what do you give him? Or what does he receive from your hand?"* — and God
  himself asks a related question at 41:11, *"Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?"*,
  which Paul quotes at Romans 11:35. In God's mouth the point is not that Job's righteousness is
  worthless to him but that it puts him under no obligation. That distinction is what Eliphaz misses:
  God is not *indebted* by Job's integrity, and he is not *indifferent* to it either.

- **Is 22:21–30 sincere?** The wiki reads it as sincere, and as Eliphaz's best writing. Nothing in the
  passage is sarcastic, and its vocabulary — *peace*, *tôrâ*, *return*, *delight in the Almighty*,
  *light on your ways* — is the vocabulary of the Psalms and the prophets at their most generous.
  That is what makes it hard. **A man can offer a real gospel on a false premise**, and the offer
  remains real: every promise in it is kept. What is false is the price — confession of crimes not
  committed — and Job's refusal to pay it is what the epilogue calls speaking rightly.
