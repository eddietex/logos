# The Restoration of Job

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 42:7–17

## Summary

*"It was so, that after Yahweh had spoken these words to Job"* (42:7). The poetry is over, and the book
goes back to the prose of chapters 1–2: the narrator's voice, the name *Yahweh*, round numbers, and a
story told in plain sequence. The epilogue has two halves. In the first (42:7–9) God gives a verdict on
the dialogue and arranges a sacrifice. In the second (42:10–17) Job's household is rebuilt and he dies.
It is eleven verses long, and it answers almost every question the dialogue asked except the one the
reader most wants answered: Job is never told why.

**1. The verdict (42:7).**

> *"My wrath is kindled against you, and against your two friends; for you have not spoken of me the
> thing that is right, as my servant Job has."* (42:7)

God speaks to [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] by name — the first friend to speak, the longest-speaking, and
the senior of the three — and takes in [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] as *"your two
friends"*. **Elihu is not in the sentence.** There were four speakers, and the verdict is about three of
them. Whether that means Elihu's speeches were added to the book later, or that he is passed over
because he said nothing God needs to judge, the text does not say; see [[Elihu the Buzite]].

*My wrath is kindled* is *ḥārâ ʾappî*. It is the phrase the narrator used four times at 32:2–5 of Elihu's
anger — *wayyiḥar ʾap* — at Job and at the friends. Elihu was angry at both; God is angry at one side.

The charge is not that the friends spoke falsely about *Job*. Every crime in Eliphaz's list of 22:6–9 was
invented, and God does not mention it. The charge is that they did not speak rightly *about God* — the
subject they spent thirty chapters defending. **The men who defended God's justice are the ones found to
have misrepresented him, and the man who accused God of hunting him is the one who spoke rightly.** The
book's overview is built around this sentence, and so is
[[Spoken of Me the Thing That Is Right - The Verdict of Job 42.7]].

Two words in it carry the weight:

- ***Nəkônâ***, *the thing that is right*, from *kûn* — what is firm, established, set straight. Not
  *pious*, and not *orthodox*: the friends' speeches are full of orthodox sentences. What they said was
  not *true to the case*. They fitted a man to a doctrine, and the doctrine returned a false verdict on him.
- ***ʾēlay***, which the WEB gives as *of me*, is most naturally *to me*. Read that way the contrast is not
  only what was said but where it was aimed: **Job spoke to God, and the friends only spoke about him.**
  Job first addressed God at 7:7; no friend addresses God in any speech. The wiki records both readings and
  does not choose — *of me* is a recognised sense of *ʾel* after verbs of speaking, and the WEB's choice is
  defensible.

What, then, did Job say that was right? Not everything. God's first words to him were *"Who is this who
darkens counsel by words without knowledge?"* (38:2), and the second speech asked *"Will you condemn me,
that you may be justified?"* (40:8). Job himself said *"I have uttered that which I did not understand"*
(42:3). The verdict does not withdraw any of that. The readings worth keeping are:

1. **His honesty.** He refused to confess sins he had not committed, and refused to say the world was
   just when it was not treating him justly. The friends said what they believed God required; Job said
   what was so. That is the sense of *nəkônâ*.
2. **His last words.** *Has* (*kəʿabdî ʾiyyôb*) is unmarked for time, and some read it as referring only to
   42:1–6, the confession that came just before. Against it: the verdict is given *to the friends*, about
   the whole dialogue they took part in.
3. **His address.** If *ʾēlay* means *to me*, what was right was the turning toward God itself — the
   lawsuit, the protest, the demand to be answered — and the friends' failure was to talk about a God they
   never once spoke to.

The readings are not exclusive, and none of them makes God's rebuke at 38:2 a mistake. **Job spoke without
knowledge and spoke rightly**: the book holds both, and the wiki records the tension rather than resolving it.
See [[Words Without Knowledge - Job 34.35, 35.16, 38.2, and 42.3]].

The verdict is said **twice**, word for word, at the end of 42:7 and again at the end of 42:8. And **God calls
Job *my servant* four times in two verses** (42:7, 8 ×3) — the title he used of him at 1:8 and 2:3, in the
heavenly court, before anything happened. The certificate the accuser challenged is reissued, in the
hearing of the men who doubted it, after the test.

**2. The sacrifice and the prayer (42:8–9).** *"Take to yourselves seven bulls and seven rams, and go to my
servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering; and my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will
accept him, that I not deal with you according to your folly."* (42:8)

- **The offering is large.** Seven bulls and seven rams is the scale of Balaam's altars (Numbers 23:1) and
  of the Levites' sacrifice when the ark was brought up (1 Chronicles 15:26). It is the burnt offering, the
  *ʿōlâ*, which Job offered for his children at 1:5 *"according to the number of them all"*. **Job is a
  priest again at the end of the book, as at the beginning** — for his children, on the hypothesis that
  they *might* have sinned; now for his friends, on God's word that they *have*. There is no priesthood, no
  altar named, and no sanctuary. See [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].
- ***I will accept him*** is *pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, literally *I will lift his face*. It is the idiom of
  partiality that Job threw at the friends at 13:8–10 — *"Will you show partiality to him?… He will surely
  reprove you if you secretly show partiality"* — when he said they were lifting God's face, flattering him
  in court. **God has done what Job said he would: reproved them.** And he lifts *Job's* face. Zophar had
  promised Job, on condition of repentance, *"Surely then you shall lift up your face without spot"*
  (11:15); Eliphaz had promised *"You shall lift up your face to God"* (22:26). Both promises come true with
  the promisers in the queue. See [[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]].
- ***According to your folly*** is *nəbālâ*. Job's word for his wife's sentence at 2:10 was the same root —
  *"You speak as one of the foolish women (nəbālôt) would speak"*. **The verdict Job passed on her speech,
  God passes on the friends'.** See [[Job's Wife]].
- **The prayer is Job's.** *"My servant Job shall pray for you"* — *yitpallēl*, the verb of Genesis 20:7,
  where God told a foreign king that Abraham *"is a prophet, and he will pray for you, and you will live"*.
  Both times God sends the party in the wrong to the person they wronged, and makes that person's prayer
  their remedy. See [[A Prophet Who Prays - The First Nabi in Scripture]] and [[Abraham and Abimelech]].

Eliphaz had asked at 5:1, *"To which of the holy ones will you turn?"* — meaning that no one could stand
between Job and God. At 42:8 **Eliphaz is the one who needs someone to stand between, and the someone is
Job.** The umpire Job could not find (9:33), the witness he hoped was in heaven (16:19), and the Redeemer he
said lives (19:25) were all figures who would plead *his* case. The book ends with Job pleading someone
else's. See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

42:9 lists the three by their full names and homes, as at 2:11 — *"Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the
Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite"* — and says they *did what Yahweh commanded them*. **They do not speak.**
None of them answers, explains, or apologises; the last words any of them says in the book are Bildad's
*"man, who is a worm"* (25:6). *"And Yahweh accepted Job"* — lifted his face.

**3. The turn (42:10).** *"Yahweh turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends. Yahweh gave Job
twice as much as he had before."*

- ***Turned the captivity*** is *šāb ʾet-šəbût*, a fixed phrase: *restored the fortunes* in most modern
  versions. Elsewhere it is almost always said of Israel or Zion — Deuteronomy 30:3, Psalm 14:7, Psalm 126:4,
  Jeremiah 29:14, Amos 9:14 — and the WEB keeps the older *captivity* here. **Job is restored in the words
  the prophets use for the return from exile.** He was never a captive, and the phrase does not need him to
  have been; but it makes his ash-heap an exile and his restoration a homecoming. See
  [[Yahweh Turned the Captivity of Job - Restoration and the Double]].
- ***When he prayed for his friends*** — *bəhitpallǝlô*. **The order is the point.** The restoration does not
  follow Job's confession (42:1–6); it follows his prayer for the men who wronged him. Nothing in the
  narrative is given back while Job is speaking for himself. It is given back while he is speaking for them.
- ***Twice as much*** — *ləmišneh*, the double. Exodus 22 makes *double* the restitution a thief pays — *"he
  shall pay double"* (22:4, 7) — and in 22:9 the double is paid by the party *"whom God condemns"*, after
  *"the cause of both parties shall come before God"*. Some readers see that law behind 42:10: Job sued for a
  hearing, got one, and was paid double, as a man is who has been wronged. **The wiki records the reading and
  does not adopt it**: God is not the party condemned in Job, and nothing in the epilogue says he is paying a
  debt. Isaiah's *double* (40:2; 61:7) and Zechariah's (9:12) — the double of comfort after exile — are the
  nearer parallels to *turned the captivity*.

**4. The comforters who come at last (42:11).** *"Then came there to him all his brothers, and all his sisters,
and all those who had been of his acquaintance before, and ate bread with him in his house. They comforted
him, and consoled him concerning all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him."*

- **These are the people of 19:13–14.** *"He has put my brothers far from me. My acquaintances are wholly
  estranged from me"* — and the same nouns come back here: *brothers*, and *those who had been of his
  acquaintance* (*yōdəʿāyw*, against 19:13's *yōdəʿay*). **The estranged return, but only after God has
  spoken and the fortunes have turned.** The narrator does not comment on the timing.
- ***Comforted him, and consoled him*** — *wayyānudû lô wayənaḥămû ʾōtô*: the two verbs of 2:11, where the
  three friends came *"to sympathize with him and to comfort him"* (*lānûd-lô ûlənaḥămô*), in reverse order.
  **The mission the friends set out on in chapter 2 is accomplished in chapter 42 — by other people.** And
  *nḥm* is the verb of Job's own last line, *"repent (niḥamtî) in dust and ashes"* (42:6). See
  [[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].
- ***Ate bread with him in his house*** — the household meal of 1:4, where his children ate *"in the house of
  each one"*. The house that fell on them (1:19) has a table again.
- ***All the evil that Yahweh had brought on him*** — the narrator's last word on the cause. **No accuser.**
  The figure who proposed the test and struck the blows (1:12; 2:7) is not named in the epilogue; the
  narrator says, in his own voice, that the evil was Yahweh's. That is what Job had said throughout —
  *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away"* (1:21); *"Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we
  not receive evil?"* (2:10) — and the friends had never contested it. **The book's final attribution agrees
  with Job's first.** See [[Satan]] and
  [[Shall We Receive Good and Not Evil - Weal and Woe from One Hand]].
- ***A piece of money*** is a *qəśîṭâ*, a unit found in only two other places in Scripture: the hundred
  *pieces of money* Jacob paid for his field at Shechem (Genesis 33:19; Joshua 24:32). It is an archaic
  word, and with it the epilogue dresses Job in the patriarchs' world. ***A ring of gold*** is a *nezem
  zāhāb* — the gold ring Abraham's servant gave Rebekah at the well (Genesis 24:22, *nezem zāhāb*) and the
  rings Jacob's household buried at Shechem (35:4). See [[The Reconciliation with Esau]].

**5. The latter end (42:12).** *"So Yahweh blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning."* The
inventory of 1:3 is doubled exactly, item by item:

| Job 1:3 | Job 42:12 |
|---|---|
| seven thousand sheep | fourteen thousand sheep |
| three thousand camels | six thousand camels |
| five hundred yoke of oxen | one thousand yoke of oxen |
| five hundred female donkeys | a thousand female donkeys |
| *"a very great household"* | — not mentioned |

*Latter end* and *beginning* are *ʾaḥărît* and *rēʾšît* — **Bildad's two nouns, in Bildad's order**: *"Though
your beginning (rēʾšîtkā) was small, yet your latter end (ʾaḥărîtkā) would greatly increase"* (8:7). Bildad's
promise is fulfilled in its own words, and its condition — *"If you were pure and upright"* (8:6) — had been
met since 1:1. *Blessed* (*bērak*) is the verb the accuser said Job would turn into a curse (1:11), and the
verb Job used when he lost everything: *"Blessed be Yahweh's name"* (1:21). The first blessing in the book
was Job's, given back to God with nothing in his hands. The last is God's.

**6. The children (42:13–15).** *"He had also seven sons and three daughters."* **Not doubled.** Every animal
count is twice that of 1:3; the children come back at the original number. [[Job's Children]] sets out the
three readings of that asymmetry — persons are not stock; the first ten are counted as living; the naming is
the compensation — and adopts none of them. The restoration is real, and it is not an answer to the deaths
of 1:19.

The sons are not named. **The daughters are**, and nobody else in the book's last generation is: *Jemimah*,
*Keziah*, and *Keren-happuch* — commonly glossed *dove*, *cassia*, and *horn of eye-paint*, a bird and two
things of beauty. The narrator adds that no women in the land were as beautiful, and that *"their father gave
them an inheritance among their brothers"* (42:15). Under the law of Numbers 27 daughters inherit only where
there are no sons; Job's daughters inherit beside seven brothers. **The man who had nothing gives more than he
is required to.** See [[An Inheritance Among Their Brothers - Job 42.15 and the Daughters of Zelophehad]].

The first daughters were sent for to eat and drink with their brothers (1:4) and died with them. Nothing in
the text says who the mother of the second ten is. [[Job's Wife]] is not mentioned in the epilogue at all.

**7. A long life (42:16–17).** *"After this Job lived one hundred forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons'
sons, to four generations. So Job died, being old and full of days."*

- **A hundred and forty** is twice seventy, the span of Psalm 90:10 — *"The days of our years are seventy"* —
  and the doubling may be meant to reach the years as well as the flocks. The text does not say so, and the
  wiki notes it as an observation only.
- ***Saw his sons, and his sons' sons*** answers 14:21, where the dead man's *"sons come to honor, and he
  doesn't know it"*, and 21:8, where it is the *wicked* whose *"child is established with them in their
  sight"*. Joseph lived to see Ephraim's children *"to the third generation"* (Genesis 50:23); Psalm 128:6
  makes *"may you see your children's children"* a blessing. **Job is given the sight he said the dead and
  the righteous did not get.** *Saw* is *rāʾâ*, the verb of 42:5. See
  [[Now My Eye Sees You - Hearing and Seeing in Job]].
- ***Old and full of days*** — *zāqēn ûśəbaʿ yāmîm*. Isaac died *"old and full of days"* (Genesis 35:29);
  Abraham *"in a good old age, an old man, and full of years"* (25:8). **Job dies a patriarch's death in the
  patriarchs' words.** It is also Eliphaz's 5:26, *"You shall come to your grave in a full age"* — fulfilled,
  but not in Eliphaz's vocabulary. See
  [[Buried in a Good Old Age - The Promise at Genesis 15.15 and Its Fulfilment]].

**What the epilogue does not do.** It does not tell Job about the wager. It does not bring the accuser back,
or return to the heavenly court to report the result; the scene of 1:6–12 has no sequel, and the reader alone
knows that the question *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* (1:9) was answered at 42:1–6 before anything was
restored. It does not narrate Job's healing; the sores of 2:7 are simply never mentioned again. It does not
mention Elihu, Job's wife, or the dead children. And **it does not explain the suffering.** The poem ended
with God answering Job without giving a reason; the prose ends with God restoring Job without giving one.

**Is the ending a retreat?** The objection is old: after forty chapters dismantling the idea that God pays
the righteous in health and cattle, the book pays Job in health and cattle. Three replies are worth keeping:

1. **The order breaks the doctrine even while delivering its outcome.** In the friends' scheme restoration
   pays for repentance. Here the restoration follows a verdict that the friends were wrong, and a prayer Job
   offers *for them*. **Job is not restored for confessing sin; he is restored after being declared right.**
   The friends predicted the ending and misnamed its cause.
2. **Job asked for vindication in this life.** *"I know that my Redeemer lives. In the end, he will stand
   upon the earth"* (19:25); *"Earth, don't cover my blood"* (16:18). The vindication he wanted was public and
   before his death, and that is what 42:7–17 is: a verdict in front of the friends, and the estranged family
   back at his table. See [[Sheol]] and [[The Witness in Heaven]].
3. **The gift comes after the test is over, not as its payment.** The accuser's question was whether Job's
   fear of God was bought. It was answered at 42:5–6 by a man with nothing. What follows is given *ḥinnām* in
   the other direction: not wages, but blessing, from a God who has already been feared for nothing.

None of these makes the ending simple, and many readers take the prose frame to be an older folk-tale that
the poet kept and set his poem inside. The wiki takes the book as received, and records that the frame and
the poem disagree in tone more than they disagree in substance: **the poem says the righteous may suffer
without cause; the epilogue says this righteous man was not left there.** James 5:11 reads the ending exactly
that way — *"You have heard of the perseverance of Job, and have seen the Lord in the outcome, and how the
Lord is full of compassion and mercy."*

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — declared by God to have spoken rightly, twice; called *my servant* four times; priest and
  intercessor for the three friends; restored *when he prayed for his friends* (42:10); the father of ten again,
  and of three named daughters who inherit; dies *old and full of days*.
- [[God]] — *Yahweh*, the narrator's name for him again; gives the verdict, sets the sacrifice, accepts Job's
  prayer, *turned the captivity*, doubles the goods, and is named by the narrator as the one who brought *"all
  the evil"* (42:11). Gives no reason.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — addressed by name: *"My wrath is kindled against you"*. His 5:1 reversed (Job is
  the one he must turn to), his 5:19–26 and 22:26–30 fulfilled with himself as the man delivered.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — one of the *"two friends"*; his 8:7 fulfilled in its own words (42:12).
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — one of the *"two friends"*; his *lift up your face* (11:15) fulfilled for Job,
  with Zophar among the petitioners.
- [[Elihu the Buzite]] — not mentioned. Neither rebuked nor commended.
- [[Satan]] — not mentioned. The narrator attributes the evil to Yahweh (42:11).
- [[Job's Children]] — seven sons and three daughters again; the daughters named and given an inheritance.
  The first ten are not mentioned.
- [[Job's Wife]] — not mentioned.
- [[Abraham]] — not named; his prayer for Abimelech (Genesis 20:7, 17) is the pattern of 42:8–10, and his death
  *"full of years"* (Genesis 25:8) the pattern of 42:17.
- [[Isaac]] — not named; *"old and full of days"* (Genesis 35:29) is said of him and of Job.
- [[Jacob]] — not named; the *qəśîṭâ* of 42:11 is the coin he paid at Shechem (Genesis 33:19).

## Places

- [[Uz]] — not named in the epilogue, and the setting throughout: Job's house, with a table again.
- [[Teman]] — Eliphaz *the Temanite*, named at 42:7 and 42:9.
- [[Shuah]] — Bildad *the Shuhite* (42:9).
- [[Naamah]] — Zophar *the Naamathite* (42:9).

## Themes

- [[Righteousness]] — *nəkônâ*, *the thing that is right* (42:7–8): the verdict on the dialogue, given to the
  man who said he was in the right.
- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine's outcome delivered, its mechanism refused: restoration after a verdict and an
  intercession, not after a confession.
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — the man who wanted an umpire becomes one; *"my servant Job shall pray for you"*.
- [[Prayer]] — *yitpallēl* (42:8, 10): Job's first recorded prayer *for* anyone, and the hinge of the restoration.
- [[Sacrifice and Offering]] — seven bulls and seven rams, a burnt offering, Job as priest (1:5; 42:8).
- [[Judgment]] — God's wrath *kindled* (42:7) and turned aside by intercession.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the verdict on what was *spoken* (42:7, 8); the friends silent.
- [[Integrity]] — the integrity of 2:3 and 27:5 vindicated by the one witness who can vindicate it.
- [[Blessing]] — *"Yahweh blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning"* (42:12), against the curse
  predicted at 1:11.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — the inventory of 1:3 doubled exactly (42:12).
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — the comfort of 2:11 given at last, by family (42:11).
- [[Hope]] — the hope of a tree that sprouts (14:7) and of vindication before death (19:25–27), answered in events.
- [[Suffering]] — not explained; *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him"* (42:11).
- [[Life and Death]] — 140 years, four generations, *old and full of days*.
- [[Testing]] — the test is over and its result is never reported to the court that set it.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"saw his sons, and his sons' sons"* (42:16), after *"now my eye sees you"* (42:5).
- [[Birthright and the Firstborn]] — daughters given an inheritance among their brothers (42:15); the sons, for once,
  unnamed and unranked.
- [[Names and Naming]] — the daughters named, the sons not.
- [[Divine Grief and Relenting]] — *nḥm*, *comforted* (42:11), the verb of 42:6.

## Notes

- **Boundary.** The plan's 42:7–17 is kept. 42:7 (*"It was so, that after Yahweh had spoken these words to
  Job"*) returns to prose. 42:7–9 (verdict) and 42:10–17 (restoration) could be two pericopes; they are kept
  together because 42:10's *"when he prayed for his friends"* ties the restoration to the verdict's sacrifice,
  and splitting them would put the hinge of the epilogue on a page break. **This is the last pericope in Job.**
- **42:7, *ʾēlay*.** *To me* or *of/about me*. The WEB's *of me* is followed; *to me* is recorded in the summary
  as a reading the wiki does not adopt or reject.
- **42:7, *has*.** The verdict's tense is not fixed by the Hebrew, and the claim that it refers only to 42:1–6 is
  recorded, not adopted.
- **42:10, the double and Exodus 22.** Recorded as a reading. The wiki notes the problem with it (God is not the
  condemned party) and does not build on it.
- **42:13, *šibʿānâ*.** The word for *seven* in *seven sons* has an unusual form, and some Jewish interpreters
  (the Targum among them, by report) read it as *twice seven* — fourteen sons, which would make the children
  doubled too. The wiki has not checked the Targum and records the reading only as reported; the WEB, with most
  translations, reads *seven*.
- **42:14, the daughters' names.** The glosses *dove*, *cassia*, and *horn of eye-paint* are the usual ones and
  are not certain; *Jemimah* is sometimes taken as *daytime* instead of *dove*. See [[Job's Children]].
- **42:15 and Numbers 27:7.** *"The daughters of Zelophehad speak right"* uses *kēn*, from the same root *kûn* as
  42:7's *nəkônâ*. The coincidence of *speaking right* and *daughters' inheritance* in the two passages is noted
  as an observation; the wiki does not claim the epilogue alludes to Numbers.
- **42:16, 140 years.** Twice seventy (Psalm 90:10) is an observation. The Septuagint gives different figures
  for Job's age; they have not been checked and are not quoted.
- **The Septuagint's appendix.** After 42:17 the Greek adds a statement about Job's resurrection and a genealogy
  identifying him with Jobab of Edom. It is not in the Hebrew or the WEB; see
  [[Jobab of Bozrah and the Septuagint's Job]]. Not quoted here, since it has not been checked against a text.
- **Job's healing.** Never narrated. The epilogue's silence about the sores of 2:7 is noted, not explained.
- **On contradictions.** **No wiki page is contradicted.** One tension is recorded rather than resolved: God says
  Job spoke *"without knowledge"* (38:2) and that he spoke *"the thing that is right"* (42:7). Both are in the text,
  and every page that quotes one of them is left as it is. [[Words Without Knowledge - Job 34.35, 35.16, 38.2, and 42.3]]
  already frames the question; it is updated, not corrected.
