# Eliphaz's Second Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 15:1–35

## Summary

The second cycle of speeches opens, and the first thing to notice is what Eliphaz has stopped
saying. His first speech (Job 4–5) had opened with an apology for speaking at all — *"If someone
ventures to talk with you, will you be grieved?"* — and it had ended with thirteen verses of
promised restoration: the man God corrects is happy, God wounds and binds up, you will come to
your grave in a full age like a shock of grain in its season. None of that returns here. Chapter
15 contains no offer of healing, no conditional *if you seek God*, and no future for Job at all.
It contains a rebuke, an attack on Job's standing to speak, and a nineteen-verse portrait of the
doom of the wicked man — a portrait built, detail by detail, out of what has already happened to
Job.

**The rebuke (15:1–6) makes Job's own speech the evidence against him.** Eliphaz's opening image
is meteorological and contemptuous: Job has filled himself with *"the east wind"*, the sirocco off
the desert that scorches crops and drives men to distraction. Wise men do not answer with wind.
Then the charge escalates from foolish to dangerous — *"you do away with fear, and hinder devotion
before God"* — which is the first time anyone has told Job that his protest is not merely wrong but
corrosive of religion itself. And in verse 5 the logic closes: *"your iniquity teaches your mouth"*.
Job's words are not an argument to be met; they are a symptom to be diagnosed. Verse 6 states the
method outright — *"Your own mouth condemns you, and not I"* — and it is worth pausing on, because
it is the move that makes the rest of the dialogue unwinnable. Job's protest of innocence is now
itself proof of guilt. The only speech that could clear him is one he cannot make without lying.

**The attack on his standing (15:7–16) is the sharpest passage in the chapter, and its opening is
a taunt with a long reach.** *"Are you the first man who was born? Or were you brought out before
the hills?"* Eliphaz is invoking a tradition of a primeval man born before the world was finished
and possessed of knowledge from its founding — and the second half of the couplet uses the verb of
being brought forth in labour that Proverbs 8:25 will use of Wisdom herself, *"before the mountains
were settled, before the hills, I was brought forth"*. The taunt is: you speak as though you were
Wisdom, present at the making of things. Then verse 8 names the thing Job would have to have
attended to speak this way: *"Have you heard the secret counsel of God?"* — the *sôd*, the divine
council where verdicts are settled before they are executed.

That verse is where the book's central irony becomes unbearable. **The reader has been in the
council.** Job 1:6–12 and 2:1–6 put us inside it, and we know what was said there: that Job is
blameless, that the affliction was proposed by the accuser and permitted by God, and that it is a
test of disinterested faith rather than a punishment for anything. Job has not heard the council,
which is exactly why he cannot explain what is happening to him. But neither has Eliphaz — and
Eliphaz is the one claiming to know what the council decided. He asks a rhetorical question whose
real answer is *no, and neither have you*, and builds an argument on his own ignorance.

The credentials he offers instead are age and consensus: *"With us are both the gray-headed and the
very aged men, much elder than your father"* (15:10). This is worth marking because it is a
different authority from the one he used last time. In chapter 4 Eliphaz staked everything on a
private night vision — a spirit passing before his face, the hair of his flesh standing up, a
voice. That did not move Job, so here he switches to received tradition, and in verses 17–19 he
will fortify the switch by insisting the tradition is uncontaminated: handed down by fathers, in a
land *"to whom alone the land was given, and no stranger passed among them"*. Pure pedigree,
unadulterated by outsiders. Elihu will later turn precisely this claim over: *"It is not the great
who are wise, nor the aged who understand justice"* (32:9).

Verses 14–16 are the theological core, and they are the second of three nearly identical statements
in this book about human uncleanness — Eliphaz's own at 4:17–19, this one, and Bildad's at 25:4–6,
each more absolute than the last. *"What is man, that he should be clean? What is he who is born of
a woman, that he should be righteous?"* The argument runs downward from heaven: God does not trust
his holy ones, the heavens themselves are not clean in his sight, *"how much less one who is
abominable and corrupt, a man who drinks iniquity like water!"* Taken as a general statement about
humanity before God this is not far from what Genesis 6:5, Psalm 143:2, and Romans 3:23 say, and
that is the difficulty. **The friends' anthropology is largely orthodox; their use of it is not.**
They are deploying a universal truth as a particular diagnosis — since no one is clean, Job's
suffering needs no further explanation — and the effect is to make the doctrine unfalsifiable and
pastorally lethal. God's verdict at 42:7 falls on these men, not on their anthropology.

**The portrait of the wicked (15:17–35) is the longest movement, and it is about Job without
naming him.** Read the details against the prologue. The wicked man hears *"a sound of terrors"*
and *"in prosperity the destroyer shall come on him"* (15:21) — Job was at ease when four
messengers arrived in a single afternoon. *"fire shall consume the tents of bribery"* (15:34) —
*"The fire of God has fallen from the sky"* is what the second messenger said (1:16). *"He shall
not be rich, neither shall his substance continue"* (15:29) — the greatest of the children of the
east lost seven thousand sheep and three thousand camels in a day. *"He has covered his face with
his fatness"* (15:27) is the prosperous man; *"his branch shall not be green"* and *"he shall shake
off his unripe grape"* (15:32–33) are the loss of children before their time. Eliphaz never says
*you*. He does not have to.

And the portrait's opening claim is a direct contradiction of what Job can see: *"the wicked man
writhes in pain all his days"* (15:20). That is the doctrine of retribution stated as an empirical
observation about the world, and Job will devote the whole of chapter 21 to answering it with the
opposite observation — *"Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow mighty in power?"* The
two men are not disagreeing about God's character in the abstract. They are disagreeing about what
happens on the ground, and one of them is describing the world and the other is describing a
system.

What the chapter accomplishes, then, is a hardening. The second cycle has begun by withdrawing the
comfort the first cycle offered, and Job's answer in the next two chapters will register the
withdrawal precisely: *"You are all miserable comforters!"*

## People

- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — the first and most substantial of the three friends, speaking for the
  second of three times. This chapter is where his tone turns: the courtesy of 4:2 and the promised
  restoration of 5:17–27 are gone, and what replaces them is a diagnosis. He is also the friend God
  addresses by name at 42:7, which makes his speeches the ones the book most wants weighed.
- [[Job (person)]] — silent throughout, addressed in every verse of 15:2–16, and described without
  being named in 15:20–35. He is spoken *about* far more than he is spoken *to*.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — present but silent, and included in
  Eliphaz's *"with us"* (15:9–10) and *"we"*. The friends speak as a bloc, which is why Job answers
  them in the plural in the next chapter.
- [[God]] — named as *God* (*ʾēl*, *ʾĕlôah*) and *the Almighty* (*šadday*), never as *Yahweh*.
  The covenant name appears in the prose frame of Job and almost never in the poetry; the debate is
  conducted in the vocabulary of a patriarchal, non-Israelite setting. Here God is the one whose
  *sôd* is inaccessible (15:8), who distrusts even his holy ones (15:15), and whose breath removes
  the wicked (15:30).
- [[Adam]] — not named, but 15:7's *"the first man who was born"* is a reach back to him, and to a
  wider tradition of a primeval man with founding knowledge. Eliphaz means it as an absurdity.

## Places

- [[Teman]] — carried in Eliphaz's gentilic rather than by any event in the chapter. It matters
  more than a name usually does here, because Teman was proverbial for wisdom (Jeremiah 49:7,
  Obadiah 8–9), and Eliphaz's whole argument in 15:17–19 is an appeal to inherited tradition. The
  book gives its most confident traditionalist a hometown famous for exactly that.

The speech names no other location. Its geography is entirely rhetorical: *"before the hills"*
(15:7), *"the heavens"* (15:15), *"desolate cities … houses which no one inhabited"* (15:28), and
*"the tents of bribery"* (15:34).

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — the chapter is the doctrine's fullest statement so far in the book. 15:20's
  *"the wicked man writhes in pain all his days"* asserts it as a fact about the observable world,
  and 15:20–35 spells out the mechanism in nineteen verses. Everything the friends will say from
  here rests on this.
- [[Wisdom]] — contested throughout. Eliphaz opens by denying Job the title (*"Should a wise man
  answer with vain knowledge"*), asks whether he has *"heard the secret counsel of God"*, and
  grounds his own claim in the aged and in a tradition kept free of strangers. Job's answer at
  17:10 will be *"I shall not find a wise man among you"*.
- [[Sin]] — 15:14–16 gives the friends' anthropology at its most absolute: not merely that people
  sin but that no one *born of a woman* can be clean, and that a man *"drinks iniquity like
  water"*. The claim is close to orthodox and is being used to close a case rather than open one.
- [[Righteousness]] — 15:14 asks whether anyone born of a woman can be *ṣaddîq*. The reader
  already has the answer for this particular man: God said it twice, at 1:8 and 2:3, before any
  human being in the book spoke.
- [[Suffering]] — treated here purely as evidence. In Eliphaz's second speech pain has no function
  except to reveal what the sufferer is, which is why the consolation of the first speech had to
  go.
- [[Judgment]] — the portrait of 15:20–35 is a judgment scene distributed across a lifetime:
  terror, want, the sword waiting, fire in the tents, a branch that does not stay green.

## Notes

- **The east wind (15:2).** *Rûaḥ qādîm* is the sirocco, and in the Hebrew Bible it is never
  neutral: it withers Pharaoh's grain (Genesis 41:6), splits the sea (Exodus 14:21), destroys the
  ships of Tarshish (Psalm 48:7), and dries up Jonah's shade (Jonah 4:8). The insult works on two
  levels at once, because *rûaḥ* is also *spirit* and *breath*. A wise man filled with the east
  wind is a man full of hot air, and a man whose spirit is a destroying one — which is what verse
  13, *"you turn your spirit against God"*, then says outright.

- **"Have you heard the secret counsel of God?" (15:8).** *Sôd* means both the council itself and
  the confidential decision it reaches; it is the assembly of 1 Kings 22:19–23, Psalm 82:1, and
  Jeremiah 23:18, 22 — where the test of a true prophet is precisely whether he *"stood in the
  council of Yahweh"*. Eliphaz's question is meant as a reductio, and it is the single most ironic
  line in the dialogue, because the book has opened by admitting the reader to that council and
  showing that its verdict was the opposite of the one Eliphaz is defending. The verse also raises
  a structural question the wiki will meet again: nothing said in Job 3–37 is informed by Job 1–2.
  The dialogue is watertight. No character ever learns what the reader knows, and God's answer from
  the whirlwind does not tell them either.

- **From night vision to tradition (4:12–17 → 15:17–19).** Eliphaz's two speeches ground the same
  doctrine in two different authorities, and the shift is worth naming because it is a familiar
  pattern in argument, not merely a feature of this chapter. His first appeal was to private
  revelation — a thing that came *"secretly"*, a form he could not discern, a voice. His second is
  to public inheritance: what wise men told, from their fathers, in a land no stranger passed
  through. When the vision failed to persuade, the ancestors were called. Two observations follow.
  The first is that the content did not change between the two appeals — 4:17 and 15:14 say the
  same thing — which suggests the authority was chosen to fit the conclusion. The second is that
  the pedigree claim in 15:19 is doing more work than it looks: *"no stranger passed among them"*
  presents insulation from outside testimony as a guarantee of truth, in a book whose entire cast,
  Job included, is foreign to Israel.

- **"Drinks iniquity like water" (15:16).** The image recurs at 34:7 in Elihu's mouth (*"who drinks
  scorn like water"*), and its force is that it makes wrongdoing appetitive and habitual rather
  than occasional — not a man who does evil but a man for whom evil is a thirst. Paired with
  *"born of a woman"* in verse 14, it gives the friends' position both halves of what later
  theology will call original and actual sin, without any of the machinery for distinguishing them.

- **What is actually wrong with 15:14–16.** It is important to be exact here, because the easy
  reading — *the friends are wrong, therefore what they say is false* — is not what the book
  supports and not what the rest of Scripture supports. Three things can be said. **(1) The
  propositions are largely defensible.** That no human being is clean before God is asserted by
  Genesis 6:5, 1 Kings 8:46, Psalm 130:3 and 143:2, Proverbs 20:9, Ecclesiastes 7:20, and Romans
  3:10–23. **(2) The inference is invalid.** From *no one is perfectly clean* it does not follow
  that a given calamity is a given man's due, and the book has already ruled out that inference by
  telling us in advance what caused this calamity. **(3) The verdict falls on the men, not the
  propositions.** Job 42:7 says Eliphaz did not speak rightly *of God* — the fault located in the
  portrait of God the argument produces, a God whose dealings can be read off a ledger. The wiki's
  standing position, recorded on [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]], is that the friends'
  error is one of application and pastoral use, not primarily of doctrine, and that this is the
  harder and more useful lesson.

- **The portrait of the wicked as a portrait of Job.** The correspondences listed in the summary
  are close enough that most commentators read 15:20–35 as deliberate indirect accusation, and the
  reading does not depend on any single detail. Two cautions are worth keeping. The first is that
  much of the imagery is stock — the fate-of-the-wicked catalogue is a fixed wisdom form, and
  Bildad's version at 18:5–21 and Zophar's at 20:4–29 draw on the same stock, which means some
  overlap with Job's losses is inevitable rather than pointed. The second is that Eliphaz never
  makes the accusation explicit until his third speech, where he finally does name crimes (22:6–9:
  taking pledges from your brother, stripping the naked of their clothing, withholding bread and
  water, sending widows away empty) — and those charges are invented, with nothing in the book
  supporting them. The trajectory across the three speeches is from courtesy, to insinuation, to
  fabrication.

- **Verse 30's "breath of God's mouth".** *"By the breath of God's mouth shall he go away"* repeats
  4:9, *"By the breath of God they perish. By the blast of his anger are they consumed"*. It is the
  same *rûaḥ* the chapter opened with as an insult, now as the instrument of the wicked man's end,
  and the same word that will carry God's own arrival in the whirlwind at 38:1. Job's book is
  unusually careful with this word.

- **The text of 15:23 and 15:29–33.** Job's Hebrew is among the most difficult in the Bible — the
  book has an unusually high proportion of rare words and unique forms, and the ancient versions
  frequently disagree. Verse 23's *"He wanders abroad for bread, saying, 'Where is it?'"* is a
  defensible rendering of a clause the Septuagint read quite differently, and the end of verse 29
  (*"neither shall their possessions be extended on the earth"*) is obscure in the Hebrew and is
  rendered variously across modern versions. None of this touches the argument of the chapter, but
  it is worth recording once, early in the book, that where this wiki quotes Job it is quoting the
  WEB's construal of a frequently uncertain text.
