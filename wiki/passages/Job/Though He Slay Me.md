# Though He Slay Me

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 12:1–14:22

## Summary

Zophar has just told Job that God *"exacts of you less than your iniquity deserves"* (11:6), and
that the measure of God is higher than heaven and deeper than Sheol, so that a man like Job had
better stop talking. **Job's answer is the longest speech of the first cycle — seventy-five verses —
and it closes the cycle by doing three things in order.** He takes the friends' wisdom away from
them (chapter 12); he dismisses them as witnesses and turns to God as the only party worth
addressing (chapter 13); and he speaks to God about the shortness of human life until hope itself
is worn away like stone under water (chapter 14). The title is the most famous line in it, and —
as the Notes explain — the line is famous in a form the WEB does not print.

**1. "Wisdom shall die with you" (12:1–6).** *"No doubt, but you are the people, and wisdom shall
die with you"* (12:2). The sarcasm is the first in the book that is aimed at the friends rather
than at God, and it is answered by what follows: *"But I have understanding as well as you; I am
not inferior to you. Yes, who doesn't know such things as these?"* (12:3). Job repeats the
sentence word for word at 13:2 — *lōʾ-nōpēl ʾānōkî mikkem*, *I do not fall below you* — so that
the whole of chapter 12 sits between two declarations of equal standing. Eliphaz will take up the
challenge at once: *"What do you know, that we don't know?"* (15:9).

Then 12:4, which is the first time anyone in the dialogue has stated Job's social situation in
plain terms: *"I am like one who is a joke to his neighbor, I, who called on God, and he answered.
The just, the blameless man is a joke."* ***Ṣaddîq tāmîm*** — *the righteous, the blameless* — the
two words of the narrator's first verdict (1:1, *tām wəyāšār*; and Genesis 6:9's *ṣaddîq tāmîm* of
Noah, the same pair exactly). **Job uses Noah's epithet of himself as a laughing-stock.** And the
reason he gives is the one the reader has watched happen: he *called on God and he answered* — he
was the man whose prayers worked, who offered for his children (1:5) — and now the doctrine the
friends hold has turned that history into a joke. *"In the thought of him who is at ease there is
contempt for misfortune"* (12:5): the comfortable despise the man whose foot slips, because his
slipping confirms their comfort. *"The tents of robbers prosper. Those who provoke God are secure"*
(12:6) — the first plain statement of the counter-evidence that chapter 21 will develop at length,
and the answer to Zophar's *"Don't let unrighteousness dwell in your tents"* (11:14). See
[[The Prosperity of the Wicked]].

**2. "Ask the animals" (12:7–12).** *"But ask the animals, now, and they shall teach you; the birds
of the sky, and they shall tell you. Or speak to the earth, and it shall teach you. The fish of the
sea shall declare to you."* Zophar had measured God by four regions — heaven, Sheol, earth, sea
(11:8–9). **Job answers with four classes of creature — beasts, birds, earth, fish — the four of Genesis
1:26's dominion, in a different order**, and says that every one of them already knows what Zophar
announced as a secret of wisdom. What they know is this:

> *"Who doesn't know that in all these, Yahweh's hand has done this, in whose hand is the life of
> every living thing, and the breath of all mankind?"* (12:9–10)

**This is the only time the name *Yahweh* occurs in the speeches of the poetic dialogue (3:1–37:24),
Job's, the friends', and Elihu's.** Every other speech, Job's and
the friends', says *ʾĕlôah*, *ʾēl*, or *Šadday*. Several Hebrew manuscripts read *ʾĕlôah* here too,
and the anomaly is usually explained by the fact that *"the hand of Yahweh has done this"* is a fixed
phrase — it occurs word for word in Isaiah 41:20 (*kî yad-yhwh ʿāśətâ zōʾt*), of the desert made to
bloom. The wiki keeps the Masoretic text and the WEB: the one time the covenant name surfaces in the
debate, Job says it, in a proverb, about creation. See [[The Names of God]].

12:10's *nepeš kol-ḥāy*, *the life of every living thing*, and *rûaḥ kol-bəśar-ʾîš*, *the breath of
all flesh of man*, are the two words of Genesis 2:7 and 6:17 — the breath God gave and the breath
the flood took. Then two proverbs that cut both ways: *"Doesn't the ear try words, even as the
palate tastes its food?"* (12:11; Job had asked at 6:30 whether his own palate could not discern
mischief, and Elihu will quote the line at 34:3), and *"With aged men is wisdom, in length of days
understanding"* (12:12). The second is either Job's concession to Bildad's appeal to the fathers
(8:8–10) or, more likely, a quotation set up to be knocked down — because the next verse replaces
it.

**3. The hymn of overthrow (12:13–25).** *"With God is wisdom and might. He has counsel and
understanding"* (12:13). Not with the aged — with God. And then thirteen verses on what God does
with his wisdom, which is to **undo**. *"He breaks down, and it can't be built again. He imprisons a
man, and there can be no release"* (12:14). *"He withholds the waters, and they dry up. Again, he
sends them out, and they overturn the earth"* (12:15) — drought and flood, the two ends of the
water, and the flood's verb *hāpak*, *overturn*, which is Sodom's (Genesis 19:25). *"The deceived
and the deceiver are his"* (12:16).

Then the procession of the great, stripped. **Counselors, judges, kings, priests, the mighty, the
trusted, the elders, princes, the strong, the chiefs of the peoples** — ten classes in eight verses
(12:17–24), each one led away, unbelted, struck dumb, made foolish, poured with contempt. It is the
inverse of the friends' doctrine and uses the friends' form: **a doxology.** Eliphaz had praised God
because *"he frustrates the devices of the crafty… He takes the wise in their own craftiness"*
(5:12–13), meaning the wicked. Job praises God in the same terms and leaves out the moral: God
humbles the counselor and the judge, and the speech never says they deserved it.

Two verses of the hymn are quoted, word for word, in the Psalter. ***"He pours contempt on princes"***
(12:21a) and ***"causes them to wander in a wilderness where there is no way"*** (12:24b) — *šôpēk
bûz ʿal-nədîbîm … wayyatʿēm bətōhû lōʾ-dārek* — are together the whole of Psalm 107:40. In the psalm
the line is followed by *"Yet he lifts the needy out of their affliction"* (107:41); **in Job it is
followed by nothing.** And the wilderness is *tōhû* — the formless waste of Genesis 1:2 — so that
the hymn's last movement runs creation backward, the way chapter 3 did: God *"uncovers deep things
out of darkness, and brings out to light the shadow of death"* (12:22), and the rulers *"grope in
the dark without light"* (12:25). That groping is Eliphaz's own image, *"They meet with darkness in
the day time, and grope at noonday as in the night"* (5:14), and both go back to the covenant curse
of Deuteronomy 28:29. See
[[He Pours Contempt on Princes - Job 12.21-24 and Psalm 107.40]] and
[[Let That Day Be Darkness - Job 3 as Counter-Creation]].

**4. The friends dismissed (13:1–12).** *"Behold, my eye has seen all this. My ear has heard and
understood it. What you know, I know also"* (13:1–2). Then the turn that organises the rest of the
speech: ***"Surely I would speak to the Almighty. I desire to reason with God"*** (13:3). The verb is
*hôkēaḥ*, *to argue a case*, from the root of *mōkîaḥ* — the *umpire* Job said at 9:33 did not exist.
**There is no arbiter, so Job will argue the case himself.** The same verb comes back twice in the
chapter: *"He will surely reprove (hôkēaḥ yôkîaḥ) you"* (13:10), and *"I will maintain (ʾôkîaḥ) my
ways before him"* (13:15). See [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

*"But you are forgers of lies. You are all physicians of no value"* (13:4). *Ṭōpəlê-šeqer* is
*plasterers of falsehood*, men who whitewash a crack; *rōpəʾê ʾĕlil* is *healers of nothing*, and
*ʾĕlîl* is the word the prophets use for idols. *"Oh that you would be completely silent! Then you
would be wise"* (13:5) — **the seven days of 2:13 named as the only wise thing they have done.** See
[[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]] and [[Seven Days of Silence]].

Then the most theologically daring passage in the speech, and the one the epilogue confirms most
precisely.

> *"Will you speak unrighteously for God, and talk deceitfully for him? Will you show partiality to
> him? Will you contend for God? … He will surely reprove you, if you secretly show partiality."*
> (13:7–10)

*Show partiality* is ***nāśāʾ pānîm***, *to lift the face* — the judge's sin of favouring one party
because of who he is (Leviticus 19:15; Deuteronomy 10:17). **Job accuses the friends of taking a
bribe from God's side of the courtroom**: of lying on God's behalf because he is the stronger party,
and he tells them God will not thank them for it. That is exactly what happens. *"My wrath is
kindled against you… for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has"*
(42:7) — and the next verse uses the same idiom the other way round: *"I will accept him"*, *ʾim-lōʾ
pānāyw ʾeśśāʾ*, *I will lift his face* (42:8). **The friends lifted God's face, and God lifted
Job's.** *"Your memorable sayings are proverbs of ashes, Your defenses are defenses of clay"*
(13:12) — *mišlê-ʾēper*, sayings made of the ash Job is sitting in. See
[[Sitting in Dust and Ashes]].

**5. "Though he slay me" (13:13–19).** *"Be silent, leave me alone, that I may speak. Let come on me
what will. Why should I take my flesh in my teeth, and put my life in my hand?"* (13:13–14). The
second phrase is the idiom of mortal risk — Jephthah *"put my life in my hand"* (Judges 12:3), and
David, facing Goliath (1 Samuel 19:5). Job knows that what he is about to do may kill him, and says
so:

> *"Behold, he will kill me. I have no hope. Nevertheless, I will maintain my ways before him."*
> (13:15)

The KJV's *"Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him"* comes from the other of the two Hebrew
readings of this verse, and the difference between them is a single letter that changes the meaning
from *no* to *him*. The two readings and what each does to the speech are set out in the Notes and on
[[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]]. On either reading the verse's
second half is the same, and it is the point: ***ʾak-dərākay ʾel-pānāyw ʾôkîaḥ***, *but my ways to
his face I will argue.* Whether or not Job hopes, he will make his case.

***"This also shall be my salvation, that a godless man shall not come before him"*** (13:16). The
reasoning is that a hypocrite would not dare to stand before God; Job's willingness to stand there
is itself evidence for him. *Yəšûʿâ*, *salvation*, is Jacob's word at Genesis 49:18 — and the
Septuagint's rendering of this half-verse, *touto moi apobēsetai eis sōtērian*, is quoted word for
word by Paul in prison: *"this will turn out for my deliverance"* (Philippians 1:19). See
[[This Will Turn Out for My Deliverance - Job 13.16 in Philippians 1.19]].

*"See now, I have set my cause in order. I know that I am righteous (ʾeṣdāq). Who is he who will
contend with me?"* (13:18–19). ***This answers the question of 9:2*** — *"how can man be just
(yiṣdaq) with God?"* — in the first person, and with the verb Zophar had used to say a man of lips
could not be justified (11:2). *ʿĀraktî mišpāṭ* is a lawyer's phrase: the case is prepared, the
pleadings in order. And *mî-hûʾ yārîb ʿimmādî*, *who will contend with me?*, is the question the
Servant asks in Isaiah 50:8 — *"Who will contend with me? Let us stand up together"* — and Paul in
Romans 8:33–34. See [[Who Will Contend with Me - Job 13, Isaiah 50, and Romans 8]].

**6. The address to God (13:20–28).** From 13:20 every *you* is God's, and the speech never returns
to the friends. Job states his two conditions — the same two as at 9:34 — *"withdraw your hand far
from me; and don't let your terror make me afraid"* (13:21) — and then offers God the choice of
procedure: ***"Then call, and I will answer; or let me speak, and you answer me"*** (13:22). The
whirlwind takes the first option and then the second: *"I will question you, then you answer me"*
(38:3; 40:7), and Job's last words, *"You said, 'Listen, now, and I will speak; I will question you,
and you will answer me'"* (42:4).

*"How many are my iniquities and sins? Make me know my disobedience and my sin"* (13:23) — the
request for a charge sheet, already made at 10:2. *"Why hide you your face, and hold me for your
enemy?"* (13:24). **The pun is in the Hebrew and has been noticed since the rabbis**: *ləʾôyēb*,
*for an enemy*, against *ʾiyyôb*, *Job*. Job asks why God treats *ʾiyyôb* as *ʾôyēb*. The name's
etymology is unknown, but the book plays on this sound at least here and again at 33:10, where Elihu
quotes the line back.

The images that close the chapter are of a man too small to be worth the pursuit: *"Will you harass
a driven leaf? Will you pursue the dry stubble?"* (13:25). *"You write bitter things against me, and
make me inherit the iniquities of my youth"* (13:26) — God as the prosecutor who keeps a written file
(Psalm 25:7, *"Don't remember the sins of my youth"*). *"You also put my feet in the stocks, and mark
all my paths"* (13:27) — Elihu will quote this line word for word as the thing he cannot let Job
say (33:11). And then *"though I am decaying like a rotten thing, like a garment that is
moth-eaten"* (13:28), the image Isaiah gives to the Servant's accusers at 50:9.

**7. Man born of a woman (14:1–6).** Chapter 14 turns from Job's case to every man's.
***"Man, who is born of a woman, is of few days, and full of trouble. He grows up like a flower, and
is cut down. He also flees like a shadow, and doesn't continue"*** (14:1–2). *Yəlûd ʾiššâ*, *born of
woman*, is the friends' phrase for human impurity — Eliphaz's *"What is man, that he should be
clean? What is he who is born of a woman, that he should be righteous?"* (15:14) and Bildad's (25:4)
— and here it is Job's first, used not for impurity but for brevity. The flower that is cut down
(*ṣîṣ … wayyimmāl*) is the image of Psalm 90:5–6, Psalm 103:15–16, and Isaiah 40:6–8, *"All flesh is
like grass, and all its glory is like the flower of the field"*, which 1 Peter 1:24 quotes; the
shadow is 8:9's, where Bildad had used it to say that the present generation knows nothing.

*"Do you open your eyes on such a one, and bring me into judgment with you?"* (14:3) — the
disproportion again: a flower, a shadow, brought to trial. Then the verse the church has read as a
text on original sin: ***"Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one"*** (14:4). In
context it is a plea, not a confession. If no man can be clean, then it is unreasonable to demand
cleanness of one; Job grants the friends' anthropology (4:17–19) and turns it into a reason for God
to look away. *"Seeing his days are determined… Look away from him, that he may rest, until he shall
accomplish, as a hireling, his day"* (14:5–6) — the hired labourer of 7:1–2, who wants only to finish
the shift. See [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]].

**8. Hope for a tree (14:7–12).** ***"For there is hope for a tree, If it is cut down, that it will
sprout again, that the tender branch of it will not cease. Though its root grows old in the earth,
and its stock dies in the ground, yet through the scent of water it will bud, and sprout boughs like
a plant."*** It is the most beautiful passage in the speech and the most desolate, because of what
it is for. The tree is the comparison that fails. *"But man dies, and is laid low. Yes, man gives up
the spirit, and where is he?"* (14:10). *"As the waters fail from the sea, and the river wastes and
dries up, so man lies down and doesn't rise. Until the heavens are no more, they shall not awake,
nor be roused out of their sleep"* (14:11–12).

Three words carry the passage. *Yaḥălîp*, *it will sprout again* (14:7), is from *ḥālap*, *to
change* or *pass on* — the root of the *release* (*ḥălîpātî*) Job will say he is waiting for in 14:14.
*Gizʿô*, *its stock*, is *gezaʿ*, a rare word that occurs in only two other places: Isaiah 11:1, *"A
shoot will come out of the stock of Jesse"*, and Isaiah 40:24, of rulers who have no stock at all.
And *rêaḥ mayim*, *the scent of water*, is enough for the stump — where Bildad's papyrus needed a
marsh (8:11). See [[There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts]] and
[[The Papyrus Without Water - Bildad's Plants, Psalm 1, and Jeremiah 17]].

**9. "If a man dies, shall he live again?" (14:13–17).** And then, out of the flattest denial in the
speech, the most daring wish in the book so far.

> *"Oh that you would hide me in Sheol, that you would keep me secret, until your wrath is past, that
> you would appoint me a set time, and remember me! If a man dies, shall he live again? All the days
> of my warfare would I wait, until my release should come. You would call, and I would answer you.
> You would have a desire to the work of your hands."* (14:13–15)

*Mî-yittēn*, *oh that*, the wish formula Zophar had borrowed at 11:5. **Sheol, everywhere else in the
book the place of no return (7:9; 10:21), becomes a hiding place** — a safe-house in which God would
keep Job until the anger is over, and then *remember* him (*tizkərēnî*), the verb of God remembering
Noah in the ark (Genesis 8:1). The question *"If a man dies, shall he live again?"* expects *no*; the
next clause proceeds as if the answer might be *yes*, and imagines it. *Ṣəbāʾî*, *my warfare*, is
the conscript service of 7:1; *ḥălîpātî*, *my release* or *my change*, is the tree's sprouting of
14:7, now hoped for the man. *"You would call, and I would answer you"* is 13:22's procedure,
transposed beyond death, and *"you would have a desire to the work of your hands"* is the potter of
10:8–12 who would at last want what he made. **This is the first time in the book that Job imagines
a relationship with God continuing past death**, and it is imagined only as a wish, in the
conditional. It is the floor from which 19:25–27 will rise. See
[[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]] and
[[Remember Me - Genesis 40.14 and the Prayers of the Condemned]].

*"But now you count my steps. Don't you watch over my sin? My disobedience is sealed up in a bag. You
fasten up my iniquity"* (14:16–17). The bag may be a purse sealed for safekeeping, or a herdsman's
pouch of tally-stones; either way God is keeping the record, and the record is sealed against
Job. See [[Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives]].

**10. "So you destroy the hope of man" (14:18–22).** The wish collapses. *"But the mountain falling
comes to nothing. The rock is removed out of its place; The waters wear the stones. The torrents of
it wash away the dust of the earth. So you destroy the hope of man"* (14:18–19). The water that
revived the tree at 14:9 is the water that erodes the mountain. The first cycle ends on a dead man
who does not know what has happened to his sons: *"His sons come to honor, and he doesn't know it.
They are brought low, but he doesn't perceive it of them. But his flesh on him has pain, and his
soul within him mourns"* (14:21–22). For a man whose ten children are dead, it is the bleakest
sentence he could have chosen, and the epilogue reverses it at its last verse: *"After this Job lived
one hundred forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons' sons, to four generations"* (42:16).

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — the speaker throughout, and the longest speech of the first cycle. It is the
  first in which he turns on the friends by name of their method (*"forgers of lies"*, 13:4), the
  first in which he says *ʾeṣdāq*, *I am righteous* (13:18), and the first in which he imagines God
  remembering him after death (14:13–15). He calls himself *ṣaddîq tāmîm* (12:4), Noah's epithet.
- [[God]] — named *ʾĕlôah* (12:4, 6), *ʾēl* (13:3, 7, 8), *Šadday* (13:3), and once, uniquely in the
  dialogue, *Yahweh* (12:9). Praised as the one with whom wisdom actually lies (12:13), and as the
  God who overturns every human authority without a reason being given (12:14–25); then addressed
  directly from 13:20 to the end.
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the speaker answered, though the plural *you* takes in all three.
  Zophar's four regions (11:8–9) answered by four creatures (12:7–8); his *"secrets of wisdom"*
  (11:6) answered by *"who doesn't know such things as these?"* (12:3); his tent (11:14) by the
  robbers' tents (12:6); his wish *mî-yittēn* (11:5) taken back by its owner (13:5; 14:4, 13).
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — addressed in the plural. His groping at noon (5:14) and his
  *"he takes the wise in their own craftiness"* (5:13) are behind 12:17–25; his *"born of a woman"*
  anthropology (4:17) is behind 14:1–4. He replies in chapter 15, and his first line answers this
  speech's first line.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — addressed in the plural. His appeal to the aged (8:8–10) is behind
  12:12, his shadow (8:9) behind 14:2, and his papyrus (8:11–13) behind 14:7–9.
- [[Noah]] — not named; *ṣaddîq tāmîm* (12:4) is his description at Genesis 6:9, and God's
  *remembering* him (Genesis 8:1) is the verb of 14:13.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — 14:13, *"Oh that you would hide me in Sheol"*: **the land of no return made into a
  hiding place.** The only time in the book anyone wishes to be *kept* in Sheol, and the only time
  it is imagined as a temporary stay rather than an end. Contrast 7:9 and 10:21–22.

## Themes

- [[Wisdom]] — chapter 12 is a dispute about who owns it: *"wisdom shall die with you"* (12:2), *"with
  aged men is wisdom"* (12:12), and then *"With God is wisdom and might"* (12:13), followed by a hymn
  in which divine wisdom unmakes human wisdom. *"Be completely silent! Then you would be wise"*
  (13:5).
- [[Hope]] — the speech's spine. The tree has hope (14:7); man, apparently, does not (14:10–12); the
  wish imagines it anyway (14:13–15); God destroys it like water wearing stone (14:19). And 13:15,
  which says either *"I have no hope"* or *"I will hope in him"*.
- [[Life and Death]] — *"few days, and full of trouble"* (14:1), the flower and the shadow (14:2),
  the dead who *"shall not awake"* (14:12), and the one question in the Old Testament that asks the
  resurrection question in so many words: *"If a man dies, shall he live again?"* (14:14).
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — *hôkēaḥ* (13:3, 10, 15), the verb of the missing *mōkîaḥ* of 9:33:
  Job will be his own advocate. And the two procedures of 13:22 — *call and I will answer* —
  which the whirlwind adopts.
- [[Judgment]] — the case prepared (*ʿāraktî mišpāṭ*, 13:18), the charge sheet requested (13:23), the
  written indictment (13:26), the defendant in the stocks (13:27), and the record sealed in a bag
  (14:17).
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣaddîq tāmîm* as a joke (12:4), and *ʾeṣdāq*, *I am righteous* (13:18): the
  answer to 9:2's *how can man be just with God?*, given in the first person.
- [[Integrity]] — *tāmîm* (12:4), and *"I will maintain my ways before him"* (13:15): integrity held
  whether or not there is hope.
- [[Retribution]] — denied by observation (12:6, the robbers' tents) and by doxology (12:17–25, the
  great overthrown for no stated reason); and inverted in 13:7–10, where it is the friends' lies for
  God that God will punish.
- [[Sin]] — *"How many are my iniquities and sins? Make me know"* (13:23); *"the iniquities of my
  youth"* (13:26); sin counted, watched, sealed, and fastened up (14:16–17).
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — 14:4, *"Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one"*, the
  friends' anthropology granted and turned into a plea.
- [[Creation]] — 12:7–10, the animals who know their maker's hand; 12:15, drought and flood; 12:24's
  *tōhû* and 12:22's darkness, creation run backward; 14:15, *"the work of your hands"*.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — 12:17–24, the Bible's longest list of the powerful humbled — counselors,
  judges, kings, priests, princes, chiefs of the nations — and God increasing and destroying nations
  (12:23).
- [[The Names of God]] — 12:9, the dialogue's only *Yahweh*.
- [[Remembrance]] — 14:13, *"appoint me a set time, and remember me"*, the verb of Genesis 8:1.
- [[Light and Darkness]] — 12:22, *"brings out to light the shadow of death"*, and 12:25, *"They grope
  in the dark without light"*.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — God's face hidden (13:24) and God's eyes watching (14:3, 16): Job is
  both unseen and surveilled.
- [[Language and Speech]] — silence as wisdom (13:5); *"proverbs of ashes"* (13:12); *"let me
  speak"* (13:13, 22). The ear that tests words (12:11).
- [[The Fear of God]] — 13:11, *"Shall not his majesty make you afraid, and his dread fall on
  you?"*: Job tells the friends they should fear God more than they do, and asks God to take his
  terror away from him (13:21).
- [[Suffering]] — the driven leaf, the dry stubble, the moth-eaten garment (13:25–28); the dead
  man's flesh that still has pain (14:22).
- [[Prayer]] — 13:20–14:22 is a prayer: the longest continuous address to God in the first cycle,
  and the one that ends the cycle.

## Notes

- **13:15, the Ketiv and the Qere.** The consonantal text (*Ketiv*) reads *hēn yiqṭəlēnî lōʾ
  ʾăyaḥēl* — *behold, he will slay me; I will not wait* (or *I have no hope*). The Masoretic margin
  (*Qere*) directs the reader to say *lô*, *for him*, giving *though he slay me, I will wait for
  him*. The two words sound identical. The Qere stands behind the Targum, the Vulgate (*etiam si
  occiderit me in ipso sperabo*), and the KJV's *"yet will I trust in him"*; the Ketiv behind the
  WEB, RSV, NRSV, NJPS (*"He may well slay me; I may have no hope"*), and most modern commentators,
  who also note that *yāḥal* means *wait* rather than *trust*. The wiki follows the WEB and the
  Ketiv as the more probable sense *in context* — the verse continues *ʾak*, *but* or *nevertheless*,
  which fits *I have no hope, but I will argue* better than *I will hope, but I will argue*; and
  13:14 has just said Job is taking his life in his hand. It records the Qere as a reading of real
  antiquity that the Masoretes themselves preferred for public reading. The two readings are
  compared on [[Though He Slay Me - The Written and the Read Text of Job 13.15]]. The page title
  uses the familiar phrase because it is the name by which the speech is known, not because the wiki
  endorses the reading.

- **A correction to [[Hope]].** The theme page said that *"the marginal Hebrew reading gives the
  opposite sense, 'I have no hope'"*. That is the wrong way round: the *written* text (Ketiv) says
  *no*, and the *marginal* reading (Qere) says *for him*. The theme page is corrected in this
  ingest, and the correction is recorded in the log.

- **12:9's *Yahweh*.** The claim that this is the dialogue's only *Yahweh* is about the speeches of
  Job, the friends, and Elihu (3:1–37:24). The name appears in the narrative headings of the
  whirlwind speeches (38:1; 40:1, 3, 6; 42:1) and freely in the prose frame, and some manuscripts
  have *ʾĕlôah* in 12:9 as well, which would make the dialogue's avoidance of the name complete. The
  wiki follows the Masoretic text.

- **12:4–6, textual difficulties.** 12:5 is obscure in Hebrew — *lappîd bûz*, *a torch of contempt*
  or, revocalised, *for misfortune, contempt* — and 12:6's *"who carry their God in their hands"*
  (*laʾăšer hēbîʾ ʾĕlôah bəyādô*) may mean that they make an idol, that their own fist is their god
  (Habakkuk 1:11), or that they think God is in their pocket. The WEB's renderings are kept; the
  sense of the whole — the comfortable despise the fallen, and the violent are safe — is clear.

- **12:12, quoted or held?** Whether *"With aged men is wisdom"* is Job's own view or a citation of
  the friends' is disputed. Read as a question (*Is wisdom with the aged?*), the verse sets up 12:13's
  correction; read as a statement, it is a concession that 12:13 then outbids. Either way, the next
  verse moves wisdom to God, and the sequence 12:12–13 is the hinge of the chapter. Elihu's opening
  (32:7–9) — *"I said, 'Days should speak'… But there is a spirit in man"* — argues the same point
  against the same three men.

- **12:17–25 and the friends' doxologies.** Every one of the friends' first speeches has a hymn in
  it: Eliphaz's at 5:9–16, Bildad's implied in 8:3, Zophar's at 11:7–9. Job's hymns (9:5–10 and
  this one) are the same form with the purpose reversed: the friends praise God's power as the
  guarantee of justice, and Job praises it as the evidence that power does not need justice to act.
  He never says God is unjust in this hymn; he says only that the great fall and nobody says why.

- **14:4 and original sin.** *"Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one"* became a
  standard text for inherited sinfulness in Christian theology from the patristic period (often in
  the Septuagint's expanded form, *"not even if his life on earth be one day"*). In Job it is
  rhetorical and pleading — Job's point is that God should not expect from a creature what the
  creature cannot supply. The wiki records the later use without reading it back into the verse.

- **14:13–15 and resurrection.** The passage is regularly cited in histories of the Israelite
  afterlife, and rightly, but its grammar matters: every clause is a wish or an unreal conditional
  (*oh that you would… you would call… you would desire*). Job does not assert a life after death;
  he imagines one, and imagines it as a relationship rather than a place — God calling, Job
  answering, God wanting what he made. Then 14:18–22 takes the wish back. The wiki reads 14:13–15 as
  the conceptual seed of 19:25–27, and 14:14's question as genuinely open in the speech rather than
  answered in it. See [[The Hope of Resurrection in the Old Testament]].

- **Boundary.** The pericope is unchanged from the book page's plan: 12:1 is the speech heading and
  14:22 ends the first cycle, with Eliphaz's reply beginning at 15:1. Chapters 12–14 are sometimes
  divided at 13:19/20, where the address turns from the friends to God; the wiki keeps them as one
  speech, since the cycle's pattern is one reply per friend and the turn at 13:20 is anticipated at
  13:3.
