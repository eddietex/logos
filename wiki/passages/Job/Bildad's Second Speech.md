# Bildad's Second Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 18:1–21

## Summary

Job has just asked for a witness in heaven, demanded that God stand surety for him, and said that
his hope may be going down to the gates of Sheol. [[Bildad the Shuhite]] answers none of it.
Chapter 18 contains no reference to the witness, no reference to the pledge, no engagement with
Job's claim of clean hands, and no reply to the plea of a dying man. It contains four verses of
irritation and seventeen verses of set piece: what happens to the wicked. The speech's most
important feature is what it leaves out.

**The rebuke (18:1–4) is short and it is about manners, not argument.** *"How long will you hunt
for words? Consider, and afterwards we will speak."* Bildad opened his first speech the same way
(*"How long will you speak these things?"*, 8:2), and the complaint is the same complaint: Job
talks too much and thinks too little. Verse 3 registers the insult he has taken — *"Why are we
counted as animals, which have become unclean in your sight?"* — which answers Job's *"I shall not
find a wise man among you"* (17:10) and, further back, his sarcastic *"ask the animals, now, and
they shall teach you"* (12:7).

Then verse 4, which is the one theologically serious sentence in the speech, and it is aimed
precisely. *"You who tear yourself in your anger, shall the earth be forsaken for you? Or shall the
rock be removed out of its place?"* Two things are happening. The verb *tear* is Job's own from two
chapters earlier: at 16:9 Job said *"He has torn me in his wrath"* — God tore, in God's anger.
Bildad hands the sentence back with the subject changed: it is Job who tears, and Job whose anger
does it. The wound is self-inflicted, and the theological problem dissolves. And the two rhetorical
questions state the friends' deepest commitment in its clearest form: the moral order of the world
is fixed furniture, and Job is asking for it to be rearranged around his case. *Shall the earth be
forsaken for you?* is not a bad question. It is the question the whirlwind will eventually ask Job
in earnest — except that when God asks it, at chapters 38–41, he does not use it to establish that
Job is guilty.

**The portrait of the wicked man (18:5–21) is the whole rest of the speech, and it is Job, item by
item, without his name.** Read against the prologue the correspondences are not subtle: fire falls
on his household (1:16), his tent is invaded, his skin is consumed, terror surrounds him, and he
has no surviving children. Bildad has produced a description of a man in the room and delivered it
as general doctrine. This is the friends' characteristic manoeuvre — [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] did
exactly the same at 15:20–35 — and it lets them make the accusation without ever having to defend
it as an accusation.

The portrait runs in movements. **The light goes out (5–6):** the lamp above him in his tent is
extinguished, which in the ancient household means both the man's life and his line. **He is
trapped (7–10):** six different words for a snare in four verses — net, mesh, snare, trap, noose,
and a second trap on the path — and the crucial detail is that *"he is cast into a net by his own
feet"*. Nobody hunts him. He walks into it, so the doom is his own doing and God's justice is
untouched. **Terror pursues (11–14):** *"Terrors shall make him afraid on every side, and shall
chase him at his heels"*, his strength famished, his body devoured by *"the firstborn of death"*,
and finally the eviction — *"He shall be rooted out of the security of his tent. He shall be
brought to the king of terrors."* **His place is sterilised (15–16):** sulfur scattered on his
habitation, roots dried beneath, branch cut off above. **His memory is erased (17–19):** *"His
memory shall perish from the earth. He shall have no name in the street … He shall have neither
son nor grandson among his people."*

**That last stroke is the cruellest thing in the speech, and Bildad has done it before.** At 8:4 he
told a freshly bereaved man that his ten children had been *"delivered … into the hand of their
disobedience"*. Here he does it without naming them, which is worse rather than better: the loss of
a line is simply listed among the standard fittings of a wicked man's ruin, as though it were a
weather report. See [[Job's Children]].

**And it is answered immediately.** Bildad's *"His memory shall perish from the earth. He shall have
no name in the street"* (18:17) is followed, in the next chapter, by *"Oh that my words were now
written! Oh that they were inscribed in a book! That with an iron pen and lead they were engraved
in the rock forever!"* (19:23–24) — see [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives]]. The reader is in a
position to adjudicate that exchange, and the adjudication is in their hands: Job's words were
written in a book, and Bildad's memory survives only because they were.

**The closing formula (18:21) tells you what kind of speech this was.** *"Surely such are the
dwellings of the unrighteous. This is the place of him who doesn't know God."* It is a summary
verdict on a category, delivered in a room containing exactly one candidate for it, and the man it
describes is the one God has twice called *"my servant Job … a blameless and an upright man, one
who fears God"*. Bildad has said nothing false about the wicked. He has simply had no way to
discover that he is not looking at one.

## People

- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — his second and longest speech, and the one that shows what the friends'
  position looks like once the offer of restoration has been dropped. Chapter 8 ended with
  *"He will still fill your mouth with laughter"*; chapter 18 ends with sulfur and an erased name.
  Nothing here is addressed to Job's condition, and nothing invites him back.
- [[Job (person)]] — spoken to in verses 2–4 and described, unnamed, in verses 5–21. He is the
  audience of a speech that is also a portrait of him, and he understands this: his reply opens
  *"How long will you torment me, and crush me with words?"* (19:2), which answers 18:2's *"How
  long"* word for word.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the *"we"* of 18:2 and 18:3. Bildad
  speaks for the bloc, and [[Zophar's Second Speech]] two chapters later is the same speech again
  with different imagery.
- [[Job's Children]] — 18:19. The dead are used as evidence a second time, now as an anonymous item
  in a list.
- [[God]] — never named in the speech. He is the unstated agent of every passive verb in it: the
  light *shall be put out*, the roots *shall be dried up*, sulfur *shall be scattered*. Bildad's
  God is not a character but a mechanism, which is exactly the difference between him and Job, who
  will not stop addressing God in the second person even while accusing him.

## Places

No geography. The speech's whole world is a tent — mentioned three times (18:6, 14, 15) — and the
darkness outside it into which the wicked man is *"driven from light … and chased out of the
world"* (18:18).

- [[Sheol]] — not named, but it is where verse 14 is going. *"The king of terrors"* is the ruler of
  the realm of the dead, and the verb — *he shall be brought*, marched under escort — makes the
  destination a place with a sovereign rather than a state of being. See the Notes on whether this
  is mythology or metaphor.

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine in its purest form yet in the book, and the purity comes from what
  has been removed. There is no advice, no summons to repent, and no promise of restoration:
  chapter 18 is retribution stated as physics. The one concession to God's justice is verse 8's
  *"by his own feet"*, which makes the trap self-entered.
- [[Judgment]] — with the whole judicial procedure deleted. Every judgment scene in Genesis has God
  come, ask, hear, sentence, and provide; here there is no coming, no question, no hearing, and no
  provision, only a sentence executing itself on a category. What Bildad describes is not a trial
  but a tariff.
- [[Life and Death]] — the extinguished lamp, the famished strength, the devoured body, the march
  to the king of terrors. Death in this speech is not an end but a public humiliation with a
  destination.
- [[Curse]] — 18:15's sulfur is covenant-curse vocabulary (Deuteronomy 29:23), and the drying of
  roots and cutting of the branch is the standard prophetic image for a line ended.
- [[Remembrance]] — 18:17 is the theme at its darkest: not being forgotten by God but ceasing to be
  remembered by anyone, *"no name in the street"*. In a world with no developed afterlife, the
  memory of a name and the survival of a line are what a man has instead of immortality, and
  Bildad's sentence takes both.
- [[Sin]] — assumed everywhere and specified nowhere. Bildad names no offence at any point in
  twenty-one verses; he describes only the punishment, from which the offence is to be inferred.
  That inference is the book's target.
- [[The Fear of God]] — 18:21's *"him who doesn't know God"* is the verdict, and it is the exact
  inverse of the narrator's *"one who feared God, and turned away from evil"* (1:1).
- [[Wisdom]] — Bildad's stock in trade is inherited doctrine (*"inquire of past generations"*, 8:8),
  and chapter 18 is what that inheritance sounds like when it is applied to a case it was not built
  for.

## Notes

- **"The firstborn of death" (18:13).** *Bəkôr māwet*. The phrase is unique in the Hebrew Bible and
  it is doing something strange. Three readings. **A superlative** — *the deadliest disease*, on the
  Hebrew idiom by which a firstborn is the foremost of its kind (*"the firstborn of the poor"*,
  Isaiah 14:30, means the poorest). This is the safest reading and is what most translations
  assume. **A personification** — Death has a firstborn, and it is pestilence; the parallel with
  *"the king of terrors"* in the next verse supports reading both as figures rather than
  abstractions.
  **A demythologised Canaanite allusion** — Ugaritic texts give Mot (*Death*) a household, and Job's
  poetry elsewhere reaches for the old cosmology it does not believe (Rahab at 9:13 and 26:12,
  Leviathan at 3:8 and 41). On this reading Bildad is speaking mythologically the way a modern
  writer says *the jaws of death*: the imagery is borrowed and the theology is not. The wiki prefers
  the second and third together — a figure, drawn from an inherited vocabulary — and notes that
  nothing in the speech's argument depends on which is right.

- **"The king of terrors" (18:14).** *Melek ballāhôt*, and the same three options apply. What is
  worth keeping is the sentence's grammar: *he shall be brought to* him — a passive, with an
  unnamed agent doing the bringing. Bildad's wicked man does not die; he is delivered, under escort,
  to a sovereign. It is the most vivid line in the speech and the only place in it where death is
  given a face. Compare [[Sheol]], which in Job is otherwise a place without a ruler, and note that
  the Bible nowhere else calls Death a king — the closest is Hosea 13:14 and Psalm 49:14, where
  Death shepherds rather than reigns.

- **Six words for a trap (18:8–10).** *Rešet* (net), *śəbākâ* (mesh or lattice), *paḥ* (a bird
  trap), *ṣammîm* (a snare, the word is uncertain), *ḥebel* (a cord or noose), and *malkōdet* (a
  catching-device, from *lākad*, to capture). Six terms in three verses is not decoration; it is the
  speech's argument in miniature. The world is comprehensively rigged against the wicked man, the
  rigging is invisible until it closes, and — verse 8 is careful about this — he supplies the
  motion himself. Bildad needs the trap to be self-entered, because a trap laid for an innocent man
  would be exactly Job's complaint. Job's reply grants the image and denies the mechanism:
  *"know now that God has subverted me, and has surrounded me with his net"* (19:6). Same word,
  opposite agent.

- **What the speech does not contain.** Chapter 18 is worth reading twice for its omissions. There
  is no reply to 16:19's witness in heaven, no reply to 17:3's demand for a pledge, no reply to
  16:17's *"there is no violence in my hands"*, no answer to 17:15's *"where then is my hope?"*, and
  — unlike chapter 8 — no offer of a way back. It is not a rebuttal. It is a recitation, and the
  fact that a recitation is what the second cycle produces is the book's evidence that the friends
  have nothing further. Their speeches will shrink from here: twenty-one verses in chapter 18, six
  in chapter 25, and none at all from Zophar in the third cycle.

- **Is Bildad wrong about anything he says?** Almost nothing in 18:5–21 is false as a general
  statement, and several lines of it are quoted approvingly elsewhere in Scripture. This is the
  difficulty the wiki keeps returning to on [[Retribution]]: the friends' sentences are orthodox and
  their use of them is condemned by God at 42:7. What makes chapter 18 an offence is not its content
  but its address — a true description of the wicked, delivered to a man who is not one, as an
  explanation of his children's deaths.
