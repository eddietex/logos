# There Is No Umpire Between Us

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 9:1–10:22

## Summary

Job's reply to Bildad, and **the speech in which the book's legal metaphor arrives in full.** Chapters
6–7 asked to be remembered and let alone. Chapters 9–10 ask for a trial — and then spend
fifty-seven verses explaining why a trial with this defendant cannot be held. The vocabulary is a
courtroom's from the second verse to the last: *rîb*, to bring a lawsuit (9:3; 10:2); *ʿānâ*, to
answer a charge (9:3, 14, 15, 16, 32); *mišpāṭ*, a case or a verdict (9:19, 32); *šōpēṭ*, the
opponent-at-law (9:15); *yāʿad*, to summon (9:19); *ṣādaq* and *rāšaʿ*, to be found in the right and
to be found guilty (9:2, 15, 20, 29; 10:2, 7, 15); *mōkîaḥ*, the arbiter (9:33); *ʿēdîm*, witnesses
(10:17). Nobody in the book has spoken like this before. Everyone after him will, Elihu most of all.

**Bildad gets one line.** *"Truly I know that it is so"* (9:2) concedes his whole speech in five
words, and then Job never addresses him — or any of the friends — again until chapter 12. There is
no plural *you* anywhere in these two chapters. In chapter 9 Job talks *about* God, slipping into the
second person at 9:28–31 as if he could not help it; in chapter 10 he says *"I will tell God"*
(10:2) and does, for twenty-one verses without a break. **The friends have become the audience to a
case they are not party to.**

It runs in seven movements.

**1. The question (9:2–4).** *"Truly I know that it is so, but how can man be just with God?"* Every
word of the second line is Eliphaz's. His night spirit had asked *"Shall mortal man be more just
than God?"* (4:17) — *hăʾĕnôš mēʾĕlôah yiṣdāq*; Job asks *û-mah-yiṣdaq ʾĕnôš ʿim-ʾēl*. **The verb is
the same and its sense has moved.** Eliphaz meant *morally purer than God*, and the answer was
obviously no. Job means *be found in the right against God* — win a case — and the answer is no for
a different reason: *"If he is pleased to contend with him, he can't answer him one time in a
thousand"* (9:3). Not because the man is guilty but because the court is God's. Elihu will pick up
the phrase *"one among a thousand"* (*ʾeḥād minnî-ʾālep*, 33:23) for the mediating angel he offers
Job — an answer to this verse and to 9:33 at once.

**2. The anti-hymn (9:5–13).** Then a hymn, in the participle style of the Psalms — *he who removes
the mountains, he who shakes the earth, he who commands the sun* — and every line of it is power
used to undo. Mountains overturned *"in his anger"*; the earth's pillars trembling; the sun ordered
not to rise and the stars sealed up (9:5–7). The middle verses are pure creation-praise: he
*"alone stretches out the heavens, and treads on the waves of the sea"*; he made *"the Bear, Orion,
and the Pleiades, and the rooms of the south"* (9:8–9). And then 9:10, which is **Eliphaz's
doxology of 5:9 almost word for word**: *"He does great things past finding out; yes, marvelous
things without number."* Eliphaz said it as comfort — so seek God, commit your cause to him (5:8).
Job says it as a threat. The same sentence about the same God, and **the difference is entirely
whether the greatness is on your side.** See [[The Bear, Orion, and the Pleiades]].

The hymn's turn comes at 9:11, and it is the speech's most haunting verse: ***"Behold, he goes by
me, and I don't see him. He passes on also, but I don't perceive him."*** The second verb is
*yaḥălōp* — the verb of Eliphaz's vision: *"a spirit passed (yaḥălōp) before my face"* (4:15).
Eliphaz had something pass him in the night and came away with a doctrine. Job has God pass him and
sees nothing. The first verb, *ʿābar*, *pass by*, is the theophany verb of Exodus 33–34 and
1 Kings 19:11, where the passing-by is the revelation; here it is the absence of one. And 9:8 and
9:11 together — God treading the sea, God passing by unseen — are the two halves of Mark 6:48. See
[[He Treads on the Waves of the Sea - Job 9.8-11 and the Walking on the Water]].

*"He snatches away. Who can hinder him? Who will ask him, 'What are you doing?'"* (9:12) —
*mah-taʿăśeh*, the question the clay may not ask the potter in Isaiah 45:9, in exactly those words.
**Job states the rule here and breaks it in chapter 10**, where he asks the potter precisely that.
The hymn closes with *"the helpers of Rahab"* stooping under him (9:13) — the sea-dragon's allies,
the creation myth's defeated rebels. If they could not stand, *"how much less shall I answer him?"*
(9:14). See [[Am I a Sea or a Sea Monster - The Guard on the Waters]].

**3. The trial that cannot be won (9:14–24).** Now the legal reasoning, and it is airtight. *"Though
I were righteous, yet I wouldn't answer him. I would make supplication to my judge"* (9:15) — the
innocent defendant reduced to begging the plaintiff for mercy. *"If I had called, and he had answered
me, yet I wouldn't believe that he listened to my voice"* (9:16). **The reader should hold this
verse until chapter 38**, where Job calls, and God answers, and what Job then says (42:5) is the
opposite of disbelief.

Then two sentences the prologue has been waiting for. ***"For he breaks me with a storm, and
multiplies my wounds without cause"*** (9:17). The storm is *śəʿārâ* — the word, in a variant
spelling, of the *whirlwind* out of which God will answer (38:1; 40:6). Job says God crushes him
with a storm; God will come to him in one. And *without cause* is ***ḥinnām*** — the accuser's word
(*"Does Job fear God for nothing (ḥinnām)?"*, 1:9) and **God's own word for what he did to Job:
*"you incited me against him, to ruin him without cause (ḥinnām)"*** (2:3). Job heard neither
conversation. He has arrived by himself at the exact word the heavenly court used, and he is right.

*"If it is a matter of strength, behold, he is mighty! If of justice, 'Who,' says he, 'will summon
me?'"* (9:19). **The two ways a dispute is settled — force and law — are both closed**, the first
because God is stronger and the second because there is no one with the authority to make him
appear. And so: *"Though I am righteous, my own mouth shall condemn me. Though I am blameless, it
shall prove me perverse"* (9:20). The procedure itself would convict him.

Then 9:21–22, the speech's centre, and the answer to Bildad's closing promise:

> I am blameless. I don't respect myself. I despise my life. "It is all the same. Therefore I say
> he destroys the blameless and the wicked."

***Tām-ʾānî*** — *I am blameless.* **It is the first time Job says the word of himself**, and it is
the narrator's word (1:1) and God's (1:8; 2:3). Bildad had said *"God will not cast away (māʾas) a
blameless (tām) man"* (8:20). Job takes both words: *I am tām*, and the one doing the despising
(*ʾemʾas*) is me — and God destroys *tām wərāšāʿ*, the blameless with the wicked. **That is Abraham's
question at Sodom answered the wrong way**: *"Will you consume the righteous with the wicked?… Shall
not the Judge of all the earth do right?"* (Genesis 18:23–25). Bildad had quoted Genesis 18:25 as a
certainty (8:3). Job denies it outright. See
[[Shall Not the Judge of All the Earth Do Right]].

*"If the scourge kills suddenly, he will mock at the trial of the innocent"* (9:23) — *massat*,
either the *testing* of the innocent (from *nāsâ*, the verb of *"God tested Abraham"*, Genesis 22:1)
or their *despair* (from *māsas*, to melt). On the first reading Job has guessed, for the second
time, that he is being tested (compare 7:18). And then the hardest verse in the speech: *"The earth
is given into the hand of the wicked. He covers the faces of its judges. **If not he, then who is
it?**"* (9:24). Job assumes there is no one else it could be. **The reader knows there is someone
else in the story** — the one into whose hand everything Job has was given (1:12; 2:6). And the
reader also knows the epilogue's last word on it: *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him"*
(42:11). The question is answered both ways by the book, and Job is not told either answer.

**4. The fleeting days, and the useless washing (9:25–31).** The register drops to the personal.
*"My days are swifter than a runner"* — *rāṣ*, a courier, and the reader remembers four of them
arriving in one day (1:14–19). *"They have passed away as the swift ships, as the eagle that swoops
on the prey"* (9:25–26): a runner on land, a skiff on the water — *ʾŏniyyôt ʾēbeh*, boats of reed,
probably the light papyrus craft of Isaiah 18:2 — and a raptor in the air. Three speeds, three
elements, and in each the thing is gone before it is seen. This is 7:6's weaver's shuttle again.

*"If I say, 'I will forget my complaint, I will put off my sad face, and cheer up'"* (9:27) — he
considers taking the friends' advice and putting a brave face on it, and cannot, because ***"I know
that you will not hold me innocent"*** (9:28). *Lōʾ tənaqqēnî.* **That is the verb of Exodus 34:7**:
the God who forgives iniquity *"will by no means clear the guilty"* (*wənaqqēh lōʾ yənaqqeh*). At 7:21
Job asked why the forgiving half of that sentence was not being applied to him. Here he says the
other half is being applied instead — to a man who is not guilty. See
[[Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives]].

*"If I wash myself with snow, and cleanse my hands with lye, yet you will plunge me in the ditch. My
own clothes shall abhor me"* (9:30–31). **Every other washing in Scripture runs the other way.**
Isaiah 1:18 has God offering to make scarlet *"white as snow"*; Psalm 51:7 asks God to wash the
sinner *"whiter than snow"*; Jeremiah 2:22 says no amount of lye can clean a guilty Israel. Job has
clean hands and says God will throw him in the pit — *šaḥat*, a ditch or the grave — so that even his
clothes recoil from him. Cleansing is not unavailable to him; it is reversed.

**5. No umpire (9:32–35).** And now the sentence the passage is named for:

> For he is not a man, as I am, that I should answer him, that we should come together in judgment.
> There is no umpire between us, that might lay his hand on us both.

*Kî-lōʾ-ʾîš kāmōnî.* **Elsewhere God's not being a man is the reassurance**: *"God is not a man,
that he should lie"* (Numbers 23:19); *"I am God, and not man… I will not come in wrath"* (Hosea
11:9). Here it is the whole difficulty. A dispute between two men goes to a third. A dispute with God
has no venue, because there is no one above both parties — no *mōkîaḥ*, from *yākaḥ*, to decide or
set right — to put a hand on each of them. The image is the arbiter's gesture of authority: a hand on
both shoulders, holding both parties to the ruling.

**This is the first of the four legal officers Job reaches for**, and it is the only one he names as
missing. At 16:19 he will assert a witness in heaven; at 17:3 he will ask God to stand surety for
him against God; at 19:25 he will say *"I know that my Redeemer lives."* The reaching begins as an
absence. (Or as a wish: the Septuagint and the Syriac read *lû*, *would that there were*, for *lōʾ*,
*there is not* — one letter's difference, and the complaint becomes a prayer. See the Notes.) The
Christian reading of the line is inevitable and old: *"one mediator between God and men, **the man**
Christ Jesus"* (1 Timothy 2:5), an answer that meets 9:32's *"not a man, as I am"* with its own
noun. See [[The Witness in Heaven]] and [[Mediation and Advocacy]].

Job then states his only terms for the trial: *"Let him take his rod away from me. Let his terror
not make me afraid; then I would speak, and not fear him"* (9:34–35). He will repeat them almost word
for word at 13:21. **He does not ask God to stop being God. He asks to be able to speak without
being crushed while he does it.**

**6. To God: what is the charge? (10:1–7).** *"My soul is weary of my life. I will give free course
to my complaint. I will speak in the bitterness of my soul"* (10:1) — 7:11 again, nearly verbatim,
and this time it is followed through. *"I will tell God, 'Do not condemn me. Show me why you contend
with me'"* (10:2). It is 6:24's request — *"cause me to understand wherein I have erred"* — now made
to the right party. **A defendant has a right to the charge**, and Job asks for it, and does not get
it. He asks again at 13:23.

Then five questions, each harder than the last. *"Is it good to you that you should oppress, that
you should despise the work of your hands?"* (10:3) — *timʾas*, ***māʾas*** for the third time in two
speeches: Job despises his life (7:16; 9:21), and now he asks whether God despises *him*, which
Bildad had said God never does to the blameless (8:20). The accuser had praised *"the work of his
hands"* — Job's — as what God had blessed (1:10). Now Job is the work of God's hands, and despised.

*"Do you have eyes of flesh? Or do you see as man sees? Are your days as the days of mortals… that
you inquire after my iniquity, and search after my sin?"* (10:4–6). **The question is whether God is
investigating like a man** — a magistrate who cannot see the heart and must dig for evidence, against
a deadline. *"For man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart"* (1 Samuel
16:7). If God sees as God, he does not need to search. And so the speech's sharpest line:
***"Although you know that I am not wicked"*** (10:7). The reader has heard God say it — twice, in
*"there is no one like him in the earth"* (1:8; 2:3). **Job's claim about what God
knows is exactly true**, and he has no way of knowing that it is.

**7. The hands that made me (10:8–22).** The speech's longest and most beautiful movement, and its
cruellest:

> Your hands have framed me and fashioned me altogether, yet you destroy me. Remember, I beg you,
> that you have fashioned me as clay. Will you bring me into dust again? Haven't you poured me out
> like milk, and curdled me like cheese? You have clothed me with skin and flesh, and knit me
> together with bones and sinews. You have granted me life and loving kindness. Your visitation
> has preserved my spirit.

This is the Bible's most detailed account of human formation in the womb: conception as milk
poured and set (10:10), then the body dressed in skin and flesh and *woven* — *təśōkəkēnî* — with
bones and sinews (10:11). **The verb is Psalm 139's**: *"you knit me together (təsukkēnî) in my
mother's womb"* (139:13). And 10:8's opening is Psalm 119:73's —
*"Your hands have made me and formed me"* — except that the psalm goes on *"give me understanding"*,
and Job goes on *"yet you destroy me."* The clay and the dust are Genesis 2:7 and 3:19, and the
second request to God to *remember* (*zəkor-nāʾ*, 10:9) follows the first (7:7). See
[[You Have Fashioned Me as Clay - Job 10.8-12, Psalm 139, and the Potter]].

And then 10:12: *"You have granted me life and loving kindness (ḥesed). Your visitation
(pəquddātəkā) has preserved my spirit."* **This is the only verse in Job's speeches so far that
remembers God's goodness.** *Pāqad* — the verb that at 7:18 meant surveillance, *"visit him every
morning"* — here means the care that kept him alive. Job knows God's attention both ways.

The turn comes in the next verse, and it is the darkest reading of creation in the Bible: ***"Yet you
hid these things in your heart. I know that this is with you"*** (10:13). The care was the cover.
**God made Job with the intention of watching him**, and the next verse says what for: *"if I sin,
then you mark me"* (10:14). *Šəmartanî* — the same verb, *šāmar*, that one verse earlier said God's
visitation had *preserved* (*šāmərâ*) his spirit. **Two verses apart, God's keeping becomes God's
watch.** The reader knows God did have something hidden in his heart concerning Job — *"Have you set
your heart on my servant Job?"* (1:8) — but not this.

No outcome is acceptable: *"If I am wicked, woe to me. If I am righteous, I still shall not lift up
my head"* (10:15). If he raises it, *"you hunt me like a lion. Again you show yourself powerful to
me"* (10:16) — *titpallāʾ*, from the root of the *marvelous things* (*niplāʾôt*) of 9:10. **God's
wonders, which Eliphaz said were for the lowly, are being performed on Job.** *"You renew your
witnesses against me… Changes and warfare are with me"* (10:17) — *ḥălîpôt wəṣābāʾ*, relays of
troops, one detachment relieving another. At 14:14 the same two words will turn into hope: *"all
the days of my warfare (ṣəbāʾî) would I wait, until my release (ḥălîpātî) should come."*

And the speech ends where chapter 3 began, but spoken *to* God: *"Why, then, have you brought me out
of the womb? I wish I had given up the spirit, and no eye had seen me"* (10:18). Then a last plea —
not for vindication, but for a little time: *"Aren't my days few? Cease then. Leave me alone, that I
may find a little comfort"* (10:20) — *ʾablîgâ*, the *cheer up* of 9:27, the brightened face he said
he could not manage. **He asks God to step back long enough for him to smile once before he dies.**

> before I go where I shall not return from, to the land of darkness and of the shadow of death; the
> land dark as midnight, of the shadow of death, without any order, where the light is as midnight.

*Ṣalmāwet*, the shadow of death, twice — chapter 3's word (3:5). *Lōʾ sədārîm*, *without any
order*, a word found nowhere else in the Bible: the land of the dead is the one place left
uncreated, where Genesis 1's ordering never reached. And the last clause — *wattōpaʿ kəmô-ʾōpel*,
*it shines like darkness* — is the light of Genesis 1:3 run backwards: in that land even the light
is dark. **The speech's final word is *ʾōpel*, gloom.** See [[Sheol]].

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — speaking for the third time, and **for the first time as a litigant.** Three
  things are new. He calls himself *tām*, blameless (9:21), the word God used of him. He names the
  thing he lacks — an umpire (9:33) — and so begins the search that runs through 16:19, 17:3, and
  19:25. And he asks God for the charge (10:2). He also says, without knowing it, two things the
  reader knows to be true: that he is wounded *ḥinnām*, without cause (9:17), and that God knows he
  is not wicked (10:7).
- [[God]] — talked about through most of chapter 9 and talked to through all of chapter 10, and in
  both modes the subject of every active verb in the speech. He removes mountains, seals stars,
  treads the sea, passes by unseen, snatches, breaks with a storm, mocks, covers judges' faces,
  plunges into the pit,
  contends, despises, searches, framed, fashioned, poured, curdled, clothed, knit, granted, preserved,
  hid, marks, hunts, renews witnesses. **The fullest portrait of God in the book so far, and Job
  drew it.**
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — answered in one line (9:2) and never addressed again. Job takes his
  closing words, *tām* and *māʾas* (8:20), and returns both at 9:21–22; and his Genesis 18 certainty
  (8:3) is denied at 9:22.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — not addressed, and quoted three times: the night-spirit's question
  (4:17 → 9:2), the doxology (5:9 → 9:10), and the verb of the passing spirit (4:15 → 9:11). The
  *ḥadrê tēmān*, *the rooms of the south* (9:9), are literally *the chambers of Teman* — the word for
  south is the name of Eliphaz's homeland. Probably no more than the ordinary word; see the Notes.
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — not addressed; he answers this speech next, in [[Zophar's First Speech]]. *"Should a man full of
  talk be justified?"* (11:2) takes 9:2's *yiṣdaq*, and *"oh that God would speak, and open his lips
  against you"* (11:5) wishes for the hearing Job has just said cannot happen.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — not named, and described at 10:21–22 as fully as anywhere in the book: the land of no
  return, *ṣalmāwet* twice, *without any order*, where the light itself is darkness. Compare its
  first description at 3:13–19, where it was rest.
- **The rooms of the south** — 9:9, *ḥadrê tēmān*: most likely the constellations of the southern
  sky, below the horizon of the other three. Not a place on earth and given no page. See
  [[The Bear, Orion, and the Pleiades]].

## Themes

- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — 9:33, the first of Job's four officers and the only one named as
  absent: the *mōkîaḥ* who could put a hand on both parties.
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣādaq* in its forensic sense, *to be in the right in a case* — four times in
  two chapters, against five of its opposite, *rāšaʿ*, *to be found guilty*. 9:2 turns Eliphaz's
  moral question (4:17) into a legal one, and 9:15, 20 say that being righteous would not help.
- [[Judgment]] — the speech is a trial with no venue: *"who will summon me?"* (9:19), *"come
  together in judgment"* (9:32), *"show me why you contend with me"* (10:2), *"you renew your
  witnesses against me"* (10:17).
- [[Integrity]] — 9:21, *tām-ʾānî*, Job's first claim of the word for himself, set against Bildad's
  conditional *tām* (8:20) and the verdict of 1:1, 8 and 2:3.
- [[Retribution]] — 9:22–24, the doctrine denied in its own terms: God *"destroys the blameless and
  the wicked"*, and the earth is in the hand of the wicked.
- [[Creation]] — twice: the cosmos (9:5–10), praised as power that unmakes; and the person
  (10:8–12), the most detailed account of formation in the womb in the Bible, praised as care and
  then read as a plot (10:13).
- [[Prayer]] — 10:2–22, the longest continuous address to God in the book so far, made of
  questions, two imperatives — *remember* (10:9) and *cease* (10:20) — and no thanksgiving.
- [[Suffering]] — given a cause the sufferer cannot reach (*"without cause"*, 9:17) and a judge he
  cannot summon.
- [[Guilt and Conscience]] — 9:28–31: guilt as a verdict imposed from outside, which no washing of
  the conscience can affect. *"You will not hold me innocent"* — *nāqâ*, the verb of Exodus 34:7.
- [[Sin]] — 10:6, 14: sin searched for and *marked*, never named. Job neither confesses nor claims
  sinlessness; he asks what the charge is.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — God is not seen (9:11), sees *"as man sees"* or not (10:4), and Job
  wishes *"no eye had seen me"* (10:18).
- [[Remembrance]] — 10:9, *zəkor-nāʾ*, Job's second request to God to remember, after 7:7.
- [[Testing]] — 9:23, *massat nəqiyyim*, possibly *the testing of the innocent*.
- [[Wisdom]] — 9:4, *"wise in heart, and mighty in strength"*: God's wisdom invoked as one more
  reason no one can stand against him.
- [[Life and Death]] — the death wish restated to God (10:18–19), and death as the land of no return
  (10:21–22).
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the sun commanded not to rise (9:7) and a land where *"the light is as
  midnight"* (10:22), the speech's last word.

## Notes

- **The speech has no addressee in the plural.** Every *you* in chapters 9–10 is singular and is God
  (9:28, 31; 10:2–22). Bildad's whole speech is conceded in one clause and the friends are never
  spoken to; compare chapters 6–7, where the friends had seventeen verses (6:14–30). **The structural
  shift of the book — from argument with men to argument with God — happens here.** The friends will
  be addressed again in chapter 12, with contempt.

- **"Truly I know that it is so" (9:2) — what does Job concede?** Most naturally Bildad's 8:3, that
  God does not pervert justice, and perhaps Eliphaz's 4:17, that no man is more righteous than God.
  The concession is real but useless: Job grants the principle and then shows that, in a court where
  God is also the opposing party and the judge, the principle cannot be applied. Some readers hear
  the line as sarcasm. The wiki reads it as sincere, because everything after it depends on Job
  believing God *is* just — otherwise there would be no point in asking for a trial.

- **9:17, "with a storm" or "for a hair"?** The Masoretic *biśʿārâ* is *with a storm*. The
  consonants also read *bəśaʿărâ*, *for a hair* — *for a trifle* — which is how the Targum and some
  rabbinic readers took it, and which suits *without cause* in the next clause. The wiki follows the
  WEB and the majority, partly because the whirlwind of 38:1 makes the storm reading resonate, and
  notes that the *trifle* reading makes the same accusation in a different image.

- **9:33, "there is no umpire" or "would that there were an umpire"?** The Hebrew is *lōʾ yēš*,
  *there is not*. The Septuagint (*eith' ēn*) and the Syriac read *lû yēš*, *would that there were* —
  one letter's difference, *lw* for *lʾ*. On the Masoretic
  reading 9:33 is a complaint about an absence, which is how [[The Witness in Heaven]] and
  [[Mediation and Advocacy]] have stated it; on the versional reading it is already a wish, and the
  progression to 16:19 is shorter. The wiki keeps the Masoretic reading as primary and flags the
  alternative on both pages.

- **The constellations (9:9).** *ʿĀš*, *kəsîl*, and *kîmâ* are conventionally the Bear, Orion, and
  the Pleiades, but only *kîmâ* (a *cluster* or *heap*) is close to certain. *ʿĀš* has been taken
  as the Great Bear, the Hyades, or Aldebaran; *kəsîl*, literally *fool*, as Orion — the giant bound
  in the sky for his rebellion in later tradition. *Ḥadrê tēmān*, *the chambers of the south*,
  is usually the southern constellations. *Tēmān* is the ordinary word for *south* and also the
  name of Eliphaz's home; the book does not seem to make anything of the coincidence, and the wiki
  does not either. See [[The Bear, Orion, and the Pleiades]].

- **9:24 and the book's two answers.** *"If not he, then who is it?"* is the one point in the
  dialogue where a character asks the question the prologue answers. The prologue's answer is
  divided: the accuser did the striking (1:12; 2:7), and God allowed it, and called it his own act
  (2:3). The epilogue is undivided: *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him"* (42:11). **The
  wiki notes that Job's assumption — that it is God — is the one the epilogue confirms**, and that
  the book never tells Job about the other party.

- **"Eyes of flesh" (10:4).** Job's three questions in 10:4–5 are a reductio. If God sees as a man
  sees, he must investigate; if his days are a man's days, he must hurry; but neither is true, so the
  investigation is not for information. It must be for something else. 10:13 says what Job thinks it
  is. The irony the reader holds is that the prologue *does* show a God who wants to find something
  out — whether Job fears God for nothing — though not by searching Job for sin.

- **Milk and cheese (10:10).** The image is ancient embryology: the seed poured like milk and set
  like curd in the womb. Wisdom of Solomon 7:2 has the same picture (*"compacted in blood, of the
  seed of man"*), and Aristotle describes conception as the curdling of milk by rennet. Job's is the
  only version in the Hebrew Bible.

- **10:13 — "these things" and "this."** *Wəʾēlleh ṣāpantā bilbābekā; yādaʿtî kî-zōʾt ʿimmāk.* The
  hidden *things* could be the care of 10:8–12 (God hid his true purpose behind them) or the hostile
  intent of 10:14–17 (God concealed it all along while caring for him). Either way the claim is the
  same: the care had an ulterior purpose. **This is the most suspicious thing Job ever says about God,
  and it is said in the middle of the most tender thing he says.**

- **"Hunt me like a lion" (10:16).** The subject of the simile is ambiguous: either God hunts Job as
  a lion hunts, or God hunts Job as one hunts a lion. The WEB follows the first. The second fits
  *"if my head is held high"* — a lion is the one animal that does not lower its head — and God's own
  question at 38:39, *"Can you hunt the prey for the lioness?"*, where the lion is fed, not hunted.
