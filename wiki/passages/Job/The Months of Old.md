# The Months of Old

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 29:1–25

## Summary

*"Job again took up his parable, and said"* (29:1). The heading that ends the hymn to wisdom is the
heading of 27:1 repeated, and it opens Job's last speech — three chapters, 29–31, that are his
closing statement in the case. They move in three tenses: **what he was** (29), **what he is** (30),
and **what he swears he has never done** (31). This first chapter is the only speech of Job's in the
book that contains no complaint, no accusation, and no address to anyone. It is a memory, held up
whole, and it is the book's only description of the life the prologue summarised in a sentence.

**1. God with him (29:2–6).** It begins with the wish formula Job has used at every turning point of
the dialogue — *mî-yittənēnî*, *"Oh that"* (6:8; 14:13; 19:23; 23:3): *"Oh that I were as in the
months of old, as in the days when God watched over me"* (29:2). Everything that follows is under
that wish. The verb, *yišmərēnî*, is the *keep* of the priestly blessing, *"Yahweh bless you, and
keep you"* (Numbers 6:24). Then four images of God's nearness: his lamp shining on Job's head, *"and
by his light I walked through darkness"* (29:3); *"the friendship of God was in my tent"* (29:4) —
*sôd ʾĕlôah*, the intimate counsel of God, the very thing Eliphaz mocked him for claiming, *"Have you
heard the secret counsel of God?"* (15:8); *"the Almighty was yet with me, and my children were
around me"* (29:5); and a land running with abundance, *"my steps were washed with butter, and the
rock poured out streams of oil for me"* (29:6). **God and the children are named in the same
verse, and both in the past tense.** It is the only line in the poetry where Job remembers his
children alive. And after 29:5, God is not mentioned again in the chapter.

**2. Honour at the gate (29:7–10).** *"When I went out to the city gate, when I prepared my seat in
the street"* (29:7) — the gate is where the elders sat as a court, where cases were heard and
contracts witnessed (Ruth 4:1–2), and Job had a seat there. Its whole society made way for him:
*"The young men saw me and hid themselves. The aged rose up and stood"* (29:8) — the old men doing
for him what Leviticus 19:32 says the young should do for the old. *"The princes refrained from
talking, and laid their hand on their mouth"* (29:9); *"the voice of the nobles was hushed"*
(29:10). These are the counselors, judges, princes and elders of 12:17–21, the men Job said God
strips and humbles at will. **He was the greatest of them, and he remembers it as silence**: the
gesture of 29:9 is the one he demanded of the friends at 21:5 and will make himself before God at
40:4. See [[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].

**3. Why: what he did (29:11–17).** The centre of the chapter, and its reason. The ear that heard him
*blessed* him (*wattəʾaššərēnî*, the *ʾašrê* of *"Blessed is the man"*), *"Because I delivered the
poor who cried, and the fatherless also, who had no one to help him"* (29:12). *"The blessing of him
who was ready to perish came on me, and I caused the widow's heart to sing for joy"* (29:13). Then
the chapter's most famous line:

> ***"I put on righteousness, and it clothed me. My justice was as a robe and a diadem."*** (29:14)

*Ṣedeq lābaštî wayyilbāšēnî* — literally *I put on righteousness, and it put me on*: the garment wore
the man. The robe (*məʿîl*) and the turban (*ṣānîp*) are the dress of rank; **his honour was not his
wealth but his justice, worn as his insignia.** *"I was eyes to the blind, and feet to the lame. I was
a father to the needy"* (29:15–16) — Psalm 68:5's title for God, *"A father of the fatherless, and a
defender of the widows"*, given in practice by a man. And *"The cause of him who I didn't know, I
searched out"* (29:16): the cause is *rîb*, a lawsuit — Job's own word for his case against God —
and the verb is *ḥāqar*, the miner's *searches out* (28:3) and God's (28:27). **Job once investigated
the case of a stranger who had no advocate; now he is a man with a case and no one will hear it.**
Last, the defence turns to force: *"I broke the jaws of the unrighteous, and plucked the prey out of
his teeth"* (29:17) — the oppressor as a beast of prey, and Job as the one who took the victim back.

**This is Eliphaz's charge sheet answered item by item, before it is sworn to in chapter 31.** At
22:6–9 Eliphaz accused Job of stripping the naked, withholding bread, sending widows away empty, and
breaking the arms of the fatherless. Here, without naming him, Job says the widow sang and the
fatherless were helped. And Eliphaz had already testified for him: *"you have strengthened the weak
hands. Your words have supported him who was falling"* (4:3–4). On this one point the only two
witnesses in the dialogue agree, and one of them is the prosecutor. See
[[Bread to the Hungry and Clothing to the Naked - Job 22.6-9, Job 31, and Matthew 25]].

**4. What he expected (29:18–20).** *"Then I said, 'I shall die in my own house, I shall count my days
as the sand. My root is spread out to the waters. The dew lies all night on my branch. My glory is
fresh in me. My bow is renewed in my hand'"* (29:18–20). A full old age, a rooted tree, an unbroken
bow: exactly the end Eliphaz promised a repentant Job — *"You shall come to your grave in a full age,
like a shock of grain comes in its season"* (5:26) — and exactly the reverse of Bildad's portrait of
the wicked, *"His roots shall be dried up beneath. Above shall his branch be cut off"* (18:16).
Job's tree is the righteous man's tree of Psalm 1:3 and Jeremiah 17:8, roots by the water. And
*"My glory is fresh in me"* is the glory of 19:9, *"He has stripped me of my glory, and taken the crown
from my head"* — the crown is the diadem of 29:14, taken off.

**5. The counsellor (29:21–25).** The chapter returns to the gate, as if the memory could not let it
go. *"Men listened to me, waited, and kept silence for my counsel. After my words they didn't speak
again"* (29:21–22) — the reverse of the dialogue, where three men have refused to be silent for
twenty-five chapters. *"They waited for me as for the rain"* (29:23), the spring rain, *malqôš*,
the late rain that ripens the harvest. *"They didn't reject the light of my face"* (29:24) — the
shining face of Numbers 6:25, on a man. And the last line, which is the sharpest in the chapter:

> *"I chose out their way, and sat as chief. I lived as a king in the army, as one who comforts the
> mourners."* (29:25)

*Kaʾăšer ʾăbēlîm yənaḥēm* — **the comforter's verb, *nḥm*, the verb of the friends' errand at 2:11
and of Job's verdict on them at 16:2.** He was what they came to be and could not. See
[[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].

**Where this sits in the book.** The chapter does three things the dialogue never did. It shows the
reader what *"the greatest of all the children of the east"* (1:3) meant — not the livestock 1:3
counted but a standing among men that was the visible return of justice. It supplies the evidence
for the prologue's *"blameless and upright"* (1:1) in Job's own voice, and in the terms of the
canonical list of righteousness that the friends' theology turned against him. And it sets up the
next chapter as its exact negative: 30:1 opens *"But now"*, and every honour of 29 is reversed —
the young who hid from him now mock him (30:1), the nobles' silence becomes spitting (30:10), and
*"my harp has turned to mourning"* (30:31), Job now the mourner no one comforts. **Chapter 29 is the
"before" photograph, and it is entered into evidence.**

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — the speaker, under a new heading (29:1) that repeats 27:1. **The book's only
  portrait of him before the catastrophe in his own words**, and the only speech of his with no
  complaint and no addressee: protector of the poor, the widow and the fatherless, eyes to the blind,
  a father to the needy, a judge at the gate, a comforter of mourners. *"I put on righteousness, and it
  clothed me"* (29:14).
- [[God]] — present only in the first four verses and only in the past: the one who *"watched over"*
  Job (29:2), whose lamp shone on his head (29:3), whose *sôd*, *friendship*, was in his tent (29:4),
  and who *"was yet with me"* (29:5). Named as *ʾĔlôah* (29:2, 4) and *Šadday* (29:5), then not again.
  **Job's grief in this chapter is first of all for God's company**, and only afterwards for honour.
- [[Job's Children]] — *"my children were around me"* (29:5): **the only time in the poetry Job
  remembers them alive**, and in the same verse as the Almighty, both gone.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — not named, and answered throughout. 29:12–17 is the positive record
  against his charge sheet of 22:6–9; 29:4's *sôd ʾĕlôah* is the secret counsel he said Job had never
  heard (15:8); 29:18's death in a full age is the end he promised (5:26); and his own testimony to
  Job's kindness (4:3–4) agrees with it.

## Places

- [[Uz]] — implied by *"the city"* (29:7), its gate, and its square (*rəḥôb*). This is the only place
  in the book where Job is shown living in a town rather than among flocks; the city is unnamed.

## Themes

- [[Remembrance]] — the whole chapter is a memory, framed as a wish (*"Oh that I were as in the months
  of old"*, 29:2); the "before" against which chapter 30's *"But now"* is measured.
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣedeq* worn as a garment and *mišpāṭ* as a robe and turban (29:14); the
  righteous man defined, as everywhere in Scripture, by what he does for those who cannot repay. See
  [[I Put On Righteousness - Job 29.14 and the Garments of Justice]].
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — the poor, the fatherless, the perishing, the widow, the blind, the lame,
  the needy, and the stranger with a lawsuit (29:12–16).
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — clothed in righteousness (29:14), against Eliphaz's charge that Job
  *"stripped the naked of their clothing"* (22:6) and Job's own *"He has stripped me of my glory"*
  (19:9).
- [[Light and Darkness]] — *"by his light I walked through darkness"* (29:3), against 19:8's *"set
  darkness in my paths"*; and *"the light of my face"* (29:24). See [[The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out]].
- [[Walking with God]] — the lamp on the head and the friendship in the tent (29:3–4): the book's
  nearest description of what Job's piety felt like from inside.
- [[Blessing]] — the ear that *"blessed me"* (29:11), *"the blessing of him who was ready to perish"*
  (29:13); and God's *keeping* (29:2), the verb of Numbers 6:24.
- [[Language and Speech]] — princes silent, nobles hushed, men who *"kept silence for my counsel"* and
  *"didn't speak again"* after his words (29:9–10, 21–22): the gate's order of speech, which the
  dialogue has overturned.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — *"I lived as a king in the army"* (29:25): royal language for a judge, and
  royal duty — the protection of the widow and orphan — as his practice.
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — *"as one who comforts the mourners"* (29:25); the next chapter makes him
  the mourner.
- [[Hope]] — the expectation of 29:18–20, a rooted tree and a full age, spoken in the past tense. See
  [[There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts]].
- [[City and Civilization]] — the city gate and its square as the place of justice and honour
  (29:7–10, 21–25).

## Notes

- **The structure.** The chapter is usually read as a ring: God's presence (2–6), honour at the gate
  (7–10), Job's deeds (11–17), his expectation (18–20), honour at the gate again (21–25). The deeds sit
  at the centre and explain both rings of honour — *"For when the ear heard me … Because I delivered the
  poor"* (29:11–12). **Several commentators move 29:21–25 to follow 29:10**, so that the gate scene is
  continuous and the chapter ends on the tree of 18–20; no manuscript supports the move, and the wiki
  reads the received order, in which the speech circles back to the gate as memory does.
- **"The ripeness of my days" (29:4).** *Yəmê ḥorpî*, literally *the days of my autumn* — *ḥōrep* is
  the harvest season, the year's fullness rather than its decline. The WEB's *ripeness* catches the
  sense: the prime of life, not old age.
- **"As the sand" (29:18).** *Wəkaḥôl ʾarbeh yāmîm*. *Ḥôl* is *sand*, and the WEB reads it so; but a
  Jewish interpretive tradition, followed by some modern translations, takes it as the name of the
  *phoenix*, the bird that dies in its nest and is reborn — which would explain *ʿim-qinnî*, *with my
  nest*, in the first half of the line (the WEB's *in my own house*). The wiki follows the WEB and
  records the alternative; on either reading the line is a wish for a long life and a death at home.
- **"It clothed me" (29:14).** *Wayyilbāšēnî* — righteousness is the subject of the second verb, and Job
  its object. The same idiom, *lābaš* with a person as the garment, describes the Spirit of Yahweh
  coming on Gideon at Judges 6:34 (the WEB renders it *came on*). See
  [[I Put On Righteousness - Job 29.14 and the Garments of Justice]].
- **A tension within Job, not between pages.** At 3:25 Job said *"the thing which I fear comes on me"*,
  as if he had dreaded catastrophe in his prosperity; at 29:18 he remembers saying *"I shall die in my
  own house."* The two need not conflict — confidence spoken aloud and dread kept inside are both
  plausible in one man, and 1:5's continual sacrifices show the anxiety — but the wiki notes both
  rather than harmonising them.
- **God absent from the honours.** After 29:5 the chapter does not mention God. Job's virtues in 29:11–17
  are named as his own acts, and the honour as men's response to them. The friends would call that
  self-righteousness, and Elihu will (32:2); but it is the form of a defence, not a boast: the facts
  of the case, laid out before the oath of chapter 31 puts them under a curse.
- **No contradiction between wiki pages found.** The existing pages anticipated this chapter (29:3 at
  [[The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out]], 29:9 at [[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]],
  29:12–17 at [[Bread to the Hungry and Clothing to the Naked - Job 22.6-9, Job 31, and Matthew 25]],
  29:19 at [[There Is Hope for a Tree - The Stump That Sprouts]]), and the text bears each of them out.
