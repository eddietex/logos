# Zophar's First Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 11:1–20

## Summary

Job has just spent a chapter and a half talking to God and not to his friends — *"Show me why you
contend with me"* (10:2), *"you know that I am not wicked"* (10:7), and at the end a request to be
let alone a little before he goes to *"the land dark as midnight"* (10:22).
[[Zophar the Naamathite]], the third friend, speaks for the first time, and he answers that prayer
as though it had been a boast. **He is the only friend who says plainly what the other two have only
implied: Job is guilty, and he is getting less than he deserves** (11:6).

The speech has twenty verses and four movements.

**1. The rebuke, and the wish (11:2–6).** *"Shouldn't the multitude of words be answered? Should a
man full of talk be justified?"* — *ʾîš śəpātayim yiṣdāq*, *a man of lips*. The verb is the one Job
had just turned into the book's legal question: *"how can man be just (yiṣdaq) with God?"* (9:2).
Zophar's answer is that a man who talks this much cannot be. Then *"When you mock, shall no man make
you ashamed?"* (11:3) — *tilʿag*, the verb Job had used of God: *"he will mock (yilʿag) at the trial
of the innocent"* (9:23). Job said God mocks; Zophar says Job does.

**And then he quotes Job, and gets him wrong.** *"For you say, 'My doctrine is pure. I am clean in
your eyes'"* (11:4). Job has said *"I am blameless"* (9:21) and, to God, *"you know that I am not
wicked"* (10:7). He has never claimed a pure *leqaḥ* — *teaching*, the word of Proverbs 4:2 and
Deuteronomy 32:2 — and the *pure* (*zak*) is not his word but Bildad's, the condition of 8:6: *"If you
were pure and upright"*. Zophar puts the friends' condition into Job's mouth as a claim, and the
*"in your eyes"* — addressed to God — shows that what he is quoting is not anything Job said to the
friends but **the prayer of chapter 10, overheard and reported as arrogance.**

*"But oh that God would speak, and open his lips against you"* (11:5). *Mî-yittēn* — *oh that*, *who
will grant* — **is Job's own formula**, the shape of every one of his wishes: *"Oh that I might have
my request"* (6:8), *"Oh that you would hide me in Sheol"* (14:13), *"Oh that my words were now
written!"* (19:23), *"Oh that I knew where I might find him!"* (23:3), *"oh that I had one to hear
me!"* (31:35). Zophar takes it and wishes for the same thing Job wishes for — that God would speak —
expecting the opposite result. **It is the most precisely fulfilled wish in the book, and it is
fulfilled against the man who made it.** God does open his lips, at 38:1; and when he has finished
with Job he turns to Eliphaz: *"My wrath is kindled against you, and against your two friends"*
(42:7). Zophar asked for God to speak against Job, and God spoke against Zophar.

What he expects God to reveal is *"the secrets of wisdom"* (11:6) — *taʿălumôt ḥokmâ*, hidden things.
**The reader holds one.** Job 1:6–12 is precisely a secret of the divine counsel, shown to no one in
the story, and it is the one fact that would demolish this speech. Zophar wishes the hidden things
of God revealed in the confidence that they would convict Job; the only hidden thing the book
actually shows us acquits him. See [[The Divine Council]].

Then the sentence for which he is remembered: ***"Know therefore that God exacts of you less than
your iniquity deserves."*** The Hebrew *yaššeh ləkā ʾĕlôah mēʿăwōnekā* is ambiguous between two verbs
spelled alike — *nāšâ*, *to forget*, giving *God has forgotten some of your iniquity* (NASB), and
*nāšâ*, *to lend or exact as a creditor*, giving the WEB's *exacts of you less*. Both say the same
thing. **The ten dead children, the lost herds and servants, the sores from sole to crown are, on
Zophar's account, a discount.** Eliphaz suggested Job was being corrected (5:17); Bildad said the
children had sinned (8:4); Zophar says Job's own sin is greater than everything that has happened to
him. The book answers him at both ends — *"he multiplies my wounds without cause"* is true by God's
own word (2:3), and the epilogue gives Job *"twice as much as he had before"* (42:10). Isaiah will use
Zophar's word for the opposite verdict: Jerusalem *"has received of Yahweh's hand double (kiplayim)
for all her sins"* (Isaiah 40:2) — the *double* of Zophar's 11:6 turned from the size of wisdom into
the size of the payment, which is more than the sin and not less.

**2. The unsearchable God (11:7–12).** *"Can you fathom the mystery of God? Or can you probe the
limits of the Almighty? They are high as heaven. What can you do? They are deeper than Sheol. What
can you know? Its measure is longer than the earth, and broader than the sea."* This is the finest
passage in the speech, and one of the finest in the friends' whole contribution. **Four dimensions —
height, depth, length, breadth** — each set against one of the four regions of the cosmos: heaven,
[[Sheol]], earth, sea. The word for *mystery* is *ḥēqer*, *the searched-out depth*: Eliphaz's
*"unsearchable"* (*ʾên ḥēqer*, 5:9), which Job had just repeated word for word (9:10), and which God
will turn on Job from the whirlwind — *"Have you walked in the recesses (ḥēqer) of the deep?"*
(38:16). **Zophar's premise is exactly God's.** What differs is the inference. Zophar concludes that
Job should stop talking; God, from the same premise, will conclude that Job should look. See
[[Higher Than Heaven, Deeper Than Sheol - The Measure of the Unsearchable]].

*"If he passes by, or confines, or convenes a court, then who can oppose him?"* (11:10). **This verse
is made of Job's words.** *Yaḥălōp*, *he passes by*, is 9:11 — *"he passes on also, but I don't
perceive him"* — and *mî yəšîbennû*, *who can turn him back*, is 9:12 exactly: *"Who can hinder
him?"* Job used both phrases to describe a God he could not bring to court. Zophar uses them to say
that God is the court — *yaqhîl*, *he convenes the assembly* — and that Job is on trial before it.

*"For he knows false men. He sees iniquity also, even though he doesn't consider it"* (11:11). Job had
asked God, *"Do you have eyes of flesh? Or do you see as man sees, that you inquire after my
iniquity?"* (10:4–6). Zophar's answer is that God sees iniquity without needing to investigate it.
The last clause, *wəlōʾ yitbônān*, can be a statement (*without considering it*, i.e., at a glance)
or a question (*will he not consider it?*), and the WEB's *"even though he doesn't consider it"* is
the less natural of the two. Either way the iniquity in view is Job's.

Then the insult that closes the movement: *"An empty-headed man becomes wise when a man is born as a
wild donkey's colt"* (11:12). *Wəʾîš nābûb yillābēb* — a pun: the *hollow* man (*nābûb*, the word
for the hollow altar of acacia boards, Exodus 27:8) will *get a heart* (*yillābēb*, from *lēb*). **It
will happen when a wild donkey gives birth to a man** — that is, never. The phrase *pereʾ ʾādām*,
*wild donkey … man*, is the same two words Genesis 16:12 uses of Ishmael, and *pereʾ* is the animal
Job had used of himself at 6:5 (*"Does the wild donkey bray when he has grass?"*) and that God will
boast of having set free at 39:5–8. See [[A Wild Donkey of a Man - Genesis 16.12 and Job 11.12]].

**3. The offer (11:13–19).** And then, after the harshest opening of any first speech, the most
generous close. *"If you set your heart aright, stretch out your hands toward him. If iniquity is in
your hand, put it far away. Don't let unrighteousness dwell in your tents."* Four conditions in two
verses, all of them the language of the liturgy: the prepared heart (*hăkînôtā libbekā*, 1 Samuel
7:3; Psalm 78:8), the spread palms of prayer (Exodus 9:29; 1 Kings 8:22, 38), and the hands cleared
of iniquity before they are raised — which is Isaiah 1:15's condition exactly: *"When you spread out
your hands, I will hide my eyes from you… Your hands are full of blood."* *"Your tents"* is the
household, and it is the third friend in a row to bring the tent into the speech (5:24; 8:22).

Then the promise, and every clause of it answers something Job has said.

- ***"Surely then you shall lift up your face without spot"*** (11:15). Job, five verses before
  Zophar began: *"If I am righteous, I still shall not lift up my head, being filled with disgrace"*
  (10:15). And *mûm*, *spot*, is the blemish that disqualifies a sacrificial animal (Leviticus
  22:20–21). Lifting the face is what God asked Cain about (*śəʾēt*, Genesis 4:7), and it is what God
  does for Job at the end: ***"Yahweh accepted Job"*** (42:9) — *wayyiśśāʾ yhwh ʾet-pənê ʾiyyôb*,
  **Yahweh lifted Job's face** — in the verse that names Zophar as one of the three standing there
  waiting for it.
- ***"you shall be steadfast, and shall not fear"*** — *mūṣāq*, *cast* like metal (37:18, in
  [[Elihu on God's Greatness]]), and
  *lōʾ tîrāʾ*. Job: *"the thing which I fear comes on me"* (3:25); *"then I would speak, and not fear
  him"* (9:35).
- ***"for you shall forget your misery. You shall remember it like waters that have passed
  away"*** (11:16). **This is Job's own image, turned inside out.** At 6:15 Job had described the
  friends as *"a brook, as the channel of brooks that pass away"* (*yaʿăbōrû*) — the wadi that fails
  the caravans when they need it. Zophar takes the same water, *mayim ʿābərû*, and makes it the
  misery that will pass. Job's figure for friends who disappear becomes Zophar's figure for grief
  that will.
- ***"Life shall be clearer than the noonday. Though there is darkness, it shall be as the
  morning"*** (11:17). Job's speech had ended in *"the land dark as midnight"* — *ʾereṣ ʿêpātâ* — and
  Zophar's *darkness* here, *tāʿupâ*, is from the same root, *ʿûp*. **He reverses the last line he
  heard.** It also reverses chapter 3, which asked for a day that would never *"see the eyelids of
  the morning"* (3:9). Isaiah 58:8–10 makes the same promise on a different condition — care for the
  hungry rather than confession — *"your light shall break forth as the morning… your obscurity
  shall be as the noonday."* See [[Light and Darkness]].
- ***"You shall be secure, because there is hope"*** (11:18). *Tiqwâ* — the noun Eliphaz offered
  (4:6), Job declared spent (7:6), and Bildad gave to the godless to lose (8:13). Zophar uses it
  twice, the second time three verses later for the wicked, and so is the friend who sets the two
  hopes side by side.
- ***"Also you shall lie down, and no one shall make you afraid"*** (11:19). *Wərābaṣtā wəʾên
  maḥărîd* — **the covenant blessing of Leviticus 26:6** (*"you will lie down, and no one will make you
  afraid"*), repeated by the prophets as the picture of the restored people (Micah 4:4; Zephaniah
  3:13; Ezekiel 34:28). *Rābaṣ* is the verb of flocks lying down. Zophar is quoting Israel's
  covenant to a man outside it. See
  [[None Shall Make You Afraid - The Covenant Blessing of Security]].
- ***"Yes, many shall court your favor."*** *Wəḥillû pānêkā rabbîm* — *many will stroke your face*,
  the idiom of a petitioner approaching a ruler with a gift (Psalm 45:12; Proverbs 19:6) and of
  intercession before God (Exodus 32:11). It happens. At 42:11 *"all his brothers, and all his
  sisters, and all those who had been of his acquaintance"* come with a piece of money and a ring of
  gold each — and before that, at 42:8–9, the three friends themselves come to Job, with bulls and
  rams, because only his prayer will turn God's wrath from them. **The first man to court Job's
  favour, in the end, is Zophar.** See
  [[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]].

**4. The warning (11:20).** *"But the eyes of the wicked shall fail. They shall have no way to flee.
Their hope shall be the giving up of the spirit."* One verse, like Bildad's closing 8:22, and with the
same function: the alternative, left hanging. *Mappaḥ-nāpeš* is *a breathing-out of life*. The
wicked's only hope is death. It is the shortest of the friends' portraits of the wicked man, and the
seed of the twenty-nine verses Zophar will give the same figure in chapter 20, by which time the offer
of 11:13–19 is gone.

## People

- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — his first speech, and the first words he says in the book. The
  harshest opening of the first cycle (*"less than your iniquity deserves"*, 11:6) and its last offer
  of a future (11:13–19), in twenty verses. He quotes Job twice without naming the source (9:11–12
  at 11:10, 6:15 at 11:16) and misquotes him once (11:4).
- [[Job (person)]] — addressed throughout in the second person, unlike chapter 20, where he is only
  described. Accused of mockery, of a claim to pure doctrine he never made, and of a hidden iniquity
  larger than his suffering. He answers with the longest speech of the first cycle (12–14), and its
  first line is *"No doubt, but you are the people, and wisdom shall die with you"* (12:2).
- [[God]] — named *ʾĕlôah* (11:5, 6, 7) and *Šadday* (11:7), never *Yahweh*. The subject of the
  speech's best passage, the God whose measure exceeds the four regions of the world, and of its
  worst sentence, the creditor who has let Job off part of his debt. Zophar wishes God would speak;
  when God does (38:1; 42:7), it is against him.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — silent. His *ḥēqer* (5:9) underlies 11:7, and his conditional promise
  (5:19–26) is the one Zophar compresses into 11:15–19 — the last time any friend offers it.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — silent. His *zak*, *pure* (8:6), is the word Zophar puts in Job's mouth at
  11:4, and his one-verse closing on the wicked (8:22) is the model for 11:20.
- [[Ishmael]] — not in the passage; *pereʾ ʾādām* (11:12) is the phrase of Genesis 16:12.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — 11:8, *"deeper than Sheol"*: the floor of the cosmos, set against *"high as heaven"*
  as the measure of what God exceeds. The first time in the book Sheol is used not as a destination
  for Job but as a dimension. Contrast the last verses Zophar heard, 10:21–22, where it was the land
  dark as midnight to which Job was going.
- [[Naamah]] — 11:1, *"Zophar, the Naamathite"*; the gentilic's second occurrence.

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — at its most severe. Eliphaz and Bildad read suffering as the measure of sin;
  Zophar says it *under*measures it (11:6). The doctrine can no longer be falsified by the size of a
  man's loss, because any loss is now less than he deserves.
- [[Wisdom]] — *"the secrets of wisdom"* (11:6) and the four dimensions of 11:7–9. True, and used to
  end an argument rather than to begin one. Chapter 28 will ask *"Where shall wisdom be found?"* with
  the same vocabulary of depth, height, and *taklît*, the farthest limit (28:3).
- [[Sin]] — as debt: an account on which God, the creditor, has not yet collected in full (11:6).
  And as something that dwells, which can be put far away from the hand and evicted from the tent
  (11:14).
- [[Forgiveness]] — on one reading of 11:6, *God forgets some of your iniquity*: the only place in
  the friends' speeches where God's forgetting of sin is mentioned, and it is used as an accusation.
- [[Prayer]] — 11:13's spread palms and prepared heart, the posture of Solomon at the dedication
  (1 Kings 8:22). Offered as the condition of restoration, and fulfilled at 42:8–10 with the roles
  reversed: Job prays, and Zophar is prayed for.
- [[Hope]] — *tiqwâ* twice, for Job (11:18) and for the wicked (11:20), three verses apart.
- [[Light and Darkness]] — 11:17, *"Though there is darkness, it shall be as the morning"*, which
  reverses the last line of Job's speech (10:22) and the first movement of chapter 3.
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — *zak* and *bar*, *pure* and *clean* (11:4), and *mûm*, the sacrificial
  blemish, at 11:15.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the speech opens on *"the multitude of words"* and *"a man of lips"*,
  the friends' standing complaint that Job talks too much. Its own central wish is that God would
  talk.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — 11:11, God sees iniquity without having to search for it: Zophar's
  answer to Job's *"Do you have eyes of flesh?"* (10:4).
- [[Remembrance]] — 11:16, misery remembered as water that has passed, and 11:6's forgetting.
- [[The Fear of God]] — *"you shall not fear"* (11:15) and *"no one shall make you afraid"*
  (11:19): fear promised away, to the man God called *"one who fears God"* (1:8).
- [[Righteousness]] — 11:2's *yiṣdaq*, *be justified*, Job's legal word of 9:2 used against him.
- [[Judgment]] — 11:10's *yaqhîl*, God convening the assembly: the court Job said could not be
  convened (9:32–33), described as already in session.
- [[Life and Death]] — 11:17's *ḥeled*, *lifespan*, made brighter than noon, against 10:20's *"Aren't
  my days few?"*; and 11:20's *"giving up of the spirit"*.

## Notes

- **Why Zophar is harsher, and why it matters that he is the third.** The first cycle's three
  speeches escalate in one direction. Eliphaz (4–5) suggests Job is being disciplined; Bildad (8)
  infers that his children sinned; Zophar (11) states that Job's own guilt exceeds his loss. Each
  speech keeps the same offer at the end and makes the accusation in front of it heavier. It is
  natural to read Zophar as a hothead, and he is — he says so himself at 20:2 — but the escalation
  is also the doctrine's own logic. Once Job refuses to confess, the only way to keep the system
  intact is to enlarge the guilt until it covers the facts. Zophar is the first to do it. Eliphaz,
  at 22:5–9, will finish the job by inventing the crimes.

- **11:6, two verbs and one meaning.** The ambiguity of *yaššeh* — *forget* or *exact* — is real and
  translations divide on it (KJV *exacteth*, NASB *forgets a part*, ESV *exacts … less*). The wiki
  records both and notes that they converge: whether God has forgotten part of the debt or declined
  to collect it, the debt is larger than what has been paid. The first half of the verse is harder.
  *Kî-kiplayim lətûšiyyâ*, literally *for double are they to sound wisdom*, is usually taken to mean
  that God's wisdom has two sides, a visible and a hidden one, or is twice what it appears; the WEB's
  *"true wisdom has two sides"* follows that. Some emend *kiplayim* to *kip(p)ləʾāyim*, *like
  wonders*, which would anticipate 11:7. The wiki follows the Masoretic text and the WEB.

- **11:12 and the WEB.** The WEB's *"when a man is born as a wild donkey's colt"* reverses the
  usual construal, which is *when a wild donkey's colt is born a man*. Either way the sense is an
  impossibility — the hollow man will gain sense on the day the impossible happens — and either way
  the insult is aimed at Job. A minority reading takes the verse as hopeful (*even a hollow man may
  gain sense, and a wild colt be born tame*), which would make it a hinge into the offer of 11:13.
  The wiki thinks the pun and the placement favour the insult and records the alternative.

- **11:18, *ḥāpartā*.** *"You shall search"* (WEB) takes the verb as *ḥāpar* I, *to dig* or *to
  search* — perhaps a nomad looking about his camp before sleeping, perhaps digging a well. *Ḥāpēr*
  II means *to be ashamed*, and some read *you were ashamed, but you will lie down in safety*. The
  WEB's reading fits the verse; the other fits the speech, whose opening asked whether Job would be
  made *ashamed* (11:3, *maklîm*). Recorded, not resolved.

- **The best passage, the worst use.** The wiki has said on [[Zophar the Naamathite]] that 11:7–9 is
  close to what God says from the whirlwind, and this ingest confirms it at the level of vocabulary:
  *ḥēqer* (11:7; 38:16), the sea and the earth and Sheol (11:8–9; 38:4–18), and the question form
  itself, *can you…?*, which is how God speaks for four chapters. What God does not repeat is
  11:6. The whirlwind speeches never mention Job's sin at all.

- **The speech's tense.** Every promise in 11:15–19 is future and every condition in 11:13–14 is
  present. Zophar is offering Job a future on the far side of a confession he does not make. The
  epilogue delivers almost every item without the confession — the face lifted, the many who court
  his favour, the darkness become morning — which is what the wiki has found with Eliphaz's 5:19–26
  and Bildad's 8:7. Three friends, three promises, all kept, none on their terms.
