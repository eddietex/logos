# The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 1:6–22

## Summary

Seventeen verses, two locations, and the most consequential scene change in the Old Testament.
Verse 6 lifts the camera off Uz and into a room the rest of the book will never see again. Verse 13
sets it back down. Everything the reader knows that no character knows was acquired in the seven
verses between.

**The scene in heaven is a court, and the accuser is a member of it.** *"Now on the day when God's
sons came to present themselves before Yahweh, Satan also came among them."* The phrase is
*bənê hāʾĕlōhîm* — the same words as Genesis 6:2, and the wiki's [[The Sons of God]] page collects
the evidence that everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible they are heavenly beings. Here they are
attending. *Hityaṣṣēb ʿal-YHWH*, to take one's stand before Yahweh, is the language of courtiers
presenting themselves to a king, and the accuser comes *"among them"* — not through a breach, not
in disguise, and not unexpected. He is questioned like a subordinate reporting in, he answers, and
when he leaves he leaves *"from the presence of Yahweh"*. **Nothing in the scene depicts a rival
power.** The figure who will become the adversary of the New Testament is here an officer whose
whole function is to ask a question, and who cannot lay a finger on anything until told the limits.

**And it is not the accuser who raises Job.** This is the hardest sentence in the prologue and it is
easy to read past: *"Yahweh said to Satan, 'Have you considered my servant, Job?'"* (1:8). The
accuser has reported only that he has been patrolling. God names Job, praises him, and puts him on
the table. Whatever the book is doing, it is not doing the thing where a righteous man attracts
malign attention and God reluctantly permits a trial; God moves first, and at 2:3 he says so — *"you
incited me against him, to ruin him without cause"* — accepting the incitement without disowning the
act.

**God's commendation is verse 1, quoted back word for word.** *"There is no one like him in the
earth, a blameless and an upright man, one who fears God, and turns away from evil."* The narrator
said it at 1:1, God says it at 1:8, and God says it again at 2:3. **Three times, from the only two
sources in the book that cannot be wrong** — and no human being inside the story hears any of them.
The friends will spend twenty-five chapters constructing a sinner to fit the ruin, and the reader
watches them do it holding a certificate they will never be shown. God also gives Job a title here
for the first time: *ʿabdî*, **my servant**, the honorific of Abraham (Genesis 26:24), Moses, David,
and the servant of Isaiah 42–53. He uses it of Job four times — 1:8, 2:3, and twice more at 42:7–8,
where it is the hinge of the epilogue's verdict.

**The accuser's question is the book.** *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* (1:9). The Hebrew word is
***ḥinnām*** — gratis, for no return, without cause. It is not a charge against Job's conduct;
nobody in this book, in heaven or on earth, ever disputes that Job is righteous. **It is a charge
against the existence of disinterested righteousness at all** — and therefore against God, because a
God who can only be loved by paying for it has never been loved. *"Haven't you made a hedge around
him, and around his house, and around all that he has, on every side? You have blessed the work of
his hands"* (1:10). Every item of that inventory was supplied by [[Job of Uz]], five verses earlier,
in a passage that reads as praise. The accuser reads the same five verses as a receipt.

**Then God gives the word back.** At 2:3 he says the accuser moved him to ruin Job ***ḥinnām*** — for
nothing, without cause. **The same word, in the same scene, from the other side.** The accuser asks
whether Job is good for nothing; God answers that Job was destroyed for nothing. **That single word
is the prologue's whole argument: the wager and the objection to the wager are stated in identical
Hebrew.** Job himself will pick it up at 9:17 — *"he multiplies my wounds without cause"* — and be
right, and have no way of knowing he is right.

**The permission is precise, and it transfers a hand.** The accuser proposes *"stretch out your hand
now, and touch all that he has"* — God's hand. God answers *"all that he has is in your power"*,
literally *in your hand*, *"only on himself don't stretch out your hand"* (1:11–12). The agency of
everything that follows is deliberately doubled by that exchange, and the book never resolves it. A
messenger will call one of the four disasters *"the fire of God"*; Job will say *"Yahweh has taken
away"*; the narrator will certify that saying so was not a sin; and 2:3 will have God say the
accuser did it. **All four statements stand, and the text makes no attempt to reconcile them.**

**The four messengers are a machine.** Each arrives *"while he was still speaking"*, so there is no
interval in which Job could absorb one loss before the next; each ends with the identical clause
*"and I alone have escaped to tell you"* — in Hebrew the same six words, four times. The agents
alternate: **human raid, act of God, human raid, act of God.** The Sabeans take the oxen and donkeys
from the south; *"the fire of God"* falls from the sky and burns the sheep; the Chaldeans in three
bands sweep down on the camels from the north; a great wind comes *"from the wilderness"* and kills
the ten children. Earth, heaven, earth, heaven — and the pattern is why the book cannot be read as a
story about bad luck or about wicked neighbours. **Two of the four are crimes committed by people who
will never be mentioned again**, and Job's verdict at 1:21 does not name them.

The order is climactic rather than the order of the inventory: draft animals, then flocks, then the
camels that represent the caravan capital, then the children. **The servants die in three of the
four reports** — *"they have killed the servants with the edge of the sword"*, *"burned up the sheep
and the servants"*, *"killed the servants with the edge of the sword"* — and the book never mentions
them again, not in the dialogue, not in the restoration at 42:10–17. They are the losses nobody in
the book counts.

**The last message is held back and told twice.** Verse 13 opens the scene by placing the children
*"eating and drinking wine in their oldest brother's house"*; verse 18 opens the fourth message with
the same clause, word for word, before the messenger says what happened to them. The narrator has
told the reader where the children are and then makes him wait through three other catastrophes to
hear it from a survivor. It is the only piece of dramatic technique in the prologue, and it is spent
on [[Job's Children]].

**Job's response is a mourning rite with something extra on the end.** He tears his robe, shaves his
head, falls to the ground — three conventional Near Eastern acts of grief, none of them piety — and
then *"worshiped"* (*hištaḥăwâ*), which is not a mourning rite at all. **The text does not have him
skip the grief and go straight to submission.** He performs the whole of it, publicly, and then does
one more thing that nobody required.

**And what he says answers the wager in the accuser's own vocabulary.** *"Naked I came out of my
mother's womb, and naked shall I return there. Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away. Blessed be
Yahweh's name."* The verb in that last clause is ***bārak***, *to bless* — the fourth time it has
appeared in the chapter, and the first time it has meant what it says. At 1:5 Job feared his children
had *bārak*-ed God in their hearts; at 1:11 the accuser predicted Job would *bārak* God to his face;
both times the sense is the opposite of the word, the scribal euphemism discussed at [[Job of Uz]].
**The accuser wagered that stripping Job would make him say *bārak* about God. Job says *bārak*
about God.** The prediction comes true in its letter and is destroyed in its meaning, and the book
gets there by using one verb in both directions inside seventeen verses.

**The narrator's verdict is exact and narrow.** *"In all this, Job did not sin, nor charge God with
wrongdoing"* (1:22). Not that Job said nothing about God — he named God as the one who took — but
that he charged him with nothing improper. The Hebrew noun is ***tiplâ***, a rare word for
unseemliness or tastelessness, which Job will himself use at 24:12 of what God does not appear to
notice. At 2:10 the same certification comes back one degree narrower — *"Job didn't sin with his
lips"* — and readers have argued for two millennia over whether the narrowing is stylistic or is
quietly making room for chapter 3.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — silent for fifteen verses while his case is argued in a room he does not know
  exists, then given eighteen Hebrew words that are the most quoted thing he ever says. Note what he
  does **not** do: he does not ask why, he does not name the Sabeans or the Chaldeans, and he does
  not send anyone to recover anything. He goes straight past every proximate cause to Yahweh. In
  chapter 3 he will begin saying the opposite kind of thing for twenty-eight chapters, and at 42:7
  God will commend him for it.
- [[Satan]] — *haśśāṭān*, **the accuser**, with the definite article in all fourteen of his
  occurrences in Job 1–2, which is why *Satan* is a translator's decision rather than the text's
  word. He appears in the book only here and at 2:1–7, is never mentioned again by anyone, in heaven
  or on earth, and is absent from the epilogue that settles everything else. See his page for the
  word, the article, and the three Old Testament passages that use it.
- [[God]] — who opens the subject, praises the man, grants the permission, and sets the limit. Named
  **Yahweh** throughout this scene, as he is throughout the prose frame and almost nowhere in the
  poetry. The prologue's God is conversational, specific, and willing to be argued with by a member
  of his own court; the God of chapters 38–41 is none of those things.
- [[The Sons of God]] — *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, present at 1:6 and 2:1 and again at 38:7 where they
  *"shouted for joy"* at the founding of the earth. Genesis 6:2 is their only other appearance in
  the Torah, and [[The Sons of God and the Corruption of the Earth]] sets out what is at stake in
  identifying them. In Job they do nothing at all: they assemble, and the text moves past them to
  the one member who speaks.
- [[Job's Children]] — all ten killed in the fourth message, in a sentence of twenty-three English
  words. The wind strikes *"the four corners of the house"*, which is a way of saying that nothing
  structural was left to shelter anyone.
- **The four messengers** — no page; they are a narrative device, not characters, and the text
  distinguishes them only by what they report. Their identical closing formula guarantees the one
  thing the test requires: **that Job is told.**
- **The Sabeans and the Chaldeans** — raiding peoples, not individuals, and given no page for the
  same reason the wiki made none for the Uz of Genesis 10:23. *Šəbāʾ* points to south-west Arabia,
  the Sheba of Genesis 10:7, 10:28 and 25:3 and of the queen in 1 Kings 10; *Kaśdîm* points to lower
  Mesopotamia and to the anachronism discussed at [[Ur of the Chaldees]]. That Job is exposed to
  both at once is one of the better arguments for locating [[Uz]] on a caravan corridor.

## Places

- [[Uz]] — where verses 13–22 happen, though the text does not name it here. The raids come from two
  directions at once and the wind comes *"from the wilderness"* (*mēʿēber hammidbār*, from across the
  desert), which is the closest the book comes to describing the country.
- **Heaven** — no page, and the text gives it no name, no description, and no location. It is *"the
  presence of Yahweh"* and a place one can be *"among"* others in, and that is the whole of it. The
  wiki has no page for it and should not make one out of this passage; see
  [[The Divine Council]] for what the rest of Scripture supplies.

## Themes

- [[Testing]] — the mechanism of Genesis 22 with the reasons argued out loud. Both narratives tell
  the reader what is happening and tell the man nothing, and both use the phrase *fears God* as the
  thing being measured. What Job 1 adds is the question of **motive**: Genesis 22 asks whether
  Abraham will obey, and Job 1 asks whether anyone obeys for nothing.
- [[The Fear of God]] — 1:9 is the verse the theme turns on. Everywhere else in Scripture the fear of
  God is commended or commanded; here, for one verse, someone asks what it is *for*, and the book's
  forty remaining chapters are the answer.
- [[Blessing]] — used in three incompatible senses within six verses: God has *"blessed the work of
  his hands"* (1:10, the ordinary sense), the accuser predicts Job will *bārak* God to his face
  (1:11, the euphemism for its opposite), and Job blesses Yahweh's name in the ruins (1:21, the
  ordinary sense again, and at the moment blessing has visibly stopped being paid).
- [[Retribution]] — 1:10 is the doctrine stated by its sharpest critic. **The accuser and the three
  friends hold exactly the same theory of the world** — prosperity tracks righteousness — and differ
  only in which direction they run it. The friends infer sin from ruin; the accuser infers hire from
  prosperity. The prologue discredits the premise before a friend has opened his mouth.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — the inventory of 1:3 removed in four sentences, and 1:21 as the
  Bible's most concentrated statement of what ownership is worth at the ends of a life.
- [[Suffering]] — the wiki's central case, and this is the passage where its cause is disclosed. The
  disclosure is not an explanation: knowing *that* the accuser asked a question does not tell the
  reader why the answer required ten funerals.
- [[Providence]] — *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away"*, said of four events of which two were
  armed robbery. Job's theology of agency is total and the narrator endorses it in the next verse; at
  2:3 God complicates it without withdrawing it.
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — 1:21's *ʿārōm*, the word of Genesis 2:25 and 3:7, used here not of
  shame but of the two ends of a life and what can be carried across them.
- [[Life and Death]] — ten deaths reported in one clause, and Job's *"naked shall I return there"*,
  whose destination the Hebrew leaves genuinely unclear.
- [[Righteousness]] — certified a second and third time by God himself, and immediately made the
  reason for the disaster rather than a defence against it. **Job suffers because he is blameless**,
  which is the sentence the whole rest of the book is built to make sayable.
- [[Judgment]] — the scene is a court, and the only party with no representation in it is the man
  whose case is being heard. [[The Witness in Heaven]] traces what Job asks for once he starts
  asking.
- [[Sin]] — 1:22's verdict, and the definition of the thing Job does not do: *nātan tiplâ*, to assign
  impropriety to God.

## Notes

- **The article on *haśśāṭān*.** Every one of the fourteen occurrences in Job 1–2 has the definite
  article: *the śāṭān*, the accuser, the adversary. **Hebrew proper names do not take the article**,
  so the text is naming an office and not a person — as though English wrote *the Prosecutor*. The
  noun *śāṭān* elsewhere means an ordinary opponent: David is called one at 1 Samuel 29:4, Solomon's
  enemies at 1 Kings 11:14 and 11:23, and at Numbers 22:22 the Angel of Yahweh himself stands in
  Balaam's road *ləśāṭān*. Translating Job 1:6 as *Satan* is not a mistranslation so much as a
  decision to read the Old Testament through the New, and it costs the reader the thing the scene is
  actually depicting: a functionary of the heavenly court doing his job. WEB, KJV, ESV and NIV all
  print *Satan*; NRSV, JPS and NABRE print *the Accuser*, *the Adversary*, or *the Satan*. See
  [[Satan]] and [[Ha-Satan - The Accuser in Job, Zechariah, and Chronicles]].

- **"From going back and forth in the earth" (1:7).** The verb is ***šûṭ***, to rove, range, patrol —
  used of ships' oars and of scouting. The answer is a patrol report, and the same picture appears
  at Zechariah 1:10–11, where riders sent to *"walk back and forth through the earth"* return to say
  that all the earth is at rest, and at 2 Chronicles 16:9, where *"Yahweh's eyes run back and forth
  throughout the whole earth"* looking for the loyal. **The accuser's job description is
  surveillance**, and the passage gives no hint that the surveillance is unauthorised. 1 Peter 5:8's
  adversary who *"walks around, seeking whom he may devour"* is very likely reaching for this verse,
  and has added the appetite.

- **Is this a wager?** The wiki's page title says so and the text does not. There are no stakes named,
  nothing is owed to the winner, and God does not say what he expects. What there is, is a claim
  (*Job fears God only because he is paid*), a proposed test (*take it away*), and an authorisation
  with a limit. **Calling it a wager is a convenience, and it imports a frivolity the scene does not
  have** — the older English habit of calling the prologue *the wager* comes largely from Goethe's
  *Faust*, which borrowed the scene. The wiki keeps the title because it is how the episode is known
  and because the alternative titles are worse, and flags the point here rather than pretending the
  word is neutral.

- **Does God lose anything if the accuser is right?** More than is usually noticed. The accuser's
  claim is not that Job is secretly wicked; it is that **the category of disinterested love of God is
  empty**. If that holds, then God is never loved, only rented, and the whole covenantal vocabulary
  of the Old Testament is a description of wages. This is why God's answer is not a denial but a
  demonstration, and why it has to be a real one: an assertion from God would settle nothing that was
  in question. The cost of the demonstration is borne entirely by a third party who is not consulted,
  and the book never apologises for that. **At 2:3 God calls it what it is — ruin, *ḥinnām* — and
  authorises the second round in the next breath.**

- **"The fire of God has fallen from the sky" (1:16).** *ʾĒš ʾĕlōhîm*. Three readings, and the choice
  changes who the messenger thinks did it. (1) **Lightning** — *fire of God* as a superlative or an
  idiom for a natural force, the way *cedars of God* means great cedars; this is how most modern
  commentators take it, and it makes the messenger a frightened man reaching for a phrase. (2)
  **Divine fire proper**, the fire of Genesis 19:24, Leviticus 10:2 and 1 Kings 18:38, in which case
  a survivor has correctly identified the agent and told Job so before Job speaks at 1:21. (3) A
  **deliberate ambiguity** by the author, since the reader knows the permission was granted in 1:12
  and the messenger cannot. The wiki takes the third as the likeliest authorial intent and notes
  that nothing in the argument depends on it.

- **"Naked shall I return there" (1:21).** *ʿārōm ʾāšûb šāmmâ* — *there*, with no antecedent but
  *"my mother's womb"* in the clause before, and no one returns to that. Three readings. **The earth
  as mother**: Psalm 139:15's *"the depths of the earth"* for the womb, and Sirach 40:1's *"the day
  they come out of their mother's womb till the day they return to the mother of all things"*; this
  is the majority reading and it makes the line a burial image. **Sheol**, the destination of the
  dead, which the book will describe at length in chapters 3, 14 and 17 — see [[Sheol]]. **A loose
  deictic**, *thither*, meaning simply *back*, with no location intended. In every reading the point
  of the line is symmetry: what a person brings and what a person takes are the same amount. The
  livestock of 1:3 were never his in the sense he is now using.

- **Job blames no one but God, and the book agrees with him.** Of the four disasters, two were raids
  by identifiable human beings. Job's summary mentions neither, and mentions no accuser, because he
  has not been told there is one. He says *Yahweh took*, and verse 22 certifies that in saying it he
  *"did not sin, nor charge God with wrongdoing."* **The narrator is not endorsing resignation; he is
  endorsing the attribution.** This matters later, because the friends will also attribute
  everything to God and be condemned at 42:7 — the difference is not the theology of agency but what
  is inferred from it. Job infers nothing about himself. They infer everything.

- **Mourning rites that Israel's law will later restrict.** Tearing the robe is universal in the Old
  Testament, but **shaving the head for the dead is prohibited at Leviticus 21:5 and Deuteronomy
  14:1**, and the prophets nonetheless describe it as ordinary practice (Isaiah 22:12, Jeremiah
  16:6, Micah 1:16, Ezekiel 7:18). Job does it without a word of comment from the narrator, which is
  one more small piece of evidence that the book is set — or means to read as set — outside Israel
  and before the law. See [[Uz]] and [[Sacrifice Before the Priesthood]].

- **The first poetry in the book.** 1:21 is three balanced lines in a prologue that is otherwise
  prose, and it is the only thing Job says in chapter 1. The book's structure is prose frame, poetic
  body; **the seam opens four verses early**, and it opens for Job.

- **Where the accuser goes.** Out of the story. He is absent from chapters 3–41, from the whirlwind
  speeches, and from the epilogue's settling of accounts; **no character ever learns he existed**,
  and the four friends who spend the book explaining Job's suffering never come within reach of the
  explanation. When 42:11 finally sums it up, it says *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on
  him."* Whatever the prologue is doing with the accuser, it is not setting up a figure the book
  intends to blame.

- **Was the loss total?** Not quite, and the gaps are worth noticing because the dialogue uses them.
  Job's wife survives (2:9; 19:17), his house stands — he is at home in 1:20 and 2:8 — and enough
  household remains for 19:15–16 to complain that his servants no longer answer him. **The accuser's
  brief was *"all that he has"*, and the narrative delivers something slightly short of it**, which
  is why the second scene is needed at all.
