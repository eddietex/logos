# Let the Day Perish

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 3:1–26

## Summary

**The poetry of the book begins here, and it begins because a silence ends.** Three men have sat on
the ground for a week without speaking ([[Seven Days of Silence]]). *"After this Job opened his
mouth, and cursed the day of his birth."* Everything said between chapter 4 and chapter 27 is an
answer to this chapter, and the friends' entire theology is developed as a reply to it — which is
worth holding onto, because **what Job actually says here contains no theology at all.**

**The first thing to notice is the object of the verb, and the narrator puts it in the first
sentence so that it cannot be missed.** The accuser staked everything on a prediction, twice: *"he
will renounce you to your face"* (1:11, 2:5). Job's wife put it as an instruction: *"Renounce God,
and die"* (2:9). The whole prologue has been aimed at one act. **And here, at the moment that looks
most like its arrival, Job curses *his day*.** Not God. The narrator names the target before Job
opens his mouth — *"cursed the day of his birth"* — and the fifteen verses that follow do not
address God at all. **The accuser's prediction fails in the verse that most resembles its
fulfilment**, and the book never comes back to say so, because the accuser has already left. See
[[Renounce God and Die - The Euphemism and the Accuser's Prediction]].

**The verb is the mild one, too.** The prologue's dangerous word is *bārak*, *bless*, used as a
euphemism for its opposite at 1:5, 1:11, 2:5 and 2:9 — the word so charged that the book will not
print the real one next to God's name. **Here the word is *qālal*, to make light of, to slight**;
the same verb Genesis 8:21 used when God undertook not to *curse* the ground again, and the mild
half of the pair in Genesis 12:3. Job does not use the heavy formulaic *ʾārar* of Genesis 3:14 and
4:11 either. **He reaches for the weakest curse-word available and aims it at a date.** See
[[Curse]].

**What he does with it is nevertheless enormous, because he tries to un-create.** Verses 3–10 are a
sustained demolition of a single day and the night before it, built out of jussives — *let* — and
every one of them is a reversal of Genesis 1. *"Let that day be darkness"* is ***yəhî ḥōšek***
against ***yəhî ʾôr***, *"let there be light."* Light is withheld, darkness reclaims what was
divided from it, a cloud is told to settle, the stars of the twilight are put out, and the day is
struck from the calendar altogether: *"Let it not come into the number of the months."* **Job is not
asking to die. He is asking that one day be taken back out of the week God made**, and the grammar
he uses to ask it is the grammar God used to make it. See
[[Let That Day Be Darkness - Job 3 as Counter-Creation]] and [[The Creation of the World]].

**Then the demolition runs out and the questions start.** *"Why didn't I die from the womb?"* The
chapter has three movements and they descend: **3–10 curses the day, 11–19 asks why he was not
stillborn, 20–23 asks why the suffering are kept alive at all**, and then 24–26 stops asking and
simply describes where he is. The questions get larger as they go — his own birth, then everyone's
misery — and then they stop, and the last three verses have no interrogative in them.

**The middle movement is the chapter's most surprising, because it is almost gentle.** Asked what
death would have been, Job does not describe oblivion or relief from pain. He describes **rest**,
and then **a society without ranks**. *"There the wicked cease from troubling. There the weary are
at rest. There the prisoners are at ease together. They don't hear the voice of the taskmaster. The
small and the great are there. The servant is free from his master."* **Three times *there*, and
each time the thing named is a hierarchy that has stopped working.** Kings and counselors who built
waste places, princes who filled their houses with silver — all of it levelled by the only
institution in the ancient world that never asked who you were. **It is the single most attractive
description of death in the Hebrew Bible, and Job produces it in the middle of Scripture's most
extreme lament.** See [[Sheol]] and [[Burial and the Grave]].

***Rest* is the chapter's hidden keyword, and the last verse takes it away.** *Nûaḥ* appears three
times: *"I would have been at rest"* (3:13), *"there the weary are at rest"* (3:17), and then
*"neither have I rest"* (3:26). **The chapter opens a place where rest exists, spends seven verses
furnishing it, and closes by saying he is not in it.** The word is the one under Noah's name, under
the Sabbath command, and under the promised land; here it belongs to the dead and to nobody else.

**And the last movement contains the line that quietly reframes the whole prologue.** *"For the
thing which I fear comes on me, that which I am afraid of comes to me"* (3:25). **This is the only
moment in the book where Job admits to a dread that predates the disaster.** Read back against
1:5 — the burnt offerings he offered *continually*, in case his children had sinned in their hearts
— it suggests a man who was never quite at ease inside his prosperity. The friends will not notice
it. **Eliphaz opens his reply four verses later by telling Job that his fear should have been his
confidence** (4:6), and the book gives no sign that he has heard 3:25 at all.

**The most important thing in the chapter, though, is a word Job cannot know he is repeating.**
*"Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in?"* (3:23). **The accuser's
opening complaint in the heavenly council was that God had put a *hedge* around Job** — *"haven't
you made a hedge around him, and around his house, and around all that he has, on every side?"*
(1:10). It is the same verb and the same preposition, and these are its only two occurrences in the
book. **The accuser saw a fence keeping harm out. Job, who was not in the room and never will be,
describes a fence keeping him in.** Neither man is wrong, and neither knows the other has spoken.
See [[The Hedge Around Him - Job 1.10 and Job 3.23]].

**Nothing here is addressed to God, and nothing here is addressed to the friends.** The chapter is
spoken in the third person throughout — *God*, *he*, never *you* — which makes it the only major
speech of Job's that is neither prayer nor argument. **He is talking to a day.** The three men
answer as though they had been challenged, and from 4:1 onward the book is a quarrel; but the
speech that starts the quarrel is not addressed to anyone in it.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — **his first words in the poetry, and they are at the opposite pole from his last
  words in the prose.** At 1:21 he said *"Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away; blessed be Yahweh's
  name"*, and at 2:10 *"shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?"* —
  two verses the tradition built *the patience of Job* on. **Nineteen verses later he is asking that
  his birthday be struck out of the calendar.** The narrator records no lapse between them and no
  verdict on this speech at all: **the formula *"in all this Job didn't sin with his lips"* appears
  at 1:22 and 2:10 and never again**, and the book leaves the reader to decide whether it has been
  withdrawn or has simply stopped being necessary. **42:7 suggests the second.**
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — silent here, and speaking at 4:1. **His first words answer 3:25 without
  appearing to have heard it**: *"Isn't your piety your confidence? Isn't the integrity of your ways
  your hope?"* (4:6). The chapter he is replying to has no doctrine in it to correct, so he supplies
  one.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the other two, whose speeches likewise treat
  chapter 3 as a thesis rather than as a lament.
- [[Job's Children]] — **not mentioned, and the omission is the chapter's loudest silence.** Ten
  people died in chapter 1 and Job's lament is entirely about his own birth. **He does not mourn them
  here**; he mourns that he was ever available to lose them.
- [[Job's Wife]] — absent again, and her instruction at 2:9 hangs over the chapter unanswered: she
  told him to renounce God and die, and he has now spent twenty-six verses wanting to be dead without
  once renouncing God.
- [[Satan]] — gone since 2:7, and **wrong.** The prediction was that Job would curse God to his face;
  what the prediction produced is a man cursing a date in the third person.
- [[God]] — spoken *about* five times and spoken *to* never. He *"seeks for"* the day at 3:4, *gives*
  light at 3:20 and 3:23, and has *hedged in* the man at 3:23. **The first thing Job says about God
  in the poetry is that God is looking for a day Job wants hidden**, and the last is that God has
  fenced him.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — **not named in the chapter, and described at more length than anywhere else in the
  book.** Verses 13–19 are Job's fullest picture of where the dead are: lying down, quiet, asleep, at
  rest, in the company of kings and princes and stillborn infants, with prisoners at ease and slaves
  out from under their masters. **Everything the wiki's page says about Sheol — dark, silent,
  administratively empty, morally undifferentiated — is here presented as its attraction.** The word
  Job does use is *"the grave"* (*qeber*, 3:22), and at 17:13–16 he will take the same place as a
  residence.
- [[Uz]] — the unchanged setting; the ash-heap of 2:8 and the ground of 2:13, with three men still
  sitting on it.

## Themes

- [[Mourning and Lament]] — **the chapter is the wiki's anchor text for lament as distinct from
  mourning.** Chapter 2 was the rite with no words in it; chapter 3 is the words with no rite. Job
  performs no gesture here, tears nothing, and puts nothing on his head. **The body has finished and
  the mouth has started**, and the book has arranged the two chapters so the difference is
  unmissable.
- [[Life and Death]] — and the chapter's exact position between them, which is easy to state wrongly.
  **Job does not ask to die and never considers taking his life.** He asks (a) that he had never been
  born, (b) that he had died at birth, and (c) why those who want death are kept alive. **The gift he
  disputes is not his continuing existence but the fact that it began**, and (c) is put as a question
  about other people.
- [[Curse]] — the mild verb *qālal* on a day, against the prologue's loaded *bārak*, and against the
  *ʾārar* formula of Genesis 3, 4 and 9. **The Bible's most famous curse is also one of its
  weakest-worded**, and it is aimed at something that cannot be harmed.
- [[Creation]] — inverted for eight verses. *"Let that day be darkness"* reverses Genesis 1:3, and
  the chapter runs the divider of 1:4 backwards by handing the day over to what it was divided from.
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the chapter is the wiki's densest text on either. **Five words for
  darkness in three verses** (darkness, the shadow of death, a cloud, blackness, thick darkness), and
  then, in the third movement, **light used twice as a metonym for life itself** — *"why is light
  given to him who is in misery"* (3:20, 3:23). **Job's problem is stated as a complaint about
  illumination**, which is what makes 38:19's *"as for darkness, where is its place?"* an answer and
  not a change of subject.
- [[Suffering]] — the first time in the book that suffering is described from inside by the sufferer
  at length. Chapters 1 and 2 reported what was done to him; 2:12–13 reported what he looked like to
  others; **3:24–26 is the only account of what it is like to be him**, and it is four lines long.
- [[Hope]] — **absent, and this is the floor the rest of the book climbs from.** There is no
  petition, no expectation, and no addressee. Everything Job later reaches for — the umpire at 9:33,
  the witness at 16:19, the Redeemer at 19:25 — is built on ground this chapter has cleared
  completely.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the hinge the whole book turns on. **Seven days of silence produce
  twenty-six verses, and the twenty-six verses produce twenty-five chapters of argument.** Job will
  tell the friends at 13:5 that silence would have been their wisdom; he is the one who ended it.
- [[Sabbath]] — obliquely, through *nûaḥ*. **The chapter's three rests are all in the grave**, and
  the third one is denied: *"neither have I rest"* (3:26).
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — 3:18–19. *"They don't hear the voice of the taskmaster… the servant is
  free from his master."* **The one place in Scripture where death is commended specifically for
  abolishing slavery**, and it is said by a man who owned *"a very great household"* (1:3).
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — 3:14–15's kings and counselors *"who built up waste places for
  themselves"* and princes *"who filled their houses with silver"*. **Job, who had more than anyone in
  the east, lists the rich among the dead as company rather than as a contrast**, and the waste places
  are the point: what they built is ruins now.
- [[Retribution]] — **conspicuously not raised.** Job does not argue that he is innocent, does not
  ask what he has done, and does not accuse God of injustice. **The doctrine enters the book with
  Eliphaz at 4:7, not here**, which means the friends are answering a charge that has not been made.
- [[Prayer]] — by its absence. This is the one substantial speech of Job's that is not addressed to
  God, and the book places it first.

## Notes

- **Where the pericope starts and stops.** Chapter 3 is taken whole, with no dispute worth recording:
  the chapter opens with a narrative frame (*"After this Job opened his mouth"*) and closes with the
  end of the speech, and 4:1 begins *"Then Eliphaz the Temanite answered."* **It is one of the few
  places in Job where the chapter division and the discourse unit coincide exactly.** The internal
  three-part structure — 3–10, 11–19, 20–26 — is marked in the Hebrew by the shift from jussive to
  interrogative at 3:11 and by the repeated *lāmmâ* at 3:11, 3:12 and 3:20, and the wiki keeps it as
  one pericope because the movements are cumulative and the last one only makes sense after the first
  two.

- **Verse 8 and the day-cursers.** *"Let them curse it who curse the day, who are ready to rouse up
  leviathan."* Job is summoning **professionals** — people whose trade is laying curses on days, and
  who are competent to wake the chaos-monster. **This is the only verse in the book that treats
  magic as a real available technique**, and it sits oddly in a book so otherwise uninterested in it.
  Two readings, and the wiki holds both. (1) **Rhetorical extremity** — Job has run out of ordinary
  curses and reaches for the most extravagant thing imaginable, without believing in it, the way a
  modern speaker invokes hellfire. (2) **Live mythology** — the Ugaritic texts have *Lôtan*, a
  seven-headed sea-serpent, and the Hebrew Bible elsewhere treats Leviathan as a real adversary God
  defeated (Psalm 74:14, Isaiah 27:1). On this reading Job is asking for the sea-monster to be roused
  against the created order, which is the same request as 3:4–6 by another route. **Reading (2) is
  what makes the chapter hang together**, because the demolition of Genesis 1 and the waking of the
  chaos it was imposed on are one idea. There is also a third proposal, that *yām* (*sea*) should be
  read for *yôm* (*day*) — *"those who curse the sea"* — which tidies the mythology and has no
  manuscript support; the wiki notes it and does not adopt it.

- **Why *"the night which said, 'There is a boy conceived'"* matters.** 3:3 curses two things, not
  one: **the day of birth and the night of conception**, and verses 4–5 deal with the day while 6–9
  deal with the night. **Job goes back further than his birth**, and the night is given a voice — it
  *said* there is a boy — which makes it an announcer of news and therefore, in the logic of the
  curse, culpable. **The chapter's target keeps receding**: from the birthday, to the night of
  conception, to the womb's doors that should have stayed shut (3:10), to the knees that received him
  and the breast that fed him (3:12). Each step is earlier than the last.

- **Is this the sin the narrator stopped certifying?** The formula *"in all this Job didn't sin with
  his lips"* (2:10; compare 1:22) does not appear after chapter 3, and some readers take its absence
  as a verdict — that Job has now crossed a line the prologue twice said he had not. **The wiki
  declines that reading**, on three grounds. The narrator says nothing, and this narrator is not shy
  about verdicts. **The formula's job was to answer the accuser's prediction, and the accuser is
  gone** — there is no longer a claim outstanding for it to refute. And 42:7 commends the man who
  said all of this, in contrast to three men who said nothing of the kind. **What changes at chapter 3
  is the register, not Job's standing**, and the reader who wants a line crossed has to find it in a
  silence.

- **A reading the wiki declines: chapter 3 as a death wish.** It is regularly described as Job wanting
  to die, and that is not what the text says. **Verses 3–10 ask that a past day be annulled; 11–19 ask
  why he did not die at birth; 20–23 ask why the suicidal are kept alive — in the third person, about
  other people.** Job never asks God to kill him here and never contemplates killing himself. The
  request to be crushed comes later and to God (6:8–9), and by 7:15 he is saying that his soul
  *chooses* strangling — so the wish does develop. **But chapter 3 is a complaint about the
  beginning, not about the continuation**, and the distinction is the reason the chapter can be read
  as lament rather than as despair.

- **What the friends heard.** Nothing in chapter 3 is a claim about God's justice, about Job's
  innocence, or about why any of this happened. **Eliphaz nevertheless replies with a complete
  doctrine of retribution**, and all three men proceed for twenty-two chapters on the assumption that
  a case has been made. **The mismatch is deliberate and it is the engine of the book**: the friends
  cannot hear a lament as a lament, because their theology has no category for suffering that is not
  evidence. **They answer the only thing they can hear**, and what they hear is an accusation Job did
  not make until they forced him to.
