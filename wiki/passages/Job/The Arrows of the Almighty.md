# The Arrows of the Almighty

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 6:1–7:21

## Summary

Job's first reply to anyone. Chapter 3 was spoken to nobody; Eliphaz then answered it as though it
had been an argument, and this is Job finding out what it is like to be answered. **The speech has
four audiences in turn, and the order is the story of the book in miniature.** For thirteen verses
he talks about his own anguish, to no one in particular (6:2–13). Then he turns on the friends, in
the plural, for seventeen (6:14–30). Then he talks about the human condition for six (7:1–6). **And
then, at 7:7, for the first time in the poetry, he speaks to God** — *"Oh remember that my life is a
breath"* — and does not stop until the end of the chapter. Nothing in chapter 3 or in this speech up
to that verse is addressed to God. Job will turn to him again in chapters 9–10, 13–14, 16–17 and
30; the friends, in twenty-nine chapters of speaking about God, never once speak to him.

It runs in five movements.

**1. The weight (6:2–7).** *"Oh that my anguish were weighed, and all my calamity laid in the
balances!"* The word for *anguish* is *kaʿśî* — the *kaʿas*, *resentment*, that Eliphaz had just
said *"kills the foolish man"* (5:2). Job takes the word that diagnosed him and asks only that it be
weighed. He concedes something at once: *"therefore have my words been rash"* (6:3). He does not
retract chapter 3; he asks that its volume be measured against its cause, which would be *"heavier
than the sand of the seas."* Then he names the cause, and this is new: ***"For the arrows of the
Almighty are within me. My spirit drinks up their poison. The terrors of God set themselves in
array against me"*** (6:4). Chapter 3 had said once, in passing, that God had *hedged* him in
(3:23). Here, for the first time, God is the archer and Job is the target, and the image will come
back sharper — *"he has also set me up for his target"* (16:12). Two animal questions follow: the
wild donkey does not bray over grass, nor the ox over fodder (6:5) — **an animal that cries has a
reason**, and so does he. And two food questions: tasteless food without salt, the *"white of an
egg"* (6:6–7). The comfort Eliphaz served is insipid, and Job's stomach turns at it.

**2. The request (6:8–13).** *"Oh that I might have my request… that it would please God to crush
me; that he would let loose his hand, and cut me off!"* Chapter 3 asked never to have been born;
this asks to die now. But it is still not addressed to God — *"that God would grant"*, third
person. And it comes with one consolation: ***"that I have not denied the words of the Holy
One"*** (6:10). That is Job's answer to Eliphaz's 4:6. Eliphaz had told him his integrity should be
his *hope*; Job says his integrity is what he will have *instead* of hope, the one thing he can die
holding. Then he refuses hope outright: *"What is my strength, that I should wait? What is my end,
that I should be patient? Is my strength the strength of stones? Or is my flesh of brass?"*
(6:11–12). Eliphaz had promised him *"a league with the stones of the field"* (5:23). Job is not
made of stone.

Note too the title in 6:10. *Qādôš*, **the Holy One** — singular, the book's first use of it as a
name for God — answers Eliphaz's plural of eight verses earlier: *"To which of the holy ones
(qədōšîm) will you turn?"* (5:1). Eliphaz said there was no holy one to appeal to. Job says the Holy
One spoke words, and he has kept them.

**3. The wadi (6:14–30).** Now the friends, and the speech's one extended image. *"My brothers have
dealt deceitfully as a brook"* — a *naḥal*, a wadi: black and swollen with ice and snowmelt in the
wet season, gone in the heat. The caravans of Tema and Sheba leave their route to find it, *"came
there, and were confounded"*, and perish in the waste (6:15–20). **Whoever has watched a caravan
die at a dry streambed knows what Job is describing: a promise of water that was visible from far
off and was not there on arrival.** The friends came from three countries to comfort him (2:11).
They arrived, and were dry. And Job says why: *"You see a terror, and are afraid"* (6:21). That is
the speech's most piercing line. If Job is innocent, nothing protects anyone; the doctrine is a
wall around the friends as much as an explanation of him, and they will not let it down.

He has asked them for nothing — no gift, no bribe, no ransom from an oppressor (6:22–23). He asks
only this: ***"Teach me, and I will hold my peace. Cause me to understand wherein I have
erred"*** (6:24). The verb is *šāgâ*, to err inadvertently, the word of the sin offering for sins
committed without knowing (Leviticus 4). **It is the same openness as the burnt offerings of 1:5**,
offered for sins his children *may* have committed in their hearts. Job does not claim to be
incapable of an unknown fault. He asks to be shown it. Nobody shows him one. Zophar is the first
of the friends to name any specific offence, in chapter 20, and Eliphaz's list in chapter 22 is
invented.

He ends with an oath: *"look at me, for surely I shall not lie to your face"* (6:28) —
*wəʿal-pənêkem ʾim-ʾăkazzēb*. **That is the accuser's form of words.** *"He will renounce you to
your face"* (1:11; 2:5) is *ʾim-lōʾ ʿal-pāneykā*, the same oath particle and the same *to your
face*. The accuser swore that Job would curse God to his face. Job swears to the friends' faces that
he will not lie. And then: *"Please return… return again. My cause is righteous"* (6:29) — *šûbû*,
the verb the friends will spend thirty chapters urging on *him* (22:23). Job is the first in the
dialogue to call anyone to turn back, and he says it to the men who came to bring him back.

**4. The hired hand (7:1–6).** The register shifts from *you* to *a man*. *"Isn't a man forced to
labor on earth?"* — *ṣābāʾ*, a term of military service or conscript labor, which Job will use of
his own life again at 14:14 (*"all the days of my warfare would I wait, until my release should
come"*). The slave longs for evening shade, the day labourer for his wage; *"so am I made to possess
months of misery"* (7:3). **That one word, *months*, is the book's only indication of how long the
suffering has lasted by the time the dialogue begins.** The prologue told the story in days — a day
of four messengers, a second council, seven days on the ground. The dialogue is set months later.

Then the body, in the first person: *"My flesh is clothed with worms and clods of dust. My skin
closes up, and breaks out afresh"* (7:5). And then the line the speech is best known for:
***"My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, and are spent without hope"*** (7:6). *Tiqwâ*,
*hope*, is also the ordinary word for *cord* or *thread* — Rahab's scarlet cord in the window is a
*tiqwâ* (Joshua 2:18). **The shuttle runs to the end of the thread**, and the days end *bəʾepes
tiqwâ* — for want of hope, or for want of thread. The pun is in the Hebrew, and it is the bleakest
joke in the book. Eliphaz had used *tiqwâ* twice; Job uses it to say the loom is empty.

**5. To God (7:7–21).** *"Oh remember that my life is a breath."* The imperative is *zəkōr*, the
verb of *"God remembered Noah"* (Genesis 8:1), and the noun is *rûaḥ* — the third *rûaḥ* of the
book, after the *"great wind"* that killed the children (1:19) and the *spirit* that brought Eliphaz
his doctrine (4:15). **Job's first word to God is not a complaint and not a question. It is a
request to be remembered.**

What he asks God to remember is that he will soon be gone. *"The eye of him who sees me shall see me
no more. Your eyes shall be on me, but I shall not be"* (7:8) — *wəʾênennî*. Sheol is a cloud that
dissipates; no one comes up; *"neither shall his place know him any more"* (7:9–10). Then the
resolve that answers the seven days of silence and explains chapter 3: ***"Therefore I will not
keep silent. I will speak in the anguish of my spirit. I will complain in the bitterness of my
soul"*** (7:11).

And then the complaint, and it is not that God is absent. **It is that God is watching.** *"Am I a
sea, or a sea monster, that you put a guard over me?"* (7:12) — *yām* and *tannîn*, the two powers
the creation hymns have God bound and set a watch on. Job asks whether he is one of them. Even his
bed is invaded: *"you scare me with dreams, and terrify me through visions"* (7:14) — *ḥezyōnôt*,
**Eliphaz's word for the night visions that brought him his doctrine** (4:13). Eliphaz's visions
taught him; Job's only frighten him. *"So that my soul chooses strangling, death rather than my
bones. I loathe my life"* (7:15–16). The verb is *māʾas* — **the verb of Eliphaz's *"do not
despise the chastening of the Almighty"*** (5:17). Eliphaz told him not to despise God's correction.
Job says what he despises is his life. He will say it again at 9:21, and *māʾas* is the verb of his
last speech in the book: *"Therefore I abhor myself"* (42:6).

Then 7:17–18, the speech's centre of gravity:

> What is man, that you should magnify him, that you should set your mind on him, that you should
> visit him every morning, and test him every moment?

**It is Psalm 8 turned inside out.** *"What is man, that you think of him? What is the son of man,
that you care for him?"* (Psalm 8:4) — the same opening, *mâ-ʾĕnôš kî*, and the same verb, *pāqad*,
to visit or attend to. In the psalm God's attention is the human being's glory: *"you have crowned
him with glory and honor."* In Job the attention is the problem — surveillance every morning, a test
every moment. **Job does not doubt God is paying attention to him. He wishes God would stop.** And
the reader knows two things he does not. God did *set his heart* on Job — *"Have you considered
(literally, set your heart on) my servant Job?"* (1:8; 2:3) — and it was the accuser he asked to do
it. And it *is* a test. Job is the first person in the book to use the word, *bāḥan* (7:18), and he
is exactly right. See
[[What Is Man That You Should Visit Him - Job 7.17-18 and Psalm 8]].

*"How long will you not look away from me, nor leave me alone until I swallow down my spittle?"*
(7:19). And then the speech's last two verses, which are the first time Job says the word *sin* of
himself at all:

> If I have sinned, what do I do to you, you watcher of men? Why have you set me as a mark for you,
> so that I am a burden to myself? Why do you not pardon my disobedience, and take away my
> iniquity? For now shall I lie down in the dust. You will seek me diligently, but I shall not be.

**This is not a confession. It is a conditional, and then a question about God's character.** *If*
he has sinned — and he has just asked the friends to show him where — what harm can it do to God?
And if there is a sin, why is it not forgiven? The verbs are the vocabulary of God's own
self-description: *nāśāʾ*, to lift or bear away, and *ʿābar*, to make pass — *"forgiving iniquity
and disobedience and sin"* (Exodus 34:7); *"who pardons iniquity, and passes over the
disobedience"* (Micah 7:18). **Job is asking why the God of Exodus 34 is not behaving like himself.**
See
[[Why Do You Not Pardon My Disobedience - Job 7.21 and the God Who Forgives]].

The last line closes the circle opened at 7:8. *"You will seek me diligently, but I shall not
be"* — *wəʾênennî* again. The verb *šāḥar* is the seeking of early morning, the hour of *"visit him
every morning"* (7:18). **One morning God will come looking, and there will be nobody there.** The
pathos of the line is in what it assumes: that God *would* look. A man who believed himself
abandoned would not say it. Job believes himself hunted, and he cannot stop imagining the hunter
missing him.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — speaking for the second time, and **for the first time to anyone.** Three things
  he does here he keeps doing to chapter 31: he names God as the cause (6:4), he refuses to buy
  peace with a confession he cannot make specific (6:24, 6:28–30), and he turns from the friends to
  God (7:7). The last is the one that matters for 42:7. The friends talk about God for twenty-nine
  chapters; Job talks to him, and this speech is where it starts.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — not named, and answered throughout. Job picks up his words one by one
  and turns them: *kaʿas* (5:2 → 6:2), the holy ones (5:1 → 6:10), the stones of the field (5:23 →
  6:12), *tiqwâ* (4:6; 5:16 → 6:8, 7:6), the night visions (4:13 → 7:14), *māʾas* (5:17 → 7:16).
  It is a close reading of the speech he has just heard, and it is delivered without once
  addressing its author by name.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] and [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — inside the plural of 6:21–29, *"you see a
  terror, and are afraid"*, *"teach me"*, *"please return."* Job answers one man and indicts three;
  Eliphaz had spoken for the three in his *"we have searched it"* (5:27), and Job takes him at his
  word.
- [[God]] — named *Šadday* (6:4, 6:14), *ʾĕlôah* (6:4, 6:8, 6:9), and for the first time in the book
  *qādôš*, **the Holy One** (6:10). In 6:4 he is an archer, in 7:12 a guard on the sea, in 7:20
  *nōṣēr hāʾādām*, the *watcher of men* — a title that elsewhere means a protector (*"he who keeps
  your soul"*, Proverbs 24:12) and here means a jailer. **And for the first time in the poetry he is
  addressed as *you*** (7:7–21).

## Places

- [[Tema]] — 6:19, *"the caravans of Tema looked."* The great oasis of north-west Arabia, on
  the incense road, named for the Ishmaelite clan of Genesis 25:15. Its caravans are the ones that
  turn off the road toward the dry wadi.
- **Sheba** — 6:19, *"the companies of Sheba waited for them."* The incense kingdom of south-west
  Arabia, and **the same people as the Sabeans who took Job's oxen and donkeys** (1:15). The
  caravans that perish looking for water belong to the raiders of chapter 1. Job does not make the
  connection; the text does. Given no page for now, as the Sabeans were not at 1:15; the place
  will earn one with the queen of 1 Kings 10.
- [[Sheol]] — 7:9, *"he who goes down to Sheol shall come up no more."* Sheol as a one-way road,
  stated flatly, seven chapters before Job asks whether it could be otherwise (14:13–15).

## Themes

- [[Suffering]] — given for the first time from the inside as an argument rather than a cry: it has
  a weight (6:2–3), a cause (6:4), and a duration (*"months"*, 7:3). And for the first time the
  sufferer names God as its agent.
- [[Hope]] — refused at 6:11 and declared exhausted at 7:6, with the cord-pun that makes the noun
  *tiqwâ* a thread the shuttle has run to the end of.
- [[Integrity]] — 6:10, *"I have not denied the words of the Holy One"*: integrity held not as a
  hope but as a consolation for dying. Job's answer to 4:6.
- [[Prayer]] — 7:7–21, **the first words in the poetry addressed to God**, and the template for all
  Job's later prayers: remembrance asked, the end of life pleaded, the surveillance protested,
  forgiveness asked about.
- [[Testing]] — 7:18, *"test him every moment"*: Job is the first character in the book to use the
  word *bāḥan*, and he guesses the one thing about his situation the prologue told the reader.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the whole of 7:7–21 is about eyes: *"my eye shall no more see good"*,
  *"the eye of him who sees me shall see me no more"*, *"your eyes shall be on me"*, *"how long will
  you not look away from me?"* Job is seen too much by God and not enough by his friends.
- [[Sin]] — 6:24 and 7:20–21: an unknown error invited (*šāgâ*), and sin spoken of hypothetically —
  *if I have sinned* — never confessed and never denied in principle.
- [[Forgiveness]] — 7:21, *"Why do you not pardon my disobedience?"* — the Exodus 34 vocabulary
  asked of God as a question.
- [[Remembrance]] — 7:7, *zəkōr*, Job's first word to God.
- [[Brotherhood]] — 6:15, *"my brothers have dealt deceitfully"*: the friends called brothers in the
  verse that accuses them. Compare 19:13, where his actual brothers are *"far from me"*, and 42:11,
  where they come back.
- [[The Fear of God]] — 6:14, *"even to him who forsakes the fear of the Almighty"*: whatever the
  verse's exact sense, it says a friend's loyalty should not wait on the sufferer's piety, which is
  the friends' whole procedure.
- [[Retribution]] — 6:21, *"You see a terror, and are afraid"*: the doctrine as the friends' own
  protection. If Job's suffering is undeserved, theirs could be too.
- [[Life and Death]] — the death wish now stated (6:9; 7:15), and Sheol as no return (7:9–10).
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — 7:11, *"I will complain in the bitterness of my soul"*: lament
  claimed as a right, and the silence of 2:13 formally ended.
- [[Work]] — 7:1–2: human life as conscript service, a hired hand's day, and a slave waiting for the
  shade.

## Notes

- **The speech's turn to God (7:7).** The wiki has said elsewhere that chapter 3 is addressed to no
  one, and that the change comes later. This is where it comes. 6:8–9's request to be crushed still
  speaks of God in the third person, and the friends are the *you* of 6:21–30. The first second
  person singular addressed to God is 7:7's *zəkōr*, and from 7:12 to 7:21 every *you* is God.
  **The pages on [[Prayer]] and [[Let the Day Perish]] previously put the first direct address at
  10:18 and at 6:8–9 respectively; both have been corrected and the corrections noted there.**

- **6:6, "the white of an egg."** *Rîr ḥallāmût* is obscure: *rîr* is slime or spittle (the WEB's
  *spittle* in 1 Samuel 21:13), and *ḥallāmût* occurs only here. The rabbinic tradition takes it as
  egg-white; many modern versions read *the juice of mallows* or *the slime of purslane*, a
  notoriously tasteless plant. The point survives either reading: something insipid enough to make
  the stomach turn, and Job means what he has just been served.

- **6:14 is one of the hardest verses in the book.** The WEB gives one reading: kindness is owed to
  the fainting man *even if* he forsakes the fear of the Almighty. Another, older reading takes the
  second line as describing the friend who withholds kindness: *he who withholds kindness from his
  friend forsakes the fear of the Almighty.* Either way the verse brings the fear of God back into
  the dialogue — the very thing the accuser questioned and Eliphaz called Job's confidence (4:6) —
  and puts friendship's *ḥesed* beside it. The wiki records both readings and adopts neither.

- **The wadi and Jeremiah 15:18.** Jeremiah's complaint, *"Will you indeed be to me as a deceitful
  brook (ʾakzāb), as waters that fail?"*, uses Job's image and aims it at God. Job aims it at his
  friends, and in the same speech uses the root of Jeremiah's word, *kāzab*, to swear he will not
  lie (6:28). **What Jeremiah fears God might be, Job says the friends are.** It is a measure of the
  difference between Job and the prophet that Job, who accuses God of nearly everything, never
  accuses him of this.

- **Tema and Sheba (6:19).** The two caravan peoples name the two ends of the Arabian incense road:
  Tema the oasis in the north-west, Sheba the kingdom in the south-west. Job's audience is assumed
  to know the trade, which fits the book's setting among *"the children of the east"* (1:3) and
  among peoples whose wisdom was proverbial (see [[Uz]], [[Teman]]). That the Sabeans of 1:15 are
  Sheba is standard; that Job picks Sheba's caravans for the image of men who die disappointed may be
  a coincidence of geography. The wiki notes it as one.

- **"Months of misery" (7:3).** The only internal clue to the dialogue's timing. The Septuagint's
  long addition to Job's wife's speech at 2:9 says *"a long time"* had passed; the Testament of Job
  makes it many years. Neither has Hebrew support. *Months* is what the text says.

- **The weaver's shuttle and *tiqwâ* (7:6).** The pun depends on *tiqwâ* meaning both *hope* and
  *cord* — certain in Joshua 2:18, 21, where the scarlet *tiqwâ* is a rope — and on *ʾepes*, *end*
  or *lack*. Not every commentator presses it, but the loom is Job's own image, and Hezekiah reaches
  for the same one facing death: *"I have rolled up, like a weaver, my life. He will cut me off
  from the loom"* (Isaiah 38:12).

- **"I don't want to live forever" (7:16).** *Lōʾ-ləʿōlām ʾeḥyeh.* The phrase is the tree of life's:
  *"lest he… take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever (wāḥay ləʿōlām)"* (Genesis
  3:22). Whether Job is alluding to it cannot be shown, but the coincidence is exact. God barred the
  way to living forever lest man take it; Job says he would not take it if it were offered.

- **7:20, "a burden to myself" — one of the scribal corrections.** The Masoretic tradition lists
  this verse among the *tiqqûnê sōpərîm*, the passages where the scribes are said to have altered an
  expression thought irreverent to God. The original is said to have read *ʿāleykā*, *a burden to
  you* — Job saying he has become a burden on God — and the Septuagint indeed reads *"a burden upon
  you."* The traditional list's reliability is disputed, but here the ancient translation supports
  it. If the original is *to you*, the verse is sharper and more coherent: *"Why have you set me up as
  your target, so that I am a burden on you?"*

- **Zophar's borrowing (20:8–9).** *"He shall fly away as a dream… chased away like a vision of the
  night. The eye which saw him shall see him no more, neither shall his place any more see him."*
  Every image in the verse is from this chapter: the dreams and visions of 7:14, the eye of 7:8, the
  place of 7:10. **Zophar has taken Job's lament for himself and turned it into a description of the
  wicked man's end** — a description he is plainly applying to Job. The friends listen closely; they
  just listen for evidence.

- **Elihu answers this speech twice.** At 33:15–18 he quotes Eliphaz's line about night visions and
  supplies the purpose Job could not see in his own: God terrifies a man in dreams *"that he may
  withdraw man from his purpose, and hide pride from man."* At 35:6 he takes Job's *"if I have
  sinned, what do I do to you?"* (7:20) and returns it as a rebuke: *"If you have sinned, what
  effect do you have against him?"* — Job's own point, that God cannot be harmed, used to tell Job
  that God has no reason to notice him. Elihu is the one speaker who seems to have read the whole
  dialogue, and this chapter is among the ones he answers.
