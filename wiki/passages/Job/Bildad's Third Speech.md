# Bildad's Third Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 25:1–6

## Summary

Job ended chapter 24 with a dare: *"If it isn't so now, who will prove me a liar, and make my speech
worth nothing?"* (24:25). He had just given every one of Eliphaz's invented crimes a real, unpunished
defendant — the boundary-mover, the man who drives away the orphan's donkey, the harvesters who carry
the sheaves hungry, the murderer who rises with the light — and asked for counter-evidence.
[[Bildad the Shuhite]] answers with six verses, and **not one of them is about anything Job said.**
There is no case, no rebuttal, no portrait of the wicked, no summons to repent and no promise of
restoration. There is a two-verse hymn to God's sovereignty and a three-verse sentence on the human
being, and then the friends stop talking for the rest of the book.

**The hymn (25:2–3) is true, and it is aimed at nothing.** *"Dominion and fear are with him. He
makes peace in his high places. Can his armies be counted? On whom does his light not arise?"* Each
clause is orthodox and several are beautiful. Two of them are also Job's own words handed back with
the meaning reversed:

- ***"Fear"*** is *paḥad*, dread. In his last speech Job confessed it: *"Therefore I am terrified at his presence. When I consider, I am afraid of him"*
  (23:15), where *afraid* is the same root. Job had described dread as what God's absence and
  silence were doing to him. Bildad makes it an attribute God possesses — dread *is with him*, part
  of his royal equipment — and so turns Job's experience into a reason for him to be quiet.
- ***"His armies"*** — *gədûdāyw*, his troops, raiding bands — is the word Job used at 19:12:
  *"His troops come on together, build a siege ramp against me, and encamp around my tent."* For Job
  God's troops are an army besieging one man. Bildad's are innumerable and cosmic, and the question
  *"Can his armies be counted?"* is meant to make one man's siege too small to mention.

***"He makes peace in his high places"*** — *ʿōśeh šālôm bimrômāyw* — implies something to make peace
*from*. Peace in the heights is a settlement, and the book's poetry knows the conflict behind it:
Rahab's helpers stooping under God (9:13), the sea and the sea-monster under guard (7:12), and in the
next chapter Rahab struck through (26:12). Bildad's line is the calm after that battle. It is also,
in the clause's later life, one of the most-prayed lines in the Hebrew Bible: *ʿōśeh šālôm bimrômāyw*
is the closing petition of the Jewish Kaddish and of the Amidah, extended there with a request that
the God who makes peace above make peace *for us*. The extension is exactly what Bildad does not
make. His peace in the heights has no bearing on the man on the ash heap.

***"On whom does his light not arise?"*** is the universalism of the hymn, and in context it is
almost a joke at Job's expense. Job's speech had just described the people on whom God's light, in
every practical sense, does not arise: the murderer who *"rises with the light"* (24:14) and the
adulterer waiting for twilight (24:15), *"rebels against the light"* (24:13) for whom *"the morning
is to all of them like thick darkness"* (24:17) — and those were the oppressors. The oppressed were
worse off. Bildad's answer to a catalogue of particulars is a universal that cannot see them. (The
New Testament says the same thing in a different key: God *"makes his sun to rise on the evil and the
good"*, Matthew 5:45 — there as a model for mercy, not as a reason to stop asking.)

**The sentence (25:4) is Job's own question, word for word, with Job's question removed from it.**
*"How then can man be just with God?"* is Job 9:2 — *"Truly I know that it is so, but how can man be
just with God?"* — the same Hebrew, *mah-yiṣdaq ʾĕnôš ʿim-ʾēl*. But Job asked it as a lawsuit
question: *just* is *ṣādaq*, to be in the right, to win a case, and the next verse made that explicit
(*"If he is pleased to contend with him, he can't answer him one time in a thousand"*, 9:3). In
chapter 23 Job had come back to it with a different answer: *"There the upright might reason with
him, so I should be delivered forever from my judge"* (23:7). Bildad's reply cancels chapter 23 by
returning to chapter 9 — and quietly changing the question's sense from *can a man win a hearing* to
*can a man be clean*. The second half of the verse supplies the change: *"Or how can he who is born
of a woman be clean?"* — Eliphaz's line (15:14), and before it Job's own *"Man, who is born of a
woman"* (14:1), a phrase Job used for brevity and the friends use for impurity.

**The a-fortiori (25:5–6) is the third and lowest version of the friends' anthropology.** *"Behold,
even the moon has no brightness, and the stars are not pure in his sight; how much less man, who is a
worm, the son of man, who is a worm!"* Eliphaz had argued down from God's angels to *"those who dwell
in houses of clay"* (4:18–19), and from *"his holy ones"* and *"the heavens"* to *"a man who drinks
iniquity like water"* (15:15–16). Bildad starts from the moon and stars and arrives at the maggot.
The full escalation is tabled at [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]].

Two things make the last verse more than a repetition.

- **It runs Psalm 8 backwards.** *"When I consider your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and
  the stars, which you have ordained; what is man, that you think of him? What is the son of man,
  that you care for him?"* (Psalm 8:3–4). The psalm's sequence is moon and stars, then *man*
  (*ʾĕnôš*), then *son of man* (*ben-ʾādām*), and it ends in a crown. Bildad's sequence is the same —
  moon and stars, *ʾĕnôš*, *ben-ʾādām* — and it ends in a worm. Whether Bildad's poet is answering the
  psalm or both draw on a common hymnic pattern cannot be settled; the inversion is exact either way,
  and it is the counterpart of what Job did with Psalm 8 at 7:17–18 (see
  [[What Is Man That You Should Visit Him - Job 7.17-18 and Psalm 8]]). **Job and Bildad both
  parody the psalm**, and in opposite directions: Job says God attends to man too much, Bildad that
  man is beneath attending to.
- **The worm is Job's word first.** Bildad uses two: *rimmâ*, the maggot of decay, and *tôlēʿâ*, the
  grub. *Rimmâ* is the word Job has used of his own flesh (*"My flesh is clothed with worms"*, 7:5),
  of the grave he called his family (*"to the worm, 'My mother,' and 'my sister'"*, 17:14), of the
  common end of rich and poor (*"The worm covers them"*, 21:26), and of the wicked's end (*"The worm
  shall feed sweetly on him"*, 24:20). In Job's mouth the worm is what death does to a man. **Bildad
  makes it what a man is**, alive. See
  [[A Worm and No Man - Job 25.6, Psalm 22.6, and Isaiah 41.14]] for the other two places the
  Hebrew Bible calls a human being a worm, and how differently they use it.

**And then the friends are finished.** Bildad's three speeches run twenty-two verses (chapter 8),
twenty-one (chapter 18), and six. [[Zophar the Naamathite]] has no third speech at all. The last
words any of the three addresses to Job are *"the son of man, who is a worm"*, and the next speech
any of them receives from God is *"you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant
Job has"* (42:7). The book's arrangement makes the doctrine's last word a description of the human
being as vermin, and gives the reply to God.

## Key verses

> **1** Then Bildad the Shuhite answered,

> **2** “Dominion and fear are with him. He makes peace in his high places.

> **3** Can his armies be counted? On whom does his light not arise?

> **4** How then can man be just with God? Or how can he who is born of a woman be clean?

> **5** Behold, even the moon has no brightness, and the stars are not pure in his sight;

> **6** How much less man, who is a worm, the son of man, who is a worm!”

## People

- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — his third speech and the last word from any of the three friends. Shorter
  than either of his others by three quarters, and the only speech by any of the friends that contains
  no second-person address at all: Job is not *you* anywhere in it. Chapter 8 ended in an offer and
  chapter 18 in a curse; chapter 25 ends in a definition.
- [[Job (person)]] — the addressee who is never addressed. His 9:2 is quoted back to him, his *paḥad*
  (23:15) and God's *troops* (19:12) are reassigned, and his worm (7:5; 17:14; 21:26; 24:20) is turned
  from what death does into what a man is. His reply opens on the speech's uselessness — *"How have you
  helped him who is without power!"* (26:2) — in the next pericope.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — the source of the speech's second half. *"Born of a woman … clean"* is his
  15:14, and the a-fortiori from the heavens down is his 4:17–19 and 15:15–16, which he presented the
  first time as a night vision. Bildad repeats it without the vision.
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — absent. The third cycle's third speech is missing, and Bildad's six
  verses are the friends' whole remaining contribution.
- [[God]] — the subject of verses 2–3, and never addressed. As in Bildad's other speeches he is
  spoken about in the third person throughout. The God of this speech is defined entirely by
  distance: ruling, feared, at peace in the heights, attended by uncountable troops, before whom even
  the moon is dim.

## Places

No earthly geography. The speech's one location is *"his high places"* — *mərômîm*, the heights of
heaven — set against the ground Job sits on, and the distance between them is the speech's argument.

## Themes

- [[Sin]] — the friends' anthropology at its lowest: not *every man sins* but *a man is a worm*. The
  speech names no sin and no sinner. It does not need to; on its terms being human is enough.
- [[Righteousness]] — *ṣādaq* at 25:4, Job's lawsuit verb from 9:2, turned from *win a case* into
  *be clean*. The move from forensic to moral righteousness is the whole of Bildad's reply to Job's
  hope of a hearing (23:7).
- [[Clean and Unclean]] — *zākâ* (*be clean*, 25:4) and *zakkû* (*are not pure*, 25:5): purity
  vocabulary applied to the moon and stars, which no law of purity covers, so that it can then be
  denied to man.
- [[Creation]] — the moon and stars as Psalm 8 sees them, and the human being placed beneath them in
  the opposite of Psalm 8's order of honour. The whirlwind will give the stars a different character:
  *"when the morning stars sang together"* (38:7).
- [[Light and Darkness]] — *"On whom does his light not arise?"* (25:3) and *"even the moon has no
  brightness"* (25:5): light as universal and as relative. Both lines answer, without engaging, Job's
  rebels against the light (24:13–17).
- [[The Fear of God]] — *paḥad*, dread, at 25:2, as a possession of God's rather than a posture of
  man's; Job's terror (23:15) made into a royal attribute.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — *"He makes peace in his high places"* (25:2), the book's one use of
  *šālôm* for God's own work, and a peace that presupposes a conflict above; later the closing line of
  the Kaddish.
- [[Retribution]] — present by omission. The speech does not argue retribution any longer; it
  replaces it with a doctrine that makes the question of Job's particular guilt unnecessary, since no
  human being could be acquitted anyway. This is the friends' last move, and it gives up the field it
  was meant to hold.

## Notes

- **Is the speech complete?** Six verses is extraordinarily short, and the third cycle is visibly out
  of pattern: Bildad's speech is a fraction of his others, Zophar has none, and Job's next speeches
  contain material that sounds like the friends'. Two families of reading, already outlined on
  [[Bildad the Shuhite]]:
  **Textual disorder.** The third cycle was damaged or rearranged in transmission. On the commonest
  reconstruction 26:5–14 — the hymn on God's mastery of Sheol, the waters, the north, the pillars of
  heaven and Rahab — is the rest of Bildad's speech, which would make chapter 25 a fragment of a
  hymn-speech about the same length as his others; and 27:13–23 is assigned to Zophar. Several
  modern translations and commentaries rearrange accordingly.
  **Deliberate collapse.** The text is as intended: Bildad has nothing left but a reprise, Zophar has
  nothing at all, and the breakdown of the cycle is the book's way of showing that the friends' case
  is exhausted. On this reading the short speech is the point, and its last word, *worm*, is the
  bottom of an argument that has been going down since 4:17.
  **The wiki keeps the received arrangement**, because the WEB and the Hebrew text both give 26:1 as
  *"Then Job answered"*, and records both readings here. The question will come up again at chapter
  26, where the passage page should weigh whether 26:5–14 reads as Job's or Bildad's. (It does, at
  [[I Will Not Put Away My Integrity]], and reads it as Job's.)
- **Nothing in the speech is false, and that is not a defence.** Every clause of 25:2–6 has a
  counterpart in a book no one calls heterodox: God's dominion and the dread of him, the heavenly host,
  universal light, the impossibility of a human being pure before God (Psalm 143:2 and Romans 3 are the
  standard references). The difficulty the wiki keeps returning to on [[Retribution]] and at
  [[What Is Man That He Should Be Clean]] is at its sharpest here because the speech has shed everything
  else — no particular charge, no doctrine of the wicked man, no advice. What remains is orthodoxy
  with nobody in it, offered to a man who asked for a hearing and for someone to prove him a liar.
- ***"Dominion"*** **(25:2).** *Hamšēl*, the infinitive of *māšal*, to rule — the verb of Genesis
  1:16's lights that *rule* the day and night. Bildad's next three clauses are about heavenly bodies
  and their light, so the echo of the fourth day may be intended; it is not certain. The pairing
  *"dominion and fear"* (*hamšēl wāpaḥad*) is otherwise unique.
- ***"Can his armies be counted?"*** **(25:3).** *Gədûdîm* are raiding parties rather than a standing
  army — the word for the bands that harried Israel's borders. Applied to heaven it means the host,
  whether stars or angelic beings (the two are paired in 38:7). Isaiah 40:26 makes the opposite
  point with the same picture: God *"brings out their army by number"* and *"calls them all by
  name"*. In Bildad the host cannot be counted; in Isaiah it can, by God, and *"Not one is lacking."*
- ***"The moon has no brightness"*** **(25:5).** Literally *the moon does not shine* or *does not pitch
  its tent* (*yaʾăhîl*, a much-discussed form). The sense is clear either way — even the brightest
  thing in the night sky is dim before God. The book will mention the moon once more, in Job's oath
  of innocence, where he swears he never kissed his hand to *"the moon moving in splendor"* (31:26):
  the moon as an object of worship he refused. Bildad's moon is deflated; Job's was resisted.
- **No second person.** Every other speech by the friends addresses Job as *you*, and Bildad's first
  two open with *"How long will you…"* (8:2; 18:2). Chapter 25 has no *you* in it. The only other
  speech in the poem with no addressee is Job's own lament in chapter 3, spoken to nobody (see
  [[Let the Day Perish]]); Bildad's is the only one spoken past a man sitting in front of him, and the
  friends' part in the book ends in it.
