# Seven Days of Silence

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 2:11–13

## Summary

Three verses, and they are the only thing Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar do that the book does not
condemn. **Everything the three friends say between chapter 4 and chapter 25 is repudiated by God at
42:7. Everything they do in these three verses is exemplary.** The difference between the two is
that here they have not started talking yet, and the book arranges the prologue so that the reader
cannot miss it.

**The scene changes cast completely.** The accuser left at 2:7 and will not be mentioned again by
anyone. Job's wife spoke her one sentence at 2:9 and will not be heard from again. What is left on
the ground is a man on an ash-heap with nobody, and into that emptiness the book brings three men
who have travelled to reach him. **The heavenly half of the story is over; the human half has not
begun; these three verses are the seam**, and the last prose the book writes before forty chapters
of poetry.

**They come because they heard.** *"Now when Job's three friends heard of all this evil that had
come on him, they each came from his own place."* The word is ***rāʿâ***, evil — **the same word
Job used to his wife nine verses earlier**, *"shall we not receive evil?"*, and the same word the
epilogue will use for the whole affair: *"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him"* (42:11).
The narrator adopts Job's own term for what happened, and nobody in the book ever calls it anything
milder.

**They are named by where they are from, and by nothing else.** No fathers, no ages, no trades —
just [[Teman]], [[Shuah]] and [[Naamah]], three gentilics pointing east and south-east into Edomite
and Arabian country. **The book will not give its three theologians a genealogy, which is exactly
what it did to Job**, who arrives at 1:1 with a land and no father either. **Nobody in this book has
a lineage**, and in a Bible where lineage is how a person is placed, that absence is the book
announcing that the case does not depend on who anyone is descended from. It also puts all four men
outside Israel, in the international wisdom tradition to which the book belongs. See
[[Uz]].

**Their coming is organised, not impulsive.** *"They made an appointment together"* — *wayyiwwāʿădû
yaḥdāw*, they met by arrangement, from *yāʿad*, the verb of a set time and a fixed place. **Three
men in three countries got word, sent word to each other, agreed a rendezvous, and travelled.**
Whatever is said later about their failure, the text is careful to establish that they did not
stumble across him. They organised a journey in order to sit in dirt with a man whose disease was
visible from a distance.

**And the purpose is stated in two verbs that will both come back to convict them.** *"To
sympathize with him and to comfort him"* — ***lānûd lô ûlənaḥămô***. ***Nûd*** is a bodily motion:
to shake, to rock, to move the head from side to side in grief beside someone. ***Nāḥam*** is to
comfort, console, bring relief. **Job's verdict on them at 16:2 uses the second word to their
faces** — *mənaḥămê ʿāmāl kullkem*, *"miserable comforters are you all"*, literally **comforters of
trouble**, comforters who are themselves the trouble. And **42:11 uses both words again**, of other
people: Job's brothers and sisters come at the end and *"comforted him"* — *wayyānudû lô
wayənaḥămû ʾōtô*, the identical pair from 2:11. **The book opens and closes its account of human
consolation with the same two verbs, and only the second attempt works.** See
[[Comforters Who Do Not Comfort]].

**Then they see him, and the verse breaks in the middle.** *"When they lifted up their eyes from a
distance, and didn't recognize him"* — *wəlōʾ hikkîrūhû*. **The disease has gone past the point
where a man's face identifies him.** They know Job; they are looking straight at him; the
recognition does not happen. It is the book's most economical statement of what chapter 2 did to
him, and it is made from outside, by witnesses, rather than by the sufferer — the only such
description in the book, since every other account of the sores is Job's own (7:5, 19:20, 30:30).
See [[They Didn't Recognize Him - Marred Beyond Knowing]].

**What they do next is a funeral.** They raise their voices and weep; each tears his robe; they
throw dust over their heads toward the sky. These are not gestures of sympathy in general. They are
**the prescribed rites for the dead**, and Job performed the same ones himself at 1:20 when his
children were killed. **The friends are burying a man who is sitting in front of them alive**, and
they do it before a word of doctrine has been spoken. Whatever else is wrong with them, they have
correctly estimated the scale. See [[Mourning and Lament]].

**The dust is thrown upward.** *Wayyizrəqû ʿāpār ʿal-rāʾšêhem haššāmāyəmâ* — literally *they threw
dust on their heads heavenward*. The usual mourning gesture is to put dust or ashes on the head;
this one casts it **up**, so that it falls back down on them. Two readings are available and the
Hebrew does not choose: a throw hard enough to be seen from a distance, a display pitched at the
scale of the grief; or a gesture **aimed at the sky** — dust flung in the direction of the one
responsible, which would make it the first accusation in the book and would make it wordless. The
wiki records both; see the Notes.

**And then they sit down and stop.** *"So they sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven
nights, and no one spoke a word to him."* **Seven days and seven nights is the standard term of
mourning for the dead** — Joseph keeps it for Jacob at the threshing floor of Atad (Genesis 50:10),
the men of Jabesh Gilead keep it for Saul (1 Samuel 31:13), Ezekiel sits out the same week among the
exiles (Ezekiel 3:15). **The friends sit shiva for a living man**, on the ground, in his ash-heap,
for a full week, saying nothing. See
[[Seven Days and Seven Nights - The Week of Mourning]].

**The reason given is that they read him right.** *"For they saw that his grief was very great"* —
*kî rāʾû kî-gādal hakəʾēb məʾōd*. ***Kəʾēb*** is pain, and it covers the physical and the mental
without distinguishing them, which is the correct word for a man with both. **The same eyes that
will misread him for twenty-five chapters read him accurately here, and the difference is that here
they are not interpreting.** They are looking. The moment they begin to explain what they are
looking at, they begin to be wrong.

**The silence is the hinge of the book, and it is broken from the inside.** *"After this Job opened
his mouth, and cursed the day of his birth"* (3:1). **The friends do not speak first.** Job does,
and everything the three of them say afterwards is an answer to what he says in chapter 3. Had the
week ended with three men going home, the book would be the story of a righteous man who held under
everything. **The week ends instead with someone finally saying something, and the something is a
curse**, and forty chapters of argument follow from it.

## Key verses

> **11** Now when Job’s three friends heard of all this evil that had come on him, they each came
> from his own place: Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite; and they
> made an appointment together to come to sympathize with him and to comfort him.

> **12** When they lifted up their eyes from a distance, and didn’t recognize him, they raised their
> voices, and wept; and they each tore his robe, and sprinkled dust on their heads toward the sky.

> **13** So they sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a
> word to him, for they saw that his grief was very great.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — silent throughout, and **not described**. The passage gives us his condition
  only through three other men's faces: they do not know him, they weep, they mourn him as dead, and
  they cannot think of anything to say for a week. **It is the only portrait of Job in the book drawn
  from outside him**, and it is drawn entirely in other people's reactions.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — named first here as he will be named first at 42:9, and as he will
  speak first at 4:1. **His first act in the book is to travel to a sick man and sit on the ground
  beside him for a week.** Read against the invented charges of 22:6–9 — stripping the naked,
  withholding bread from the hungry — the trajectory from this verse to that one is the book's
  quietest indictment of what a doctrine can do to a decent man.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — the bluntest of the three, whose first recorded speech (8:4) will explain
  Job's dead children as a sentence they earned. **He also tore his robe for them here**, and the
  book gives no sign that he sees any tension between the two.
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — the harshest, who will tell Job at 11:6 that God is exacting *less
  than his iniquity deserves*. **He kept the same week of silence as the others.** The three verses
  are a standing rebuke to any reading of the friends as villains: they are men who did the right
  thing first and then reasoned their way out of it.
- [[Job's Wife]] — absent, and the absence is loud. She spoke at 2:9, the friends arrive at 2:11, and
  she is not mentioned in the scene, in the seven days, or in the thirty-nine chapters that follow
  until 19:17. **The only person in the house is not recorded as sitting the week.**
- [[Satan]] — gone since 2:7. **The three men who arrive have no idea he exists**, and the whole
  argument they are about to conduct is conducted without the fact that would settle it. The reader
  holds it and no one on the ground does.
- [[Job's Children]] — the reason the mourning rites are so exactly right and so exactly misapplied:
  ten funerals have already happened, and the friends are performing an eleventh for a man who is
  still breathing.
- [[God]] — absent from the passage, spoken of by no one, and the only party who knows what the
  friends are walking into. **His next words in the book after 2:6 are addressed to Job out of a
  whirlwind, thirty-six chapters later.**

## Places

- [[Uz]] — the setting, unnamed here as in every scene since 1:1. What the verse adds is that it is
  **reachable**: three men in three other countries heard, corresponded, arranged a meeting, and
  arrived. The book's one geographical fact about Job's home is that news gets out of it.
- [[Teman]] — Eliphaz's home, an Edomite district proverbial for wisdom (Jeremiah 49:7, Obadiah 8).
  **The one of the three that can be located**, and the one whose reputation the book is about to
  spend forty chapters dismantling.
- [[Shuah]] — Bildad's, unlocated; usually connected with the Shuah whom [[Keturah]] bore to
  [[Abraham]] and who was sent *"eastward, to the east country"* (Genesis 25:2, 6), and sometimes
  with Assyrian **Sūḫu** on the middle Euphrates.
- [[Naamah]] — Zophar's, unlocated and attested nowhere else as his people's home; the Judean
  Naamah of Joshua 15:41 is in the wrong country for this book.

## Themes

- [[Mourning and Lament]] — the wiki's anchor text for the full rite. Torn robe, raised voice, dust
  on the head, sitting on the ground, and a seven-day term, all in two verses, and all performed for
  someone who has not died.
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — **the friends' high-water mark.** Everything the New Testament asks of a
  comforter — *"weep with those who weep"* (Romans 12:15), *"bear one another's burdens"* (Galatians
  6:2) — is done here by three men who will shortly become the Bible's standing example of how to do
  it wrong.
- [[Language and Speech]] — the book's most eloquent silence, and its structural point: **the
  disaster in Job is not that the friends came, it is that they eventually spoke.** Set this beside
  Job's own *"I have heard many such things. Miserable comforters are you all!"* (16:2) in
  [[Miserable Comforters and the Witness in Heaven]] and beside Proverbs 17:28.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the passage turns on two acts of sight that go opposite ways. **They
  lift their eyes and do not recognise him** (2:12); **they see that his pain is very great** (2:13).
  The first is a failure of sight and the second is its success, and the second is what buys the week
  of silence.
- [[Suffering]] — measured here by a third party for the first time. Chapters 1 and 2 reported what
  was done to Job; this verse reports what it looks like to people who knew him before.
- [[Wisdom]] — three men from the wisdom lands, and the wisest thing any of them ever does is say
  nothing for a week. **Job 28 will ask where wisdom is found; the book has already shown one place
  it was, and the men who were sitting in it did not notice.**
- [[Retribution]] — not yet stated, and already arriving. **Nothing in these verses is doctrinal**,
  but three men have just performed the death rites over a man marked with the boil of Deuteronomy
  28, and they will spend twenty-five chapters saying what that must mean. See
  [[The Boil of Deuteronomy 28 on a Blameless Man]].
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — the last peace in the book. From 3:1 to 42:6 there is no scene in
  which any two people in it are not arguing.
- [[Burial and the Grave]] — the rites are funeral rites and the term is a funeral term. **Job will
  ask for a grave repeatedly in chapter 3** (*"Why didn't I die from the womb?"*), eleven verses
  after three men mourned him as though he had.
- [[Hospitality]] — inverted. In Genesis the visit of three men to a suffering household is the
  Bible's set piece of welcome and announcement ([[The Three Visitors and Abraham's Intercession]]);
  here three men arrive at a household with nothing left to offer them and sit on the floor.
- [[The Fear of God]] — not mentioned, and worth marking as absent: the friends never raise the
  question the accuser raised. **They will ask what Job did, never what his piety is for.**
- [[Integrity]] — *tummâ* is not in these verses, and the men who arrive will spend the book trying
  to get Job to put it down. They arrive before they have any position on it at all.

## Notes

- **Why the three verses are a pericope and not a tail on chapter 2.** They could be read as the
  closing movement of the second test, and some outlines do exactly that. The wiki separates them
  because **the cast, the location of the action, and the register all change at 2:11**: the accuser
  and the council are gone, the scene is on the ground in Uz for the rest of the book, and three
  characters who will speak for thirty chapters walk in. Keeping them attached to 2:1–10 also buries
  the single most important structural fact about them, which is that **they are the last prose
  before the poetry**, and that the poetry starts because this silence ends.

- **The two verbs, and why the wiki weights *nûd*.** *Nāḥam* is the ordinary word for comfort and
  carries no surprise. *Nûd* is more specific and more physical: to sway, to shake the head, to rock.
  Its other biblical uses run in two directions. **Toward grief** — Jeremiah 16:5, 22:10, and Isaiah
  51:19's *"who will bemoan you?"*; **and toward derision** — Psalm 44:14 and Jeremiah 18:16 use the
  same motion of the head as mockery, *"a shaking of the head"*. **The gesture is identical and the
  meaning is read off the context**, which is a small, exact anticipation of the friends' entire
  problem: the outward act of sympathy and the outward act of contempt look the same, and Job will
  say at 16:4–5 that he could produce either at will — *"I also could speak as you do… I could shake
  my head at you."* **He uses their verb against them.**

- **Dust toward the sky (2:12).** The construction is unusual. Standard mourning puts dust or earth
  *on* the head (Joshua 7:6, 1 Samuel 4:12, 2 Samuel 1:2, Nehemiah 9:1, Lamentations 2:10), and
  those texts say so plainly. Here the dust goes *ʿal-rāʾšêhem haššāmāyəmâ*, on their heads
  *heavenward*. **Three readings.** (1) **Manner** — they threw it high, so it rained down; a
  description of vehemence, and the commonest reading. (2) **Direction of address** — the dust is
  flung *at heaven*, an inarticulate protest against the party they hold responsible; this is
  attractive because it makes the friends' first act an accusation of God, which is precisely what
  they will spend the book forbidding Job to make. (3) **Textual** — *haššāmāyəmâ* has been thought
  intrusive. **The wiki records all three and adopts none**, but notes that reading (2) would give
  the friends and Job the same instinct at the outset and make their divergence a matter of what they
  did with it.

- **Seven days, and the rabbinic rule.** The seven-day mourning term is standard in the Hebrew Bible
  (Genesis 50:10, 1 Samuel 31:13) and is the basis of the Jewish *shivah* — literally *seven*. What
  makes 2:13 significant in Jewish practice is the second half of the verse rather than the first:
  **the rule that a visitor to a mourner does not speak until the mourner speaks first** is derived
  from *"no one spoke a word to him"* (b. *Moʿed Qatan* 28b), with 3:1 read as the mourner opening.
  **On that reading the friends observe the law of comforting exactly and are ruined by what they say
  once it releases them.** The wiki records this as reception rather than as the text's own claim;
  the narrator gives a different reason for the silence — that they saw how great the pain was — and
  attributes no rule to them.

- **Did they do anything wrong yet?** No, and the wiki says so plainly, because the popular reading
  of the friends as callous from the start is not what chapter 2 describes. They heard, they
  coordinated, they travelled, they wept, they tore their clothes, they sat on the ground in a
  diseased man's ashes for seven days, and they did not speak. **The failure is entirely downstream
  of this scene, and it begins with an explanation.** What the passage sets up is not villainy but
  the far more uncomfortable proposition that **sincere, orthodox, genuinely compassionate people can
  be turned into instruments of cruelty by a doctrine they hold in good faith**, and that the turning
  happens at the moment they start accounting for what they see. See [[Retribution]].

- **The friends never learn what the reader knows.** They arrive twelve verses after the accuser's
  second permission and four verses after he struck. **No one tells them, and no one ever will** —
  not Job, who does not know; not the narrator, who addresses only the reader; and not God, who at
  42:7 rebukes them without explaining anything. The epilogue attributes the whole affair to Yahweh
  (*"all the evil that Yahweh had brought on him"*, 42:11) with the accuser unmentioned. **The men
  who get the book's only unambiguous divine rebuke are never given the information that would have
  prevented the offence.**

- **A reading the wiki declines.** It is sometimes proposed that the seven days are already hostile —
  a silence of judgment, three men waiting for a confession. **Nothing in the text supports it.** The
  narrator supplies a motive in the same verse and it is compassionate (*"for they saw that his grief
  was very great"*), the accompanying acts are mourning acts and not accusatory ones, and Job's later
  complaints about the friends are all complaints about things they *said*. The hostile reading is
  back-formed from chapters 4–25 and imported into a passage that does not contain it.
