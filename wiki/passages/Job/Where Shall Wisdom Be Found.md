# Where Shall Wisdom Be Found

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 28:1–28

## Summary

The dialogue is over. Three cycles of speeches have ended with Bildad's six verses, Zophar's silence,
and Job's oath (27:2–6), and the next voice is not an argument at all. Chapter 28 is a poem — calm,
symmetrical, and addressed to no one — which asks one question twice and answers it once. It has no
heading of its own, so as the text stands it is still Job speaking under 27:1; but it sounds like no
other speech in the book, and its place in the book's structure is that of an **interlude**: the
pause between the debate that has failed and the three speakers (Job, Elihu, God) who will each try
to go past it. Who speaks it is set out in the Notes. What it says has three movements.

**1. Man can find anything in the earth (28:1–11).** *"Surely there is a mine for silver, and a place
for gold which they refine. Iron is taken out of the earth, and copper is smelted out of the ore"*
(28:1–2). The poem opens in a mine, and the description is a working one: a shaft sunk *"away from
where people live"*, men lowered on ropes who *"hang far from men, they swing back and forth"*
(28:4), rock broken *"as it were by fire"* beneath the fields that grow bread (28:5), channels cut
through the stone and underground water dammed (28:10–11). Copper and iron were mined in exactly
this way in the Arabah — at Timna and Faynan, on the edge of Edom, which is the region of
[[Teman]] and, on the usual identification, of [[Uz]] — so the poem's first image would have been familiar
technology to anyone in Job's world.

Everything in the stanza praises human reach. The miner *"sets an end to darkness, and searches out,
to the furthest bound, the stones of obscurity and of thick darkness"* (28:3). He goes where *"no
bird of prey knows, neither has the falcon's eye seen it"* and where *"the proud animals have not
trodden"* (28:7–8) — the sharpest eye in the sky and the strongest beasts on the ground have never
been where he has been. He *"overturns the mountains by the roots"* (28:9), the thing God was praised
for at 9:5. And the stanza's last line is its claim: ***"The thing that is hidden he brings out to
light"*** (28:11). Light is the miner's tool and darkness his material — the book's controlling
image, which chapter 3 tried to run backwards, here used of a man doing something well.

**2. But wisdom cannot be found or bought (28:12–22).** Then the refrain, the first time:

> *"But where shall wisdom be found? Where is the place of understanding?"* (28:12)

Wisdom (*ḥokmâ*) has a *place* (*māqôm*), the poem assumes, as silver has a mine — and no one can
reach it. *"Man doesn't know its price; neither is it found in the land of the living"* (28:13). The
deep (*təhôm*, Genesis 1:2's word) and the sea, the two places furthest from the miner's shaft, say
*"It isn't in me … It isn't with me"* (28:14). Then five verses of the finest goods in the world,
each declared insufficient: gold refined and unrefined, silver weighed, *"the gold of Ophir"*, onyx,
sapphire, glass, coral, crystal, rubies, *"the topaz of Ethiopia"*, pure gold (28:15–19). The
miner of the first stanza could produce every one of them, and none buys what the second stanza is
looking for. The refrain comes again — *"Where then does wisdom come from? Where is the place of
understanding?"* (28:20) — and this time the search goes up and down: hidden *"from the eyes of all
living, and kept close from the birds of the sky"* (28:21), and at the bottom of the world
*"Destruction and Death say, 'We have heard a rumor of it with our ears'"* (28:22). Abaddon, which
26:6 said was *naked before God*, has only heard of wisdom by report.

**3. God knows its way (28:23–28).** *"God understands its way, and he knows its place"* (28:23). The
reason is given as a view: *"he looks to the ends of the earth, and sees under the whole sky"*
(28:24) — the view the miner, the falcon and the lion do not have. And it is given as an occasion:
when God weighed the wind, measured the waters, *"made a decree for the rain, and a way for the
lightning of the thunder; then he saw it, and declared it. He established it, yes, and searched it
out"* (28:25–27). **Wisdom was seen at the making of the world, by the one who made it**, which is
Proverbs 8:22–31's claim without Proverbs 8's personification — here wisdom is not a lady at God's
side but something he looked at, counted, set in place, and examined.

Then the last verse, which changes everything about the chapter's question:

> ***"To man he said, 'Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom. To depart from evil is
> understanding.'"*** (28:28)

**The answer is not where wisdom is but what it is for a human being.** God keeps the wisdom that runs
the wind and the rain; what he gives *ʾādām*, mankind as such, is a posture and a practice. And the
words are the book's first verse. Job 1:1 called Job ***yərēʾ ʾĕlōhîm wəsār mērāʿ***, *one who feared
God and turned away from evil*; 28:28 defines wisdom as ***yirʾat ʾădōnāy … wəsûr mērāʿ***, *the fear
of the Lord … and turning away from evil*. **The man the friends have spent twenty-five chapters
calling a fool is, by the hymn's own definition, the wisest man in the book**, and the prologue said so
before any of them spoke. What the definition does *not* supply is the thing Job has been demanding —
the reason for his suffering. 28:28 is wisdom as a way to live, not as an explanation, and it leaves
the lawsuit exactly where it was: chapters 29–31 resume it at once.

**Where this sits in the book.** Every voice so far has claimed to know where wisdom is: the friends
in the fathers (8:8; 15:18) and Eliphaz in his vision (4:12–17); Job, sarcastically, in the friends
(12:2), and then in God alone (12:13). Chapter 26 ended with the limit of knowledge — *"How small a
whisper do we hear of him!"* (26:14). Chapter 28 turns that limit into a poem, and in doing so
anticipates God's own answer. The whirlwind speech will ask Job about exactly the things 28:25–26
lists: *"Who has cut a channel for the flood water, or the path for the thunderstorm"* (38:25) —
the same Hebrew as 28:26's *"a way for the lightning of the thunder"*, *wəderek laḥăzîz qōlôt* — and
*"Who has put wisdom in the inward parts?"* (38:36). **What chapter 28 says in the third person, God
will say to Job in the second.** And the chapter's quiet line about *"the proud animals"*
(*bənê-šaḥaṣ*, 28:8) will come back only once, as the last line of the last speech: Leviathan *"is
king over all the sons of pride"* (41:34).

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — the speaker as the text stands, with no new heading after 27:1; but the poem has
  no first person, no complaint and no address, and is unlike every other speech of his. **Its last
  verse is the description of him in 1:1, turned into a definition**: the hymn calls wise the man the
  friends call guilty, without naming him.
- [[God]] — the only one who *"understands its way"* (28:23), because he sees *"under the whole sky"*
  (28:24) and was present when wind, water, rain and lightning were measured (28:25–26). The four
  verbs of 28:27 — saw, declared, established, searched out — and **the only words the poetry
  reports him as having said** (9:19 only imagines him speaking), addressed not to Job but *"to man"*
  (28:28). The divine name there is *ʾădōnāy*, the Lord
  (see the Notes).

## Places

- [[Ophir]] — *"It can't be valued with the gold of Ophir"* (28:16). The byword for the finest gold,
  in its second and last appearance in Job; at 22:24 Eliphaz told Job to throw it into a wadi.
- [[Cush]] — *"The topaz of Ethiopia shall not equal it"* (28:19). *Piṭdat kûš*: the stone of the far
  south, from the Nubian kingdom on the upper Nile, the edge of the known world in that direction.
- [[Sheol]] — present as **Abaddon** (*ʾăbaddôn*, *Destruction*) and **Death** (*māwet*), speaking:
  *"We have heard a rumor of it with our ears"* (28:22). The underworld that is naked to God (26:6)
  knows wisdom only by hearsay. See
  [[Abaddon Has No Covering - Job 26.6, Proverbs 15.11, and Revelation 9.11]].

## Themes

- [[Wisdom]] — the chapter's subject: *ḥokmâ* and *bînâ*, wisdom and understanding, sought in a
  place, priced against gold, found by God, and defined for man. The theme's centre in Job.
- [[The Fear of God]] — 28:28, *"the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom"*: not the *beginning* of
  wisdom as in Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10, but wisdom itself, and Job 1:1's words. See
  [[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]].
- [[Sin]] — *"To depart from evil is understanding"* (28:28): *sûr mērāʿ*, the negative half of the
  definition, and the second half of Job's description in 1:1, 8 and 2:3.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — the miner's riches (28:1–11) and the catalogue of gold and gems that
  cannot buy wisdom (28:15–19). See [[Better Than Gold - The Price of Wisdom]].
- [[Work]] — the Bible's fullest description of mining, and one of its most admiring accounts of human
  skill: *"His eye sees every precious thing"* (28:10). The craft praised in full, and then shown its
  limit.
- [[Light and Darkness]] — *"Man sets an end to darkness"* (28:3), and *"The thing that is hidden he
  brings out to light"* (28:11): human ingenuity as the bringing of light into darkness, in a book
  whose hero began by asking for the reverse (3:4–6).
- [[Creation]] — 28:24–27: the wind weighed, the waters measured, a decree for the rain, a way for the
  lightning; and wisdom seen and established at the founding. The deep, *təhôm*, says *"It isn't in
  me"* (28:14).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the falcon's eye has not seen the miner's path (28:7); the miner's eye
  sees *"every precious thing"* (28:10); wisdom is hidden *"from the eyes of all living"* (28:21);
  God *"looks to the ends of the earth"* (28:24) and *"saw it"* (28:27). The chapter's search is a
  contest of eyes, and only one sees far enough.
- [[Life and Death]] — wisdom *"not found in the land of the living"* (28:13), and Death itself only
  rumoured to have heard of it (28:22).

## Notes

- **Who speaks chapter 28?** No heading separates it from 27:1, and 29:1's *"Job again took up his
  parable"* implies that what precedes it was also Job's. **On the received text, it is Job's.** But
  three features set it apart: it has no *I* and no *you* and is addressed to no one; its calm
  resolution sits awkwardly between the oath of 27:2–6 and the renewed complaint of chapters 29–31,
  where Job still demands an answer as if 28:28 had never been said; and it anticipates the divine
  speeches so closely that, if Job could say this, it is hard to see what the whirlwind tells him he
  did not already know. The readings: **(a) Job's**, a moment of composure in which he states what he
  believes about wisdom and then, in 29–31, insists that it does not settle his case; **(b) the
  narrator's or the poet's interlude**, a choral pause that tells the reader what none of the speakers
  has grasped, comparable to the prologue's privileged view; **(c) an independent poem** placed here by
  an editor, perhaps later than the dialogue; **(d) part of the friends' reconstructed third cycle**,
  occasionally proposed and generally not adopted, since its doctrine is not theirs. **The wiki keeps
  the received attribution and treats the chapter structurally as an interlude**, which is how the
  book page has always described it. On any reading, its last verse describes Job.
- **The divine name in 28:28.** *ʾĂdōnāy*, *the Lord*, which the WEB renders accordingly instead of its
  usual *Yahweh*. It is the only occurrence of the title in the book; the poetry otherwise prefers
  *ʾĒl*, *ʾĔlôah* and *Šadday*, and between chapters 3 and 37 *Yahweh* appears once, at 12:9. With
  12:9, it is the dialogue's only step outside the generic divine vocabulary of the poetry. Some
  Hebrew manuscripts read *Yahweh* here. Either way the formula is Israel's own — *the fear of Yahweh
  is the beginning of wisdom* — and it stands out in a book set among foreigners. See
  [[The Names of God]].
- **"Man doesn't know its price" (28:13).** *ʿErkāh*, its value, its equivalent. The Greek reads *its
  way*, a single letter different in Hebrew and matching *"God understands its way"* (28:23). The WEB
  follows the Hebrew; both make sense, and the price reading prepares for 28:15–19.
- **The precious things (28:15–19).** Five words for gold in five verses — *sāgûr* (refined), *ketem*
  (fine gold, twice: *of Ophir* and *pure*), *zāhāb*, *paz* — and a catalogue of stones whose
  identifications are uncertain throughout; the WEB's *onyx*, *sapphire*, *coral*, *crystal*, *rubies*
  and *topaz* are conventional. *Zəkôkît*, *glass*, occurs only here in the Old Testament — a luxury
  material in the ancient Near East, not a cheap one. *Piṭdâ*, *topaz*, is the second stone of the
  high priest's breastplate (Exodus 28:17) and is among the stones of the king in Eden (Ezekiel 28:13).
- **The hidden and the found.** The verb of 28:3, the miner who *"searches out"* (*ḥôqēr*), is the verb
  of 28:27, God who *"searched it out"* (*ḥăqārāh*); and the noun *ḥēqer* was Zophar's *"Can you
  fathom the mystery of God?"* (11:7). The miner brings *taʿălumâ*, the hidden thing, to light (28:11);
  wisdom is *neʿelmâ*, hidden, from all living (28:21) — the same root, *ʿ-l-m*. **The chapter is built
  on two searches with the same verb and two hidden things with the same root, and one search
  succeeds.**
- **"The proud animals" (28:8).** *Bənê-šaḥaṣ*, literally *sons of pride*, occurs only here and at
  41:34, the last line of God's description of Leviathan. The meaning is uncertain (perhaps *the
  majestic beasts*), but the echo is exact.
- **The question taken up later.** Later Jewish wisdom literature took up the hymn's
  question — Baruch 3:15, 29–37 asks who has gone up to heaven or across the sea to fetch wisdom, and
  answers that God found it and gave it to Israel as the Law. Cited, not quoted: Baruch is outside the
  sixty-six books this wiki covers.
- **No contradiction between wiki pages found.** The tension between 28:28's settled answer and the
  unsettled Job of 29–31 is recorded above as a question about the speaker, not as a conflict between
  pages.
