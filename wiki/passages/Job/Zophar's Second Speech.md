# Zophar's Second Speech

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 20:1–29

## Summary

Job has just said *"I know that my Redeemer lives"* and warned the three men to be afraid of the
sword. [[Zophar the Naamathite]] answers the warning and nothing else, and he answers it by
talking for twenty-nine verses about a rich man choking on his own food. It is the last thing
Zophar ever says in the book.

**The opening (20:1–3) admits, unusually, that the speech is a reaction.** *"Therefore do my
thoughts give answer to me, even by reason of my haste that is in me. I have heard the reproof
which puts me to shame."* The *reproof* is 19:28–29, and Zophar says outright that he is stung and
in a hurry. None of the other friends concedes as much. What follows is not reasoning but
recitation, and Zophar tells us so before he starts.

**The thesis (20:4–5) is a single sentence and it is the whole speech**: *"Don't you know this from
old time, since man was placed on earth, that the triumphing of the wicked is short, the joy of the
godless but for a moment?"* Like [[Bildad the Shuhite]] appealing to *"past generations"* (8:8),
Zophar grounds the doctrine in antiquity — here reaching back to the creation of man, which is as
far back as an appeal can go. And the thesis is a **concession disguised as a claim**. Zophar is
conceding that the wicked do triumph. His whole position now rests on the word *short*, and that is
precisely the ground Job will fight him on in chapter 21: *"Why do the wicked live, become old,
yes, and grow mighty in power?"*

**The first movement (20:6–11) is the fall from height.** *"Though his height mount up to the
heavens, and his head reach to the clouds, yet he shall perish forever like his own dung."* The
elevation-and-collapse figure is the standard biblical shape for pride — the tower *"whose top
reaches to the sky"* at Genesis 11:4 (see [[The Tower of Babel]]), the king of Babylon's *"I will
ascend into heaven"* at Isaiah 14:13, Edom's nest among the stars at Obadiah 4 — and the bathos of
the second half is deliberate and coarse. Then the man himself becomes insubstantial: *"He shall
fly away as a dream, and shall not be found … The eye which saw him shall see him no more."*
Verse 10 does to his children what Bildad did two chapters earlier: *"His children shall seek the
favor of the poor"* — the heirs reduced to begging from the class their father robbed.

**The second movement (20:12–19) is the speech's real invention, and it is a digestive metaphor
carried further than any other in Scripture.** Wickedness is sweet in the mouth, hidden under the
tongue, saved and savoured — and then, in the belly, *"it is cobra venom within him."* *"He has
swallowed down riches, and he shall vomit them up again. God will cast them out of his belly."* He
sucks venom and the viper's tongue kills him. He will never *"look at the rivers, the flowing
streams of honey and butter"* — the language of the good land, refused. What he laboured for he
restores without ever having tasted it.

The conceit is exactly suited to the speaker. Zophar is the friend who told Job at 11:6 that *"God
exacts of you less than your iniquity deserves"*, and appetite that poisons its own owner is
retribution made **internal**: the punishment is not sent, it is metabolised. It is also, as
theology, the most economical statement any of the friends manages — sin is not merely punished, it
is self-consuming.

**And in the middle of it, at verse 19, comes the first specific charge anyone has made.** *"For he
has oppressed and forsaken the poor. He has violently taken away a house, and he shall not build it
up."* Every previous speech has inferred guilt from suffering without naming a crime. Here a crime
is named — extortion of the poor and seizure of a house — and it is still in the third person, of
*a* wicked man. Two chapters later [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] will drop the third person and read the
same charge sheet to Job's face, inventing the details (22:6–9). Chapter 20 is where the indictment
is drafted.

**The third movement (20:20–29) is the execution, and it is unrelenting.** No quietness within
him; nothing left that he did not devour; distress arriving *"in the fullness of his sufficiency"*.
Then the most vivid image in the speech: *"When he is about to fill his belly, God will cast the
fierceness of his wrath on him. It will rain on him while he is eating."* Then the weapons — he
flees the iron and the bronze arrow takes him, drawn out through the liver, glittering. Then
darkness stored up in his treasuries, *"an unfanned fire"* — a fire nobody lit — devouring *"that
which is left in his tent."*

Job's livestock and servants were destroyed by *"the fire of God … fallen from the sky"* (1:16),
and his children died in a house that collapsed. Like Bildad, Zophar is describing the man in front
of him and calling it doctrine.

**Verse 27 is the sentence that should be read against Job 16.** *"The heavens shall reveal his
iniquity. The earth shall rise up against him."* Four chapters earlier Job said *"Earth, don't cover
my blood"* and *"my witness is in heaven"* (16:18–19) — appealing to precisely these two witnesses
for the defence. Zophar, who cannot have known what he was answering, calls the same two as
witnesses for the prosecution. Heaven and earth are the standing witnesses of the covenant lawsuit
(Deuteronomy 32:1, Isaiah 1:2, Micah 6:1–2), and the two men have summoned them to opposite ends of
the same case.

**The closing formula (20:29) matches Bildad's.** *"This is the portion of a wicked man from God,
the heritage appointed to him by God."* Chapter 18 ended *"Surely such are the dwellings of the
unrighteous"*; chapter 20 ends with *portion* and *heritage* — inheritance words, the terms for what
is allotted to a man by right. The friends have now said the same thing twice in three chapters, in
different images, with the same verdict attached, and neither speech has engaged a single sentence
of Job's argument. Chapter 21 is his answer to both at once, and Zophar does not speak again.

## People

- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — his second and final speech. He is the friend who speaks least and
  concedes least, and this is the only place any of the three admits to being provoked (*"the
  reproof which puts me to shame"*, 20:3). He never speaks again: in the third cycle
  [[Bildad the Shuhite]] manages six verses and Zophar has none at all, which is either the
  dramatic exhaustion of the friends' position or a disturbance in the text of chapters 25–27.
- [[Job (person)]] — addressed only in verse 4's *"Don't you know this"* and described, unnamed, for
  the remaining twenty-five verses. Notably absent from Zophar's chapter 20 is anything from his
  chapter 11: no call to repent, no *"put away unrighteousness"*, no *"you shall be secure"*. The
  door he offered in the first cycle is not offered again.
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — whose chapter 18 this repeats in a different key. Both speeches are
  set-piece portraits of the wicked man's ruin with a summary formula at the end, and neither
  engages Job.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — who will take the charge of 20:19 and make it personal at 22:6–9,
  itemising crimes against the poor that the book gives no evidence for.
- [[God]] — named three times (20:15, 23, 29) and always as the agent of destruction: he empties the
  belly, casts down the wrath, and appoints the heritage. There is no other divine action anywhere
  in the chapter.

## Places

No geography, though verse 17's *"rivers, the flowing streams of honey and butter"* is land
language — the promised land's *milk and honey* (Exodus 3:8) in a wisdom register — used here for a
plenty the wicked man is not allowed to look at.

- [[Babel]] — not named, but 20:6's *"though his height mount up to the heavens, and his head reach
  to the clouds"* is the tower's ambition in a single line; see [[The Tower of Babel]].

## Themes

- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine's most compressed statement in the book, and its most vulnerable.
  By conceding at 20:5 that the wicked *do* triumph and resting everything on *short*, Zophar moves
  the claim onto ground where it can be checked, and Job checks it in chapter 21.
- [[Judgment]] — as a stored inheritance rather than an event: *"the portion of a wicked man from
  God, the heritage appointed to him by God"* (20:29), and *"All darkness is laid up for his
  treasures"* (20:26). Judgment here is a deposit accruing against a man, not a hearing he attends.
- [[Sin]] — the speech's account of it is genuinely interesting: sin as something eaten, held in the
  mouth for the taste of it, and then acting as a poison inside the body that swallowed it. This is
  the closest any friend comes to describing sin as intrinsically destructive rather than merely
  penalised.
- [[Pride and Self-Exaltation]] — 20:6–7's ascent to the clouds and collapse into dung is the
  biblical pattern of the high brought low, in its crudest form.
- [[Work]] — 20:18's *"That for which he labored he shall restore, and shall not swallow it down"*:
  labour whose fruit is confiscated before it can be enjoyed, which is the wisdom tradition's
  recurring nightmare (Ecclesiastes 2:18–21, Deuteronomy 28:30–33).
- [[Life and Death]] — 20:11's *"His bones are full of his youth, but youth shall lie down with him
  in the dust"* is the speech's one quiet line, and its best: a man dies with his strength still in
  him.
- [[Vengeance]] — 20:22's *"The hand of everyone who is in misery shall come on him"* has the
  oppressed doing the striking, which is the only place in the friends' speeches where judgment
  arrives through human hands.
- [[The Fear of God]] — by its absence. Zophar's *"godless"* (20:5, *ḥānēp*, the profane or
  impious) is the negative of the term the narrator applies to Job at 1:1, and the whole speech
  assumes the two categories exhaust the field.
- [[Wisdom]] — appealed to at 20:4 as immemorial common knowledge, *"from old time, since man was
  placed on earth"*. The friends' authority is always antiquity, never observation, and Job's
  chapter 21 answers with observation.

## Notes

- **The concession at 20:5, and why it loses the argument.** *"The triumphing of the wicked is
  short, the joy of the godless but for a moment."* Read carefully, this grants Job's premise. The
  friends' original claim was that the wicked *suffer*, from which Job's suffering could be read
  backwards. Zophar's claim is now that the wicked suffer *eventually*, which no longer supports the
  inference at all — a man's present ruin cannot be read as a verdict if the timing of ruin is
  variable. The doctrine has begun to protect itself by becoming unfalsifiable, and chapter 21 is
  Job pointing out that on the visible evidence the wicked often die old, rich, and mourned. See
  [[Retribution]].

- **The digestive metaphor, and where it goes in the canon.** The swallow-and-vomit figure of
  20:12–15 has two lives. In Proverbs it is a warning to the reader about tainted food and tainted
  gain (23:6–8, and 20:17's *"bread of falsehood … his mouth will be filled with gravel"*). In the
  Torah and the prophets the eater is the **land**, which vomits out its inhabitants when they
  defile it (Leviticus 18:25, 28; 20:22) — a national version of the same idea. Zophar's use is the
  individual one, and its distinctive move is that the poison is the *swallowed thing itself*
  rather than a punishment sent after it. Nothing in the friends' theology elsewhere is as close to
  saying that sin is its own sentence.

- **20:19 and the drafting of the indictment.** This verse deserves marking because of what happens
  to it. It is the first time in nineteen chapters that anyone names an actual offence — oppressing
  the poor, seizing a house — and it is still generic, spoken of a type. At 22:6–9 Eliphaz will make
  it particular: *"you have taken pledges from your brother for nothing … You haven't given water
  to the weary to drink … You have sent widows away empty."* The book supplies no evidence for any
  of it, and Job's oath of innocence at 31:16–22 answers the charges point by point. The trajectory
  from 20:19 to 22:6 is the friends' argument completing itself: having inferred guilt from
  suffering, they must eventually supply crimes, and when the evidence does not exist they invent
  them. Chapter 20 is the last stage before that step.

- **Heaven and earth, twice summoned (20:27).** *"The heavens shall reveal his iniquity. The earth
  shall rise up against him."* This is covenant-lawsuit language — the two witnesses called in
  Deuteronomy 32:1, Isaiah 1:2, and Micah 6:1–2 because they were present at the making of the
  agreement and outlast both parties. Zophar calls them for the prosecution. Job, at 16:18–19, had
  already called the same two for the defence: the earth was not to cover his blood, and his witness
  was in heaven. Neither man can hear the other's use of the figure, and the collision is the
  cleanest illustration in the second cycle of how completely the two sides are arguing past each
  other. Both are certain the cosmos will testify; each is certain of what it will say.

- **"Like his own dung" (20:7).** *Kəgelălô*, from *gālāl*, dung — a deliberately degrading word,
  and one of a small set of Hebrew terms for excrement (*gēl*, *ṣōʾâ*, *pereš*) that the prophets
  use to insult idols (*gillûlîm*, Ezekiel's standard sneering term for them, is from the same
  root). Some ancient versions soften it and a minority of commentators read *gālāl* here as
  *stone-heap* instead. The harsher reading is almost certainly right, and the bathos is the point:
  Zophar takes the wicked man from the clouds to the dungheap in the space of one verse.

- **Where the friends' speeches stop.** With chapter 20 the second cycle's three speeches are
  complete and the friends have said everything they have. What remains for them is six verses from
  Bildad at chapter 25 and, from Zophar, silence. It is worth registering that the collapse begins
  here rather than there: chapters 18 and 20 are already repetitions, and neither answers a word of
  what Job said in chapters 16, 17, or 19. The argument does not break down in the third cycle. It
  breaks down in the second, and the third cycle is where that becomes visible.
