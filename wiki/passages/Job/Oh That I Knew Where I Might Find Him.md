# Oh That I Knew Where I Might Find Him

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 23:1–24:25

## Summary

Eliphaz has just named Job's crimes and offered him peace on condition of confessing them
([[Eliphaz's Third Speech]]). **Job does not answer either.** He does not deny the charges here —
that waits for the oath of chapter 31 — and he does not mention the offer. He answers a different
speaker, the one who has not spoken: *"Oh that I knew where I might find him!"* The two chapters
are the dialogue's purest statement of the hidden God, and they divide cleanly in two.

**Chapter 23 is about Job and God.** It is a soliloquy: **nobody is addressed in it**, neither the
friends nor God, who is *he* and *him* in every verse. **Chapter 24 is about the world and God.** It
turns from Job's own case to everyone else's — the poor driven off their land, the labourers who
starve while they harvest, the murderer and the adulterer who work by night — and asks the same
question of them: why does the court never sit? Only the last verse turns to the friends, and it is a
dare.

**1. The lawsuit he cannot file (23:2–7).** *"Even today my complaint is rebellious. His hand is
heavy in spite of my groaning."* Then the wish, in the form every one of Job's wishes takes, *mî
yittēn*, *who will give* (6:8; 14:13; 19:23; and 31:35 to come): *"Oh that I knew where I might find
him! That I might come even to his seat!"* The *seat* is ***təkûnâ***, a rare word for a fixed
establishment — the place where a ruler can be found and petitioned. **What Job wants is a hearing,
not an explanation and not relief**: *"I would set my cause in order before him, and fill my mouth
with arguments. I would know the words which he would answer me."*

And then the most confident thing he has said about a hearing with God. *"Would he contend with me
in the greatness of his power? No, but he would listen to me. There the upright might reason with
him, so I should be delivered forever from my judge."* **This reverses chapter 9.** There Job was
sure that a trial with God was hopeless because God would overpower him: *"If it is a matter of
strength, behold, he is mighty!"* (9:19), *"he would break me with a storm"* (9:17), and the only
remedy an umpire who could *"lay his hand on us both"* (9:33). Here there is no umpire, and Job no
longer thinks he needs one. If he could only get into the room, God would *listen*, and an upright
man would win. The mediator of 9:33, 16:19 and 19:25 is not invoked; **the obstacle has moved from
God's power to God's absence.** See [[Mediation and Advocacy]] and
[[Who Will Contend with Me - Job 13, Isaiah 50, and Romans 8]].

*"Delivered forever from my judge"* — ***mišōpṭî*** — is the boldest phrase in it: the judge is God,
and Job expects to be acquitted *by* the judge *from* the judge.

**2. The four directions (23:8–9).**

> **8** “If I go east, he is not there; if west, I can’t find him;
> **9** He works to the north, but I can’t see him. He turns south, but I can’t catch a glimpse of
> him.

The Hebrew words are the body's: ***qedem***, *forward*; ***ʾāḥôr***, *behind*; ***śəmōʾl***,
*left*; ***yāmîn***, *right*. A man facing the sunrise has east before him, west behind, north on his
left and south on his right, and the WEB translates the compass. Job turns all the way round and
finds nobody.

**It is Psalm 139 turned inside out.** *"Where could I go from your Spirit? Or where could I flee
from your presence? If I ascend up into heaven, you are there. If I make my bed in Sheol, behold, you
are there! If I take the wings of the dawn, and settle in the uttermost parts of the sea, even there
your hand will lead me"* (Psalm 139:7–10). The psalmist goes in every direction and cannot get away
from God; Job goes in every direction and cannot get to him. Amos 9:2–4 is the psalm's frightening
version — no height or depth hides the guilty from God. Job has already lived that version — *"you
watcher of men"* (7:20) — and now complains of its opposite, and the two complaints do not
contradict each other: the God who is everywhere watching is nowhere to be addressed. See
[[Where Can I Go from Your Spirit - Job 23.8-10 and Psalm 139.7-12]].

**3. Gold (23:10–12).** And then, with no transition:

> **10** But he knows the way that I take. When he has tried me, I shall come out like gold.

**The man who cannot see God says that God sees him.** *"The way that I take"* is ***derek
ʿimmādî***, *the way that is with me*. *"When he has tried me"* is ***bəḥānanî***, from ***bāḥan***,
to assay metal — the verb Job used bitterly at 7:18, *"that you should … test him every moment"*.
Here the same verb is his confidence. **This is the one moment in the poem where Job names his
suffering as a test, and he is right.** The reader has known it since 1:12; Job has not been told
and is not told now. He reaches the prologue's category by himself, and he does not use it to
explain anything — he does not say *this is why I suffer* — but to predict the outcome: *I shall come
out like gold.* The epilogue keeps the prediction.

The image also answers Eliphaz, a chapter late. Eliphaz told Job to throw his gold into the wadi —
*"the gold of Ophir among the stones of the brooks. The Almighty will be your treasure"* (22:24–25)
— on the assumption that the gold was Job's god. **Job's answer is that the gold is Job**, and the
one assaying it is God. See [[I Shall Come Out Like Gold - Job 23.10 and the Refiner's Fire]].

Verses 11–12 give the assay's evidence, and answer Eliphaz again. *"My foot has held fast to his
steps. I have kept his way, and not turned away. I haven't gone back from the commandment of his lips.
I have treasured up the words of his mouth more than my necessary food."* Eliphaz had urged:
*"Please receive instruction from his mouth, and lay up his words in your heart"* (22:22). **Job
says he already has**, in Eliphaz's own words — *ʾimrê-pîw*, *the words of his mouth*, for Eliphaz's
*ʾămārāyw*, *his words* — and with a stronger verb: ***ṣāpantî***, *I have hidden, stored up as
treasure*, the verb of Psalm 119:11, *"I have hidden your word in my heart"*. **This is what
Eliphaz's second-person *return* (22:23) cannot reach:** a man who says he never left. *"More than
my necessary food"* is Deuteronomy 8:3 lived out before it was written: man does not live by bread
alone.

**4. Terror (23:13–17).** The confidence lasts three verses.

*"But he stands alone, and who can oppose him? What his soul desires, even that he does. For he
performs that which is appointed for me. Many such things are with him."* God is ***bəʾeḥād***,
literally *in one* — singular, without a counterpart, and without a court above him. He does what he
wants (Psalm 115:3 and 135:6 say the same thing in praise). And what he wants for Job is a ***ḥōq***,
a decree, *"appointed for me"*, with *"many such things"* still in store. **The same sovereignty that
made 23:10 possible makes 23:15 inevitable.** If God is the assayer, God is also the fire.

*"Therefore I am terrified at his presence. When I consider, I am afraid of him. For God has made my
heart faint. The Almighty has terrified me."* The word is not *yirʾâ*, the settled fear of God that
the narrator credited Job with at 1:1, but ***bāhal*** and ***paḥad***, panic and dread — the
*paḥad* Job said at 3:25 had come upon him. The man who wanted to find God's seat is afraid of what
would be in it. **The wish of 23:3 and the dread of 23:15 are the same wish**: Job wants God present,
and God present is terrifying, and Job does not withdraw the wish.

The last verse is among the most disputed in the book. The WEB's *"Because I was not cut off before
the darkness, neither did he cover the thick darkness from my face"* takes it as a lament that he
did not die before the darkness came — chapter 3 in one line. Others read *"Yet I am not silenced by
the darkness"* (ESV), Job refusing to be shut up by it. The darkness is ***ḥōšek*** and ***ʾōpel***,
and both readings put Job in it, still speaking. See the note below.

**5. Why doesn't the court sit? (24:1).** Chapter 24 opens with the question that unites the two
halves of the speech:

> **1** “Why aren’t times laid up by the Almighty? Why don’t those who know him see his days?

The *times* are ***ʿittîm***, appointed times of judgment, and *his days* are the days of reckoning —
the idea the prophets will call *the day of Yahweh*. Job's complaint in chapter 23 was that he could
not get a hearing for his case. Here it is that **nobody can** — that the docket of the universe has
no court dates, and *those who know him*, the righteous, never see them come.

**6. The catalogue of the oppressed (24:2–12).** What follows is the longest passage of social
description in the Wisdom literature, and it reads as an answer to Eliphaz's charge sheet
(22:6–9) without ever mentioning it.

| 24:2–12 | the crime | the law it breaks | Eliphaz's charge against Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| *"remove the landmarks"* (24:2) | land-grab by moving boundary stones | Deuteronomy 19:14; 27:17; Proverbs 22:28; 23:10 | — |
| *"violently take away flocks, and feed them"* (24:2) | open theft, grazed in plain sight | Exodus 22:1 | — |
| *"drive away the donkey of the fatherless"* (24:3) | the orphan's one working animal | Deuteronomy 24:17 | *"the arms of the fatherless have been broken"* (22:9) |
| *"take the widow's ox for a pledge"* (24:3) | the widow's plough-ox seized as collateral | Deuteronomy 24:6, 17 | *"You have sent widows away empty"* (22:9) |
| *"turn the needy out of the way"* (24:4) | the poor pushed off the road, out of court | Amos 2:7; 5:12 | — |
| *"pluck the fatherless from the breast, and take a pledge of the poor"* (24:9) | a nursing child seized for a debt | 2 Kings 4:1; Nehemiah 5:5 | *"taken pledges from your brother for nothing"* (22:6) |
| *"they go around naked without clothing"* (24:10; cf. 24:7) | the pledged cloak not returned | Exodus 22:26–27 | *"stripped the naked of their clothing"* (22:6) |
| *"Being hungry, they carry the sheaves"* (24:10) | harvesters starving among the grain | Deuteronomy 24:19; 25:4 | *"withheld bread from the hungry"* (22:7) |
| *"They tread wine presses, and suffer thirst"* (24:11) | pressers forbidden what they press | Deuteronomy 24:14–15 | *"You haven't given water to the weary to drink"* (22:7) |

**Every count of 22:6–9 is here, and it has a defendant.** Eliphaz invented the crimes to explain
Job's suffering; Job finds them being committed in the open, by people who are not suffering at all.
See [[Bread to the Hungry and Clothing to the Naked - Job 22.6-9, Job 31, and Matthew 25]].

The description of the victims (24:5–8) is unusually close and unusually tender. Driven from the
land, the poor live like ***pərāʾîm***, *wild donkeys*, foraging in the desert for something to feed
their children; they *"glean the vineyard of the wicked"* — the gleaning law of Leviticus 19:9–10
and Deuteronomy 24:19–21, kept, and kept by the wrong people; *"They lie all night naked without
clothing, and have no covering in the cold. They are wet with the showers of the mountains, and
embrace the rock for lack of a shelter."* The wild donkey has been Ishmael's untamed freedom
(Genesis 16:12) and Zophar's byword for the stupid (11:12); here it is what a man becomes when his
land is taken from him, and God will claim it as his own creature at 39:5–8. See
[[A Wild Donkey of a Man - Genesis 16.12 and Job 11.12]].

The harvest images of 24:10–11 are the sharpest. **The ox treading out grain may not be muzzled**
(Deuteronomy 25:4); **these men carry the sheaves hungry and tread the winepress thirsty.** The law
gives the animal more than the oppressor gives the labourer. And then the verse that ends the
catalogue:

> **12** From out of the populous city, men groan. The soul of the wounded cries out, yet God doesn’t
> regard the folly.

The *folly* is ***tiplâ***, the rare word of 1:22: *"In all this, Job did not sin, nor charge God with
wrongdoing (tiplâ)."* **In the prologue Job declined to attribute *tiplâ* to God. Here he says God
declines to attribute it to anyone** — *lōʾ-yāśîm tiplâ*, he does not *put down* the outrage, does
not charge it. It is not the charge the narrator said Job never made. It is something nearer the
bone: that the outrage is real, and God does not file it. Some ancient versions read ***təpillâ***,
*prayer* — *God does not hear their prayer* — which is one letter's difference and comes to the same
thing. See [[The Wager in Heaven and the Four Messengers]].

**7. The rebels against the light (24:13–17).** The scene turns from day to night and from the
oppressors of the field to the criminals of the city. *"These are of those who rebel against the
light. They don't know its ways, nor stay in its paths."* Three of them: the murderer who *"rises
with the light"* and is a thief by night; the adulterer whose eye *"waits for the twilight, saying,
'No eye shall see me.' He disguises his face"*; and the burglars who *"dig through houses"* in the
dark — through the mud-brick wall, the act Exodus 22:2 contemplates and the verb behind Matthew
6:19's thieves who *"break through"*. *"For the morning is to all of them like thick darkness"* —
***ṣalmāwet***, the deep darkness of 3:5 — *"for they know the terrors of the thick darkness."*

**They have inverted the first day of creation.** Light is their darkness and darkness is their
light; dawn is what they fear. John will say it of everyone: *"men loved the darkness rather than the
light; for their works were evil. For everyone who does evil hates the light"* (John 3:19–20). See
[[Light and Darkness]].

And the adulterer's *"No eye shall see me"* is answered by the speech itself, ten verses later: **God
does see** — *"His eyes are on their ways"* (24:23). This matters for the record, because Eliphaz had
just told Job that Job's creed was *"What does God know? Can he judge through the thick darkness?"*
(22:13). **Job's complaint is the opposite.** It is not that God cannot see the crimes in the dark.
It is that he sees them and does nothing. See [[Seeing and Being Seen]].

**8. The crux (24:18–24).** The last section is the hardest passage in the speech, because it
sounds like the friends:

> **18** “They are foam on the surface of the waters. Their portion is cursed in the earth. They
> don’t turn into the way of the vineyards.
> **19** Drought and heat consume the snow waters, so does Sheol those who have sinned.
> **20** The womb shall forget him. The worm shall feed sweetly on him. He shall be no more
> remembered. Unrighteousness shall be broken as a tree.

That is retribution: the wicked swept away like scum on a flood, their land cursed, swallowed by
[[Sheol]] as fast as heat swallows meltwater, forgotten by the womb that bore them. It is very hard to
square with *"God doesn't regard the folly"* twelve verses earlier, and readers have answered the
problem in four ways, none of them certain:

- **Job is quoting the friends** — *you say …* — and the quotation marks are unmarked, as they are
  elsewhere in Hebrew poetry (21:19 is probably one). Some translations add *You say* at 24:18.
- **Job is cursing them.** The verbs can be read as wishes: *let their portion be cursed, let Sheol
  take them.* The imprecation would be Job's demand that the court he cannot find should sit.
- **The text has been displaced.** The third cycle is broken — Bildad's speech is six verses, Zophar's
  is missing — and 24:18–24, with 27:13–23, is often reassigned to Zophar's lost third speech. The
  wiki records this as a hypothesis about the text, not a reading of it; no manuscript supports it.
- **Job means it, with a qualification.** 24:22–24 is the qualification: *"Yet God preserves the
  mighty by his power … God gives them security, and they rest in it. His eyes are on their ways.
  They are exalted; yet a little while, and they are gone. Yes, they are brought low, they are taken
  out of the way **as all others**, and are cut off as the tops of the ears of grain."* The wicked
  die, but they die like everybody else, after a lifetime of security God himself gave them — the
  argument of 21:23–26 again. Death is not a verdict; it is the harvest that takes everyone.

**The wiki reads 24:18–21 as most likely Job's imprecation or a citation of the friends' view, and
24:22–24 as Job's own answer to it**, because 24:22–24 is so plainly Job's voice — God *preserving*
the mighty, God giving them *security* — and because it ends where chapter 21 ended. The reading is a
judgment, not a finding. What is certain is the closing image: *"cut off as the tops of the ears of
grain"*. Eliphaz had promised the righteous a death *"like a shock of grain comes in its season"*
(5:26). Job's wicked come in at the same harvest. See
[[A Shock of Grain in Its Season - Eliphaz's Promise and the Epilogue]] and
[[The Prosperity of the Wicked]].

**9. The dare (24:25).** *"If it isn't so now, who will prove me a liar, and make my speech worth
nothing?"* The first words of the speech addressed to anyone, and they are addressed to the friends
as a challenge to produce counter-evidence. **Nobody does.** Bildad's reply (25:1–6) is six verses
on God's majesty and man's worthlessness — *"How much less man, who is a worm"* — that does not touch
one case in chapter 24. Zophar does not reply at all. The friends' side of the dialogue ends here,
in effect, without an answer to this chapter.

## People

- [[Job (person)]] — speaking, and for the first time since chapter 3 **addressing nobody** until
  the last verse. Does not deny Eliphaz's charges and does not accept his offer; asks for God's seat,
  says he would win there, says God is nowhere, says *"I shall come out like gold"*, and is terrified.
  Then turns from his own case to the world's and finds the crimes he was accused of committed by
  others, unpunished.
- [[God]] — *ʾēl*, *ʾĕlôah* and *šadday*, never Yahweh; spoken about in the third person throughout
  and never addressed — the second speech running (after chapter 21) in which Job does not once say
  *you* to him. Absent in every direction (23:8–9), yet knowing Job's way and assaying him (23:10);
  alone, unopposable, carrying out decrees (23:13–14); keeping no court days (24:1); seeing the
  wicked (24:23) and not *putting down* their outrage (24:12); giving the mighty their security
  (24:22–23).
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] — not named and not answered, but answered all the same: the *"words of his
  mouth"* treasured (23:12, against 22:22), the gold that is Job (23:10, against 22:24–25), the
  charges of 22:6–9 given real defendants (24:2–11), and a God who sees in the dark (24:23, against
  22:13).
- [[Bildad the Shuhite]] — silent; the dare of 24:25 is answered by his six verses (25:1–6), which do
  not take it up.
- [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — silent; and on one hypothesis the author of 24:18–24, displaced from
  his missing third speech.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — *"Drought and heat consume the snow waters, so does Sheol those who have sinned"*
  (24:19): Sheol as the swallower of the wicked, in the passage's most disputed section.

## Themes

- [[Suffering]] — Job's own (23:2, 15–17), and then for the first time in the dialogue the suffering
  of other people, described at length and with sympathy (24:4–12).
- [[Retribution]] — the doctrine refuted by observation (24:1–17) and then apparently restated
  (24:18–20); the crux, and the wiki's reading of it.
- [[Testing]] — *"When he has tried me, I shall come out like gold"* (23:10): the one place Job
  names his ordeal as a test, which the reader has known since 1:12.
- [[Integrity]] — *"I have kept his way, and not turned away"* (23:11): the claim the oath of chapter
  31 will make under curse.
- [[Walking with God]] — *"My foot has held fast to his steps"* (23:11), the walk of Enoch and Noah
  described by the walker, as a fact God knows.
- [[Righteousness]] — *"There the upright might reason with him"* (23:7): righteousness as a case
  that would win if it could be heard.
- [[Judgment]] — the court with no sitting days (24:1), and a judge from whom Job expects to be
  *"delivered forever"* (23:7).
- [[Mediation and Advocacy]] — 23:3–7, the hearing imagined for the first time without an umpire,
  witness or redeemer.
- [[Providence]] — *"he performs that which is appointed for me. Many such things are with him"*
  (23:14): sovereignty as the source of both hope and terror.
- [[The Fear of God]] — *bāhal* and *paḥad*, terror at his presence (23:15–16), as distinct from the
  *yirʾâ* of 1:1.
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — God invisible in every direction (23:8–9) and seeing Job's way (23:10);
  the adulterer's *"No eye shall see me"* (24:15) and *"His eyes are on their ways"* (24:23).
- [[Light and Darkness]] — the darkness Job is not cut off from (23:17) and the rebels against the
  light, to whom *"the morning is … like thick darkness"* (24:13–17).
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — the widow, the fatherless, the needy and the naked, described from their
  side for the first time in the book (24:3–12).
- [[Nakedness and Covering]] — *"naked without clothing, and have no covering in the cold"* (24:7,
  10), the pledged cloak not returned.
- [[Work]] — the starving harvesters and thirsty winepress-treaders of 24:10–11, worse off than the
  unmuzzled ox.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — the moved landmark (24:2) and the poor driven into the desert (24:4–5).
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — the child taken from the breast for a debt (24:9), and the labourers
  of 24:10–11.
- [[Sexual Sin]] — the adulterer at twilight with his face disguised (24:15).
- [[Life and Death]] — Sheol, the worm and the forgetting womb (24:19–20), and the wicked *"cut off
  as the tops of the ears of grain"* *"as all others"* (24:24).
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — *"my complaint"*, *śîḥî* (23:2), and *"men groan … The soul of the
  wounded cries out"* (24:12).
- [[Language and Speech]] — *"fill my mouth with arguments"* (23:4), the words of God's mouth
  treasured above food (23:12), and the dare to be proved a liar (24:25).

## Notes

- **Two chapters, one speech.** The pericope keeps chapters 23 and 24 together because the speech
  has one opening formula (*"Then Job answered"*, 23:1) and one closing dare (24:25), and because
  24:1 is the hinge that makes chapter 23's private question public. The two halves have different
  subjects and the same complaint: God is not to be found where a judge should be.

- **23:2: "rebellious" or "bitter"?** The WEB follows the Hebrew ***mərî***, *rebellion* — *even
  today my complaint is defiance*, Job owning what the friends call it. Many versions emend to ***mar***,
  *bitter*, the word of 3:20, 7:11 and 10:1 (KJV, ESV: *"my complaint is bitter"*). The second half
  has the same problem the other way: the Hebrew reads ***yādî***, *my hand* is heavy upon my
  groaning, and the WEB follows the Septuagint and Syriac's *his hand*. The wiki follows the WEB on
  both and records the alternatives.

- **23:12, "more than my necessary food".** The Hebrew ***mēḥuqqî*** is literally *more than my
  portion* or *more than my statute*; the Septuagint read ***bəḥêqî***, *in my bosom* — *I have hidden
  his words in my breast* — which is close to Psalm 119:11. *Ḥōq* returns two verses later, of what
  God has *"appointed for me"* (23:14): Job treasured God's words more than his *ḥōq*, and God
  carries out his *ḥōq* on Job.

- **23:13, "he stands alone".** ***Wəhûʾ bəʾeḥād*** is grammatically odd — *he is in one*. Some
  emend to ***bāḥar***, *he has chosen* (*"he has decided, and who can turn him?"*); others read it
  as *he is unique, he is one*, close in sense though not in form to the *"Yahweh is one"* of
  Deuteronomy 6:4. The WEB's *stands alone* takes the second line. Either way the point is that God
  has no peer and no appeal.

- **23:17.** The Hebrew is ***kî-lōʾ niṣmattî mippənê-ḥōšek ûmippānay kissâ-ʾōpel***. *Niṣmattî*
  can mean *I was destroyed / cut off* or *I was silenced*. The WEB reads a lament that death did not
  come first; the ESV and NIV read a defiance, *"Yet I am not silenced by the darkness, by the thick
  darkness that covers my face."* The wiki does not choose: the first reading continues chapter 3,
  the second anticipates *"Though he slay me"* (13:15), and both leave Job in the dark and talking.

- **Is chapter 24 in order?** The chapter has the worst textual condition in the book after chapters
  25–27, and many reconstructions rearrange it. The wiki reads it as it stands in the WEB, with the
  crux of 24:18–24 handled above. The landmark, pledge, gleaning and hired-labour laws it echoes are
  from Exodus and Deuteronomy, but **Job lives outside Israel, and the book never cites the Law** — it
  assumes the same moral order the Law codifies, as it did for Eliphaz's charge sheet.

- **The poor are not wicked here.** Every catalogue of the poor before this in the dialogue has been
  a catalogue of the wicked man's ruin — the friends' portraits end with the wicked impoverished
  (15:29; 18:12; 20:10, 15). In chapter 24 the poor are victims and nothing else, and they are given
  twelve verses of close attention. **It is the first time in the book anyone's suffering but Job's
  is described at length**, and it is Job who describes it — the man the friends had just accused
  of making the poor suffer.

- **Hidden, not absent.** The wiki's theme pages treat the *hiddenness of God* under
  [[Seeing and Being Seen]] rather than as a separate theme. Chapter 23 is its key text in the
  Wisdom literature, as Psalm 13 (*"How long will you hide your face from me?"*), Psalm 22:1–2 and
  Isaiah 45:15 (*"Most certainly you are a God who has hidden yourself"*) are elsewhere. If the theme
  recurs enough across the Psalms and Prophets to need its own page, this passage should anchor it.
