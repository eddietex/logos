# Elihu Speaks in Anger

**Book:** [[Job]]
**Reference:** Job 32:1–33:33

## Summary

*"So these three men ceased to answer Job, because he was righteous in his own eyes"* (32:1). Job has
sworn his oath and signed it — *"Let the Almighty answer me!"* (31:35) — and the next voice is not the
Almighty's. It is a man the book has not mentioned in thirty-one chapters, introduced with a fuller
pedigree than anyone else in it, and he is angry. [[Elihu the Buzite]] speaks for six chapters, four
speeches in all (headed at 32:6, 34:1, 35:1, 36:1), and nobody answers him: not Job, not the friends,
and not God, who begins at 38:1 as though he had not spoken. This page covers the prose introduction
(32:1–5) and the first speech (32:6–33:33), which is in two halves — a long preface to the friends
about why a young man has the right to speak at all (32:6–22), and then the speech proper, to Job by
name (33:1–33).

**1. The narrator's introduction (32:1–5).** Five verses of prose, the first since 2:13, and the first
narrative between the friends' speeches. They do three things.

*They close the dialogue.* The friends stop *"because he was righteous in his own eyes"* (32:1) —
which is the friends' reason, not the narrator's verdict; the narrator's verdict was given at 1:1 and
the reader has it. (The Septuagint and some other ancient versions read *"in their eyes"*, which would
make 32:1 say the opposite; the wiki follows the WEB and the Hebrew as received.)

*They name him in full.* *"Elihu the son of Barachel, the Buzite, of the family of Ram"* (32:2). No one
else in the book has a father's name. Job has none, nor any genealogy; the three friends have a town
apiece. Elihu has a name, a father, a clan, and a family. **The names are themselves a creed.**
*ʾĔlîhûʾ* means *He is my God*; *Barakʾēl*, *God blesses*. And *Buz* is the brother of Uz in
[[Nahor's Line and the Burial of Sarah]] — *"Uz his firstborn, Buz his brother"* (Genesis 22:21) — so
the one speaker with a pedigree is, by that pedigree, kin to Abraham and to Job's own country. See
[[Buz]] and [[Uz]]. *Ram* is unidentified; the Ram of Ruth 4:19, ancestor of David, is a different
line, and nothing connects them but the name.

*They make him angry four times.* *Wayyiḥar ʾap* — *"the wrath of Elihu … was kindled"* — at 32:2, twice
more in 32:2–3, and again at 32:5. Against Job, *"because he justified himself rather than God"* (32:2);
against the three friends, *"because they had found no answer, and yet had condemned Job"* (32:3).
Elihu's anger is the book's rough draft of God's: **at 42:7 God says *"My wrath is kindled against you,
and against your two friends"* with the same verb and noun**, and it falls on the friends only. Elihu
was angry at four men; God is angry at three, and Elihu is not among them, because Elihu is not
mentioned at all. (Rabbinic tradition counts 32:3 among the *tiqqune sopherim*, the "corrections of the
scribes", holding that the verse once read *"and yet had condemned God"* — the friends having made God
the guilty party by failing to answer Job — and was softened. The wiki records the tradition; the WEB
and the received text read *"Job"*.)

**2. The preface: age, spirit, and a full belly (32:6–22).** *"I am young, and you are very old;
therefore I held back, and didn't dare show you my opinion"* (32:6). Elihu has waited out of
deference (32:4, 11), and the deference was a mistake: *"I said, 'Days should speak, and multitude of
years should teach wisdom.' But there is a spirit in man, and the breath of the Almighty gives them
understanding. It is not the great who are wise, nor the aged who understand justice"* (32:7–9). This
is aimed precisely. Eliphaz claimed the authority of age — *"With us are both the gray-headed and the
very aged men, much elder than your father"* (15:10) — and Job had half-conceded it, *"With aged men is
wisdom, in length of days understanding"* (12:12), before taking it back in the next verse. Elihu
replaces age with *rûaḥ* and *nišmat šadday*, the spirit and **the breath of the Almighty** — the
breath of Genesis 2:7, which he will claim for himself in a moment (33:4). He is the book's first
speaker to say that wisdom is given rather than accumulated, which is half of what chapter 28 said;
the other half, that it belongs to God and is found in fearing him, Elihu does not say. See
[[Wisdom]] and [[A Man in Whom Is the Spirit of God]].

He then tells the friends what they did wrong: *"there was no one who convinced Job, or who answered
his words, among you"* (32:12). ***ʾÊn ləʾiyyôb mōkîaḥ*** — *convinced* is *mōkîaḥ*, the word Job used
at 9:33 for the **umpire** who does not exist: *"There is no umpire between us."* Job said there was
no *mōkîaḥ* between him and God; Elihu says there has been no *mōkîaḥ* for Job among the friends, and
proposes to be one. And he refuses their last line of retreat: *"Beware lest you say, 'We have found
wisdom, God may refute him, not man'"* (32:13) — which is exactly what the book is about to do. Elihu
forbids the friends to leave Job to God, and then God takes him.

The preface ends in the book's most self-conscious image. *"For I am full of words. The spirit within
me constrains me. Behold, my breast is as wine which has no vent; like new wineskins it is ready to
burst. I will speak, that I may be refreshed"* (32:18–20). The image is of fermentation — new wine in
a sealed skin, which bursts if not opened — and its New Testament counterpart is the saying of Matthew
9:17, *"the skins would burst"*. Elihu offers it as a sign of inspiration; read beside 15:2, where
Eliphaz scorned a wise man who would *"fill himself with the east wind"*, and 8:2's *"mighty wind"*, it
also describes a man full of *rûaḥ* in the other sense. Jeremiah's compulsion is the nearest parallel:
*"a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I am weary with holding it in, and I can't"* (Jeremiah 20:9).
Then, the promise of impartiality: *"Please don't let me respect any man's person, neither will I give
flattering titles to any man"* (32:21) — the charge Job laid against the friends at 13:8–10, that they
*"show partiality"* to God. See [[Language and Speech]].

**3. To Job, by name (33:1–7).** *"However, Job, please hear my speech, and listen to all my words"*
(33:1). **No friend has called Job by name in thirty chapters; Elihu does it at once**, and will do it
again at 33:31. *"See now, I have opened my mouth"* (33:2) — the phrase of 3:1, *"Job opened his
mouth"*, where the poetry began. And then his credentials, which are the opposite of Eliphaz's: not a
vision in the night (4:12–16) but the common origin of every man —

> *"The Spirit of God has made me, and the breath of the Almighty gives me life."* (33:4)

> *"Behold, I am toward God even as you are. I am also formed out of the clay. Behold, my terror shall
> not make you afraid, neither shall my pressure be heavy on you."* (33:6–7)

This is a precise answer to Job's two complaints about the trial he could not have. Job said God *"is
not a man, as I am, that I should answer him"* (9:32), and asked, *"Let his terror not make me
afraid"* (9:34; again at 13:21). **Elihu presents himself as the man-sized party Job asked for**:
formed of clay like Job (10:9, *"you have fashioned me as clay"*), with no terror to make him afraid,
standing *"toward God even as you are"*. He invites the case Job wanted to bring to God — *"If you can,
answer me. Set your words in order before me, and stand up"* (33:5), the *ʿārak* of 13:18 and 23:4,
*"I have set my cause in order"*. The offer is real and it is also the trouble: Job did not ask for a
man to argue with. He had three. See
[[You Have Fashioned Me as Clay - Job 10.8-12, Psalm 139, and the Potter]] and
[[Mediation and Advocacy]].

**4. The charge, in Job's own words (33:8–13).** *"Surely you have spoken in my hearing"* (33:8). Elihu
is the first speaker to **quote Job**, and he does it almost exactly:

| Elihu's quotation | What Job said |
|---|---|
| *"I am clean, without disobedience. I am innocent, neither is there iniquity in me"* (33:9) | a summary, not a quotation — *"I am blameless"* (9:21), *"I know that I am righteous"* (13:18), *"my prayer is pure"* (16:17); the word *clean* is Zophar's version of Job, *"I am clean in your eyes"* (11:4) |
| *"Behold, he finds occasions against me. He counts me for his enemy"* (33:10) | *"Why hide you your face, and hold me for your enemy?"* (13:24); *"He counts me among his adversaries"* (19:11) |
| *"He puts my feet in the stocks. He marks all my paths"* (33:11) | *"You also put my feet in the stocks, and mark all my paths"* (13:27), word for word |

33:10 keeps the pun the rabbis noticed at 13:24 — *ʾôyēb*, *enemy*, against *ʾiyyôb*, *Job*. And his
reply is the friends' reply in a single line: *"In this you are not just, for God is greater than
man"* (33:12). What is new is the next sentence. *"Why do you strive against him, because he doesn't
give account of any of his matters?"* (33:13) — Job's complaint, as Elihu hears it, is that God does
not answer. **Elihu's answer is that he does, and Job has not been listening.**

**5. God speaks twice: the dream and the sickbed (33:14–22).** *"For God speaks once, yes twice, though
man pays no attention"* (33:14) — a graded numerical saying, like Psalm 62:11's *"God has spoken once;
twice I have heard this"*. The first way is the dream: *"In a dream, in a vision of the night, when
deep sleep falls on men"* (33:15) — the second line of Eliphaz's night vision (4:13) repeated word for
word, *tardēmâ*, the deep sleep of Adam (Genesis 2:21) and of Abram (Genesis 15:12). God *"opens the
ears of men, and seals their instruction, that he may withdraw man from his purpose, and hide pride
from man. He keeps back his soul from the pit"* (33:16–18). It is Job's own complaint turned inside
out: *"you scare me with dreams, and terrify me through visions"* (7:14). Elihu agrees that God sends
the dreams, and says they are warnings meant to save. Genesis has the pattern already in
[[Abraham and Abimelech]]: God comes to Abimelech *"in a dream of the night"* (Genesis 20:3) to stop him
before he sins. See [[Warned in a Dream - Abimelech and Laban]].

The second way is pain. *"He is chastened also with pain on his bed, with continual strife in his
bones; so that his life abhors bread … His flesh is so consumed away, that it can't be seen. His bones
that were not seen stick out. Yes, his soul draws near to the pit"* (33:19–22). **This is Job's body
described** — the bones of 7:15 and 19:20, the appetite of 3:24 and 6:7 — and Elihu does not say so.
*Chastened* is *yākaḥ* again, the verb of *mōkîaḥ* (32:12): the umpire, the reproof, and the correction
are one word in this book. Eliphaz said it first and in one verse — *"happy is the man whom God
corrects"* (5:17) — and Elihu gives it its fullest form. **Suffering, on this account, is speech**: not
payment for sin already committed, which was the friends' doctrine, but a warning against the pride and
the sin that would otherwise follow. See
[[Do Not Despise the Chastening - Job 5.17, Proverbs 3.11-12, and Hebrews 12]], [[Suffering]], and
[[Retribution]].

**6. The angel, the interpreter, and the ransom (33:23–30).** Then the passage the book has been asking
for since chapter 9:

> *"If there is beside him an angel, an interpreter, one among a thousand, to show to man what is right
> for him; then God is gracious to him, and says, 'Deliver him from going down to the pit, I have found
> a ransom.'"* (33:23–24)

***Malʾāk mēlîṣ ʾeḥād minnî-ʾālep.*** *Malʾāk* is a messenger, and in a heavenly setting an angel.
*Mēlîṣ* is the word for Joseph's **interpreter** in Genesis 42:23 — *"there was an interpreter between
them"* — the go-between who carries speech across a gap neither party can cross; it is the root Job
used at 16:20, where the WEB's *"My friends scoff at me"* is *məlîṣay rēʿāy*, and where some readers
hear *my interpreters are my friends* — the friends as the advocates who failed. *One among a
thousand* is 9:3 answered: a man *"can't answer him one time in a thousand"*, and Elihu supplies the one
in a thousand who can speak for him. Every one of Job's legal wishes is gathered here: the umpire
(9:33), the witness *"in heaven"* who would *"maintain the right of a man with God"* (16:19–21), the
Redeemer who *"lives"* (19:25). See [[The Witness in Heaven]].

But Elihu's advocate does something Job's never did. Job's witness would prove him right; Elihu's
angel *"show[s] to man what is right for him"* — *yošrô*, his uprightness, his duty — and the man
repents. God then **finds a ransom**: *kōper*, the price paid to release a life forfeited, the word of
Exodus 21:30 and of Psalm 49:7, where *"none of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give God a
ransom for him"*. In Elihu's speech the ransom is not said to be paid by anyone; God says he has
*found* it. The restoration follows: *"His flesh shall be fresher than a child's. He returns to the days
of his youth"* (33:25) — the language of Naaman's healing, *"his flesh was restored like the flesh of a
little child"* (2 Kings 5:14), and of Psalm 103:5, *"your youth is renewed like the eagle's"*. He prays,
*"sees his face with joy"*, and God *"restores to man his righteousness"* (33:26). And then he
confesses, publicly:

> *"He sings before men, and says, 'I have sinned, and perverted that which was right, and it didn't
> profit me. He has redeemed my soul from going into the pit. My life shall see the light.'"*
> (33:27–28)

This is the confession the friends wanted from Job, given a better reason. The whole cycle — dream,
sickness, angel, ransom, confession — is repeated *"twice, yes three times, with a man, to bring back his
soul from the pit, that he may be enlightened with the light of the living"* (33:29–30), the *"light of
the living"* of Psalm 56:13. See
[[I Have Found a Ransom - Job 33.24, Psalm 49, and Mark 10.45]], [[Mediation and Advocacy]], and
[[Forgiveness]].

**7. "I desire to justify you" (33:31–33).** *"Mark well, Job, and listen to me. Hold your peace, and I
will speak. If you have anything to say, answer me. Speak, for I desire to justify you. If not, listen
to me. Hold your peace, and I will teach you wisdom"* (33:31–33). ***Ḥāpaṣtî ṣaddəqekā*** — *I desire to
justify you*, the piel of *ṣādaq*, the same verb as 32:2's charge that Job *"justified himself"*. **Elihu
was angry because Job declared himself in the right, and ends by offering to declare him in the right
himself** — on condition that he confess. Job does not take the offer, or answer at all. Elihu waits a
verse, and begins again at 34:1.

**How to read him.** Elihu is the book's open question. He is unannounced in the prologue, unanswered
in the dialogue, and unmentioned in the epilogue, which rebukes the three friends by name (42:7–9) and
does not include him. Readers have taken that silence both ways: as a sign that he spoke rightly and
needed no rebuke, or as a sign that his speeches are something the book sets aside — and, for many
modern scholars, as a sign that they were added later. On the evidence of this first speech, three things
are true at once. **He is a better reader of the dialogue than any of the friends**: he has listened to
Job (33:8–11), quotes him correctly, and answers the real complaint — that God is silent — rather than an
invented one. **He has something genuinely new to say**: suffering as warning rather than punishment,
and a heavenly mediator who brings a man back from the pit. And **he still begins from Job's guilt**:
the dream is sent to stop a man's *"purpose"* and *"pride"* (33:17), the sickbed ends in *"I have
sinned"* (33:27), and the reader knows from 1:8 and 2:3 that neither was the reason for Job's. Elihu is
the most sympathetic version of the wrong answer, and God, when he speaks, gives neither it nor its
opposite.

## People

- [[Elihu the Buzite]] — the speaker; introduced at 32:2 with father, clan, and family, angry four times,
  and speaking from 32:6 to the end of chapter 37 without a reply.
- [[Job (person)]] — addressed by name (33:1, 31), quoted (33:9–11), and invited to answer (33:5, 32). He
  does not.
- [[Eliphaz the Temanite]], [[Bildad the Shuhite]], [[Zophar the Naamathite]] — *"these three men"*, who
  *"ceased to answer Job"* (32:1), are rebuked by Elihu for condemning without an answer (32:3, 12), and
  are the audience of the preface (32:6–22). Eliphaz is answered in particular: his appeal to age (15:10)
  at 32:7–9, and his night vision (4:13) repeated at 33:15.
- [[God]] — *"the Almighty"*, whose breath gives understanding (32:8) and life (33:4); who *"speaks once,
  yes twice"* (33:14) by dream and by pain, is gracious, and finds a ransom (33:24).
- **The angel, the interpreter** (33:23) — a hypothetical figure in Elihu's conditional, *"If there is
  …"*; no page. See [[The Witness in Heaven]].
- **Barachel, Ram** — Elihu's father and the head of his family (32:2), named nowhere else in Job; no pages.
  See [[Elihu the Buzite]].

## Places

- [[Buz]] — Elihu's clan, *"the Buzite"* (32:2, 6); the brother-name of Uz in Genesis 22:21.
- [[Sheol]] — *the pit* (*šaḥat*), five times in 33:18–30, the thing the warning and the ransom keep a man
  from.

- [[Wisdom]] — given by *"a spirit in man"* (32:8), not by years (32:7–9); *"I will teach you wisdom"*
  (33:33).

## Notes

- **The fourth speaker.** Elihu is absent from the prologue, which introduces the three friends (2:11),
  and from the epilogue, which rebukes them (42:7–9); his speeches are framed by their own prose
  introduction (32:1–5), and he is never answered. Many modern scholars regard chapters 32–37 as a later
  addition to the book on these grounds and on grounds of style. Others read his isolation as
  deliberate: a speaker who is neither condemned nor endorsed, set between Job's last word and God's
  first. The wiki records both positions and reads the book as received.
- **"In his own eyes" (32:1).** The WEB follows the Hebrew; the Septuagint (and, by most accounts, the
  Syriac) reads *in their eyes*. Nothing on this page depends on choosing.
- **The scribal correction at 32:3.** Listed among the *tiqqune sopherim* in rabbinic tradition, with
  *God* as the supposed original object of *condemned*. Whether any of the listed passages were in fact
  altered is disputed. Recorded as tradition, not as the text.
- **33:16, "seals their instruction".** *Yaḥtōm*; the Septuagint and many modern commentators read
  *yəḥittēm*, *terrifies them*, which would echo 7:14 more closely. The WEB's reading is kept. Where
  [[The Arrows of the Almighty]] says that Elihu has God *terrify* a man in dreams, that is a fair summary
  of 33:15–18 on either reading, since the warning is meant to frighten; it is not a quotation.
- **33:18, "the sword".** *Šelaḥ* may be a weapon, or — as some read it, from its use at Nehemiah 3:15 —
  the channel or river crossed into death. The WEB's *sword* is kept.
- **"My pressure" (33:7).** *ʾEkpî*, a rare word, sounds like *kappəkā*, *your hand*, in Job's *"withdraw
  your hand far from me; and don't let your terror make me afraid"* (13:21). Many commentators take the
  echo as deliberate; the wiki notes it and builds nothing on it.
- **Who is the angel?** Elihu's *malʾāk mēlîṣ* is hypothetical — *"If there is …"* — and he never says
  whether such a figure exists, or who pays the ransom. Jewish interpretation has tended to read it of
  the angels and of a man's good deeds (one defender among a thousand accusers is a later rabbinic
  image built on this verse); Christian interpretation has read the mediator and the ransom toward 1
  Timothy 2:5–6. See [[The Witness in Heaven]] and
  [[I Have Found a Ransom - Job 33.24, Psalm 49, and Mark 10.45]].
- **Persons and places not paged.** Barachel and Ram are named only here; neither gets a page. [[Buz]] is
  given a place page, following [[Uz]], [[Shuah]] and [[Naamah]] — the homelands of the other speakers.
  Buz the son of Nahor (Genesis 22:21) still has no person page; if one is ever made, `AGENTS.md`'s
  person/place rule applies and both pages take qualifiers.
- **A misquotation corrected.** [[There Is No Umpire Between Us]] quoted 33:6 as *"I also am formed out
  of the clay"*; the WEB reads *"I am also formed out of the clay"*, and the page now does.
