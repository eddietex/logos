# The Song of Deborah

**Book:** [[Judges]]
**Reference:** Judges 5:1–31

## Summary

Judges 4 told the war against Jabin and Sisera in prose. Judges 5 tells it again as a song, and the two
accounts are set side by side without comment, as the crossing of the sea is told in prose and then
sung in Exodus 14–15. The Song is not a second report of the same facts. It adds a theophany, a roll of
the tribes that came and the tribes that stayed, the stars and the river fighting, a curse on a town,
a blessing on Jael, and a last scene the prose never visits: Sisera's mother at her window, waiting. See
[[Prose and Song - The Sea in Exodus 14-15 and the Kishon in Judges 4-5]].

**The heading (1).** *Then Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam sang on that day, saying*. The verb is
feminine singular, *wattāšar*, *and she sang* (WLC, `HVqw3fs`), though two people are named as its
subject. Hebrew can let a verb agree with the first of two subjects, so the form does not by itself prove
that Deborah composed the Song; it does put her first. The Song itself speaks in her voice: *until I,
Deborah, arose* (7).

**Bless Yahweh (2–3).** *Because the leaders took the lead in Israel, because the people offered
themselves willingly, be blessed, Yahweh!* (2). The opening phrase, *biprōaʿ pərāʿôt* (WLC), is
uncertain: WEB's *the leaders took the lead* is one reading; another, *when locks hung loose*, takes it
of warriors with unshorn hair, comparing the noun *peraʿ*, the Nazirite's uncut *locks* (Numbers 6:5,
WLC; OpenScriptures gives the noun a separate lemma, 6545, from the verb, 6544). That second reading
and the relation of the two words are reported, not verified. *Offered themselves willingly* is clear: *bəhitnaddēb*,
from *nādab*, the root of the free offering for the sanctuary, *everyone whom his spirit made willing*
(Exodus 35:21, 29; lemma 5068 in the WLC files read). The Song begins by thanking Yahweh for a people
who volunteered. Then it turns outward: *Hear, you kings! Give ear, you princes! I, even I, will sing to
Yahweh* (3). The audience is foreign rulers, the class Sisera's master belonged to.

**Yahweh comes from the south (4–5).** *Yahweh, when you went out of Seir, when you marched out of the
field of Edom, the earth trembled, the sky also dropped. Yes, the clouds dropped water. The mountains
quaked Yahweh's presence, even Sinai at the presence of Yahweh, the God of Israel.* The battle was in the
north, at the Kishon, but the Song begins with Yahweh setting out from Seir and Edom, far to the south,
and with storm and earthquake. *Zeh sînay* (5, WLC), *even Sinai* in WEB, is literally *this Sinai*;
some read it as a title, *the One of Sinai*, a reading reported here and not settled. Moses' blessing
opens the same way, *Yahweh came from Sinai, and rose from Seir to them* (Deuteronomy 33:2), and Psalm
68:7–8 repeats Judges 5:4–5 nearly word for word (see the connection). The water from the clouds may be
what fills the Kishon in 21; the Song does not draw the line itself. See
[[Yahweh Marched from Seir - Deuteronomy 33.2, Judges 5.4-5, and Psalm 68.7-8]].

**Before Deborah (6–8).** *In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways
were unoccupied. The travelers walked through byways* (6). Shamgar, who *saved Israel* in 3:31, is here
a date for a time when no one used the roads. Jael is named in the same breath, before her deed is told.
*The rulers ceased in Israel. They ceased until I, Deborah, arose; Until I arose a mother in Israel*
(7). *Pərāzôn* (WLC), WEB's *rulers*, occurs in the WLC files read only here and in 11; others take it as
*village life* or *peasantry*, and the sense is not settled. *Šaqqamtî* can be read *I arose*, as WEB
does, or as an old second-person feminine, *you arose*; reported, not verified. Either way the title is
the same: *ʾēm bəyiśrāʾēl*, *a mother in Israel*, not a commander or a judge. Then the cause: *They chose
new gods. Then war was in the gates. Was there a shield or spear seen among forty thousand in Israel?*
(8). The Song gives in one line the cycle of 2:11–15: other gods, then the enemy at the gate. And an
unarmed people, forty thousand without a shield.

**The call (9–12).** Deborah's heart is *toward the governors of Israel, who offered themselves willingly
among the people* (9), the root of 2 again. Three classes are summoned to tell it: *you who ride on white
donkeys, you who sit on rich carpets, and you who walk by the way* (10), the rich and the poor alike. The
place they will tell it is the watering place, *far from the noise of archers*, where *they will rehearse
Yahweh's righteous acts* (11). *Ṣidqôt yhwh* (WLC), *the righteous acts of Yahweh*, are his victories:
the Song calls the rescue *righteousness*. Then a cry within the Song: *Awake, awake, Deborah! Awake,
awake, utter a song! Arise, Barak, and lead away your captives, you son of Abinoam* (12). The four
*awake* are feminine imperatives (`HVqv2fs`, WLC).

**The roll of the tribes (13–18).** *Then a remnant of the nobles and the people came down. Yahweh came
down for me against the mighty* (13). The tribes are then named one by one, those who came first:

- *Ephraim*, *those whose root is in Amalek*; *after you, Benjamin*; *governors* out of *Machir*; *those
  who handle the marshal's staff* out of *Zebulun* (14);
- *the princes of Issachar were with Deborah. As was Issachar, so was Barak. They rushed into the valley
  at his feet* (15);
- *Zebulun was a people that jeopardized their lives to the death; Naphtali also, on the high places of
  the field* (18).

Between them stand those who did not come, each with a question: Reuben by his *watercourses*, with
*great resolves of heart* and then *great searchings of heart*, *Why did you sit among the sheepfolds? To
hear the whistling for the flocks?* (15–16); *Gilead lived beyond the Jordan*; *Why did Dan remain in
ships?*; *Asher sat still at the haven of the sea* (17). Ten names, six that came and four that stayed.
Judah, Simeon, Levi, and Gad are not named; if *Gilead* stands for Gad, as is often suggested, that is
a reading, not stated. The tribes are judged by one thing: did they come. Compare the tribal poems of
Genesis 49 and Deuteronomy 33, which bless the same tribes by name and set Zebulun, not Asher, *at the
haven of the sea* (Genesis 49:13). See
[[The Tribes Called by Name - Genesis 49, Deuteronomy 33, and Judges 5]].

**A difference from Judges 4, recorded.** The prose names two tribes, *Zebulun and Naphtali*, ten
thousand men (4:6, 10). The Song names six that came. The two are not said to conflict, and the Song
puts Zebulun and Naphtali last and highest, *to the death* (18), as the prose puts them alone. Whether
the others came to the battle itself or to the muster, the text does not say. The wiki records both
lists and does not harmonize them.

**The battle (19–22).** *The kings came and fought, then the kings of Canaan fought at Taanach by the
waters of Megiddo. They took no plunder of silver* (19). The prose has one king, Jabin, and his captain;
the Song has *kings*, and never names Jabin at all. *From the sky the stars fought. From their courses,
they fought against Sisera* (20). *The river Kishon swept them away, that ancient river, the river
Kishon* (21). The prose said *Yahweh confused Sisera* (4:15); the Song gives his agents as the sky and
the river. *Gərāpām*, *swept them away* (WLC, lemma 1640), occurs in the WLC files read only here.
Whether the chariots were caught by a flood, as the rain of 4–5 and the river of 21 suggest, the text
does not narrate; it is the common reading and the most natural one, and the wiki takes it as a reading.
Joshua 10:14 said *Yahweh fought for Israel* when stones fell from heaven at Gibeon; here the stars
fight. Then a half-line to herself, *My soul, march on with strength*, and the sound of hooves (22).

**Curse Meroz (23).** *'Curse Meroz,' said Yahweh's angel. 'Curse bitterly its inhabitants, because they
didn't come to help Yahweh, to help Yahweh against the mighty.'* Meroz is named nowhere else in the WLC
files read, and its site is unknown. It is not a tribe but a town, presumably Israelite, near enough to
have come. The angel who spoke at Bochim (2:1–5) speaks here a curse. The phrase *to the help of
Yahweh* (*ləʿezrat yhwh*, WLC) is bold: Yahweh is the one helped. Judges will deal twice more with those
who did not come, at Succoth and Penuel (8:5–17) and at Jabesh Gilead (21:8–12), each time with violence.
See [[Those Who Did Not Come - Meroz, Succoth and Penuel, and Jabesh Gilead]].

**Blessed Jael (24–27).** Against the curse, a blessing: *Jael shall be blessed above women, the wife of
Heber the Kenite; blessed shall she be above women in the tent* (24). *Təbōrak minnāšîm* (WLC): *blessed
from among women*, the comparative *min*. Then the killing, slower than in the prose: *He asked for
water. She gave him milk. She brought him butter in a lordly dish* (25). *She put her hand to the tent
peg, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer. With the hammer she struck Sisera. She struck through
his head. Yes, she pierced and struck through his temples* (26). Four verbs for one blow: *hālmâ*,
*struck*; *māḥăqâ*, *struck through*, which occurs in the WLC files read only here; *māḥăṣâ*, *pierced*;
*ḥālpâ*, *struck through* (WLC). *Māḥaṣ* is the verb of Balaam's star that *shall strike through the
corners of Moab* (Numbers 24:17) and of Yahweh's *I wound and I heal* (Deuteronomy 32:39). Then his
fall, three verbs twice and a fourth: *At her feet he bowed, he fell, he lay. At her feet he bowed, he
fell. Where he bowed, there he fell down dead* (27). *Kāraʿ nāpal šākab … šādûd* (WLC). The repetition
holds the moment, as if the poem will not let him finish falling. *Kāraʿ*, *bowed*, is also the
lion's *crouching* in Genesis 49:9 and Numbers 24:9.

**Asleep or standing — the tension from 4:21, weighed.** The prose says *he was in a deep sleep* (4:21).
The Song does not mention sleep and has him *bow* and *fall* *at her feet*, which some read as a man
standing, or drinking, when struck. The previous ingest left this open. Weighed here: the Song nowhere
says he was standing; *kāraʿ* in the files read describes a crouching or a collapse, not only a fall
from standing (Genesis 49:9; Judges 7:5–6); and the poem's three verbs repeated are its way of slowing
a death, not a stage direction. Read so, the two accounts differ in what they show and do not plainly
contradict. Read the other way, the Song tells a different killing, face to face. Both readings are
recorded on [[Jael]]; the wiki does not choose. *At her feet* (*bên raglêhā*, WLC) has also been read
with sexual or birth overtones; that is reported, not verified, and not adopted. The noun *regel*, *foot*,
runs through both chapters: Barak's men *went up at his feet* (*bəraglāyw*, 4:10, WLC; WEB *followed
him*), Issachar *rushed into the valley at his feet* (5:15), Sisera *fled away on his feet* (4:15, 17),
and he falls *at her feet* (5:27).

**Sisera's mother (28–30).** The Song ends where the prose never goes, in an enemy house. *Through the
window she looked out, and cried: Sisera's mother looked through the lattice. 'Why is his chariot so long
in coming? Why do the wheels of his chariots wait?'* (28). *Wattəyabbēb*, *and cried* (WLC, lemma 2980),
occurs in the WLC files read only here. Her *wise ladies* answer, *Yes, she returned answer to herself*
(29), with the explanation a general's mother would want: *Have they not found, have they not divided the
plunder? A lady, two ladies to every man; to Sisera a plunder of dyed garments …* (30). WEB's *a lady, two
ladies* softens the Hebrew, *raḥam raḥămātayim* (WLC), *a womb, two wombs*: the women of Israel counted by
the body, as plunder. The noun is the *womb* of Jacob's blessing on Joseph, *blessings of the breasts, and
of the womb* (Genesis 49:25; lemma 7356 in the WLC files read). So the Song's last picture of the enemy is
of men imagined dividing captured women, while the reader knows their captain lies dead at a woman's feet.
The scene is not mocking in tone; it is quiet, and it stops before she learns the truth. See
[[Sisera's Mother]] and [[Through the Window - Proverbs 7.6, Genesis 26.8, and Judges 5.28]].

**The close (31).** *So let all your enemies perish, Yahweh, but let those who love him be as the sun when
it rises in its strength.* *Kəṣēʾt haššemeš*, *as the going out of the sun* (WLC): the verb is the
*yāṣāʾ* of 4, *when you went out of Seir* (lemma 3318 both times). The Song opens with Yahweh going out
and closes with those who love him going out like the sun. That frame is a reading of the repeated verb,
not something the Song states. Psalm 19:5 also has the sun *like a strong man rejoicing to run his
course* (WEB, checked at bible-api.com). Then the narrator's formula, held back since 4:24: *Then the land
had rest forty years.* See [[The Land Had Rest - Joshua 11.23, 14.15, and the Judges Formula]].

**What the Song adds to the story.** Three things the prose did not say. First, the victory was
Yahweh's from the start: he set out from Seir, and the sky and the river fought. Second, the war was a
test of the tribes, and some failed it: the Song praises those who *offered themselves willingly* and
shames or curses those who sat still. Third, it gives Jael a verdict the prose withheld: *blessed above
women*. Whether that verdict settles the moral question of a host killing a guest, the Song does not
argue; it blesses. The Song says nothing of Jabin, of Deborah's palm, of Kedesh, or of Tabor; the prose
says nothing of Meroz, of Reuben, or of the stars.

## Notes

**Text and translation.** The Song is among the hardest Hebrew poems in the Old Testament, and many of
its lines are read differently by different translations. The wiki follows WEB throughout and flags, at
the point of each claim, where the Hebrew is uncertain. Where this page says a word occurs *only here*,
the claim is limited to the WLC files read for this ingest: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers,
Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, and Psalms.

**"She sang."** *Wattāšar dəbôrâ ûbārāq* (1, WLC). Exodus 15:1 has the other order and number: *ʾāz
yāšîr mōšeh ûbənê yiśrāʾēl*, *then Moses and the children of Israel sang*, a masculine singular verb with
Moses first (WLC). In both, the verb agrees with the leader named first.

**"Offered themselves willingly."** *Bəhitnaddēb* (2) and *hammitnaddəbîm* (9), hitpael of *nādab*
(WLC, lemma 5068). In the files read, the qal is the freewill giving of Exodus 25:2; 35:21, 29; the
hitpael appears only in the Song.

**"Hear, you kings."** *Šimʿû məlākîm* (3, WLC). The kings addressed are presumably foreign; the Song
does not say whose.

**"This Sinai."** *Zeh sînay* (5). Psalm 68:9 (Hebrew numbering; 68:8 in WEB) has the same two words,
*mippənê ʾĕlōhîm zeh sînay* (WLC), which WEB renders *the God of Sinai*. Judges 5:5 and Psalm 68:8–9
(Hebrew) also share *bəṣēʾtəkā*, *bəṣaʿdəkā*, *ʾereṣ rāʿāšâ*, and *šāmayim nāṭəpû* (WLC). Psalm 68 has
*ʾĕlōhîm* where Judges has *yhwh*.

**"A mother in Israel."** *ʾĒm bəyiśrāʾēl* (7, WLC). Deborah is not called anyone's mother in Judges
4; the title is for her role toward the people.

**"New gods."** *Yibḥar ʾĕlōhîm ḥădāšîm* (8, WLC): a singular verb, *he* or *one chose*. WEB's *they
chose* takes Israel as the subject. Some read *ʾĕlōhîm* as the subject, *God chose new things* or *new
leaders*; that reading is reported, not verified. WEB's sense is followed.

**"Yahweh came down for me."** *Yərad* in 13 (twice, WLC) is parsed by OpenScriptures under lemma 7287,
*rādâ*, *to rule*, not *yārad*, *to go down*, which is the verb in 14 (*yārədû*, lemma 3381). WEB reads
*came down* in 13 as in 14. Whether 13 means *came down* or *ruled*, and whether the vowels are original,
is disputed; not settled here.

**The marshal's staff.** *Mōškîm bəšēbeṭ sōpēr* (14, WLC): literally *those who draw with the staff of a
scribe* or *of a muster officer*. WEB's *marshal's staff* is a translation choice.

**Reuben's heart.** *Ḥiqqəqê lēb*, *resolves of heart* (15), and *ḥiqrê lēb*, *searchings of heart* (16)
(WLC): one consonant different. Reuben resolved, and then deliberated, and did not come.

**Dan in ships.** *Wədān lāmmâ yāgûr ʾŏniyyôt* (17, WLC). Dan's lot in Joshua 19:40–46 ran *over against
Joppa*, though Judges 1:34 has the Amorites pressing Dan into the hill country. Whether Dan *in ships*
means sailors, hired labor at a port, or a tribe already in the north, the text does not say.

**"They took no plunder of silver"** (19). The kings came for plunder and took none. Sisera's mother, in
30, imagines the plunder they are dividing.

**"From their courses."** *Mimməsillôtām* (20, WLC). *Məsillâ* is a highway or raised road. It is not
the word of *the highways were unoccupied* in 6, which is *ʾŏrāḥôt* (WLC), but the image is close: the
stars keep to their roads while Israel's were empty. That contrast is a reading.

**Butter.** *Ḥemʾâ* (25, WLC), *curds* or *butter*; *bəsēpel ʾaddîrîm*, *in a bowl of nobles* (WLC). The
*ʾaddîrîm* are the *nobles* of 13, and the *mighty* of the Song's battle are *gibbôrîm* (13, 23).

**"Workmen's hammer."** *Halmût ʿămēlîm* (26, WLC), not the *maqqebet* of 4:21. Both are tools of the
tent.

**"Fell down dead."** *Šādûd* (27, WLC), a passive participle of *šādad*, *to destroy*: *there he fell,
destroyed*. WEB's *dead* is a translation choice.

**The womb.** *Raḥam raḥămātayim ləroʾš geber* (30, WLC), *a womb, two wombs, to the head of each man*.
The dual *raḥămātayim* is lemma 7361 in OpenScriptures, a separate entry from the singular *raḥam*
(7356). The crudeness is in the Hebrew; WEB smooths it.

**What the prose and the Song share.** Deborah, Barak son of Abinoam, Sisera, Jael wife of Heber the
Kenite, the river Kishon, Zebulun and Naphtali, the water asked and the milk given, the tent peg and
the hammer, the temple struck, the rest of the land. What each has alone is listed at the end of the
Summary.
