# The Fleece

**Book:** [[Judges]]
**Reference:** Judges 6:33–40

## Summary

The previous pericope ended in Ophrah, with a new name for Gideon and a challenge to Baal (6:32). This
one opens on the enemy, gathered for the first time in a single place:

> **6:33** Then all the Midianites and the Amalekites and the children of the east assembled themselves together; and they passed over, and encamped in the valley of Jezreel.

**The muster (6:33–35).** The three raiders of 6:3 are named again, now *assembled … together*. They
cross over, presumably the Jordan from the east, though the verse does not name a river, and camp in
*the valley of Jezreel*. Until now the wiki knew that valley only as the place of the Canaanites' iron
chariots (Joshua 17:16). Against this army, *Yahweh's Spirit came on Gideon*. The Hebrew does not use
the verb of Othniel's verse (3:10). It says the Spirit *clothed* Gideon (see the Notes). He blows the
ram's horn, as Ehud did in the hill country of Ephraim (3:27), and *Abiezer was gathered together to
follow him*. His own clan comes first, though the men of his town had demanded his death the morning
before (6:30). Then messengers go *throughout all Manasseh*, and to Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali. Barak
had called up Zebulun and Naphtali (4:10). Asher had *sat still at the haven of the sea* when Deborah
called (5:17), and this time it comes.

**The first fleece (6:36–38).** With an army gathered, Gideon asks for a sign:

> **6:36** Gideon said to God, “If you will save Israel by my hand, as you have spoken,

> **6:37** behold, I will put a fleece of wool on the threshing floor; if there is dew on the fleece only, and it is dry on all the ground, then shall I know that you will save Israel by my hand, as you have spoken.”

He quotes his commission back twice, *as you have spoken*. Yahweh had said *Go in this your might, and
save Israel from the hand of Midian* (6:14) and *Surely I will be with you* (6:16). Gideon's version
adds *by my hand*. The test is set on *the threshing floor*, the open place where grain was beaten out.
When the story began, Gideon was beating wheat in a winepress instead, *to hide it from the Midianites*
(6:11). The first test could be explained without God: a heap of wool holds moisture longer than
bare ground. The text does not raise that point, but the second test reverses the conditions, which
suggests Gideon thought of it. In the morning he *rose up early*, wrung out the fleece, and got *a bowl
full of water*.

**The second fleece (6:39–40).** Then he asks again, and his preface is Abraham's at Sodom:

> **6:39** Gideon said to God, “Don’t let your anger be kindled against me, and I will speak but this once. Please let me make a trial just this once with the fleece. Let it now be dry only on the fleece, and on all the ground let there be dew.”

Abraham, bargaining for Sodom, began with *Oh don't let the Lord be angry, and I will speak* and ended
with *I will speak just once more* (Genesis 18:30, 32). The Hebrew of Gideon's request matches
Abraham's last petition almost word for word. See
[[Don't Let Your Anger Be Kindled - Genesis 18.30-32 and Judges 6.39]]. The two men use the formula
for different things. Abraham is pleading for a city. Gideon is asking for proof. The verb he uses for
it is *test*: *let me make a trial*, *ʾănassê* (WLC). That is the verb Judges used for Yahweh testing
Israel by the nations left in the land (2:22; 3:1, 4), and the verb of Massah, *You shall not tempt
Yahweh your God* (Deuteronomy 6:16). Gideon knows he is asking for something risky, and that is why he
begins by asking God not to be angry. God is not said to be angry. *God did so that night*, and the
fleece is dry while the ground is wet.

**What the narrator does and does not say.** There is no rebuke, no comment, and no word from God.
The sign is given, twice, in silence. Nothing in the text condemns the fleece, and nothing in it
praises the fleece either. Two readings are common and both have support in the text. In the first,
the fleece is a fault indulged: Gideon has a promise (6:14, 16), a sign already given in fire (6:21),
and the Spirit on him (6:34), and he still asks twice, calling it a test, in the vocabulary of
Massah. In the second, it is a weak man asking for help and being given it: he asks permission, he
names his fear of God's anger, and God answers each time without complaint, as he answered the first
sign request (6:17–21). The wiki records both readings and does not choose between them. The rest of
Gideon's story continues the pattern. Before the battle God offers him a third sign without being
asked, *if you are afraid to go down* (7:10, stored WEB; not yet ingested), and the dream of the barley
loaf follows.

**God, not Yahweh.** Through verses 36–40 Gideon speaks *to God*, *hāʾĕlōhîm*, and it is *ʾĕlōhîm* who
does it (40), though verse 34 named *Yahweh's Spirit* (all WLC). The call (6:11–24) and the altar scene
(6:25–32) spoke mostly of *Yahweh*. The narrator does not explain the change, and the wiki does not
build an interpretation on it. It is recorded here because the fleece is the only scene so far in
Gideon's story where the divine name is entirely absent.

**How the pericope sits in Gideon's story.** The army is gathered before Gideon is reassured, so the
fleece is not a condition for the muster but something asked after it. And the request turns on *my
hand*. The next pericope answers it. Yahweh sends most of the army home, *lest Israel brag against me,
saying, "My own hand has saved me"* (7:2, stored WEB; not yet ingested). Gideon wants to know that God
will save Israel *by my hand*. God answers by making sure no one could say Israel's own hand did it.

## Key verses

> **6:33** Then all the Midianites and the Amalekites and the children of the east assembled themselves together; and they passed over, and encamped in the valley of Jezreel.

> **6:34** But Yahweh’s Spirit came on Gideon, and he blew a trumpet; and Abiezer was gathered together to follow him.

> **6:36** Gideon said to God, “If you will save Israel by my hand, as you have spoken,

> **6:37** behold, I will put a fleece of wool on the threshing floor; if there is dew on the fleece only, and it is dry on all the ground, then shall I know that you will save Israel by my hand, as you have spoken.”

> **6:38** It was so; for he rose up early on the next day, and pressed the fleece together, and wrung the dew out of the fleece, a bowl full of water.

> **6:39** Gideon said to God, “Don’t let your anger be kindled against me, and I will speak but this once. Please let me make a trial just this once with the fleece. Let it now be dry only on the fleece, and on all the ground let there be dew.”

> **6:40** God did so that night; for it was dry on the fleece only, and there was dew on all the ground.

## Notes

**"Came on Gideon."** *Wərûaḥ YHWH lābəšâ ʾet-gidʿôn* (34, WLC). *Lābaš* (3847) is the ordinary verb for
putting on clothes, and *ʾet-gidʿôn* is its object: the Spirit *put on* Gideon, or *clothed itself with*
him. In the WLC of Judges, *lābaš* occurs only here. The Spirit verses of Judges are 3:10 (Othniel) and
11:29 (Jephthah), with *wattəhî … rûaḥ YHWH*, *came to be on*; 14:6, 19 and 15:14 (Samson), with
*ṣālaḥ*; and 13:25, where the Spirit *began to move* Samson, *wattāḥel … ləpaʿămô* (WLC).
WEB's *came on* covers several of these. The same use of *lābaš* with *rûaḥ* is commonly cited at
1 Chronicles 12:18 and 2 Chronicles 24:20; those books are not stored, and the occurrence there is
reported, not checked. Whether *clothed* means the Spirit covered Gideon like a garment or filled him
like a body is a question of interpretation the grammar does not settle.

**"He blew a trumpet."** *Wayyitqaʿ baššôpār* (34, WLC), word for word the phrase of Ehud's muster
(3:27). *Šôpār* is the ram's horn. Gideon will give his three hundred *šôpārôt* in 7:16–22 (by
reference). See [[Trumpet and Ram's Horn - Two Instruments, One English Word]].

**"Was gathered together."** *Wayyizzāʿēq ʾăbîʿezer ʾaḥărāyw* (34) and *wayyizzāʿēq gam-hûʾ ʾaḥărāyw*,
of Manasseh (35, WLC). The verb is *zāʿaq* (2199) in the niphal, *was called out* or *rallied*. It is
the verb of Israel's cry in this chapter: *the children of Israel cried to Yahweh*, *wayyizʿăqû* (6:6;
also 6:7, WLC). It is also the verb of Barak calling up Zebulun and Naphtali, *wayyazʿēq* (4:10, hiphil,
WLC). Israel *cried out* to Yahweh, and now the men of Abiezer and Manasseh are *called out* after
Gideon. The connection is in the Hebrew. WEB's *gathered together* does not show it.

**"Came up to meet them."** *Wayyaʿălû liqrāʾtām* (35, WLC). *Them* is plural and unexplained. It may be
Gideon's men or the messengers. The text does not say where the tribes met.

**"Save Israel by my hand."** *ʾIm-yeškā môšîaʿ bəyādî ʾet-yiśrāʾēl kaʾăšer dibbartā* (36), and in 37
*tôšîaʿ bəyādî ʾet-yiśrāʾēl kaʾăšer dibbartā* (WLC). *Yāšaʿ* (3467) in the hiphil, the verb of the
commission, *wəhôšaʿtā ʾet-yiśrāʾēl*, *save Israel* (6:14, WLC). In 6:14 Gideon was to save Israel *from
the hand of Midian*, *mikkap midyān*; here Gideon speaks of God saving it *by my hand*, *bəyādî*. In 7:2
Yahweh warns against Israel saying *yādî hôšîʿâ lî*, *my hand has saved me* (WLC). The three verses
together are the wiki's reading, and none of them refers to the others.

**"A fleece of wool."** *Gizzat haṣṣemer* (37, WLC). *Gizzâ* (1492) is a shorn fleece, from *gāzaz*,
*shear*. In the WLC of Genesis through Deuteronomy and Judges, the word occurs only in Judges 6:37–40,
seven times. *Goren* (1637), *threshing floor*, occurs in the WLC of Judges only in 6:37. In 6:11
Gideon was threshing in a *gat*, *winepress* (WLC).

**"Dew."** *Ṭal* (2919), four times (37–40, WLC). In the stored books dew is a sign of blessing: Isaac
blesses Jacob with *the dew of the sky* (Genesis 27:28), and Moses' blessing speaks of *the dew* for
Joseph (Deuteronomy 33:13) and of a heaven that *drops down dew* (33:28). Dew also came before the manna
(Exodus 16:13–14). The WLC occurrences in the stored books are these, Genesis 27:39, and Deuteronomy
32:2. In the fleece, dew is a sign only in the sense Gideon sets: wet on one, dry on the other. The text
does not use it as a symbol of blessing, and any symbolic reading belongs to the reader.

**"A bowl full of water."** *Məlôʾ hassēpel māyim* (38, WLC). *Sēpel* (5602) is a bowl. The word occurs
in the WLC of Judges only once more, at 5:25, the *lordly dish* in which Jael *brought him butter*
(stored WEB). *Wayyāzar*, *pressed together* (2115), and *wayyimeṣ*, *wrung out* (4680), are
both rare verbs (WLC). How rare was not counted.

**"Don't let your anger be kindled against me."** *ʾAl yiḥar ʾappəkā bî waʾădabbərâ ʾak happaʿam* (39,
WLC). Genesis 18:32: *ʾal nāʾ yiḥar laʾdōnāy waʾădabbərâ ʾak happaʿam* (WLC). The last four words are the
same in both. Abraham says *let it not burn for the Lord*, in the third person; Gideon says *let your
anger not burn against me*, in the second, and adds *ʾappəkā*, *your anger*. Genesis 18:30 has the
first half without *ʾak happaʿam*. See
[[Don't Let Your Anger Be Kindled - Genesis 18.30-32 and Judges 6.39]].

**"Let me make a trial."** *ʾĂnassê-nnāʾ raq-happaʿam baggizzâ* (39, WLC). *Nāsâ* (5254) in the piel, *to
test, try, put to the proof*. In the WLC of Judges it occurs four times: Yahweh testing Israel by the
nations (2:22; 3:1, 4), and Gideon testing with the fleece (6:39). In the stored Torah, *nāsâ* has God as
tester (Exodus 15:25; 16:4; 20:20; Deuteronomy 8:2, 16; 13:3 WEB, 13:4 WLC) and Israel as tester of God
(Exodus 17:2, 7; Deuteronomy 6:16), and Deuteronomy 33:8 has God *proving* Levi's godly one at Massah
(all WLC). Gideon's object is the fleece, *baggizzâ*, *with the fleece*, not God. Whether that makes his
request different in kind from Massah is a question of interpretation. Exodus 17:7 says Israel asked
*Is Yahweh among us, or not?* Gideon asks whether God will save *by my hand*. The comparison is the
wiki's, and the text does not make it.

**"Just this once," twice.** *ʾAk happaʿam* and *raq-happaʿam* (39, WLC). Gideon promises it will be the
last time twice in one verse.

**Why the second test.** That a fleece would hold dew while the ground dried is often given as the
reason Gideon reversed the test. The text does not give a reason, and the explanation is reported here,
not checked.

**Judgment calls.** The boundary was kept as planned: the muster (33) begins a new movement after the
naming (32), and 7:1, *Then Jerubbaal … rose up early*, starts the next scene at the spring of Harod.
The muster and the fleece stay in one pericope because the fleece is asked once the army has
gathered. No page is made for *the threshing floor*, whose location the text does not give, or for the
messengers. The new connection to Genesis 18 is made because the Hebrew phrase was checked. The verb
*nāsâ* is added to the Massah connection rather than given a connection of its own.
