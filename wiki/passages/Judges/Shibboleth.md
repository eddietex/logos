# Shibboleth

**Book:** [[Judges]]
**Reference:** Judges 12:1–7

## Summary

Right after the vow and the custom of mourning, *the men of Ephraim were gathered together* (12:1). They
cross the Jordan, come to Jephthah, and make Gideon's complaint over again: *Why did you pass over to fight
against the children of Ammon, and didn't call us to go with you?* This time they add a threat: *We will
burn your house around you with fire!*

Jephthah does not soothe them as Gideon did. He tells them they are wrong. *When I called you, you didn't
save me out of their hand* (2). Then *I put my life in my hand, and passed over*, and *Yahweh delivered
them into my hand. Why then have you come up to me today, to fight against me?* (3). He musters *all the
men of Gilead* and fights Ephraim (4). The verse adds a reason, an insult from Ephraim: *You are fugitives
of Ephraim, you Gileadites, in the middle of Ephraim, and in the middle of Manasseh.*

Gilead takes the fords of the Jordan (5). Every Ephraimite who tries to cross home is asked *Are you an
Ephraimite?* If he says no, they tell him to say *Shibboleth*. He says *Sibboleth*, *for he couldn't manage
to pronounce it right*, and they kill him at the fords. *At that time, forty-two thousand of Ephraim fell*
(6). Then the account closes in the form of the short judge notices: *Jephthah judged Israel six years.
Then Jephthah the Gileadite died, and was buried in the cities of Gilead* (7).

It is the first war in Judges between Israelite tribes, and the deliverer's last act before his death notice
is that war.

**The complaint and the threat (12:1).**

> **12:1** The men of Ephraim were gathered together, and passed northward; and they said to Jephthah, "Why did you pass over to fight against the children of Ammon, and didn't call us to go with you? We will burn your house around you with fire!"

*Were gathered together*, *wayyiṣṣāʿēq* (WLC), the niphal of *ṣāʿaq*, *to cry out*: they were *called out*.
In the singular it is used in Judges at 7:23, 7:24 (Ephraim, at Gideon's summons) and here. The tribe that
answered a call in chapter 7 now comes out on its own. See
[[The Fords of the Jordan - Joshua 2, Judges 3, 7, and 12]].

*Passed northward*, *wayyaʿăbōr ṣāpônâ* (WLC). The OpenScriptures tagging, followed by the WEB, reads the
common noun *north* with the ending that marks direction. The same consonants could also be read as *to
Zaphon*, the Gadite town in the Jordan valley named at Joshua 13:27 (*wəṣāpôn*, WLC). The wiki records that
possibility and does not decide it. See [[Zaphon]].

*Why did you pass over*, *maddûaʿ ʿābartā* (WLC). The verb is *ʿābar* again. It came four times in 11:29–32
for Jephthah's crossing toward Ammon. Now Ephraim crosses (*wayyaʿăbōr*) to complain about his crossing,
and in verse 5 it is Ephraim asking to cross back, *ʾeʿĕbōrâ*, *let me go over*.

*Didn't call us to go with you*. Ephraim said nearly the same to Gideon: *Why have you treated us this way,
that you didn't call us when you went to fight with Midian?* (8:1). Both times Ephraim complains after the
battle, about honor and the fight it did not share. Gideon had in fact called it, late, to take the waters
(7:24).

*We will burn your house around you with fire*, *bêtəkā nisrōp ʿālêkā bāʾēš* (WLC). The form *nisrōp*, *we
will burn*, is in the WLC of Genesis–Judges only here and at 14:15, where the Philistines tell Samson's
wife *lest we burn you and your father's house with fire*. At 15:6 they carry it out. See
[[We Will Burn Your House - Judges 12.1, 14.15, and 15.6]]. The house they threaten is the one his
daughter came out of in 11:34. The wiki notes that without claiming the narrator meant it.

**Jephthah's reply (12:2–3).**

> **12:2** Jephthah said to them, "I and my people were at great strife with the children of Ammon; and when I called you, you didn't save me out of their hand.

> **12:3** When I saw that you didn't save me, I put my life in my hand, and passed over against the children of Ammon, and Yahweh delivered them into my hand. Why then have you come up to me today, to fight against me?"

*At great strife*, *ʾîš rîb hāyîtî … məʾōd* (WLC), literally *a man of strife was I*. The noun *rîb*
occurs in Judges only here. The verb has been used through the book: Gideon's name *Jerub-Baal*, *Let Baal
contend against him* (6:32); Ephraim's quarrel with Gideon, *wayrîbûn*, *they rebuked him sharply* (8:1);
and Jephthah's question about Balak, *Did he ever strive against Israel?* (11:25). Jephthah calls his war
with Ammon a *rîb*, a dispute. He argued it like one (11:12–28). Now a second *rîb* is coming, with
Ephraim.

*When I called you*, *wāʾezʿaq ʾetkem* (WLC). The verb is *zāʿaq*, the cry Israel raises to Yahweh in
distress (3:9, 15; 6:6–7; 10:10). Yahweh's answer in 10:14 was *Go and cry to the gods which you have
chosen. Let them save you*. Jephthah tells Ephraim *you didn't save me*, *wəlōʾ hôšaʿtem* (2), and *you
didn't save me*, *ʾênəkā môšîaʿ*, *you were no savior* (3). It is the language of 10:12–14 used of one
tribe toward another.

The book never narrates this call. Between 10:17 and 11:40 Ephraim is not named. Jephthah's message went to
Ammon's king, and his march went through Gilead and Manasseh (11:29). Ephraim's claim and Jephthah's
counter-claim stand side by side, and the narrator does not say which is true. Ammon had already attacked
*the house of Ephraim* west of the river (10:9), so Ephraim had its own reason to answer a call.

*I put my life in my hand*, *wāʾāśîmâ napšî bəkappî* (WLC), *I set my soul in my palm*. The phrase does not
occur elsewhere in the WLC of Genesis–Judges. The wiki's [[Though He Slay Me]] page links it to Job 13:14
and to 1 Samuel 19:5 and 28:21 as the idiom of mortal risk; those three verses have not been checked in
Hebrew in this run.

*Yahweh delivered them into my hand*, *wayyittənēm YHWH bəyādî* (WLC). This is 11:32, *wayyittənēm YHWH
bəyādô*, now in the first person. It is also his own condition from 11:9 and 11:30. Jephthah's defense is
that Yahweh gave the victory, which is the same point Gideon made in 8:3, *God has delivered into your hand
the princes of Midian*. Gideon gave the credit to Ephraim. Jephthah keeps it.

*Why then have you come up to me today, to fight against me?* He answers Ephraim's *why* with his own *why*.
In 11:12 he asked Ammon's king *What have you to do with me, that you have come to me to fight against my
land?* He meets Ephraim with the same kind of question. With Ammon he sent two embassies. With Ephraim
there is one speech, and then war.

**The battle and the insult (12:4).**

> **12:4** Then Jephthah gathered together all the men of Gilead, and fought with Ephraim. The men of Gilead struck Ephraim, because they said, "You are fugitives of Ephraim, you Gileadites, in the middle of Ephraim, and in the middle of Manasseh."

*Because they said*, *kî ʾāmərû* (WLC). The subject is not stated, but the words are Ephraim's: *pəlîṭê
ʾeprayim ʾattem gilʿād*, *fugitives of Ephraim are you, Gilead*. Ephraim mocks Gilead as runaways from
Ephraim living among Ephraim and Manasseh, men without a tribe of their own. The verse is hard. Its
grammar is short and the WEB has to supply words. The wiki reads it as the insult that makes this a war and
not a quarrel.

The insult comes back on Ephraim at once. *Pəlîṭ*, *fugitive* or *escaped one* (lemma 6412), occurs in the
WLC of Genesis–Judges at Genesis 14:13 (*one who had escaped* came to Abram), Numbers 21:29, Joshua 8:22,
and twice here. In verse 4 Ephraim calls Gilead *fugitives of Ephraim*. In verse 5 the narrator uses the
same words, *pəlîṭê ʾeprayim*, for Ephraimites running for the river. The name Ephraim used as an insult
now names Ephraim's own men.

It also matters who Gilead is. In Joshua and Numbers the land beyond the Jordan went to Reuben, Gad, and
half of Manasseh, and *Gilead* was a clan of Manasseh (Numbers 26:29). Ephraim and Manasseh were Joseph's
two sons, blessed together and adopted by Jacob together (Genesis 48). The war of 12:4–6 is between
brothers' tribes. See [[Two Tribes in Place of One - The Adoption of Ephraim and Manasseh]] and
[[Brotherhood]].

**The fords (12:5–6).**

> **12:5** The Gileadites took the fords of the Jordan against the Ephraimites. When the fugitives of Ephraim said, "Let me go over," the men of Gilead said to him, "Are you an Ephraimite?" If he said, "No";

> **12:6** then they said to him, "Now say 'Shibboleth;'" and he said "Sibboleth"; for he couldn't manage to pronounce it right: then they seized him, and killed him at the fords of the Jordan. At that time, forty-two thousand of Ephraim fell.

*Took the fords of the Jordan*, *wayyilkōd … maʿbərôt hayyardēn* (WLC). The tactic Ehud used against Moab
(3:28) and Ephraim itself used against Midian (7:24) is used against Ephraim. See
[[The Fords of the Jordan - Joshua 2, Judges 3, 7, and 12]].

*Are you an Ephraimite?*, *haʾeprātî ʾattâ* (WLC). The gentilic *ʾeprātî* occurs in the WLC of
Genesis–Judges only here. A man can say *no*. He cannot change how he says the word.

*Shibboleth* and *Sibboleth*, *šibbōlet* and *sibbōlet* (WLC). The two words differ in their first
consonant, *šîn* (*sh*) against *sāmek* (*s*). *Sibbōlet* (lemma 5451) occurs nowhere else in the WLC of
Genesis–Judges. *Šibbōlet* has two senses in the OpenScriptures lemma data. In Genesis 41 it is the *heads
of grain* of Pharaoh's dream (41:5–7, 22–27), filed as lemma 7641 b. Here the data files it under 7641 a,
the sense *stream* or *flowing water*, which would suit a test given at a river. The text does not say
which sense the Gileadites meant, and the test needed only the sound. The usual explanation, that
Ephraimites west of the Jordan said *s* where Gileadites said *sh*, is the wiki's paraphrase of the
narrator's *he couldn't manage to pronounce it right*. The verse does not describe the dialects.

*He couldn't manage to pronounce it right*, *wəlōʾ yākîn lədabbēr kēn* (WLC), *he did not make ready to
speak so*. *Yākîn* is the causative of *kûn*, *to set firm, prepare*.

*They seized him, and killed him*, *wayyōʾḥăzû ʾôtô wayyišḥāṭûhû* (WLC). The verb *šāḥaṭ* (lemma 7819)
occurs in Judges only here. In Genesis through Joshua it is mostly the verb for slaughtering an animal for
sacrifice or for food: the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:6, 21), the burnt offering at the altar's north side
(Leviticus 1:5, 11), the goat for Joseph's coat (Genesis 37:31). It is used of killing people twice in
those books. Abraham *took the knife to kill his son* (Genesis 22:10, *lišḥōṭ*), and Moses imagines the
nations saying of Israel *he has slain them in the wilderness* (Numbers 14:16). The pericope after
Jephthah's vow uses Genesis 22's verb for the killing at the fords. That echo is the wiki's observation;
Judges does not draw it. See [[Your Only Son - Genesis 22 and Judges 11]].

*Forty-two thousand of Ephraim fell*, *ʾarbāʿîm ûšənayim ʾālep* (WLC). At the fords against Moab, Israel
struck *about ten thousand men* (3:29). Here Israel loses four times as many, at its own hand. Whether
*ʾelep* should be read as *thousand* or as a smaller military unit is a question asked of numbers across
these books; the wiki notes it and does not decide it here.

**The notice (12:7).**

> **12:7** Jephthah judged Israel six years. Then Jephthah the Gileadite died, and was buried in the cities of Gilead.

*Jephthah judged Israel six years*, *wayyišpōṭ yiptāḥ ʾet-yiśrāʾēl šēš šānîm* (WLC). This is the form of
the notices of Tola and Jair before it (10:2–5) and of Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon after it (12:8–15): *judged
Israel* so many years, *died*, *was buried*. The story of Jephthah ends in the frame of the minor judges.
There is no *the land had rest* (see [[Jephthah's Vow]] on 11:33). See
[[The Minor Judges - Judges 10.1-5 and 12.8-15]] and
[[The Land Had Rest - Joshua 11.23, 14.15, and the Judges Formula]].

Six years is the shortest span the book has yet given to a judge. Tola had twenty-three (10:2), Jair
twenty-two (10:3), and the rests after Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, and Gideon ran forty, eighty, forty, and
forty years (3:11, 30; 5:31; 8:28). Only Abimelech's term is shorter, *prince over Israel three years*
(9:22), and he is not called a judge.

*Buried in the cities of Gilead*, *bəʿārê gilʿād* (WLC). The plural is unusual. Every other burial notice
in the book names one place: Shamir, Kamon, Bethlehem, Aijalon, Pirathon, the tomb of Joash, and Samson's
father's burial site (8:32; 10:2, 5; 12:10, 12, 15; 16:31). The text does not say which city of Gilead.
The wiki recalls, without verifying, that some ancient versions and modern commentators read a singular
here; the WLC has the plural. See [[Burial and the Grave]].

**What the passage does.** Jephthah's story began with Gilead's elders asking *Who is the man who will
begin to fight against the children of Ammon?* (10:18), and it ends with Gilead's men killing Ephraimites.
The man who argued with Ammon's king about borders and history now tests his own people at the border by
how they speak. Words carry the whole of chapters 11 and 12: the contract with the elders, the long
message to Ammon, the vow he *can't go back* from, and now one word that a man either can or cannot say.

Gideon's answer to Ephraim's complaint ended with *their anger was abated toward him* (8:3). Jephthah's
answer ends with forty-two thousand dead. See
[[A Gentle Answer - Gideon's Reply to Ephraim and Proverbs 15.1]]. The book has moved from deliverance
from outsiders to war between tribes. That will reach its end in chapters 19–21, when all Israel fights
Benjamin.

## Notes

**Boundaries.** The plan's 12:1–7 is kept. 12:1 begins a new episode after the custom of 11:40, and 12:7
closes Jephthah's life. 12:8 opens the next notice with *after him*.

**Readings recorded, not resolved.** (1) *Northward* or *to Zaphon* (12:1). (2) Whether Jephthah had
called Ephraim (12:2); the earlier chapters do not narrate it. (3) The grammar and speaker of the insult in
12:4. (4) Which sense of *šibbōlet* the Gileadites meant. (5) *ʾelep* as *thousand* or a smaller unit
(12:6). (6) The plural *cities of Gilead* (12:7) and the singular read by some versions, recalled and not
verified.
