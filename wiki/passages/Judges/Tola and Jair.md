# Tola and Jair

**Book:** [[Judges]]
**Reference:** Judges 10:1–5

## Summary

Five verses, two judges, forty-five years. *After Abimelech*, Tola the son of Puah, a man of
Issachar, *arose to save Israel*. He lived at Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim, judged Israel
twenty-three years, died, and was buried there (1–2). *After him* Jair the Gileadite arose and
judged Israel twenty-two years. He had thirty sons who rode thirty donkey colts and held thirty
cities in Gilead, *called Havvoth Jair to this day*. He died and was buried in Kamon (3–5).

Nothing else happens. No sin of Israel is reported, no enemy is named, no one cries out, the
Spirit comes on no one, and no battle is fought. The land is not said to rest. God is not
mentioned. These are the first two of the book's five so-called *minor judges*. The other three,
Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon, follow Jephthah in the same short form (12:8–15). The label *minor* is a
later scholarly convention, not a word of the text. See
[[The Minor Judges - Judges 10.1-5 and 12.8-15]].

**Tola (10:1–2).**

> **10:1** After Abimelech, Tola the son of Puah, the son of Dodo, a man of Issachar, arose to save Israel. He lived in Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim.

> **10:2** He judged Israel twenty-three years, and died, and was buried in Shamir.

*Arose*, *wayyāqom* (WLC, lemma 6965). The verb was used for the generation that *arose … who
didn't know Yahweh* (2:10) and for Deborah, *until I arose a mother in Israel* (5:7). Here it is
used of a man, and no one is said to raise him up. Yahweh *raised up* Othniel and Ehud (3:9, 15),
and the summary at 2:16 says *Yahweh raised up judges*. The text gives Tola's rise no agent.

*After Abimelech*, *ʾaḥărê ʾăbîmelek*. Abimelech is the reference point, as if he belonged to the
sequence of Israel's leaders. The narrator never called him a judge. He was *made king* (9:6) and
was *prince over Israel* (9:22). Yet the next man in line is counted from him, just as Shamgar was
counted *after* Ehud (3:31). See [[Abimelech (son of Gideon)]].

*To save Israel*, *ləhôšîaʿ ʾet-yiśrāʾēl* (WLC, lemma 3467, the verb *yāšaʿ*). It is the verb of
the judges summary, *who saved them* (2:16), and of Othniel, Ehud, Shamgar, and Gideon. But Tola
saves Israel from nothing named. Abimelech's war in chapter 9 had no outside enemy. If Tola saves
Israel from anything, the text leaves the reader to infer it is the wreckage of that civil war. That
is an inference, not a statement of the text. The next time *yāšaʿ* appears, it is in Yahweh's
mouth, and it is a refusal: *Therefore I will save you no more* (10:13, *lōʾ-ʾôsîp ləhôšîaʿ
ʾetkem*, WLC). Tola is the last man in the book who is said simply to arise *to save Israel*.

*Tola the son of Puah, the son of Dodo, a man of Issachar.* Two generations of ancestry, unusually
full for this book. The names repeat Issachar's own family. Issachar's sons in Genesis 46:13 are
*Tola, Puvah, Iob, and Shimron*, and the census lists *the family of the Tolaites* and the family of
Puvah (Numbers 26:23). In the WLC both *Tola* (*tôlāʿ*, lemma 8439) and *Puah/Puvah* (lemma 6312) are
the same names in all three places. The spelling of the second name differs. It is *pûʾâ* in
Judges 10:1 and *puwwâ* in Genesis 46:13 and Numbers 26:23. 1 Chronicles 7:1, read in the WLC only
for this comparison, spells it *pûʾâ* as in Judges. So the judge bears the names of his tribe's
founding clans. Whether that reflects a naming custom, a clan remembered as a person, or simple
recurrence of family names, the text does not say. Puah the father is not the Hebrew midwife Puah
of Exodus 1:15. Her name is *pûʿâ* (lemma 6326), spelled with a different letter. See
[[Issachar]] and [[Shiphrah and Puah]].

*Dodo*, *dôdô*. In the WLC books read for this ingest (Genesis–Judges, and 1 Chronicles), the name
occurs here and twice in 1 Chronicles 11 (12, 26), of other men. It looks like *dôd*, *beloved* or
*uncle*, but that derivation is reported, not checked.

*He lived in Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim.* An Issacharite living outside Issachar's lot.
Issachar's inheritance lay in the north, toward the valley of Jezreel (Joshua 19:17–23). Shamir is
in Ephraim, the central hills where Deborah judged (4:5) and Ehud blew his trumpet (3:27). Why a man
of Issachar lived there, the text does not explain. Shamir's site is unknown. It is not the Shamir
of Judah's hill country (Joshua 15:48). See [[Shamir (Ephraim)]] and
[[The Hill Country of Ephraim]].

*He judged Israel*, *wayyišpōṭ ʾet-yiśrāʾēl*. Tola is the first man since Othniel of whom the
narrator says plainly that he *judged Israel* (3:10). Of Ehud, Gideon, and Shamgar the narrator
never says it. Deborah *judged Israel* (4:4). For Tola and Jair, judging is the whole of what is
said, and it is given a number of years.

**Jair (10:3–5).**

> **10:3** After him Jair, the Gileadite arose, and he judged Israel twenty-two years.

> **10:4** He had thirty sons who rode on thirty donkey colts, and they had thirty cities, which are called Havvoth Jair to this day, which are in the land of Gilead.

> **10:5** Jair died, and was buried in Kamon.

*Jair, the Gileadite*, *yāʾîr haggilʿādî*. The first judge from east of the Jordan. *The Gileadite*
is the title the book will give Jephthah, *Jephthah the Gileadite* (11:1; 12:7, WLC
*haggilʿādî* in both). Gilead was the land Reuben and Gad asked for, which Moses promised on condition
and Machir of Manasseh took (Numbers 32:1, 29, 39–40). The text gives Jair no tribe and no father.

*Thirty sons who rode on thirty donkey colts, and they had thirty cities.* The Hebrew plays on the
words. *Donkey colts* is *ʿăyārîm*, the plural of *ʿayir*, a young donkey (lemma 5895). The
ordinary word for *cities* is *ʿîr*, plural *ʿārîm* (lemma 5892). In 10:4 the *cities* are spelled
*ʿăyārîm*, the same form as the colts. The morphhb lemma data tags both words 5895. So the verse
reads *thirty sons riding thirty ʿăyārîm, and thirty ʿăyārîm they had*. In the WLC books of Genesis
through Judges, *ʿayir* occurs in only five verses. Two are in Genesis: Jacob's gift to Esau of
*ten foals* (Genesis 32:15 WEB, 32:16 WLC) and the blessing of Judah, *his donkey's colt to the
choice vine* (Genesis 49:11). The other three are in Judges: the two in 10:4, and Abdon's sons and
grandsons *who rode on seventy donkey colts* (12:14). Whether *ʿăyārîm* in 10:4 is a deliberate
spelling of *cities*, a variant form, or a scribal assimilation to the word before it is reported,
not checked. The WLC text as it stands has the same letters both times.

To ride a donkey is a mark of standing in Judges. The Song of Deborah calls on *you who ride on
white donkeys, you who sit on rich carpets* (5:10). Jair's thirty sons each ride one, and each holds
a city. Gideon had seventy sons (8:30), and refused to rule. Abimelech killed his brothers, *seventy
persons, on one stone*, all but Jotham, to rule alone (9:5). Jair has thirty sons and thirty cities, and he judges. The text draws no
comparison. The juxtaposition with chapter 9 is the wiki's observation. See
[[Kingship and Empire]].

*Called Havvoth Jair to this day.* *Ḥawwōt yāʾîr*, *the villages of Jair* (WLC, lemma 2334). This is
the fourth passage to explain the name. Numbers 32:41 credits it to *Jair the son of Manasseh*, who
took villages in Gilead. Deuteronomy 3:14 has the same Jair take *all the region of Argob* in Bashan
and name it. Joshua 13:30 counts *all the villages of Jair, which are in Bashan, sixty cities*.
Judges 10:4 gives the name to a judge's thirty sons, in *the land of Gilead*. The accounts differ in
the man, the region, and the number. The text does not reconcile them, and neither does the wiki.
See [[The Villages of Jair - Numbers 32.41, Deuteronomy 3.14, Joshua 13.30, and Judges 10.4]],
[[Havvoth Jair]], [[Jair (son of Manasseh)]], and [[Jair (the Gileadite)]].

*Buried in Kamon*, *bəqāmôn*. In the WLC books of Genesis–Judges the town is named only here. Its
site is not given. It is presumably in Gilead, but the text does not say so. See [[Kamon]].

**The short form.** Each of the five minor-judge notices uses the same parts in the same order:
*after him*; the judge's name and origin; *he judged Israel* so many years; sometimes a note of
sons, daughters, or donkeys; *and he died, and was buried in* a named place (10:1–5; 12:8–15). The
notices give no oppression and no rest. They give a burial for every judge. The cycle stories also
record burials, but not always. Gideon was buried *in the tomb of Joash his father* (8:32), and
Jephthah *in the cities of Gilead* (12:7). There *cities* is the ordinary *ʿārê* (lemma 5892, WLC),
not the *ʿăyārîm* of Jair's verse. Othniel, Ehud, and Deborah have none. See
[[Burial and the Grave]].

Read together, the two notices put forty-five quiet years between the civil war of chapter 9 and
the Ammonite oppression of 10:6–18. In that next passage Israel *again did that which was evil*, and
serves the gods of seven nations (10:6). The quiet is also a frame. The judge from the west, of
Issachar, comes before the judge from the east, of Gilead. The next story is about Gilead under
Ammon, and it will end with Gilead's judge, Jephthah, at war with Ephraim (12:1–6; see [[Shibboleth]]).

## Notes

**Is the pericope one unit or two?** The plan kept Tola and Jair together, and this ingest keeps
them so. The two notices share one form and are joined by *after him* (3). Separating five verses
into two pages would cut a single literary unit. 10:6 starts a new cycle with *again did that which
was evil*.

**Twenty-three and twenty-two.** These are the first judge-years in the book that are not forty or
eighty or a multiple of twenty. Othniel's rest was forty years (3:11), Ehud's eighty (3:30),
Deborah's and Gideon's forty (5:31; 8:28). Here the numbers are what the text gives for each man's
judging, not for the land's rest. Some interpreters read the odd numbers as archival rather than
schematic. That reading is reported, not checked, and the text itself says only the numbers.

**Issachar is a strong donkey.** Jacob's blessing called Issachar *a strong donkey* (Genesis 49:14),
and the next judge after the Issacharite has sons on donkeys. The word in Genesis 49:14 is
*ḥămōr*, not *ʿayir*. The verbal link is weak, and the wiki does not claim the text intends it.
