# Gideon Throws Down Baal's Altar

**Book:** [[Judges]]
**Reference:** Judges 6:25–32

## Summary

The previous pericope ended at an altar to Yahweh in Ophrah, named *Yahweh is Peace* (6:24). This one
begins *That same night*, in the same town, at another altar:

> **6:25** That same night, Yahweh said to him, “Take your father’s bull, even the second bull seven years old, and throw down the altar of Baal that your father has, and cut down the Asherah that is by it.

**The command (6:25–26).** Yahweh's first instruction to the man he has just sent to *save Israel*
(6:14) is not about Midian. It is about his father's house. The altar is *the altar of Baal that your
father has*, and an Asherah stands *by it*. Joash, whose oak sheltered Yahweh's angel (6:11), also
keeps the town's Baal altar. Gideon is to do four things: throw down the altar, cut down the Asherah,
build *an altar to Yahweh your God on the top of this stronghold, in an orderly way*, and offer the
second bull as a burnt offering, using *the wood of the Asherah* for the fire. The Asherah becomes
firewood for Yahweh's offering. The bull is *seven years old*, and Midian had oppressed Israel *seven
years* (6:1). The text does not draw the link, but the number is the same in the Hebrew of both
verses.

The law had already given this command, in the plural, to all Israel: *you shall break down their
altars, and dash in pieces their pillars, and you shall cut down their Asherah poles* (Exodus 34:13;
compare Deuteronomy 7:5; 12:3). At Bochim the angel had repeated it and charged Israel with neglecting
it: *You shall break down their altars.' But you have not listened to my voice* (Judges 2:2). Gideon is
given the same task, but the altar is now Israel's own, and it belongs to his father. See
[[Break Down Their Altars - Exodus 34.13, Deuteronomy 7.5, Judges 2.2, and Judges 6.25-32]].

**Obedience by night (6:27).** *Then Gideon took ten men of his servants, and did as Yahweh had spoken
to him. Because he feared his father's household and the men of the city, he could not do it by day,
but he did it by night.* He obeys at once and in full, and he is afraid. The prophet had said, *You
shall not fear the gods of the Amorites* (6:10). Gideon does not fear the god. He fears the men who
worship it. The man who hid his wheat from Midian (6:11) now hides his obedience from his own town.
He also has *ten men of his servants*, which sits oddly beside *my family is the poorest in Manasseh*
(6:15). The text does not reconcile the two.

**The morning (6:28–30).** *The men of the city arose early in the morning, and behold*: the altar of
Baal broken down, the Asherah cut down, and the second bull offered on the new altar. They ask *Who has
done this thing?*, inquire, and learn the answer: *Gideon the son of Joash*. Then they go to Joash:
*Bring out your son, that he may die, because he has broken down the altar of Baal, and because he has
cut down the Asherah that was by it.* The law's death sentence is turned around. Deuteronomy 17:2–5
told Israel to *bring out* the man or woman who *served other gods* and stone them. Here the town
demands that a man be *brought out* to die for destroying another god's altar. *Bring out* is also
the demand of *the men of the city* at Sodom (Genesis 19:5), and it will be the demand of *the men of
the city* at Gibeah (Judges 19:22). See
[[Bring Him Out - Genesis 19.5, Deuteronomy 17.5, Judges 6.30, and Judges 19.22]].

**Joash's answer (6:31).** *Joash said to all who stood against him, "Will you contend for Baal? Or will
you save him? He who will contend for him, let him be put to death by morning! If he is a god, let him
contend for himself, because someone has broken down his altar!"* The owner of the altar turns on the
men who came to defend it. He makes three points. A god who needs men to plead his case is no god. To
*save* Baal is to reverse the roles of god and worshiper, and *save* is the verb of Gideon's commission
(6:14–15). And the death sentence is turned back on whoever argues for Baal, *by morning*, before the
day is out. The text does not say why Joash changed sides. He may have been persuaded overnight, or he
may have been protecting his son, or he may simply have used the town's own logic against it. What the
text does say is that a man who kept a Baal altar is the first in Judges to say aloud that Baal cannot
defend himself.

**The name (6:32).** *Therefore on that day he named him Jerub-Baal, saying, "Let Baal contend against
him, because he has broken down his altar."* The new name means *Let Baal contend*. It is a challenge,
and the rest of the story is its answer: Baal never contends. From here the narrator uses both names.
The next chapter opens *Jerubbaal, who is Gideon* (7:1), and chapters 8–9 call him *Jerubbaal*
repeatedly, especially when speaking of his house and his seventy sons (8:29, 35; 9:1–5). See
[[Names and Naming]].

**How the pericope sits in Gideon's story.** It is a small version of the larger one. Gideon is sent,
afraid, and obeys anyway. The battle is fought first at home, against Israel's own god, before it is
fought against Midian. The end of the story reverses this one. Gideon makes an ephod and puts it *in
Ophrah, his city*, and *all Israel played the prostitute with it there* (8:27). After his death Israel
*played the prostitute following the Baals, and made Baal Berith their god* (8:33). The town whose
Baal altar he threw down becomes the site of his own idol.

## Notes

**"Throw down" and "broken down."** Yahweh's command uses *wəhārastā*, from *hāras* (lemma 2040), *tear
down* (25, WLC). The report of the deed and every later mention use a different verb, *nātaṣ* (5422):
*nuttaṣ* in the morning (28), *nātaṣ* in the town's charge (30), and *nātaṣ* twice more in 31–32 (WLC).
*Nātaṣ* is the verb of the law's command, *mizbəḥōtām tittōṣûn*, *you shall break down their altars*
(Exodus 34:13; Deuteronomy 7:5; compare *wənittaṣtem*, Deuteronomy 12:3), and of the angel's at Bochim
(Judges 2:2), all WLC. In the WLC of Judges, *hāras* occurs only in 6:25. *Nātaṣ* occurs in 2:2,
6:28–32, and three later verses: Gideon breaks down the tower of Penuel (8:9, 17), and Abimelech breaks
down Shechem (9:45; [[Ephraim, Succoth, and Penuel]], [[The Fall of Abimelech]]). The command and the report use different verbs. Why the narrator changes verbs
is not explained, and this page does not explain it either.

**"Cut down the Asherah."** *Tikrōt* (25), *tikrōt* (26), *kōrātâ* (28), *kārat* (30), all from
*kārat* (3772), WLC. Exodus 34:13 uses the same verb for the same object: *ʾăšērāyw tikrōtûn* (WLC).
Deuteronomy 7:5 and 12:3 use other verbs, *gādaʿ* (1438), *hew down*, and *śārap*, *burn*. *Gādaʿ* is
the verb commonly given as the root of the name *Gidʿôn*. That etymology is the standard one but is
reported here, not checked. If it holds, the man named *Hewer* is sent to do what Deuteronomy 7:5 says
to do to an Asherah, though Judges 6 itself uses *kārat* and not *gādaʿ*. In the WLC of Judges,
*gādaʿ* occurs only at 21:6.

**"Your father's bull, even the second bull."** *Par-haššôr ʾăšer ləʾābîkā ûpar haššēnî šebaʿ šānîm*
(25, WLC). The Hebrew can be read as one bull described twice or as two bulls, the father's bull and a
second one. Only the *second* is offered in 26 and 28. What happened to the first, if there were two,
is not said. WEB's *even* reads one bull; other readings are common, and the question is reported, not
settled. *Šebaʿ šānîm*, *seven years*, is the phrase of 6:1, Midian's *seven years* (WLC).

**"This stronghold."** *Rōʾš hammāʿôz hazzeh*, *the top of this stronghold* (26, WLC; *māʿôz*, 4581).
The *strongholds* where Israel hid from Midian in 6:2 are *məṣādôt* (4679), a different word. *Māʿôz*
occurs in the WLC of Judges only here. *Bammaʿărākâ*, *in an orderly way* (4634), is a word for an
arrangement or row, also used for the battle line. What it means here, laid out properly or built in
courses, is not certain, and is reported, not settled.

**"Ten men of his servants."** *ʿĂśārâ ʾănāšîm mēʿăbādāyw* (27, WLC). A man who has ten servants is not
destitute. Gideon called his clan *the poorest*, *haddal* (6:15). The two statements are both in the
text and are left side by side here.

**"By day … by night."** *Yômām* and *laylâ* (27, WLC). The night of 25 (*ballaylâ hahûʾ*) is the night
of the command, and the deed is done at night as well. The night here is cover from the town, so the
page is not filed under [[Light and Darkness]].

**"Who has done this thing?"** *Mî ʿāśâ haddābār hazzeh* (29, WLC). The town's question uses
*haddābār hazzeh*, *this thing*, and the answer repeats it: *Gideon the son of Joash has done this
thing*. Deuteronomy 17:5 calls the idolater's deed *haddābār hāraʿ hazzeh*, *this evil thing* (WLC).
*Wayyidrəšû waybaqšû*, *they inquired and asked* (29, WLC): *dāraš* and *bāqaš*, two verbs of
searching, also paired, in the other order, in Deuteronomy 4:29, *you shall seek* (*biqqaštem*) …
*when you search after him* (*tidrəšennû*) (stored WEB; WLC).

**"Bring out your son, that he may die."** *Hôṣēʾ ʾet-binkā wəyāmōt* (30, WLC). *Hôṣēʾ* is the hiphil
imperative of *yāṣāʾ* (3318). The same form is the demand of *the men of the city* at Gibeah, *hôṣēʾ
ʾet-hāʾîš* (Judges 19:22), and the plural form at Sodom is *hôṣîʾēm*, *bring them out* (Genesis 19:5).
Deuteronomy 17:5 commands *wəhôṣēʾtā*, *you shall bring out*, the idolater to be stoned (all WLC). The
phrase *ʾanšê hāʿîr*, *the men of the city*, appears in Judges 6:27, 28, 30 and 19:22 and Genesis 19:4
(WLC). The comparison is the wiki's; the text of Judges 6 does not point to the others.

**"Contend."** *Rîb* (7378) occurs four times in 6:31–32: *tərîbûn*, *will you contend*; *yārîb*, *he
who will contend*; *yāreb*, *let him contend* (31); and *yāreb bô habbaʿal*, *let Baal contend against
him* (32), all WLC. *Rîb* is a word for a quarrel and for a lawsuit, so Joash's question can also be
read as *Will you plead Baal's case?* The name *Yərubbaʿal* (3378) is explained by the text as *yāreb
bô habbaʿal*. Whether the name originally meant something else, such as *Baal contends* or *Baal is
great*, is a common question; it is reported, not checked. In Judges, *rîb* recurs in 8:1 (Ephraim
quarrels with Gideon; WEB *They rebuked him sharply*), 11:25, and 21:22 (WLC).

**"Will you save him?"** *ʾIm-ʾattem tôšîʿûn ʾôtô* (31, WLC). *Yāšaʿ* (3467) in the hiphil, the verb of
6:14, *wəhôšaʿtā ʾet-yiśrāʾēl*, *save Israel*, and of 6:15, *bammâ ʾôšîaʿ*, *how shall I save* (WLC).
Gideon was sent to save Israel; the town proposes to save Baal.

**"Let him be put to death by morning."** *Yûmat ʿad-habbōqer* (31, WLC). *Yûmat* is the hophal
imperfect of *mût*, the form of the law's death sentences, as in *môt yûmat*, *shall surely be put to
death* (Exodus 21:12, WLC). Joash answers the town's *that he may die* (*wəyāmōt*, qal) with the legal
form, turned on them. *By morning*: the men had come *early in the morning* (28).

**"If he is a god."** *ʾIm-ʾĕlōhîm hûʾ* (31, WLC). *ʾĕlōhîm* without the article; the sense is *if he is
a god* or *if he is God*, and WEB's *a god* is a reasonable reading of the syntax, not the only one.

**The men of the city.** They are a collective with one voice and no names, and they do not
reappear as a group. No page is made for them. The town's later relationship to Gideon is in 8:27 and
in Abimelech's slaughter of the sons of Jerubbaal *at Ophrah* (9:5).

**Judgment calls.** The boundary was kept as planned: *That same night* (25) opens a new scene, and
the naming (32) closes it before Midian's muster (33). *Jerub-Baal* stays on [[Gideon]] as an alias; no
separate page is made, because the text says *Jerubbaal, who is Gideon* (7:1). The new connection
[[Bring Him Out - Genesis 19.5, Deuteronomy 17.5, Judges 6.30, and Judges 19.22]] is made now, though
Judges 19 is not yet ingested, because the shared phrase is verified now; that pericope will extend
it. The Penuel and Shechem uses of *nātaṣ* are recorded here and on the altars connection for those
later pericopes to take up.
