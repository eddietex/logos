# Do Not Consent to Sinners

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 1:8–19

## Summary

The first lecture of Proverbs, and the preface's promise kept at once. [[The Purpose of the Proverbs]]
said wisdom is something *received* (1:3, 5); here is the first thing handed on, by a father to a son,
and it is not about sex, money management, or the tongue — the subjects the book will spend most of
its length on — but about a gang. The young man's first test is an invitation from his peers to join
a robbery that will need a killing. The father answers it in twelve verses built in a ring:

- **1:8–9 — the appeal to listen.** Father's instruction, mother's teaching, worn like ornaments.
- **1:10 — the thesis.** *"My son, if sinners entice you, don't consent."*
- **1:11–14 — the gang's speech, quoted.** Ambush, plunder, a shared purse.
- **1:15–16 — the father's counter.** Don't walk their path; their feet run to blood.
- **1:17–18 — the net.** A bird and a trap, and the trap turned round on the trappers.
- **1:19 — the summary.** *"So are the ways of everyone who is greedy for gain."*

**Both parents teach (1:8).** *"My son, listen to your father's instruction, and don't forsake your
mother's teaching."* The father's word is *mûsār* — the *instruction* of the preface (1:2, 3, 7),
discipline as well as information. The mother's word is ***tôrâ***, the word the rest of the Bible
uses for the Law. In Proverbs it means a parent's teaching, not the Sinai code, and the line should
not be read as though the mother taught the Pentateuch; but the household is where the vocabulary of
instruction lives before it is anything else, and the book puts the mother inside it from its first
lecture. The same line returns word for word at 6:20 after a different first half (*"keep your
father's commandment"*). This is the fifth commandment in the form of a curriculum: *"Honor your
father and your mother"* (Exodus 20:12) becomes *listen* to them, and Deuteronomy's command that the
words be taught *"diligently to your children"* (6:7) is pictured here from the child's side. See
[[Honor Your Father and Mother - Exodus 20 and Ephesians 6]] and [[Law and Obedience]].

The reward (1:9) is decoration, not a promise of long life: *"a garland to grace your head, and chains
around your neck."* The *garland* is *liwyat ḥēn*, a wreath of *favor* — the word for the grace Noah
found (Genesis 6:8) — and the *chains* are necklaces. Instruction kept is worn where others can see
it. **The gang will offer the son plunder for his house (1:13); the father offers him something to
wear.**

**The thesis is four Hebrew words (1:10).** *Bənî ʾim-yəpattûkā ḥaṭṭāʾîm ʾal-tōbēʾ* — *my son, if
sinners entice you, don't consent.* Two of the words do most of the work.

- ***Entice*** is *yəpattûkā*, from the root *p-t-h*, and the *simple* whom the preface promised to
  make prudent (1:4) are *pətāʾyim*, from the same root. On the standard lexical account, the
  *simple* are precisely the *enticeable*: people open to persuasion, not yet set either way. **The
  first lecture's first threat is the one the simple are by definition exposed to** — not ignorance
  but suggestibility. The same verb is used of Delilah being told to *entice* Samson (Judges 16:5),
  and of the spirit who will *entice* Ahab (1 Kings 22:20–22); those uses are noted from the lexical
  tradition and not quoted here, as neither book is stored.
- ***Sinners*** is *ḥaṭṭāʾîm*, the word of Psalm 1:1's *"path of sinners"* — *derek ḥaṭṭāʾîm* — and
  the father's next instruction is *"don't walk on the path (bəderek) with them"* (1:15). Proverbs
  and Psalm 1 open on the same picture, and with the same two words: a road, and the people on it
  you should not walk with. See [[Walking with God]].

**The gang's speech is quoted in full (1:11–14), and it is the most attractive thing in the
lecture.** The father lets the son hear the invitation in its own voice before answering it, which
is a teaching choice: he does not caricature the offer. What the voice offers is adventure (*"Come
with us"*), power over others (*"Let's swallow them up alive"*), wealth (*"We'll find all valuable
wealth. We'll fill our houses with plunder"*), and — last, as the clinching line — **belonging**:
*"You shall cast your lot among us. We'll all have one purse."* The son is being offered a family.
The book has just given him one (1:8).

The speech also convicts itself, in three ways, without the speakers noticing:

1. **It names its victim innocent.** *"Let's lurk secretly for the innocent without cause"* —
   *lənāqî ḥinnām*, *for the innocent, gratuitously*. The gang does not pretend its target deserves
   it. *Ḥinnām*, *without cause*, is the word the Satan uses in asking *"Does Job fear God for
   nothing?"* (Job 1:9) and God uses of Job's afflictions, *"to ruin him without cause"* (2:3); here
   it is the motive clause of a murder, and it will come back in 1:17. **Job 2:3's *"ruin"* is
   *ləballəʿô*, *to swallow him*** — the verb of the gang's next line, *"Let's swallow them up"*
   (*nibləʿēm*, 1:12). *Swallow* and *without cause* stand together in both verses: in Proverbs as a
   gang's plan, in Job as God's own description of what was done to the blameless man.
2. **It casts the gang as death itself.** *"Let's swallow them up alive like Sheol, and whole, like
   those who go down into the pit"* (1:12). [[Sheol]] is the insatiable one in Proverbs — *"Sheol and
   Abaddon are never satisfied"* (27:20; compare 30:16) — and the men propose to be it, swallowing
   their victims *alive*, as the earth *"swallowed them up"* and Korah's company *"went down alive into
   Sheol"* (Numbers 16:32–33, WEB, checked against bible-api.com). The *pit* is *bôr*, the cistern
   Joseph's brothers threw him into (Genesis 37:20–24). *"Whole"* is *təmîmîm*, which elsewhere means
   *blameless* — the adjective of Noah, *"blameless among the people of his time"* (Genesis 6:9),
   and of the family of Job's *"blameless"* (*tām*, Job 1:1; 2:3); in this verse it is physical (*entire, intact*) and is so rendered in the WEB,
   but the word chosen for the victims is the Bible's word for integrity. See [[Integrity]].
3. **It uses the lot.** *"You shall cast your lot among us"* — *gôrāləkā tappîl bətôkēnû*. The lot
   is how Israel divides the land and discerns God's will; Proverbs itself will say *"The lot is
   cast into the lap, but its every decision is from Yahweh"* (16:33). The gang offers the son a
   lot cast among thieves, where nobody's decision is from Yahweh, and the purse it fills is the
   one 1:19 says will kill its owners.

**The father answers with feet (1:15–16).** *"My son, don't walk on the path with them. Keep your
foot from their path, for their feet run to evil. They hurry to shed blood."* The answer is not an
argument against the gang's logic; it is a description of where their road goes. The second line is
almost word for word in Isaiah 59:7 — *raglêhem lāraʿ yārūṣû wîmahărû lišpōk dām*, with Isaiah adding
*nāqî*, *innocent* — and Paul's catena of indictments in Romans 3 reaches *"Their feet are swift to
shed blood"* (3:15) by way of Isaiah. A gang's road in Proverbs is a nation's in Isaiah and
humanity's in Romans. See [[Their Feet Run to Evil - Proverbs 1.16, Isaiah 59.7, and Romans 3.15]].

**The net (1:17) is the lecture's one figure, and its sense is disputed.** *"For in vain is the net
spread in the sight of any bird."* Two readings are live:

- **The bird sees and escapes** — a net spread in plain sight is wasted on a bird, which has the
  sense to avoid it. Then the men are stupider than birds, because they walk into their own trap
  with open eyes (1:18). On this reading the son, who has now *seen* the net because his father has
  shown it to him, has no excuse.
- **The bird sees and is caught anyway** — the net is spread *"in vain"* in the sense of *with no
  apparent danger*: the bird watches it laid, does not grasp what it is, and goes for the bait. Then
  the men are exactly like birds, blind to what they are watching being prepared for them.

Both readings lead to 1:18, and the Hebrew favours neither decisively. What the Hebrew does show is
the word: *ḥinnām məzōrâ hārāšet*, *in vain is the net spread* — the same *ḥinnām* as the gang's
*"without cause"* in 1:11. **They lie in wait for the innocent *ḥinnām*, and the net is spread
*ḥinnām*.** The gratuitousness of their violence is answered by a futility. And *"any bird"* is
*kol-baʿal kānāp*, literally *any owner of a wing* — which matters in two verses' time.

**1:18 turns the ambush round.** *"But these lay wait for their own blood. They lurk secretly for
their own lives."* The verbs are the gang's own from 1:11: *neʾerbâ lədām*, *let's lay in wait for
blood*, becomes *lədāmām yeʾĕrōbû*, *they lay wait for their own blood*; *niṣpənâ*, *let's lurk*,
becomes *yiṣpənû*, *they lurk*, *for their own lives*. The father does not need a new vocabulary to
describe their end; he repeats their plan with the possessive changed. This is
[[Retribution]] in its self-acting form, the form of Psalm 7:15–16 (*"He has dug a hole, and has
fallen into the pit which he made"*) and of the book's own *"Whoever digs a pit shall fall into it"*
(26:27): **no judge appears, and God is not named anywhere in the lecture.** The deterrent the
father offers is the shape of the act itself.

**1:19 generalises.** *"So are the ways of everyone who is greedy for gain. It takes away the life of
its owners."* *Greedy for gain* is *bōṣēaʿ bāṣaʿ*, *one who cuts off a cut* — *beṣaʿ*, profit, often
ill-gotten. It is the word Judah uses at the pit in Dothan: *"What profit is it if we kill our brother
and conceal his blood?"* (Genesis 37:26, *mah-beṣaʿ*). And *its owners* is *bəʿālāyw*, from *baʿal*
— the word that made the bird an *"owner of a wing"* two verses back. **The bird owns its wing; the
greedy man's gain owns him, and takes his life.** The lecture began with something to wear and ends
with something that wears its wearer.

**Joseph's brothers are this lecture as narrative.** They saw Joseph *"afar off"* and *"conspired against
him to kill him"* (Genesis 37:18); they said *"Come … let's kill him, and cast him into one of the
pits"* (37:20, *ləkû* where the gang says *ləkâ*, both from *hālak*); [[Reuben]] said *"Shed no blood"*
(37:22, *ʾal-tišpəkû-dām*, the verb and noun of Proverbs 1:16); [[Judah]] asked *"What profit is
it?"* (37:26); and they sold him for silver (37:28). Years later, in Egypt, they said to one another *"We are certainly guilty
concerning our brother"* and Reuben answered *"behold, his blood is required"* (42:21–22): the men who
lay in wait for their brother's blood found it waiting for them. The family the gang offers its
recruit (1:14) is in Genesis an actual family, and the lecture's *"one purse"* is twenty pieces of
silver. See [[What Profit Is It - Joseph's Brothers and the Ambush of Proverbs 1]].

**Read after Job, 1:18–19 is a sentence about roads, not a verdict on cases.** Job had looked at the
same men and seen them flourish: *"The tents of robbers prosper. Those who provoke God are secure"*
(Job 12:6); *"The murderer rises with the light. He kills the poor and needy"* (24:14), and in Job 24
nothing happens to him. Proverbs 1 says such men ambush themselves. **The two books are not quite
contradicting each other, and the difference should be stated rather than smoothed.** The father is
describing where a way of life leads (*"So are the ways"*, 1:19) to a son deciding whether to take
it — a warning addressed forward, before the act. Job is describing what he has seen happen to men
already on the road, and asking why it has not happened yet. Job's friends took sentences like
Proverbs 1:18 and ran them backwards, from a man's ruin to his guilt; the father in Proverbs runs it
forwards, from a choice to its likely end, and never claims to be able to read it the other way.
The wiki records both claims: Proverbs 1:18–19 (the greedy take their own lives) and Job 12:6; 24:14
(robbers and murderers prosper, and nothing is seen to happen to them). See [[Retribution]].

## People

- *The son* — addressed three times as *"My son"* (1:8, 10, 15). Unnamed and generic: the reader is
  meant to stand where he stands. No page.
- *The father and the mother* — the voices of instruction (1:8). The speaker of the whole lecture is
  the father; the mother's teaching is named beside his. Unnamed; no page. The book's heading
  attaches the collection to [[Solomon]], but the lecture does not present itself as Solomon speaking
  to a particular son, and the wiki does not read it so.
- *The sinners* — a gang, quoted but unnamed. No page.
- [[God]] is **not named** in 1:8–19. After the preface's motto (1:7) the first lecture argues
  entirely from consequence. Recorded here because it bears on how the lecture's retribution
  should be read.

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — 1:12; the gang compares itself to Sheol swallowing the living. With it, *the pit*
  (*bôr*), the cistern-word of Genesis 37.

## Themes

- [[Wisdom]] — the first instruction handed on: wisdom as a parent's word, received and worn.
- [[Folly]] — the *simple* (1:4) are the *enticeable* (1:10); the lecture is addressed to the person
  most exposed to persuasion.
- [[Sin]] — *ḥaṭṭāʾîm*, *sinners*, the first use of the word in Proverbs, defined by what they invite
  others into.
- [[Deception]] — enticement; an offer whose cost is left out of the speech that makes it.
- [[Blood]] — *lying in wait for blood* (1:11), *running to shed blood* (1:16), and *lying in wait for
  their own blood* (1:18).
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — plunder, the shared purse, and *greed for gain* that takes the life of
  its owners (1:13–14, 19).
- [[Retribution]] — the ambush turned back on its makers, by repeating their own verbs (1:11, 18).
- [[Walking with God]] — the two paths, here in their first form: the road you do not walk with them.
- [[Law and Obedience]] — the mother's *tôrâ* and the father's *mûsār*; the fifth commandment as a
  curriculum.
- [[Integrity]] — *təmîmîm*, the victims *"whole"* (1:12), with the Bible's word for blamelessness.

## Notes

**Hebrew.** Claims above about the Hebrew wording of Proverbs 1:8–19 — *mûsar ʾābîkā* and *tôrat
ʾimmekā* (1:8, and 6:20's identical second line), *liwyat ḥēn* (1:9), *yəpattûkā ḥaṭṭāʾîm ʾal-tōbēʾ*
(1:10), *neʾerbâ lədām* / *niṣpənâ lənāqî ḥinnām* (1:11), *kišʾôl ḥayyîm* / *ûtəmîmîm* / *bôr* (1:12),
*gôrāləkā tappîl bətôkēnû* / *kîs ʾeḥād* (1:14), *bəderek ʾittām* (1:15), *raglêhem lāraʿ yārûṣû
wîmahărû lišpok-dām* (1:16), *ḥinnām məzōrâ hārāšet … kol-baʿal kānāp* (1:17), *lədāmām yeʾĕrōbû
yiṣpənû lənapšōtām* (1:18), *bōṣēaʿ bāṣaʿ … bəʿālāyw* (1:19) — were checked against Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, as were Isaiah 59:7, Psalm 1:1 (*derek ḥaṭṭāʾîm*), and Genesis 37:20
(*ləkû … habbōrôt*), 37:22 (*ʾal-tišpəkû-dām*), and 37:26 (*mah-beṣaʿ*). Lexical statements — that
*yəpattûkā* and *pətāʾyim* share the root *p-t-h*, that *beṣaʿ* is gain *cut off* and often ill-gotten,
that *təmîmîm* belongs to the family of *tām* — are standard lexical descriptions, not readings of an
edition. The uses of *p-t-h* in Judges 16:5 and 1 Kings 22:20–22 are given from that lexical tradition
and were not checked against a text here. Job 1:9 (*haḥinnām*), Job 2:3 (*ləballəʿô ḥinnām*), Genesis
6:8 (*ḥēn*), and Genesis 6:9 (*tāmîm*) were checked in the same edition.

**1:10's *ʾal-tōbēʾ*** is an unusual spelling of the verb *ʾābâ*, *to be willing* — *don't be willing*,
rendered *"don't consent"*. The form is as printed in the edition above; its explanation as a
by-form of *ʾābâ* is the standard grammatical account.

**The Septuagint and 1:16.** It is commonly reported that the major Septuagint codices Vaticanus and
Sinaiticus lack Proverbs 1:16, the verse being present in Alexandrinus and in the Hebrew. This was not
verified against a Septuagint edition in this pass, and is noted only because it bears on whether
Romans 3:15 could be drawing on Proverbs at all; the connection page argues from the English and the
Hebrew, which point to Isaiah regardless.

**The lecture's unit.** 1:8–19 is bounded by *"My son"* at 1:8 and the *pe* paragraph marker after
1:19 in the edition above, and by Wisdom's entrance at 1:20, which changes speaker. Within it, *"My
son"* recurs at 1:10 and 1:15; the wiki treats 1:8–9 as the lecture's opening rather than a separate
unit, because 1:10 depends on it for its addressee.
