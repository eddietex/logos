# Do Not Boast About Tomorrow

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 27:1–27

## Summary

The third chapter of the collection *"the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out"* (25:1) has none
of chapter 26's tight grouping. It moves from boasting (1–2) to provocation and jealousy (3–4), to
friendship tested by rebuke, counsel, and distance (5–10), to a father's plea (11), and then through a
run of single sayings — several of them repeated from earlier collections — to a fool who cannot be
ground out of his folly (22). The Masoretic text marks a paragraph break (*petuchah*) after 27:22. The
chapter then ends with something the sentence collections have not had before: a five-verse poem on
keeping flocks (23–27), the only sustained piece of instruction in chapters 25–29.

Yahweh is not named in the chapter, as in chapter 26. Its one theological horizon is the opening
verse's *"you don't know what a day may bring"*, and its one glimpse below the earth is *"Sheol and
Abaddon"* (20).

Two threads run through it. The first is **praise** — the root *hll*. *"Don't boast"* is *ʾal-tiṯhallēl*
(1); *"Let another man praise you"* is *yəhallelḵā zār* (2); and *"man is refined by his praise"* is
*ləp̄î mahălālô* (21). Praise one gives oneself is forbidden, praise from another is permitted, and
praise received is a test. The second is the **friend** — *rēaʿ* or *ʾôhēḇ* in 6, 9, 10, 14, and 17 —
whose love is proved by the wound he gives (6), the counsel he offers (9), the nearness of his door
(10), and the edge he puts on his friend (17).

**27:1–2 — Boasting and praise.** *"Don't boast about tomorrow; for you don't know what a day may
bring"* (1). *Bring* is *yēleḏ*, *gives birth to*: a day is pregnant with what no one can see. The
saying does not forbid planning; 16:1 and 16:9 had already said that the heart plans and that Yahweh
directs the steps. It forbids the boast, the plan spoken as if tomorrow were already owned. James 4:13–16
says the same about the merchant who says *"Today or tomorrow let's go into this city"*: *"you don't
know what your life will be like tomorrow"*, and *"All such boasting is evil"*. See
[[Do Not Boast About Tomorrow - Proverbs 27.1, Luke 12.16-21, and James 4.13-16]]. Then: *"Let another
man praise you, and not your own mouth; a stranger, and not your own lips"* (2). *Another man* is
*zār* and *a stranger* is *noḵrî* — the masculine forms of the pair used of the *"strange woman"*
(*ʾiššāh zārāh … noḵriyyāh*, 2:16), and *zār* is the stranger whose debt one guarantees (6:1; 20:16;
27:13). Here, for once, the stranger is the right
person to have in the room. The saying is the courtier's rule of 25:6–7 applied to speech, and 25:27
had already said that *"it is not honorable to seek one's own honor"*.

**27:3–4 — Weights that cannot be carried.** *"A stone is heavy, and sand is a burden; but a fool's
provocation is heavier than both"* (3). The Hebrew plays on weight: *kōḇeḏ-ʾeḇen*, the *heaviness* of
a stone, and *kāḇēḏ*, *heavy*, of the fool's vexation. *Provocation* is *kaʿas*, the word of 12:16,
*"A fool shows his annoyance the same day"*. A stone can be set down; the fool's anger stays on
whoever has to bear it. *"Wrath is cruel, and anger is overwhelming; but who is able to stand before
jealousy?"* (4). *Wrath* is *ḥēmāh*, *jealousy* is *qinʾāh* — the two nouns of 6:34, *"jealousy arouses
the fury of the husband"*, where the husband's jealousy *"won't spare in the day of vengeance"*. Wrath
and anger pass like a flood; jealousy holds its ground. See [[Envy]].

**27:5–6 — Open rebuke and hidden love.** *"Better is open rebuke than hidden love"* (5). *Open* is
*məḡullāh*, *uncovered*; *hidden* is *məsuttāreṯ*. One verse after chapter 26 said hatred would be
*uncovered* in the assembly (26:26), this one prefers a rebuke uncovered to a love that never shows
itself. A friend who sees the fault and says nothing has loved too quietly to help. See
[[Better Is Little - The Better-Than Sayings of Proverbs]]. *"Faithful are the wounds of a friend;
although the kisses of an enemy are profuse"* (6). *Faithful* is *neʾĕmānîm*, the root of the
*"faithful messenger"* (25:13). *Enemy* is *śônēʾ*, *one who hates* — the word of 26:24's *"malicious
man"* who disguises himself with his lips. The kiss is the same lip-disguise made physical. *Profuse*
renders *naʿtārôṯ*, whose sense is uncertain; it may mean *abundant* or *excessive*, and the WEB's
choice is followed here. The wound and the kiss reverse what each seems: the wound is loyalty, the
kiss is hatred. The appendix before had made the honest answer the true kiss — *"An honest answer is like a kiss on
the lips"* (24:26). See [[The Field of the Sluggard]] and [[A Friend Loves at All Times - Proverbs 17.17 and Job 6.14]].

**27:7–10 — Appetite, home, and the near neighbour.** *"A full soul loathes a honeycomb; but to a
hungry soul, every bitter thing is sweet"* (7). *Full* is *śəḇēʿāh*, the verb *to be satisfied* that
27:20 will use of Sheol; *honeycomb* is *nōp̄eṯ*, the honey that dripped from the strange woman's lips
(5:3). Appetite decides taste: the same honey is loathed or longed for. The chapter before had warned
against eating honey to excess (25:16, 27). *"As a bird that wanders from her nest, so is a man who
wanders from his home"* (8). *Wanders* is *nôḏēḏ* both times, and *home* is *məqômô*, *his place*. The
saying does not say why he left; it only says that a bird out of the nest is exposed. *"Perfume and
incense bring joy to the heart; so does earnest counsel from a man's friend"* (9). The Hebrew of the
second line, *ûmeṯeq rēʿēhû mēʿăṣaṯ-nāp̄eš*, is difficult — literally something like *and the
sweetness of his friend from counsel of soul* — and the WEB's *earnest counsel* is one reading of it.
*Sweetness* (*meṯeq*) picks up 27:7's *sweet* (*māṯôq*): a friend's counsel is the honey the full soul
still wants.

*"Don't forsake your friend and your father's friend. Don't go to your brother's house in the day of
your disaster: better is a neighbor who is near than a distant brother"* (10). The friend inherited
from one's father is to be kept. *Disaster* is *ʾêḏ*, the calamity at which Wisdom laughed (*bəʾêḏḵem*,
1:26) and at which the gloater rejoices (17:5). The saying sets itself against 17:17's *"a brother is
born for adversity"*, and the wiki keeps both: the brother is born for adversity, and in adversity the
brother's house may be the wrong door. 18:24 had already found *"a friend who sticks closer than a
brother"*. Whether *distant* means geographical or relational distance the verse does not say. See
[[Brotherhood]].

**27:11 — The father's plea.** *"Be wise, my son, and bring joy to my heart, then I can answer my
tormentor"* (11). *My son* is the voice of chapters 1–9 and of 23:15, *"My son, if your heart is wise,
then my heart will be glad, even mine"*, here compressed into three Hebrew words, *ḥăḵam bənî
wəśammaḥ libbî*. The verse adds a reason the father needs a wise son: *wəʾāšîḇāh ḥōrəp̄î dāḇār*,
*that I may return a word to him who reproaches me*. *Return* is *šûḇ* again, the verb that ran through
chapter 26 (26:11, 15–16, 27). A wise son is the father's answer to his critics; 10:1 had put the same
claim in terms of a glad father and a grieving mother.

**27:12–13 — Two repeated sayings.** *"A prudent man sees danger and takes refuge; but the simple pass on,
and suffer for it"* (12) repeats 22:3 almost word for word in Hebrew; the only differences are two
missing conjunctions and the written form of *takes refuge* (see Notes). *"Take his garment when he puts
up collateral for a stranger. Hold it for a wayward woman!"* (13) repeats 20:16 with small differences:
20:16 opens *ləqaḥ* and has a written-and-read variant on its last noun, and 27:13 opens *qaḥ* and reads
*noḵriyyāh*, *a foreign woman*, with no variant. The suretyship warning of 6:1–5 is reapplied from the
creditor's side: whoever vouches for a stranger has given up his cloak already.

**27:14 — The loud blessing.** *"He who blesses his neighbor with a loud voice early in the morning, it
will be taken as a curse by him"* (14). *Curse* is *qəlālāh*, the noun of 26:2's undeserved curse. The
saying observes that the right words at the wrong time and volume become their opposite. 25:20 had made
the same observation about singing songs to a heavy heart. Whether the blessing is insincere flattery or
only tactless noise the saying leaves open.

**27:15–16 — The contentious wife.** *"A continual dropping on a rainy day and a contentious wife are
alike: restraining her is like restraining the wind, or like grasping oil in his right hand"* (15–16).
*Continual dropping* is *delep̄ ṭôrēḏ*, the phrase of 19:13, *"A wife's quarrels are a continual
dripping"*; *contentious wife* is *ʾēšeṯ midyānîm*, with the same written-and-read variant as 21:9 and
25:24. Verse 16 is obscure in Hebrew (*ṣōp̄nęhā ṣāp̄an-rûaḥ wəšemen yəmînô yiqrāʾ*, literally *whoever
hides her hides wind, and oil his right hand calls*); the WEB's *restraining … grasping* is an
interpretive rendering. On any reading the point is what cannot be held: wind, oil, and a quarrel.
See [[The Contentious Woman - Proverbs 19.13, 21.9, 19, 25.24, and 27.15]].

**27:17–19 — Iron, fig tree, and water.** Three sayings about what a person becomes in relation to
another. *"Iron sharpens iron; so a man sharpens his friend's countenance"* (17). *Barzel bəḇarzel
yāḥaḏ*: iron on iron, and the verb is usually taken from a root meaning *to be sharp*. *Countenance* is
*pənê*, *face*. Friendship here is friction, and it improves both. *"Whoever tends the fig tree shall
eat its fruit. He who looks after his master shall be honored"* (18). *Tends* is *nōṣēr* and *looks
after* is *šōmēr*, two verbs of guarding; *shall be honored* is *yəḵubbāḏ*, from *kāḇôḏ*, the honour that
did not fit the fool (26:1, 8). The servant is honoured as the fig-keeper eats, by the slow return on
care. *"Like water reflects a face, so a man's heart reflects the man"* (19). The Hebrew is spare:
*kammayim happānîm lappānîm kēn lēḇ-hāʾāḏām lāʾāḏām*, *as in water face to face, so the heart of man
to man*. It can be read as self-knowledge — a man sees himself in his own heart as in water — or as
mutual knowledge — one heart answers another. Both readings are recorded; the WEB takes the first.

**27:20–22 — What is never satisfied, what is tested, what is not removed.** *"Sheol and Abaddon are
never satisfied; and a man's eyes are never satisfied"* (20). The verb *lōʾ ṯiśbaʿnāh* is repeated for
both halves, and *śbʿ* is the root of 27:7's *full soul*. 15:11 had paired Sheol and Abaddon as open
before Yahweh; here they are paired as hungry. The eye's appetite is ranked with death's. Ecclesiastes
1:8 says *"The eye is not satisfied with seeing"* with the same verb and noun, and Proverbs 30:15–16
counts Sheol among the things that never say *"Enough"*. See
[[Never Satisfied - Proverbs 27.20, 30.15-16, Ecclesiastes 1.8, and Habakkuk 2.5]],
[[Abaddon Has No Covering - Job 26.6, Proverbs 15.11, and Revelation 9.11]], and [[Sheol]].

*"The crucible is for silver, and the furnace for gold; but man is refined by his praise"* (21). The
first line is identical in Hebrew to 17:3, *maṣrēp̄ lakkesep̄ wəḵûr lazzāhāḇ*. 17:3 ended *"but Yahweh
tests the hearts"*; 27:21 ends *wəʾîš ləp̄î mahălālô*, *and a man according to his praise*. The WEB
makes it *refined by*. The phrase can also mean that a man is tested by how he handles praise given to
him, or that he is assessed by what people praise him for. Either way, praise does the furnace's work,
and it closes the chapter's *hll* thread: do not praise yourself (1–2), and when others praise you, that
is the test (21). See [[I Shall Come Out Like Gold - Job 23.10 and the Refiner's Fire]] and
[[Testing]].

*"Though you grind a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with grain, yet his foolishness will not be
removed from him"* (22). *Fool* is *ʾĕwîl*, not chapter 26's *kəsîl*; *foolishness* is *ʾiwwaltô*. The
image is the grain pounded to separate the husk. The fool is pounded with it, and the husk stays.
22:15 had said that *"the rod of discipline drives it far from him"* of folly bound in a child's heart;
this saying describes the adult in whom no pounding reaches it, and 26:3's rod for the fool's back is
outdone. The Masoretic paragraph break follows.

**27:23–27 — Know your flocks.** *"Know well the state of your flocks, and pay attention to your
herds: for riches are not forever, nor does the crown endure to all generations"* (23–24). *State* is
*pənê*, *face* again: know the face of your flock as a friend's face is sharpened (17) and a heart's
face is seen in water (19). *Riches* is *ḥōsen*, *stored wealth*; *crown* is *nēzer*. The reason given
for tending flocks is that accumulated wealth and even royal dynasty do not last. 23:4–5 had said
riches *"sprout wings"*. See [[Riches Sprout Wings - Proverbs 23.4-5 and the Uncertainty of Riches]].
Then the cycle that does last: *"The hay is removed, and the new growth appears, the grasses of the
hills are gathered in. The lambs are for your clothing, and the goats are the price of a field. There
will be plenty of goats' milk for your food, for your family's food, and for the nourishment of your
servant girls"* (25–27). *Removed* is *gālāh*, the verb of *uncovered* (26:26; 27:5): the cut hay
uncovers the ground and the new shoots show. Wool clothes, goats buy land, milk feeds the house down to
its servant girls.

The poem is the chapter's answer to its first verse. No one knows what a day may bring (1), riches do
not last (24), and a crown passes; but the herdsman who knows his flock has clothing, land, and food
each season. It sits at the end of a collection copied in a king's court (25:1), and its point is that
the crown is less secure than the flock. See [[Work]] and [[Wealth and Possessions]].

**What the chapter adds.** Chapter 25 taught the courtier, and chapter 26 the reader who must deal with
fools, sluggards, and troublemakers. Chapter 27 turns to the people nearest: the friend, the father's
friend, the neighbour, the brother, the wife, the master, the household fed by goats' milk. Its ethic
is about trust under uncertainty. One cannot know tomorrow (1), praise one's own work (2), or read
another's kiss at face value (6); one can keep a friend who wounds, a neighbour who is near, and a flock
whose face one knows.

## People

- No named person appears. The chapter falls under the heading of 25:1, which attributes the
  collection to [[Solomon]] and its copying to the men of [[Hezekiah]]; neither is named here, and
  neither page is revised for this passage.
- [[God]] is not named in the chapter — neither *Yahweh* nor *ʾĕlōhîm*. The uncertainty of tomorrow (1)
  is stated without naming who knows it.
- The friend, the father's friend, the neighbour, the brother, the stranger, the contentious wife, the
  master, the fool, and the servant girls are types, not individuals.

## Notes

**Boundary and title.** The planned 27:1–27 unit is kept: the chapter has no internal heading, its
pastoral poem (23–27) closes it, and 28:1 begins a new run of antithetical sayings. The provisional
title, *Do Not Boast About Tomorrow*, is taken from 27:1 and kept. The Masoretic text marks a
*petuchah* after 27:22. Exactly one queue item is advanced.

**Language.** Read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* on 2026-10-26:
- 27:1 has *ʾal-tiṯhallēl bəyôm māḥār kî lōʾ-ṯēḏaʿ mah-yyēleḏ yôm*; 27:2 *yəhallelḵā zār wəlōʾ-p̄îḵā
  noḵrî wəʾal-śəp̄āṯeḵā*; 27:21 *wəʾîš ləp̄î mahălālô*.
- 27:3 has *kōḇeḏ-ʾeḇen … wəḵaʿas ʾĕwîl kāḇēḏ miššənêhem*; 12:16 *ʾĕwîl bayyôm yiwwāḏaʿ kaʿsô*.
- 27:4 has *ʾaḵzəriyyûṯ ḥēmāh wəšeṭep̄ ʾāp̄ … qinʾāh*; 6:34 *kî-qinʾāh ḥămaṯ-gāḇer*.
- 27:5 has *ṭôḇāh tôḵaḥaṯ məḡullāh mēʾahăḇāh məsuttāreṯ*; 26:26 *tiggāleh*; 27:25 *gālāh ḥāṣîr*.
- 27:6 has *neʾĕmānîm piṣʿê ʾôhēḇ wənaʿtārôṯ nəšîqôṯ śônēʾ*; 26:24 *yinnāḵēr śônēʾ*. The sense of
  *naʿtārôṯ* is not checked against a lexicon here.
- 27:7 has *nep̄eš śəḇēʿāh tāḇûs nōp̄eṯ … kol-mar māṯôq*; 5:3 *kî nōp̄eṯ tiṭṭōp̄nāh*; 27:20 *lōʾ
  ṯiśbaʿnāh* twice.
- 27:8 has *nôḏeḏeṯ min-qinnāh … nôḏēḏ mimməqômô*.
- 27:9 has *šemen ûqəṭōreṯ yəśammaḥ-lēḇ ûmeṯeq rēʿēhû mēʿăṣaṯ-nāp̄eš*. The second line's sense is
  disputed; no commentary was consulted.
- 27:10 has a ketiv/qere: written *wrʿh*, read *wərēaʿ* (*and the friend of*). *Bəyôm ʾêḏeḵā*; 1:26
  *bəʾêḏḵem*; 17:5 *śāmēaḥ ləʾêḏ*.
- 27:11 has *ḥăḵam bənî wəśammaḥ libbî wəʾāšîḇāh ḥōrəp̄î dāḇār*; 23:15 *bənî ʾim-ḥāḵam libbeḵā yiśmaḥ
  libbî gam-ʾānî*; 17:5 *ḥērēp̄ ʿōśēhû*.
- 27:12 has *ʿārûm rāʾāh rāʿāh nistār pəṯāʾyim ʿāḇrû neʿĕnāšû*; 22:3 has *wənistār* (written *wystr*)
  and *ûp̄əṯāyim … wəneʿĕnāšû*.
