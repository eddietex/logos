# Answer a Fool

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 26:1–28

## Summary

The second chapter of the collection *"the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out"* (25:1) is the
most tightly grouped chapter in Proverbs. It has three portraits, each built from a run of sayings on
one figure. **The fool** (*kəsîl*) is named in eleven of the first twelve verses — every verse but
26:2 — and **the sluggard** (*ʿāṣēl*) in each of the next four (13–16). The last twelve verses
(17–28) have no single name. They describe the man who makes trouble with his mouth: the meddler, the
prankster, the whisperer, the quarrelsome man, and the one who hides hatred behind charming speech.
The Masoretic text marks a paragraph break (*petuchah*) after 26:21.

The chapter keeps the form of chapter 25: an image laid beside a human type. But the images have
turned harsh. Chapter 25 had snow at harvest, apples of gold, cold water. Here there are a whip and a
bridle, a lame man's legs, a thorn in a drunkard's hand, a dog at its vomit, a door on its hinges, a
madman with torches, and a pit. Yahweh is not named anywhere in the chapter.

One phrase links the three portraits: *wise in his own eyes* (*ḥāḵām bəʿênāyw*). The fool must be
answered *"lest he be wise in his own eyes"* (5). Then *"Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There
is more hope for a fool than for him"* (12). Then *"The sluggard is wiser in his own eyes than seven
men who answer with discretion"* (16). The self-assured man is the end point of the fool's portrait
and of the sluggard's. See
[[Wise in His Own Eyes - Proverbs 3.7, 26.12, Isaiah 5.21, and Romans 12.16]].

**26:1–3 — What fits a fool.** *"Like snow in summer, and as rain in harvest, so honor is not fitting
for a fool"* (1). Chapter 25 had used snow at harvest as an image of refreshment (25:13). Here snow in
summer and rain in harvest are things out of season, as honour on a fool is. *Honor* is *kāḇôḏ*, the
word that opened and closed chapter 25 (25:2, 27), and it returns at 26:8. Then the one verse in the
run with no fool in it: *"Like a fluttering sparrow, like a darting swallow, so the undeserved curse
doesn't come to rest"* (2). *Undeserved* is *ḥinnām*, *for nothing* — the word of 3:30's *"Don't
strive with a man without cause"* and of Satan's *"Does Job fear God for nothing?"* (Job 1:9). A curse
with no cause behind it flies about and does not land. The Hebrew has a written-and-read variant here
(see the Notes): the written text says the curse does *not* come, the reading tradition says it comes
*to him*. The WEB follows the written text. Then: *"A whip is for the horse, a bridle for the donkey,
and a rod for the back of fools!"* (3). This is 10:13's *"a rod is for the back of him who is void of
understanding"*, with the same *šēḇeṭ ləḡēw*. The fool is put among the animals, as creatures moved by
force rather than by words.

**26:4–5 — Answer him; don't answer him.** *"Don't answer a fool according to his folly, lest you
also be like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes."* Two
contradictory commands stand side by side, and the second repeats the first word for word without the
*don't*: *ʿănēh kəsîl kəʾiwwaltô* against *ʾal-taʿan kəsîl kəʾiwwaltô*. The placement must be
deliberate. A compiler who put them in adjacent verses saw the contradiction and meant the reader to
see it too. They are told apart only by their reasons, and each names a different danger. Answer the
fool on his own terms, and you become like him. Leave him unanswered, and he takes your silence as
his victory. Neither command can be applied alone. Knowing which danger is the greater in a given case
is the wisdom the pair requires, and no rule supplies it.

The Talmud raises exactly this difficulty. In Shabbat 30b the Sages are said to have *"sought to
suppress the book of Proverbs as well because its statements contradict each other"*, and this pair is
the example. They resolve it by assigning the verses to different cases: one answers a fool *"making
claims about Torah matters"*, and does not answer one *"making claims about mundane matters"*
(Sefaria, William Davidson English). The wiki records the resolution without adopting it. The chapter
itself suggests a different point: the next five verses are about what happens when a proverb is used
by someone who does not know when to use it.

**26:6–12 — The fool and the proverb.** *"One who sends a message by the hand of a fool is cutting off
feet and drinking violence"* (6). The faithful messenger of 25:13 refreshed *"those who send him"*;
the sluggard of 10:26 was smoke to the eyes of *"those who send him"*. The fool sent with a message
undoes the errand and harms its sender: *cutting off feet* is cutting off his own means of getting
anywhere, and *drinking violence* (*ḥāmās*) is swallowing the damage. Then a pair with one refrain:
*"Like the legs of the lame that hang loose, so is a parable in the mouth of fools"* (7), and *"Like a
thorn bush that goes into the hand of a drunkard, so is a parable in the mouth of fools"* (9). In
Hebrew the second line of both is identical, *ûmāšāl bəp̄î ḵəsîlîm*. *Parable* is *māšāl*, the word
in the book's own title, *mišlê šəlōmōh*, *"The proverbs of Solomon"* (1:1). **Here Proverbs reflects
on itself.** A proverb in a fool's mouth is either useless, like a lame leg, or dangerous, like a
thorn waved by a drunk who cannot feel it. The saying is true either way; what the fool lacks is the
judgment 26:4–5 just demanded.

Between the two comes *"As one who binds a stone in a sling, so is he who gives honor to a fool"* (8).
A stone tied into the sling cannot be thrown; honour given to a fool is wasted the same way. The rare
noun *margēmāh* is usually taken as *sling*, but this reading was not checked against a lexicon in
this pass. *"As an archer who wounds all, so is he who hires a fool or he who hires those who pass
by"* (10) is among the most obscure verses in the book. Its first words (*rab məḥôlēl-kōl*) have been
read in very different ways, and the WEB's *archer* is one choice among several. The wiki takes the
WEB as it stands: hiring a fool or a passer-by does indiscriminate harm.

*"As a dog that returns to his vomit, so is a fool who repeats his folly"* (11). *Returns* is *šāḇ*,
from *šûḇ*. The *vomit* (*qēʾô*) has the root of 25:16's honey eaten to excess, *wahăqēʾṯô*. 2 Peter
2:22 quotes this as *"the true proverb"* about those who knew *"the way of righteousness"* and turned
back. See [[The Dog Returns to His Vomit - Proverbs 26.11 and 2 Peter 2.22]]. And the portrait ends
with a figure worse than its subject: *"Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a
fool than for him"* (12). The second line, *tiqwāh liḵsîl mimmennû*, comes back word for word at
29:20, said there of *"a man who is hasty in his words"*. It is the book's only comparison in which
the fool comes out ahead. The fool can still be corrected, if only by the rod of 26:3. The man sure of
his own wisdom cannot.

**26:13–16 — The sluggard.** Four sayings, two of them already heard in some form. *"The sluggard
says, 'There is a lion in the road! A fierce lion roams the streets!'"* (13) varies 22:13, *"There is
a lion outside! I will be killed in the streets!"* 22:13 had one word for lion (*ʾărî*); 26:13 has two
(*šaḥal … ʾărî*), and drops the sluggard's *"I will be killed"*. *"As the door turns on its hinges, so
does the sluggard on his bed"* (14) is new: he moves all night and goes nowhere. *"The sluggard buries
his hand in the dish. He is too lazy to bring it back to his mouth"* (15) repeats 19:24's first line
exactly in Hebrew (*ṭāman ʿāṣēl yāḏô baṣṣallāḥaṯ*). Its second line differs: 19:24 said he *"will not
… bring it to his mouth again"*, and 26:15 says he is *nilʾāh*, *weary*, too tired to do it. And
*"The sluggard is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who answer with discretion"* (16). There is a
play on one verb here: the hand the sluggard is too weary *to bring back* (*lahăšîḇāh*, 15) and the
seven who *bring back* a sensible answer (*məšîḇê ṭāʿam*, 16) both use the causative of *šûḇ*. The
sluggard returns nothing, not even food to his mouth, and still rates himself above seven men who
return wise answers. 22:21 had named that skill as the goal of the collection, *"to give sound answers
to the ones who sent you"*. See
[[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]].

**26:17–19 — The meddler and the prankster.** *"Like one who grabs a dog's ears is one who passes by
and meddles in a quarrel not his own"* (17). He has hold of something he cannot safely keep holding or
let go of. Hebrew sets two forms of one root side by side, *ʿōḇēr miṯʿabbēr*, *passing by … meddling*.
*Miṯʿabbēr* is the same verb form that 14:16 used of the fool (*"hot headed"*) and 20:2 of the one who
*provokes* a king. It can therefore also be read as *becoming furious* over a quarrel not his own,
rather than *meddling*. The wiki notes both readings and does not decide. *"Like a madman who shoots
torches, arrows, and death, is the man who deceives his neighbor and says, 'Am I not joking?'"*
(18–19). These two verses are a single comparison. The deceiver's harm is real, and his excuse —
*məśaḥēq*, *joking* — does not unshoot the arrow.

**26:20–22 — Fuel for the quarrel.** *"For lack of wood a fire goes out. Without gossip, a quarrel
dies down"* (20). Strife is a fire, and the *nirgān*, the whisperer, is its wood; take him away and it
dies down. 16:28 had said *"A whisperer separates close friends"* with the same noun. *"As coals are to
hot embers, and wood to fire, so is a contentious man to kindling strife"* (21). The *contentious man*
is *ʾîš midyānîm* in the read text, the masculine counterpart of the *ʾēšeṯ midyānîm*, the
*contentious woman*, of 25:24 and its doublets. See
[[The Contentious Woman - Proverbs 19.13, 21.9, 19, 25.24, and 27.15]]. Then *"The words of a
whisperer are as dainty morsels, they go down into the innermost parts"* (22) — a repeat, letter for
letter, of 18:8, which the WEB translated there as *"The words of a gossip are like dainty morsels"*.
The Hebrew is identical; only the English varies. Gossip is swallowed eagerly and lodges deep.

**26:23–26 — The disguised hater.** *"Like silver dross on an earthen vessel are the lips of a fervent
one with an evil heart"* (23). *Dross* is *sîḡîm*, the word of 25:4, *"Take away the dross from the
silver"*. A cheap clay pot coated with the residue of smelting shines like silver and is worth
nothing. *Fervent* is *dōlqîm*, *burning*: warm lips over a bad heart. *"A malicious man disguises
himself with his lips, but he harbors evil in his heart"* (24). The *malicious man* is *śônēʾ*, *one
who hates*. *"When his speech is charming, don't believe him; for there are seven abominations in his
heart"* (25). *Seven abominations* is the number of 6:16, *"six things which Yahweh hates; yes, seven
which are an abomination to him"*, and the list there began with *"a lying tongue"* (6:17), which ends
this chapter (26:28). *"His malice may be concealed by deception, but his wickedness will be exposed
in the assembly"* (26). *Malice* is *śinʾāh*, *hatred*; *concealed* is *tikkasseh*, *it is covered*;
*exposed* is *tiggāleh*, *it is uncovered*. 10:12 used the same two words: *"Hatred stirs up strife,
but love covers all wrongs."* There love did the covering. Here hatred covers itself, and the
covering does not hold. The exposure is public (*bəqāhāl*, *in the assembly*), and no agent is named.
See [[Love Covers Wrongs - Proverbs 10.12, 1 Peter 4.8, and James 5.20]].

**26:27–28 — The pit and the stone.** *"Whoever digs a pit shall fall into it. Whoever rolls a stone,
it will come back on him"* (27). This is retribution in its self-acting form, with no judge and no
God named. The pit catches its digger, and the stone *returns* (*tāšûḇ*, *šûḇ* again) on the one who
rolled it. The first lecture made the same point about the gang that *"lay wait for their own blood"*
(1:18), and the image of the pit recurs in Psalm 7:15–16, Psalm 57:6, Ecclesiastes 10:8, and
Proverbs 28:10. See [[Whoever Digs a Pit - Proverbs 26.27, Psalm 7.15, and Ecclesiastes 10.8]]. Then
the chapter's last line: *"A lying tongue hates those it hurts; and a flattering mouth works ruin"*
(28). The verb is *yiśnāʾ*, *hates*, the same root as 26:24's *śônēʾ* and 26:26's *śinʾāh*. The
section that began with the hater's disguise ends by naming what the lie itself is: hatred of its
victim. *Flattering* is *ḥālāq*, *smooth*.

**What the chapter adds.** Chapter 25 taught the reader how to stand before a king. Chapter 26
teaches him to recognise three kinds of men he will have to deal with, and what each costs: the fool
who cannot be given honour, sent on an errand, or trusted with a proverb; the sluggard who is sure he
is wiser than seven; and the troublemaker whose quarrel is fed by whispers and whose hatred is
covered by charm. The verb *šûḇ* runs through all three portraits — the dog returning to its vomit
(11), the hand not brought back and the answers brought back (15–16), the stone returning on its
roller (27). The fool goes back to his folly, the sluggard brings nothing back, and the schemer's own
stone comes back on him. The paired commands of 26:4–5 stand over all of it. Proverbs is a book of
rules that cannot be applied by rule, and 26:7 and 9 say so in the book's own word for itself.

## People

- No named person appears. The chapter falls under the heading of 25:1, which attributes the
  collection to [[Solomon]] and its copying to the men of [[Hezekiah]]; neither is named here, and
  neither page is revised for this passage.
- [[God]] is not named in the chapter — neither *Yahweh* nor *ʾĕlōhîm*. The pit and the rolled stone
  of 26:27 act without him, and the exposure of 26:26 has no stated agent.
- The fool, the sluggard, the messenger, the meddler, the madman, the whisperer, the contentious man,
  and the disguised hater are types, not individuals.

## Places

- None. The *road* and *streets* of 26:13 and the *assembly* of 26:26 are unnamed, and the *pit*
  (*šaḥaṯ*) of 26:27 is a trap dug in the ground, not [[Sheol]].

## Notes

**Boundary and title.** The planned 26:1–28 unit is kept: chapter 26 is unified by its three
portraits, and 27:1 turns to boasting about tomorrow. The provisional title, *Answer a Fool*, is taken
from 26:5 and kept. The Masoretic text marks a *petuchah* after 26:21. Exactly one queue item is
advanced.

**Language.** Read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* on 2026-10-26:
- *Kəsîl* or its plural occurs in 26:1, 3–12 and in no other verse of the chapter; *ʿāṣēl* in 26:13–16.
- 26:1 has *kaššeleḡ baqqayiṣ wəḵammāṭār baqqāṣîr kēn lōʾ-nāʾweh liḵsîl kāḇôḏ*; 26:8 *kēn-nôṯēn
  liḵsîl kāḇôḏ*. 25:13 has *kəṣinnaṯ-šeleḡ bəyôm qāṣîr*.
- 26:2 has *kēn qilĕlaṯ ḥinnām* followed by a ketiv/qere: written *lʾ* (*lōʾ*, *not*), read *lô* (*to
  him*), then *ṯāḇōʾ*. The WEB's *"doesn't come to rest"* follows the written form. 3:30 has
  *ʾal-tārîḇ ʿim-ʾāḏām ḥinnām*; Job 1:9 *haḥinnām yārēʾ ʾiyyôḇ ʾĕlōhîm*.
- 26:3 has *wəšēḇeṭ ləḡēw kəsîlîm*; 10:13 *wəšēḇeṭ ləḡēw ḥăsar-lēḇ*.

  pen-yihyeh ḥāḵām bəʿênāyw*.
- 26:6 has *ḥāmās šōṯeh šōlēaḥ dəḇārîm bəyaḏ-kəsîl*; 25:13 *ṣîr neʾĕmān ləšōlḥāyw … yāšîḇ*; 10:26
  *kēn heʿāṣēl ləšōlḥāyw*.
- 26:7 and 26:9 both end *ûmāšāl bəp̄î ḵəsîlîm*. 1:1 opens *mišlê šəlōmōh*.
