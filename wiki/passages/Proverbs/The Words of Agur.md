# The Words of Agur

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 30:1–14

## Summary

A new heading introduces Agur, son of Jakeh, speaking to Ithiel and Ucal in the WEB (1).
Agur begins with a confession of ignorance and deficient understanding (2–3). Questions about
heaven, wind, waters, and the earth challenge anyone who claims mastery over creation, ending
with the names of its governor and his son (4). The questions receive no named answer here.

The human speaker’s limitation stands beside God’s flawless words and protection of those who
take refuge in him. Adding to those words risks reproof and exposure as a liar (5–6).
Agur then makes two requests: distance from falsehood, and food sufficient for his need, without
poverty or riches. Fullness might bring denial of Yahweh; deprivation might tempt him to steal
and dishonor God’s name (7–9).

The remaining sayings turn toward social conduct. Do not slander a servant to his master (10).
Four portraits describe a generation that dishonors parents, mistakes its own filth for purity,
looks down on others, and consumes poor people with teeth likened to weapons (11–14).

## Notes

### A different voice, not a recovered biography

The heading ends the attribution to Solomon’s collection copied by Hezekiah’s men (25:1–29:27)
and introduces Agur. This page follows the stored WEB’s treatment of Jakeh, Ithiel, and Ucal as
names. It does not infer their nationality, date, professions, or a teacher-pupil relationship.
The WEB calls the utterance a revelation; no vision or call narrative follows. No exact Hebrew
wording or alternative parsing is asserted without a source-language check.

The confession in verses 2–3 need not mean that all wisdom is impossible. The speaker immediately
teaches reliance on God’s word. Proverbs 9:10 commends knowledge of the Holy One; this speaker
refuses to claim that knowledge as his possession. His humility anticipates the exposure of
self-declared purity in verse 12. The distinction between teachability and mastery is an
interpretation of the unit’s movement, not an additional quotation.

### Questions that reach beyond the speaker

Job 28:23–27 associates God’s knowledge of wisdom with wind and waters; Job 38:4–18 asks about
creation and the limits of human access; Job 42:3 confesses knowledge exceeded. These are close
comparisons for Agur’s questions, without establishing which author knew the other.

Verse 4 leaves the son unnamed. Reading it alongside John 3:12–13, which identifies the Son of Man
as the one descending from heaven, is a Christian canonical comparison. Proverbs does not name
Jesus, and John does not introduce his statement as a quotation of Agur. No separate person page
is created for an identity the present text leaves unresolved.

### Received words and their limits

Psalm 18:30 and 2 Samuel 22:31 pair God’s tested word with refuge under his shield; Proverbs 30:5
closely resembles that pairing in the WEB. Verse 6 adds a warning against addition, comparable to
Deuteronomy 4:2. It is not a table of contents for a later canon or a prohibition on every act of
explanation. It does hold the person claiming to speak God’s words answerable for what is added.

**Explicit tension:** the shield of verse 5 stands beside Job 2:3, 7, where a man whose integrity
God affirms is harmed. Trust is commended; freedom from all injury cannot be inferred as a test
of the trusting person’s character.

### Enough, and the danger on either side

The two requests concern falsehood and provision. Agur asks for neither extreme of possessions,
with food enough to meet his need (8). He fears his own possible denial or theft (9); he is not
classifying all rich people as unbelievers or all poor people as thieves. Nor is he prescribing
an amount that everyone must own.

**Explicit tension within Proverbs:** Wisdom offers riches and full treasuries in 8:18–21;
Agur asks to be spared riches in 30:8–9. Wealth can be a gift and can endanger allegiance. That
interpretation preserves both statements rather than silently converting either into its opposite.
The question he fears saying is Pharaoh’s question in Exodus 5:2, now imagined in an ordinary
person’s fullness. Manna’s measured sufficiency (Exodus 16:16–21) and Matthew 6:9–13’s prayer for
bread, holiness of God’s name, and deliverance from temptation supply further comparisons.

### The servant and the four generations

Verse 10 addresses the person with access to a master, forbidding slander against a servant.
The warning gives the servant’s grievance moral weight; it specifies no tribunal or punishment.
**Explicit tension of perspective:** the servant portrayed as unresponsive in Proverbs 29:19
must be read beside this protection from a false accuser and Job 31:13–15’s obligation to hear
servants. None of these sayings should erase the others.

Verses 11–14 repeat the generation formula in the WEB. They describe kinds of conduct without
identifying four historical generations or dating an era. Dishonoring father and mother recalls
Exodus 20:12; supposed purity exposes self-deception rather than prescribing a washing rite.
The teeth and jaws in verse 14 are predatory imagery for consuming the poor, not a report of
literal cannibalism. The social warning prevents the prayer for personal sufficiency from
becoming indifference to other people’s need.
