# The Wicked Flee When No One Pursues

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 28:1–28

## Summary

Proverbs 28 returns to a dense sequence of contrasts: wickedness and righteousness, rich and poor,
law and refusal, wise conduct and self-trust. It opens with the wicked fleeing without pursuit and
the righteous bold as a lion (1). Rebellion and wise government follow (2); the public consequences
of wicked ascendancy recur at 12 and 28. The chapter is not a single argument, but these recurring
concerns connect its household, economic, and political sayings.

Law shapes allegiance, understanding, family honor, and prayer (4–9). Poverty and wealth do not
settle anyone’s moral standing: a needy person can oppress (3), a poor person can have integrity and
discernment (6, 11), and a rich person can mistake wealth for wisdom. Interest taken at others’
expense eventually benefits someone who pities the poor (8). Confession and renunciation open the
possibility of mercy (13); hardening leads to trouble (14).

The second half brings the same tests to rulers, killers, workers, and households. Predatory rule is
condemned (15–16); bloodguilt leaves a fugitive (17); integrity and work are commended (18–20).
Partiality, grasping for riches, flattery, theft from parents, and self-trust are exposed (21–26).
Giving to the poor closes the economic thread (27), and the fall of the wicked lets the righteous
thrive (28).

## Notes

### Fear, boldness, and people who hide

The lion is an image of righteous confidence in verse 1 and of a wicked ruler’s menace in verse 15.
The same animal does different work in the two comparisons. Flight without pursuit recalls Leviticus
26:36’s covenant warning, but Proverbs does not identify its saying as a quotation. See
[[Bold as a Lion - Proverbs 28.1, Leviticus 26.36, and Job 3.25]].

**Tension within the chapter and with Job:** verse 18 promises safety to the blameless, yet verses
12 and 28 acknowledge that wicked power makes people hide; those hiding people are not identified as
wicked. Job is explicitly blameless and retains integrity while suffering (Job 1:1; 2:3, 7), and
speaks of fear and trouble (3:25–26). Reading 28:1 as a diagnostic rule that fear proves guilt would
contradict that witness. Verse 14 also blesses ongoing fear; its contrast with hardening suggests
moral vigilance. The WEB does not supply an object of fear there, so “fear of Yahweh” is a
contextual interpretation, not a quotation of the verse.

### Keeping the law and hearing before speaking

Verses 4, 7, and 9 name the law in the WEB: it separates allegiance to wickedness from resistance,
makes a son wise, and governs the acceptability of prayer. Verse 5 places seeking Yahweh beside
understanding justice; its “fully” concerns that subject and does not promise omniscience. The
chapter does not identify a specific law code. Comparisons with Exodus concern shared ethical
subjects: interest (Exodus 22:25), partiality (23:3, 6–8), and honoring parents and refusing theft
(20:12, 15).

Prayer in verse 9 is no automatic exception to moral accountability. Read with verse 13, however,
the warning does not prohibit a wrongdoer from confessing and turning. The chapter rejects refusal
to listen, not every approach to God by someone who has sinned.

### Poverty, wealth, and what they cannot prove

Verse 3’s oppressor is a needy man in the stored WEB. His oppression is destructive rain, removing the crops rain
should nourish. Verse 6 resembles 19:1, but explicitly names the opposing figure as rich and speaks
of perverse ways rather than perverse lips and folly. Verse 11 makes the poor person the discerning
critic of the wealthy one.

Verse 8 condemns “excessive interest” in the WEB; Exodus 22:25 forbids charging interest to the poor
person among God’s people. The English qualifier in Proverbs should not be used to rewrite the scope
of the Exodus command. No Hebrew lexical comparison is asserted. Verse 19 resembles 12:11, pairing
work on one’s land with food and fantasies with failure, but concludes with poverty rather than lack
of understanding. Oppression in verse 3 remains an internal check on blaming every hungry farmer for
laziness.

Haste for riches and stinginess (20, 22) oppose faithfulness and generosity. Giving to the poor and
closing one’s eyes (27) have opposite outcomes. The words remain strong promises, but Job’s losses
keep them from becoming guarantees of uninterrupted wealth or a way to blame every poor person.

### Confession, correction, and denial

Verse 13 joins confessing sins with renouncing them. It does not name the giver of mercy, the
audience of confession, or a ritual procedure. It is different from the covering of another person’s
offense commended in 10:12 and 17:9: the wrongdoer cannot call concealment love. Job 31:33–34 denies
concealing wrongdoing; the duty to confess cannot justify demanding that he invent the crimes his
friends allege.

Rebuke earns favor afterward (23), continuing 27:5–6’s praise of open rebuke and faithful wounds.
Verse 24 then puts an excuse in the parental thief’s mouth. Denial does not make the act harmless;
he is allied with destruction. The wise son of verse 7 and this child belong in the same chapter,
setting obedience against both bad company and damage to the household.

### Public power, bloodguilt, and competing kinds of conflict

Verse 2 contrasts unstable rule with understanding that sustains order. Verses 15–16 judge
government from the helpless person’s position: power can be predatory, and tyranny reveals
deficient judgment. Hatred of ill-gotten gain is commended beside it. No king, dynasty, or location
is named.

Verse 17 describes a man tormented by life blood, a fugitive until death, without support. It does
not state whether the killing was intentional or provide trial rules. Exodus 21:12–14 distinguishes
deliberate killing from an unintentional death and grants a place to flee in the latter case. The
proverb’s generality and the law’s distinctions are retained; it cannot supply an exceptionless
instruction to deny every killer refuge.

Verse 21 condemns partiality and observes wrongdoing for a piece of bread. A judicial setting is
plausible beside the chapter’s justice sayings, but no judge is explicitly named. Verse 25 locates
strife in greed; verse 4 approves contending with wickedness. They distinguish corrupt motives from
necessary moral opposition rather than forbidding every dispute.
