# The Glory of Kings

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 25:1–28

## Summary

A new heading opens the book's last great collection: *"These also are proverbs of Solomon, which
the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out"* (25:1). The heading names not an author but the men who
handled the sayings after him, and it places the compilation of chapters 25–29 in the court of
[[Hezekiah]], king of Judah, more than two centuries after [[Solomon]]. The
*"also"* looks back to 10:1 and asks the reader to hear these sayings as a second Solomonic
collection, with the *"words of the wise"* (22:17–24:34) between them.

The form changes as the heading does. The antithetical couplets of chapters 10–15 — *this* man,
*but* that one — give way to comparisons: snow at harvest, apples of gold, clouds without rain, a
bad tooth, a muddied spring, a city without walls. Most of the chapter's sayings set an image beside
a human type and leave the reader to see the likeness. In Hebrew many have no *like* at all; the two
halves are simply laid side by side (see the Notes).

The chapter has two halves, which the Masoretic text divides with a paragraph mark after 25:13 and
25:20 (*petuchah*), and which run in sequence: the king and his court (2–7), the neighbour and the
court of law (8–10), words and their effects (11–15), excess and the neighbour (16–20), the enemy
(21–22), and a closing set of comparisons that return to where it began (23–28).

**25:2–3 — Concealing and searching.** *"It is the glory of God to conceal a thing, but the glory of
kings is to search out a matter. As the heavens for height, and the earth for depth, so the hearts
of kings are unsearchable."* God (*ʾĕlōhîm*, not Yahweh) and kings share one noun, *glory*
(*kāḇôḏ*), and divide two verbs: God's is to *conceal* (*hastēr*), the king's is to *search out*
(*ḥăqōr*). The world as Proverbs sees it is not transparent. Its order is real but hidden, and
finding it out is a royal task — and the task this book sets its reader. Then the turn: the searcher cannot himself be searched. *Unsearchable* is
*ʾên ḥēqer*, *there is no searching* — the noun of the verb just given to kings. The king who
investigates every matter is, to his subjects, as far beyond reach as the sky and the deep. The
saying is not cynical; it states the distance at which a subject stands from power, which the next
four verses then teach him to live with. Compare *"The king's heart is in Yahweh's hand"* (21:1):
unsearchable to men, not to God. And compare the law's *"The secret things belong to Yahweh our God;
but the things that are revealed belong to us"* (Deuteronomy 29:29), which uses the same Hebrew root
for what is hidden. See [[The Secret Things - Proverbs 25.2 and Deuteronomy 29.29]].

**25:4–5 — The refiner and the throne.** *"Take away the dross from the silver, and material comes
out for the refiner; Take away the wicked from the king's presence, and his throne will be
established in righteousness."* Two verses with one verb at their head, *hāḡô*, *remove*, and an
image followed by its application. The dross is the impurity separated out when silver is smelted;
removed, it leaves material the smith can use. The wicked at court are the dross of the kingdom.
*"His throne will be established in righteousness"* repeats the end of 16:12 almost word for word
(*kî ḇiṣḏāqāh yikkôn kissēʾ*, *"for the throne is established by righteousness"*), but where 16:12
said kings must not *do* wrong, 25:5 says they must not *keep* it near them. The righteousness of a
reign depends on its counsellors.

**25:6–7 — The place at court.** *"Don't exalt yourself in the presence of the king, or claim a
place among great men; for it is better that it be said to you, 'Come up here,' than that you should
be put lower in the presence of the prince, whom your eyes have seen."* Advice for a courtier, and
from the subject's side: having been told how the king should treat the wicked, the reader is now
told how to stand before him. *Exalt yourself* is *tiṯhaddar*, a reflexive form of the noun for
splendour — do not put on your own majesty. The reason is put as a *better-than* saying, and as
public speech: what matters is what is *said to you* in the hearing of the court. *"Come up here"*
is an invitation; *"put lower"* (*hašpîl*) is a demotion, seen by everyone. Jesus' parable of the
wedding guests (Luke 14:7–11) tells the same scene at a feast — the guest told *"Friend, move up
higher"* rather than shamed down to the lowest place — and gives it the reason this proverb leaves
unsaid: *"everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be
exalted."* See [[Come Up Here - Proverbs 25.6-7 and Luke 14.7-11]]. The final clause, *"whom your
eyes have seen"*, is difficult; the Masoretic text attaches it to 25:7, while many translations
carry it into 25:8 (*"what your eyes have seen, do not hastily bring to court"*). The WEB follows
the verse division and the wiki does not decide.

**25:8–10 — The neighbour in court.** *"Don't be hasty in bringing charges to court. What will you
do in the end when your neighbor shames you? Debate your case with your neighbor, and don't betray
the confidence of another; lest one who hears it put you to shame, and your bad reputation never
depart."* From the royal court to the court of law, and from honour to shame: the verbs are *shame*
(*haḵlîm*, 8) and *put to shame* (10). The danger is not losing the case but winning it badly. Go to
law in haste, and you may have no answer *in the end* — *ʾaḥărîṯ*, the latter end that ran through
[[Do Not Envy Sinners]] — when your neighbour turns the matter round. Better to argue it out with him
directly, and in doing so not to use what someone else told you in confidence (*sôḏ*, the secret
counsel 11:13 and 20:19 say a gossip *reveals*, here with the same verb *gālāh*). The penalty for
that is permanent: *dibbāh*, an evil report, *"never depart"*. The sequence follows the
prohibitions of 24:28–29 about the neighbour at law, and it leans the same way: settle, do not
escalate. Jesus' instruction to agree with an adversary on the way to court (Matthew 5:25) has a
similar shape, though nothing marks it as an allusion.

**25:11–15 — Words in their place.** Five sayings about speech, each built on a comparison.
*"A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver"* (11): the word is the right one
for its moment — the Hebrew has *a word spoken on its* ʾ*opnāyw*, a rare noun often taken as *its
circumstances* or *its time*, though its exact sense is uncertain. 15:23 had said *"a word at the
right time, how good it is!"*; this is the same idea made into an ornament. *"As an earring of gold,
and an ornament of fine gold, so is a wise reprover to an obedient ear"* (12): reproof is beautiful
only when it lands on a *hearing ear* (*ʾōzen šōmaʿaṯ*), the phrase 20:12 says Yahweh made. The
reprover and the listener make the ornament between them. *"As the cold of snow in the time of
harvest, so is a faithful messenger"* (13): not snowfall, which would ruin the crop, but the coolness
of snow brought down to labourers in the heat. 13:17 had already said the faithful envoy (*ṣîr*)
brings healing; here he *"refreshes the soul of his masters"*. Then the reverse: *"As clouds and wind
without rain, so is he who boasts of gifts deceptively"* (14) — the promise of rain in a dry land,
withheld; a man who boasts of a gift he never gives. And the close: *"By patience a ruler is
persuaded. A soft tongue breaks the bone"* (15). *Patience* is *ʾōreḵ ʾappayim*, length of nostrils,
slowness to anger; *persuaded* is *yəputteh*, from the verb that meant *entice* at 1:10 and
*deceive* at 24:28, here used without censure. *Soft* is *rakkāh*, the adjective of 15:1's *"gentle
answer"*. A tongue that is soft breaks what is hard — the paradox carried by an image of bone.

**25:16–17 — Enough.** *"Have you found honey? Eat as much as is sufficient for you, lest you eat too
much, and vomit it. Let your foot be seldom in your neighbor's house, lest he be weary of you, and
hate you."* A pair joined by one verb: *śāḇaʿ*, to be *full*. Eat honey until you are *sated* with it
and you vomit it up; visit your neighbour until he is *sated* with you and he hates you. Honey was
commended at 24:13 (*"My son, eat honey, for it is good"*); this does not retract the commendation
but measures it. What is good in its measure is spoiled by excess, and the neighbour is one such
good.

**25:18–20 — Three betrayals.** *"A man who gives false testimony against his neighbor is like a
club, a sword, or a sharp arrow"* (18). The second half is, in Hebrew, the ninth commandment turned
from command to description: *ʾîš ʿōneh ḇərēʿēhû ʿēḏ šāqer* against Exodus 20:16's *lōʾ-taʿăneh
ḇərēʿăḵā ʿēḏ šāqer* — the same verb, preposition, noun, and object. The image says what such
testimony does: it is a weapon at close, middle, and long range. See
[[A False Witness Shall Not Be Unpunished - Proverbs 19 and Exodus 23]]. *"Confidence in someone
unfaithful in time of trouble is like a bad tooth, or a lame foot"* (19): the trusted thing gives way
exactly when weight is put on it. *"As one who takes away a garment in cold weather, or vinegar on
soda, so is one who sings songs to a heavy heart"* (20): cheer at the wrong time is not neutral but
an injury, like stripping a coat in the cold or pouring acid on soda — *neṯer*, probably natron, which
fizzes. The three sayings descend from the courtroom to the friend to the bereaved, and each names a
wrong done by someone who should have helped.

**25:21–22 — The enemy fed.** *"If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat. If he is thirsty,
give him water to drink: for you will heap coals of fire on his head, and Yahweh will reward you."*
*Enemy* is *śōnaʾăḵā*, *the one who hates you* — personal hostility, not a foreign army. The command
is concrete: bread and water, the basic needs. Its reasons are two, and only the second is clear.
*Coals of fire on his head* has been read as shame that burns, as remorse that leads to repentance,
and — on the basis of an Egyptian penitential rite sometimes cited — as a sign of contrition; none
is given in the text, and this pass checked none of them. *"Yahweh will reward you"* is plain: the verb
is *yəšallem*, *he will repay*, the root of 20:22's *ʾăšalləmāh-rāʿ*, *"I will pay back evil"*.
What the son was forbidden to say of his enemy, Yahweh does for the son. Paul quotes the couplet
(Romans 12:20) straight after *"Don't seek revenge yourselves … 'Vengeance belongs to me; I will
repay, says the Lord'"* (12:19), and follows it with *"overcome evil with good"* (12:21). See
[[Help for an Enemy - Exodus 23, Proverbs 25, and Matthew 5]] and
[[Wait for Yahweh - Proverbs 20.22 and the Refusal of Revenge]].

**25:23–28 — Closing comparisons.** *"The north wind produces rain; so a backbiting tongue brings an
angry face"* (23). In Hebrew the *backbiting tongue* is *ləšôn sāṯer*, *a tongue of secrecy* — the
root of 25:2's *conceal*. What is God's glory in 25:2 is, in a slanderer's mouth, a weather system
that brings storms of anger. (The *angry face* is *nizʿāmîm*, from the verb that described the
peoples' *abhorring* of the partial judge at 24:24.) Whether a north wind does bring rain in Judah is
debated — the north wind is usually dry there — and some interpreters suppose a different direction
was meant; the wiki takes the WEB as it stands. *"It is better to dwell in the corner of the
housetop, than to share a house with a contentious woman"* (24) repeats 21:9 in the WEB word for
word; the Hebrew differs by one letter (see the Notes, and
[[The Contentious Woman - Proverbs 19.13, 21.9, 19, 25.24, and 27.15]]). *"Like cold water to a
thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country"* (25) answers 25:13's snow in harvest and 25:21's
water for the thirsty enemy: three images of refreshment in one chapter. *"Like a muddied spring,
and a polluted well, so is a righteous man who gives way before the wicked"* (26) inverts 10:11,
where *"The mouth of the righteous is a spring of life"* (*məqôr ḥayyîm*); the same *spring*
(*māqôr*) is here trampled into mud, and *before the wicked* (*lip̄nê-rāšāʿ*) answers 25:5's *"Take
away the wicked from the king's presence"* (*lip̄nê-meleḵ*). A righteous man who yields to the
wicked poisons what others drink from. *"It is not good to eat much honey; nor is it honorable to
seek one's own honor"* (27) closes the honey saying of 25:16 and, with it, the whole chapter: its
second line returns to the two words of 25:2 — *ḥēqer*, *searching*, and *kāḇôḏ*, *glory* — and the
chapter thus ends where it began. The Hebrew is difficult (see the Notes); the WEB's reading makes it
a counterpart to 25:6–7, the man seeking honour for himself. And *"Like a city that is broken down
and without walls is a man whose spirit is without restraint"* (28) is the reverse of 16:32, where
*"one who rules his spirit"* is better *"than he who takes a city"*. There the self-ruled man is
stronger than a conqueror; here the unruled man is the city already taken.

**What the chapter adds.** The book's last Solomonic collection opens with the king — not the
king's power, which 21:1 had put in Yahweh's hand, but the king's knowledge. God conceals, the king
searches, the subject cannot search the king. Everything after is the wisdom of the one standing
below: how to stand at court, how to argue with a neighbour, when to speak and how softly, when to
stop eating and stop visiting, what not to say against a neighbour, what to do for an enemy, and
what self-restraint is worth. The chapter's comparisons are its method of instruction, and the
images it chooses — water, snow, cloud, wind, honey, a spring, a wall — make wisdom a matter of
reading the world. That is the task 25:2 gave to kings, and the chapter gives it to its reader.

## People

  him what the heading attributes and no more.
- [[Hezekiah]] — *"king of Judah"*, whose men *"copied out"* the collection (25:1). His first appearance
  in the wiki by name in an ingested passage.
- [[God]] — named once as *God* (*ʾĕlōhîm*, 25:2), whose glory is to conceal, and once as *Yahweh*
  (25:22), who repays the one who feeds his enemy.
- The king, the prince, the neighbour, the messenger, the false witness, the enemy, and the
  contentious woman are generic types. No king in 25:2–7 is identified with Solomon or Hezekiah.

## Notes

**Boundary and title.** The planned 25:1–28 unit is retained: 25:1 opens with its own heading, and
26:1 turns at once to a cluster on the fool that belongs with chapter 26. The provisional title, *The
Glory of Kings*, matches the WEB of 25:2 and is kept. The Masoretic text marks paragraph breaks
(*petuchah*) after 25:13 and 25:20. Exactly one queue item is advanced.

**Language.** Read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* on 2026-10-26:
- 25:1 has *gam-ʾēlleh mišlê šəlōmōh ʾăšer heʿtîqû ʾanšê ḥizqiyyāh meleḵ-yəhûḏāh*. *Heʿtîqû* is a
  causative of *ʿāṯaq*, a verb of moving on; the WEB's *"copied out"* takes it as transcription
  (from one collection to another). That gloss is standard, but not checked against a lexicon in this
  pass.
- 25:2 has *kəḇōḏ ʾĕlōhîm hastēr dāḇār ûḵəḇōḏ məlāḵîm ḥăqōr dāḇār*; 25:3 *wəlēḇ məlāḵîm ʾên ḥēqer*;
  25:27b *wəḥēqer kəḇōḏām kāḇôḏ*. The last is literally *and the searching of their glory is glory*,
  and is notoriously difficult; the WEB's *"nor is it honorable to seek one's own honor"* reads a
  negative carried over from the first line and a sense for *their glory* that the wiki has not
  checked against commentators. That 25:27 repeats both *ḥēqer* and *kāḇôḏ* from 25:2–3 is in the
  text; what it means is not settled here.
- 25:4–5 both open with *hāḡô*. 25:5 has *wəyikkôn baṣṣeḏeq kisʾô*; 16:12 has *kî ḇiṣḏāqāh yikkôn
  kissēʾ* — the same verb and throne, *ṣeḏeq* in 25:5 and *ṣəḏāqāh* in 16:12.
- 25:6 has *ʾal-titʰaddar lip̄nê-meleḵ*; 25:7 *ʿălēh-hēnnāh* and *mēhašpîləḵā lip̄nê nāḏîḇ ʾăšer rāʾû
  ʿênêḵā*.
- 25:8 has *bəʾaḥărîṯāh* and *bəhaḵlîm ʾōṯəḵā rēʿeḵā*; 25:9 *wəsôḏ ʾaḥēr ʾal-təḡal*; 11:13 has
  *məḡalleh-sôḏ*, 20:19 *gôleh-sôḏ*. 25:10 has *pen-yəḥassedḵā šōmēaʿ wəḏibbāṯəḵā lōʾ ṯāšûḇ*. The
  verb *yəḥassedḵā* is usually explained as a rare homonym of *ḥesed* meaning *to reproach*; that
  explanation is not checked against a lexicon here.
- 25:11 has *dāḇār dāḇur ʿal-ʾop̄nāyw*. The sense of *ʾop̄nāyw* is uncertain.
- 25:12 has *ʿal-ʾōzen šōmaʿaṯ*; 20:12 opens *ʾōzen šōmaʿaṯ wəʿayin rōʾāh*.
- 25:13 has *ṣîr neʾĕmān*; 13:17 *wəṣîr ʾĕmûnîm marpēʾ*.
- 25:14 has *nəśîʾîm wərûaḥ wəḡešem ʾāyin* and *mitʰallēl bəmattaṯ-šāqer*, *boasting of a gift of
  falsehood*.
- 25:15 has *bəʾōreḵ ʾappayim yəp̄utteh qāṣîn wəlāšôn rakkāh tišbor-gārem*. 15:1 has *maʿăneh-raḵ*;
  16:32 *ʾereḵ ʾappayim*. *Yəp̄utteh* is from *pāṯāh*, the verb of 1:10 and 24:28.
- 25:16 has *pen-tiśbāʿennû* and 25:17 *pen-yiśbāʿăḵā* — the same verb. 24:13 has *ʾĕḵol-bənî ḏəḇaš
  kî-ṭôḇ*; 25:16 and 25:27 have the same *dəḇaš*.
- 25:18 has *ʾîš ʿōneh ḇərēʿēhû ʿēḏ šāqer*. Exodus 20:16 (20:13 in Sefaria's Hebrew numbering) has
  *lōʾ-ṯaʿăneh ḇərēʿăḵā ʿēḏ šāqer*.
- 25:19 has *mibṭāḥ bôḡēḏ bəyôm ṣārāh*; 25:20 *ḥōmeṣ ʿal-nāṯer*. That *neṯer* is natron (sodium
  carbonate) is the usual identification, not checked here.
- 25:21 has *ʾim-rāʿēḇ śōnaʾăḵā haʾăḵilēhû leḥem wəʾim-ṣāmēʾ hašqēhû māyim*; 25:22 *kî ḡeḥālîm ʾattāh
  ḥōṯeh ʿal-rōʾšô wayhwh yəšallem-lāḵ*. 20:22 has *ʾal-tōʾmar ʾăšalləmāh-rāʿ*.
- 25:23 has *rûaḥ ṣāp̄ôn təḥôlēl gāšem ûp̄ānîm nizʿāmîm ləšôn sāṯer*. 24:24 has *yizʿāmûhû*.
- 25:24 has *ṭôḇ šeḇeṯ ʿal-pinnaṯ-gāḡ mēʾēšeṯ midyānîm* (written *mdwnym*, read *midyānîm*) *ûḇêṯ
  ḥāḇer*; 21:9 has *ṭôḇ lāšeḇeṯ* — the same saying with the infinitive *lāšeḇeṯ* for *šeḇeṯ*.
- 25:26 has *maʿyān nirpāś ûmāqôr mošḥāṯ ṣaddîq māṭ lip̄nê-rāšāʿ*; 10:11 *məqôr ḥayyîm pî ṣaddîq*.
