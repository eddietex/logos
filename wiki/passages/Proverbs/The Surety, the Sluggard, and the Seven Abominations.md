# The Surety, the Sluggard, and the Seven Abominations

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 6:1–19

## Summary

Chapters 1–5 were long lectures, each held to one road. Proverbs 6:1–19 breaks the pattern: four
short units, each closed in the Masoretic text by a *petuḥah* (after 6:5, 6:11, 6:15, and 6:19),
on four different subjects. **The surety** (6:1–5): a son who has pledged himself for another's
debt is caught by his own words, and must go now, sleepless, and beg his way free. **The
sluggard** (6:6–11): go to the ant, who works without an overseer; a little sleep, and poverty
arrives like a robber. **The worthless man** (6:12–15): a crooked mouth, winking eyes, signalling
feet and fingers, a heart that ploughs evil and sends out strife — and a sudden fall with no
healing. **The seven abominations** (6:16–19): six things Yahweh hates, yes seven, listed as body
parts and then as persons.

The four look miscellaneous, and the wiki reads them as one sequence held together by repeated
words. The surety is told to give *"no sleep to your eyes, nor slumber to your eyelids"* (6:4);
the sluggard takes *"a little sleep, a little slumber"* (6:10) — the same pair of nouns, *šēnâ*
and *tənûmâ*. The worthless man's body (6:12–14) is the seven abominations' list (6:17–19) told in
advance, and the list's last item is the phrase that ended his portrait. Each unit ends with a
catastrophe that arrives from outside and at once: the hunter's hand (6:5), the robber (6:11),
the sudden breaking (6:15), and Yahweh's hatred (6:16).

**6:1–5 — Caught by your own mouth.** *"My son, if you have become collateral for your neighbor,
if you have struck your hands in pledge for a stranger"* (6:1). The verb is *ʿāraḇtā*, the root
[[Judah]] used when he offered himself for [[Benjamin]] (*ʾeʿerḇennû*, Genesis 43:9); the gesture
is *tāqaʿtā … kappeḵā*, *you struck your palms*, a handshake that closed the deal. *"Stranger"* is
*zār* — the masculine of *zārâ*, the strange woman of 2:16 and 5:3 — and here plainly just
*someone outside the household*, not a sexual figure (Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according
to the Masorah*). The father's diagnosis is about speech, not money: *"You are trapped by the
words of your mouth. You are ensnared with the words of your mouth"* (6:2) — the same phrase twice,
*bəʾimrê-p̄îḵā*. The son struck his palm; now *"you have come into the hand of your neighbor"*
(6:3, *bəḵap̄-rēʿeḵā*, the same word *kap̄*). The cure is humiliation and haste: *"Go, humble
yourself. Press your plea with your neighbor"* — the last verb, *rəhaḇ*, is usually glossed
*importune* or *pester*, and its sense here is the conventional one, not checked against a lexicon
this pass. Do not sleep until it is done; get free *"like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter,
like a bird from the snare of the fowler"* (6:5). The trap words chain together: *nôqaštā*,
*ensnared* (6:2), and *yāqûš*, *fowler* (6:5), share a root. See [[Wealth and Possessions]] and
[[Language and Speech]].

**Proverbs against Judah?** The wiki already holds the tension, on
[[I Will Be Surety for Him - Judah's Pledge and the Guarantor in Scripture]]. Proverbs warns against
suretyship four more times (11:15; 17:18; 20:16; 22:26–27); Genesis makes Judah's suretyship the
turn on which his family is saved (44:32–33), and Job asks God himself to be his surety, with both
of this verse's verbs: *"Now give a pledge, be collateral for me with yourself. Who is there who
will strike hands with me?"* (Job 17:3; *ʿorbēnî*, *yittāqēaʿ*). The wiki does not dissolve the
tension; it records what the texts themselves distinguish. Proverbs' warnings are about a pledge
given lightly, for a *neighbor* or a *stranger*, in words the son must then escape from; Judah
pledged himself for a brother, named the term — *"forever"* — before he gave it, and kept it.
Proverbs tells a man how not to fall into another's hand. Job and the psalmist ask to fall into
God's.

**6:6–11 — Go to the ant.** *"Go to the ant, you sluggard. Consider her ways, and be wise"* (6:6).
The sluggard (*ʿāṣēl*) is Proverbs' stock figure of the idle man; this is his first appearance,
and he returns at 10:26, 24:30–34, and 26:13–16. The lesson is taken from observation of a
creature, as Job 12:7 told Job's friends to *"ask the animals"*, and Agur will cite the ant again
as one of *"four things which are little on the earth, but … exceedingly wise"* (30:24–25). The
ant has *"no chief, overseer, or ruler"* (6:7): *qāṣîn*, *šōṭēr*, *mōšēl*. *Šōṭēr* is the word
Exodus used for the Israelite *"officers"* set over the brickmakers under Pharaoh's taskmasters
(Exodus 5:6, 14; *šōṭərāyw*, *šōṭərê*). Israel in Egypt worked because it was driven; the ant works
with nobody driving her. *"Provides her bread in the summer, and gathers her food in the harvest"*
(6:8) — she works ahead of need, in the season when work is possible. Then the sluggard's lullaby,
which quotes himself back to him: *"A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the
hands to sleep"* (6:10). No single nap is the problem; *little* repeated three times is. And the
result is not described as a slow decline but as an assault: *"so your poverty will come as a
robber, and your scarcity as an armed man"* (6:11). *"As a robber"* renders *kimhallēḵ*, literally
*like one walking*, *a vagrant* or *prowler*; *"armed man"* is *ʾîš māḡēn*, *a man with a shield*.
The renderings vary and the WEB's are followed. Verses 10–11 return almost word for word at
24:33–34, at the end of [[Proverbs]]' second collection of *"sayings of the wise"*, where they are
drawn from a ruined vineyard. See [[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]] and
[[Work]].

**The ant and the garden.** The ant's work is not the curse. Before the fall, *"Yahweh God took the
man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it"* (Genesis 2:15); the sluggard
refuses the first vocation, not the toil of 3:17–19. And the ant's summer stores are what
[[Joseph]] did for Egypt in the seven years of plenty (Genesis 41:47–49): gather in the good season
against the lean one. See [[Famine and Plenty]].

**6:12–15 — The worthless man.** *"A worthless person, a man of iniquity, is he who walks with a
perverse mouth"* (6:12). *"Worthless person"* is *ʾāḏām bəliyyaʿal* — *a man of belial*, the word
later used as a name for the devil (2 Corinthians 6:15, *"Belial"*; not yet ingested); in Proverbs
it is a moral description, not a name. His *"perverse mouth"* is *ʿiqqəšûṯ peh*, exactly the
phrase the father told the son to *"put away from yourself"* at 4:24. He speaks in signs: *"who
winks with his eyes, who signals with his feet, who motions with his fingers"* (6:13) — a man who
communicates beside his words, so that what he says and what he means are two different things.
His heart holds *tahpuḵôṯ*, *"perverseness"* (6:14), the word for the *"perverse things"* spoken
by the men of 2:12 and the *"perverseness of evil"* they delight in (2:14). He *"devises evil
continually"* — *ḥōrēš rāʿ*, literally *ploughs* evil — which 3:29 forbade outright (*"Don't
devise evil against your neighbor"*, *ʾal-taḥărōš … rāʿâ*). **He is [[Keep Your Heart]] turned
inside out.** That lecture guarded heart, mouth, eyes, and feet (4:23–27); this man has given all
four to evil. And his end comes as the sluggard's did, without warning: *"his calamity will come
suddenly. He will be broken suddenly, and that without remedy"* (6:15). *"Remedy"* is *marpēʾ*,
the word 4:22 used for the father's words as *"health"* to the whole body. He refused the
medicine, and there is none left. See [[Deception]] and [[Retribution]].

**6:16–19 — Six things, yes seven.** *"There are six things which Yahweh hates; yes, seven which are
an abomination to him"* (6:16). The *x, x+1* form is a fixed device of Hebrew wisdom; Eliphaz used
the same six and seven (*"He will deliver you in six troubles; yes, in seven no evil shall touch
you"*, Job 5:19), and Agur will use three and four (Proverbs 30). The second number intensifies;
the list itself has seven items, and the weight falls on the seventh. *"Abomination"* is
*tôʿăḇâ*, which Proverbs first used at 3:32 (*"the perverse is an abomination to Yahweh"*); the
phrase here is *tôʿăḇaṯ napšô*, *an abomination of his soul* (the Masoretic text writes a plural,
*tôʿēḇôṯ*, and reads it as a singular). The list:

| | 6:17–19 | the worthless man, 6:12–14 | the son, 4:23–27 |
|---|---|---|---|
| eyes | *"haughty eyes"* | *"winks with his eyes"* | *"look straight ahead"* |
| mouth | *"a lying tongue"* | *"a perverse mouth"* | *"put away … a perverse mouth"* |
| hands | *"hands that shed innocent blood"* | *"motions with his fingers"* | — |
| heart | *"a heart that devises wicked schemes"* | *"devises evil continually"* | *"keep your heart"* |
| feet | *"feet that are swift in running to mischief"* | *"signals with his feet"* | *"remove your foot from evil"* |
| person | *"a false witness who utters lies"* | — | — |
| person | *"he who sows discord among brothers"* | *"always sows discord"* | — |

The first five are body parts, from the top down, and they are the body of
[[Keep Your Heart]] put to the opposite use. The hands and feet repeat the gang of 1:11–16, who lay
wait for *"the innocent"* and whose *"feet run to evil … hurry to shed blood"*; the verbs of 6:18,
*məmahărôṯ lārûṣ*, are the two of 1:16 (*yārûṣû wîmahărû*). See
[[Their Feet Run to Evil - Proverbs 1.16, Isaiah 59.7, and Romans 3.15]]. Haughty eyes stand first,
which is where Proverbs keeps pride: compare 3:7, *"Don't be wise in your own eyes"*. See
[[Pride and Self-Exaltation]].

**The sixth and seventh are people**, and both are crimes against a community rather than a
victim. *"A false witness"* is *ʿēḏ šāqer*, the phrase of the ninth commandment, *"You shall not
give false testimony against your neighbor"* (Exodus 20:16; MT 20:13), and the list runs close to
the Decalogue's second table: murder (*"innocent blood"*), false witness, and — in the lecture
that follows, 6:20–35 — adultery and theft. See
[[Six Things Yahweh Hates - Proverbs 6.16-19 and the Ten Commandments]]. The seventh, the sower of
discord, is the worthless man of 6:14 (*midyānîm yəšallēaḥ*, *he sends out strife*), now named for
the one thing Yahweh hates most in the climactic position: *məšallēaḥ mədānîm bên ʾaḥîm*, *one who
sends out strife between brothers*. The list began with a proud look and ends with a ruined
family.

**Discord among brothers.** Genesis is the wiki's long case study. Cain and Abel, Jacob and Esau,
and Joseph and his brothers are all stories of strife between brothers, and Genesis 37 opens the
Joseph story with talk carried between them: *"Joseph brought an evil report of them to their
father"*, and his brothers *"hated him, and couldn't speak peaceably to him"* (Genesis 37:2, 4).
Proverbs does not name any of them. The wiki records the resonance without making Proverbs a
comment on Genesis. See [[Brotherhood]] and [[Strife and Peacemaking]].

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 6:1–19 unit is kept, though it is four units rather than one lecture.
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks a *petuḥah* after 6:5, 6:11, 6:15, and 6:19, and
6:20 opens a new lecture with *"My son"*. Splitting it into four pericopes was considered and
rejected: the shared vocabulary of sleep (6:4, 10) and the matched lists of 6:12–14 and 6:16–19
make the four read as a sequence, and the queue's division of chapters 1–9 at *"My son"* puts 6:1
and 6:20 as the boundaries.
