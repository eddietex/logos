# Where There Is No Vision

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 29:1–27

## Summary

The final chapter of the collection copied by Hezekiah’s men (25:1) moves repeatedly between
household discipline and public justice. It opens with the person repeatedly rebuked who stiffens
his neck and meets sudden destruction (1). Righteous rule brings public rejoicing; wicked rule
brings groaning (2). Wisdom delights a father, justice stabilizes a land, and flattery and sin
entrap (3–6).

Concern for the poor tests righteousness and kingship (7, 14), while Yahweh gives sight to poor
and oppressor alike (13). Mockery and anger unsettle the city and the courtroom; wise people turn
anger away and control themselves (8–11). A ruler’s willingness to hear lies spreads corruption
through his officials (12).

Correction, neglect, revelation, and response occupy the central household sayings (15–21). Pride
and anger then give way to warnings about complicity, fear of people, and reliance on royal favor
(22–26). Justice comes from Yahweh. The closing verse recognizes mutual aversion between the
upright and the wicked (27); righteousness does not guarantee everyone’s approval.

## Notes

### Revelation, law, and the title

The planned title is retained, but verse 18 says “revelation” in the stored WEB. Its second half
is essential: the one who keeps the law is blessed. The contrast concerns received guidance and
obedience; applying the first half to strategic planning alone would leave the second unexplained.
No particular prophet or law code is identified. Exodus 32:25 supplies a comparison for failed
restraint, not proof of a direct quotation.

### Public justice and shared dependence

The people’s rejoicing or groaning (2) judges rule from below. Verse 4 explicitly condemns bribes
in the WEB; verse 12 holds the ruler responsible for wanting lies. Fair judgment of the poor is a
condition of enduring rule (14), not permission to favor an unjust claim: Exodus 23:3, 6–8 forbids
both partiality and denial of the poor person’s justice.

Verse 13 resembles Proverbs 22:2, but replaces rich and poor sharing a Maker with poor and
oppressor receiving sight. The oppressor’s life depends on Yahweh too; this does not endorse
oppression. Verse 26 distinguishes favor from justice, locating justice in Yahweh without excusing
kings from the responsibility stated in verse 14. No historical throne is identified by the
promise of lasting establishment.

### Anger, haste, and moral opposition

Mockers inflame a city (8); the wise turn anger away. The courtroom fool rages or scoffs without
producing peace (9), and the fool who vents anger is contrasted with self-control (11). Verse 22
treats anger as productive of further strife and sin. Haste in words is worse than folly (20),
using the same closing sentence as 26:12’s warning about being wise in one’s own eyes in the WEB.
That English comparison does not establish Hebrew identity.

The closing mutual aversion (27) and the bloodthirsty hostility toward integrity (10) prevent
peacemaking from becoming a promise that all parties will agree. The WEB’s verse 10 makes the
upright the target of violent people; no source-language or alternative-translation claim is made
here.

### Correction and the household’s unequal relations

Verse 1 warns against repeated refusal of reproof, with a sudden, irremediable end like 6:15.
Verses 15 and 17 pair correction with wisdom and parental joy, while neglect brings shame to the
mother. These are purposes, not a specified method or measure of discipline. The chapter provides
no basis for assuming that every injured child or adult was being justly corrected.

Verse 19 depicts a servant who understands words but will not respond. It does not specify what
further action should follow. Verse 21’s pampered servant becomes a son in the WEB; the saying
narrates neither a formal adoption nor an emancipation procedure, and grants no stated inheritance
right. The evaluation of that outcome is not independently explained. These household observations
should not erase Job 31:13–15’s obligation to hear a servant’s complaint because master and
servant share a Maker. That tension of social perspective remains explicit.

### Complicity, silence, and fear

Verse 24 exposes the cost of joining a thief: the accomplice becomes his own enemy and will not
testify under oath. Leviticus 5:1 supplies a legal comparison for culpable silence, but the WEB’s
taking an oath and hearing an adjuration are different descriptions; exact Hebrew equivalence is
not established.

Fear of people becomes a snare (25), echoing the chapter’s nets of flattery and sin (5–6). The
contrary is trust in Yahweh, followed immediately by the distinction between ruler’s favor and
Yahweh’s justice (26). The midwives’ refusal of a royal command in Exodus 1:17, 21 illustrates a
comparable allegiance to God without being an explicit allusion in Proverbs.

### Promised outcomes and Job’s counterevidence

**Explicit tension:** the righteous can sing (6), see the wicked fall (16), and be safe through
trust (25); Job 21:7–13 describes secure, singing, prosperous wicked people, while Job 2:3, 7
shows harm to the man whose integrity God affirms. Proverbs 29 itself admits wicked rule (2) and
violent hatred of integrity (10). No timetable is supplied to reconcile the outcomes. Reading the
sayings as moral guidance rather than diagnostic rules is the wiki’s interpretation; it does not
remove their strong promises or assign Job hidden guilt.
