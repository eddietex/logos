# Wisdom Calls in the Street

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 1:20–33

## Summary

The father stops speaking and a second voice takes over. In
[[Do Not Consent to Sinners]] the son heard two speakers — his father, and the gang the father quoted.
Now **Wisdom herself speaks, in public, in the first person**, and the speech she gives is not a
lecture but a prophet's oracle of judgment: an appeal, an indictment, a sentence, and a closing
promise. It is the first of three speeches by Wisdom personified in Proverbs 1–9 (with 8:1–36 and
9:1–6), and the one that sounds least like a teacher and most like Jeremiah.

- **1:20–21 — where she speaks.** The street, the squares, the noisy corners, the city gates.
- **1:22–23 — the appeal.** *"How long?"* to three kinds of hearer, then *"Turn at my reproof."*
- **1:24–27 — the indictment and the laughter.** *Because* you refused, *I also* will laugh.
- **1:28–31 — the turn to the third person.** They will call and not be answered; they will eat
  their own fruit.
- **1:32–33 — the two ends.** The simple killed by their turning away; the listener at ease.

**She is out of doors (1:20–21).** *"Wisdom calls aloud in the street. She utters her voice in the
public squares. She calls at the head of noisy places. At the entrance of the city gates, she utters
her words."* Four locations, every one of them public: *baḥûṣ*, *outside*; *birḥōḇôt*, the open
squares; *bərōʾš hōmiyyôt*, at the head of the *noisy* (crowded, bustling) streets; *bəpiṯḥê šəʿārîm
bāʿîr*, at the gate-openings in the city, where the elders sat and cases were heard. The gang of
1:11 said *"Let's lurk secretly"*; Wisdom does the opposite of lurking. She competes for attention in
the same streets where the son will meet the sinners, and later the adulteress (7:12, *"Now she is in
the streets, now in the squares"* — a later pericope). **Wisdom in Proverbs is not esoteric**: she is
available where ordinary life is noisiest.

**Read after Job, this is a tension, and the wiki states it rather than smoothing it.** Job 28 asked
*"But where shall wisdom be found?"* (28:12) and answered that it *"is hidden from the eyes of all
living"* (28:21) — *neʿelmâ*, concealed — known only to God, who gives it as *"the fear of the
Lord"* (28:28). Proverbs 1 has her shouting at the city gates. Both claims are in the canon: wisdom
is hidden (Job 28:12, 21) and wisdom is public (Proverbs 1:20–21; 8:1–3). The wiki's reading is that
they are about different things — Job 28 about wisdom as the knowledge of how the world is made and
governed, which no one can mine; Proverbs 1 about wisdom as the summons to fear Yahweh, which
everyone has heard — and the two books meet at exactly that point (Job 28:28; Proverbs 1:7, 29). But
it is a reading. See [[Where Shall Wisdom Be Found]] and [[Wisdom]].

**Her name is plural (1:20).** The Hebrew is *ḥoḵmôt* — *wisdoms* — with a feminine singular verb,
*tārōnnâ*, *she cries out*. The verb is from *r-n-n*, usually a ringing shout of joy; here a loud
public cry. The plural form is generally explained as intensive (*Wisdom par excellence*, the
fullness of wisdom), not a plurality of wisdoms; that is a grammatical judgement, noted as such. The
same form returns at 9:1.

**Three hearers, one *"How long?"* (1:22).** *"How long, you simple ones, will you love simplicity?
How long will mockers delight themselves in mockery, and fools hate knowledge?"* The verse names the
three kinds of person Proverbs spends the rest of its length on, each paired with its own vice in a
noun from the same root:

- ***pətāyim … pet̲î*** — the *simple*, who *love simplicity*. The same root, *p-t-h*, as the
  *entice* of 1:10. The preface promised *"to give prudence to the simple"* (1:4); here the simple
  are addressed as people who have chosen to stay that way.
- ***lēṣîm lāṣôn*** — *mockers*, who *delight in mockery*. Proverbs' scoffer is the person who has
  stopped being teachable, and the first time the word appears it is beside *"How long?"*.
- ***kəsîlîm*** — *fools*, who *hate knowledge*. *Hate* is the verb the indictment returns to at
  1:29.

**The appeal is a call to turn (1:23).** *"Turn at my reproof. Behold, I will pour out my spirit on
you. I will make known my words to you."* *Turn* is *tāšûḇû*, from *šûḇ*, the Hebrew Bible's
ordinary word for repentance; *reproof* is *tôḵaḥtî*, correction, the word the speech repeats at
1:25 and 1:30. Then a promise that sounds like Joel: *ʾabbîʿâ lāḵem rûḥî* — *I will pour out my
spirit on you*. Two things should be said about it.

1. **The verb is not Joel's.** Joel 2:28 (3:1 in the Hebrew) has *ʾešpôk ʾet-rûḥî*, from *šāpaḵ*,
   the verb of 1:16's *"to shed blood"*. Proverbs has *ʾabbîʿâ*, from *nāḇaʿ*, *to bubble up, gush
   forth* — a spring's verb, used elsewhere in Proverbs of speech: *"The words of a man's mouth are
   like deep waters. The fountain of wisdom is like a flowing brook"* (18:4, [[A Strong Tower]]), where
   the *flowing* brook is *naḥal nōḇēaʿ*.
2. ***Rûaḥ* here may mean *mind* rather than *Spirit*.** The parallel line is *"I will make known my
   words to you"*, and some modern translations are reported to render the first line with
   *thoughts* rather than *spirit* (not checked against any of them here). On that reading Wisdom
   promises to pour out what is in her, as a spring pours water, in words. The WEB's *"my spirit"* keeps the word literal, and so leaves the echo of Joel audible.

See [[I Will Pour Out My Spirit - Proverbs 1.23, Joel 2.28, and Acts 2.17]].

**The indictment is a prophet's (1:24–25).** *"Because I have called, and you have refused; I have
stretched out my hand, and no one has paid attention; but you have ignored all my counsel, and wanted
none of my reproof."* It opens *yaʿan qārāʾṯî*, *because I called* — the same two words with which
Isaiah 65:12 opens God's indictment of Jerusalem, *"because when I called, you didn't answer"*, and
which Isaiah 66:4 and Jeremiah 7:13 repeat in their own forms. **Wisdom speaks with the grammar of
divine judgment.** The details:

- *"stretched out my hand"* — *nāṭîṯî yāḏî*. The gesture is a beckoning, but the phrase is the one
  the plague narratives use of Moses and Aaron: *"stretch out your hand over the waters of Egypt"*
  (Exodus 7:19, *nəṭēh-yāḏəḵā*). In Exodus the outstretched hand brought the plague; here it is
  offered first, and the calamity comes because it was refused. Isaiah 65:2 has the image with a
  different verb, *"I have spread out my hands all day to a rebellious people"* (*pēraśtî*), which
  Paul quotes as God's word to Israel at Romans 10:21.
- *"you have ignored all my counsel"* — *wattip̄rəʿû*, from *pāraʿ*, *to let loose, let go
  unchecked*. It is the verb of the golden calf: *"the people had broken loose, (for Aaron had let
  them loose …)"* (Exodus 32:25, *pāruaʿ … pərāʿōh*). To ignore counsel is to let it go slack, like
  reins. See [[The Golden Calf]].
- *"wanted none of my reproof"* — *lōʾ ʾăḇîṯem*, and 1:30 *lōʾ-ʾāḇû*: the verb *ʾāḇâ*, *to be
  willing*. It is the verb of the father's thesis, *ʾal-tōḇēʾ*, *"don't consent"* (1:10). **The son
  was told not to be willing when sinners called; the simple were not willing when Wisdom called.**
  The book's first two speeches turn on the same verb, pointed in opposite directions.

**Then she laughs (1:26–27).** *"I also will laugh at your disaster. I will mock when calamity
overtakes you; when calamity overtakes you like a storm, when your disaster comes on like a
whirlwind; when distress and anguish come on you."* *ʾeśḥāq … ʾelʿaḡ* — *laugh … mock*. The pair is
Psalm 2:4's, word for word in the verbs: *"He who sits in the heavens will laugh (yiśḥāq). The Lord
will have them in derision (yilʿaḡ)."* Wisdom laughs as God laughs at the kings who plot against him.
The *storm* is *šôʾâ*, devastation (the written text has an unusual spelling; the reading tradition
corrects it), and the *whirlwind* is *sûp̄â*, the word of Job 27:20, *"a storm steals him away in the
night"*. Twice the speech says *paḥdəḵem* — rendered *"calamity"* in the WEB, but literally *your
dread, the thing you fear*. Hold that word; it comes back in the last line.

**The laughter is the speech's hardest line, and the wiki records the tension it creates with Job.**
Job accused God of exactly this: *"If the scourge kills suddenly, he will mock at the trial of the
innocent"* (Job 9:23) — *yilʿāḡ*, the same verb as Wisdom's *ʾelʿaḡ* here and Psalm 2:4's. In Job
the mockery is an accusation against heaven, made by a blameless man and never endorsed by the book;
in Proverbs it is a sentence, pronounced on people who were called and refused. **The difference is
the object** — the *innocent* in Job 9:23, the refusers in Proverbs 1:26 — and Proverbs never claims
that Wisdom laughs at calamity as such. But both texts use the language of divine mockery, and the
wiki notes that Job's complaint is the shadow side of this verse: what it would look like if the
laughter fell on the wrong people. See [[Retribution]] and [[Judgment]].

**She turns away from them mid-speech (1:28–31).** Through 1:27 Wisdom says *you*. At 1:28 she
says *they*: *"Then will they call on me, but I will not answer. They will seek me diligently, but
they will not find me."* The grammar enacts the sentence — she has stopped addressing them and is
now talking *about* them, to whoever is still listening. **The reader of the book, who is still
listening, becomes the audience for the verdict on those who would not.**

The verbs of 1:28 are *yiqrāʾunənî wəlōʾ ʾeʿĕneh*, *they will call me and I will not answer*, and
*yəšaḥărunənî wəlōʾ yimṣāʾunənî*, *they will seek me early and not find me*. *Šāḥar* is *to seek
eagerly*, related to *šaḥar*, *dawn* (a lexical connection, not a claim about this verse's meaning).
**The same two verbs return, with the negative removed, in Wisdom's second speech**: *"Those who seek
me diligently will find me"* — *ûməšaḥăray yimṣāʾunənî* (8:17). Proverbs states both: seek and
find (8:17), seek and not find (1:28). The difference is time — *"Then"*, *ʾāz*, 1:28: after the
calamity, when seeking is a response to the storm rather than to the call. Isaiah 55:6 has the
same logic positively: *"Seek Yahweh while he may be found."*

**This is not quite the promise of Deuteronomy 4:29 and Jeremiah 29:13, and the difference should
be stated.** Those passages say that Israel in exile *"shall seek Yahweh your God, and you shall
find him, when you search after him with all your heart"* — seeking after the disaster, and finding.
Proverbs 1:28 says the people who seek after the disaster will not find. **Contradiction flagged,
not resolved.** The wiki's reading is that Proverbs describes seeking as a way out of the storm
(*"when distress and anguish come on you"*, 1:27), without the change of heart Deuteronomy
specifies (*"with all your heart and with all your soul"*) — and that the prophets, too, can say
*"they will cry to Yahweh, but he will not answer them"* (Micah 3:4; compare Jeremiah 11:11; Hosea
5:6). But the texts do not say this in so many words, and both claims stand.

**The charge sheet (1:29–30).** *"Because they hated knowledge, and didn't choose the fear of
Yahweh. They wanted none of my counsel. They despised all my reproof."* **The fear of Yahweh is
something chosen** — *lōʾ bāḥārû*, *they did not choose* — which makes the motto of 1:7 a decision
and not a temperament. This is the only place in 1:20–33 where Yahweh is named, and it is in the
motto's own phrase: the preface said the fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge (1:7), and
Wisdom's first speech says that refusing it is what *hating knowledge* means. See
[[The Fear of God]].

**They eat what they cooked (1:31).** *"Therefore they will eat of the fruit of their own way, and
be filled with their own schemes."* *Schemes* is *mōʿăṣōṯêhem*, their own *counsels* — from the root
*y-ʿ-ṣ*, the root of *ʿăṣāṯî*, *my counsel*, which they ignored (1:25) and did not want (1:30). **They
refused her counsel and are filled with their own.** This is the self-acting [[Retribution]] of
1:18 again — no judge, no blow from outside; a way of life followed to its fruit — and it is the
image Paul uses: *"whatever a man sows, that he will also reap"* (Galatians 6:7).

**The last two verses use the speech's own words against each other (1:32–33).** *"For the
backsliding of the simple will kill them. The careless ease of fools will destroy them. But whoever
listens to me will dwell securely, and will be at ease, without fear of harm."*

- ***Backsliding*** is *məšûḇat*, from *šûḇ* — the root of *"Turn"* in 1:23. **They were called to
  turn, and their turning — away — kills them.** The same verb is the appeal and the cause of death.
- ***Careless ease*** is *šalwat*, the complacency of fools, which destroys them; the listener is
  *šaʾănan*, *at ease*, and lives. The WEB's *"ease"* in both lines is exact: there are two kinds of
  quiet, one that comes from not listening and one that comes from having listened.
- ***Without fear of harm*** is *mippaḥad rāʿâ*. *Paḥad* is the word the WEB rendered *"calamity"*
  twice in 1:26–27, *your dread*. **The refusers' dread came upon them; the listener is free of
  dread.** The speech ends on *paḥad* the way the preface ended on *yirʾâ* — two different words for
  fear, and Proverbs' first chapter has now set them side by side: the fear of Yahweh, chosen, which
  is the beginning of knowledge, and the dread that comes uninvited to people who did not choose it.

**Wisdom speaks as God speaks, and the New Testament hears Jesus in her.** Her speech uses the
prophetic forms of divine address — *"How long?"*, *"because I called"*, *"I will pour out my
spirit"*, the refusal to answer — but she never says *"thus says Yahweh"*, and she names Yahweh in
the third person (1:29). **She is not God, and she speaks in God's register.** Jesus, in Luke,
attributes a saying to *"the wisdom of God"* — *"I will send to them prophets and apostles; and some
of them they will kill"* (Luke 11:49, *hē sophia tou theou eipen*) — where Matthew's parallel has
Jesus say it in the first person, *"I send to you prophets"* (Matthew 23:34, *egō apostellō*), and
follows it with *"How often I would have gathered your children together … and you would not!"*
(23:37). That lament is Wisdom's speech in Proverbs 1 in miniature: the call, the refusal, the
consequence. See [[Wisdom Sends Prophets - Proverbs 1.20-33, Luke 11.49, and Matthew 23.34-37]] and
[[When I Called, You Didn't Answer - Proverbs 1.24, Isaiah 65.12, and Jeremiah 7.13]].

## People

- *Wisdom* — personified as a woman, the speaker of 1:22–33. **Not given a person page**: she is a
  figure of speech within the book rather than a character in a narrative, and her page is the theme
  [[Wisdom]], which now records this speech. If Proverbs 8 makes a separate page necessary, that is
  the pericope to decide it in.
- *The simple, the mockers, and the fools* — addressed collectively (1:22). No pages; see [[Folly]].
- [[God]] — named once, as *Yahweh*, in *"the fear of Yahweh"* (1:29). He does not speak; Wisdom
  speaks in his register.

## Places

- No named place. The *street*, the *public squares*, and the *city gates* (1:20–21) are generic
  public spaces of any city; see [[City and Civilization]].

## Themes

- [[Wisdom]] — personified for the first time, speaking in public and in the first person.
- [[Folly]] — the simple, the mockers, and the fools named together (1:22), and their end (1:32).
- [[The Fear of God]] — something one can refuse to *choose* (1:29).
- [[Retribution]] — eating the fruit of one's own way (1:31); the dread that comes (1:26–27, 33).
- [[Judgment]] — the speech's form is a prophetic oracle of judgment; the laughter of Psalm 2:4.
- [[Prayer]] — calling and not being answered (1:28); the tension with Deuteronomy 4:29.
- [[Prophecy and Vision]] — Wisdom in the prophet's voice; the spirit poured out (1:23).
- [[Hardening of the Heart]] — the refusal to listen, which becomes the inability to find.
- [[City and Civilization]] — wisdom located in the city's noisiest public spaces.

## Notes

**Hebrew.** Claims above about the Hebrew wording of Proverbs 1:20–33 — *ḥoḵmôt … tārōnnâ* (1:20),
*bərōʾš hōmiyyôt … bəpiṯḥê šəʿārîm bāʿîr* (1:21), *pətāyim təʾēhăḇû-p̄eṯî … lēṣîm lāṣôn … kəsîlîm
yiśnəʾû-dāʿaṯ* (1:22), *tāšûḇû lətôḵaḥtî … ʾabbîʿâ lāḵem rûḥî ʾôdîʿâ dəḇāray* (1:23), *yaʿan qārāʾṯî
wattəmāʾēnû nāṭîṯî yāḏî* (1:24), *wattip̄rəʿû … lōʾ ʾăḇîṯem* (1:25), *ʾeśḥāq … ʾelʿaḡ … paḥdəḵem*
(1:26), *kəšôʾâ … kəsûp̄â* (1:27, with its *ketiv*/*qere*), *yiqrāʾunənî wəlōʾ ʾeʿĕneh yəšaḥărunənî wəlōʾ
yimṣāʾunənî* (1:28), *wəyirʾaṯ YHWH lōʾ bāḥārû* (1:29), *lōʾ-ʾāḇû … nāʾăṣû* (1:30), *mippərî darkām …
mimmōʿăṣōṯêhem* (1:31), *məšûḇaṯ pətāyim … šalwaṯ kəsîlîm* (1:32), *yiškon-beṭaḥ wəšaʾănan mippaḥad
rāʿâ* (1:33) — were checked against Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*, as were Proverbs 1:7,
1:10 (*ʾal-tōḇēʾ*), 8:1–4, and 8:17; Joel 3:1 (*ʾešpôk*); Isaiah 65:2 (*pēraśtî*) and 65:12 (*yaʿan
qārāʾṯî*); Psalm 2:4; Exodus 7:19 and 32:25; Job 9:23, 27:20, and 28:21. Lexical statements — *r-n-n*
as a ringing cry, *nāḇaʿ* as *to gush*, *šāḥar* related to *dawn*, *mōʿēṣâ* and *ʿēṣâ* from *y-ʿ-ṣ*,
*məšûḇâ* from *šûḇ* — are standard lexical descriptions, not readings of an edition. The
explanation of *ḥoḵmôt* as an intensive plural is a grammatical judgement. Proverbs 18:4's *nōḇēaʿ*
was checked in the same edition.

**Greek.** Luke 11:49 (*hē sophia tou theou eipen*), Matthew 23:34 (*egō apostellō*), 23:37
(*ēthelēsa … ouk ēthelēsate*), and Acts 2:17 (*ekcheō apo tou pneumatos mou*) were checked against
the SBLGNT (MorphGNT edition). The Septuagint of Proverbs 1:23 was not checked, and nothing is
claimed about it.

**Other translations** of 1:23 are mentioned only as reported, and none is quoted.

**The unit.** 1:20–33 is bounded by Wisdom's entrance at 1:20, which changes speaker, and by the
*pe* paragraph marker after 1:33 in the edition above; 2:1 returns to *"My son"*. The speech's shift
from second person (1:22–27) to third (1:28–32) and back to a general *"whoever"* (1:33) is in the
Hebrew and is preserved by the WEB.
