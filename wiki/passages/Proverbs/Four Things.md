# Four Things

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 30:15–33

## Summary

The rest of [[Agur]]'s words are built mostly of numbered lists. A leech's two daughters cry
*"Give, give"*, and four things never say *"Enough"*: [[Sheol]], the barren womb, the dry earth, and
fire (15–16). A single saying interrupts the counting: the eye that mocks a father and scorns a
mother's obedience will be picked out by ravens and eaten by young eagles (17).

Four more lists follow. Four ways are too wonderful for the speaker to understand — an eagle in the
air, a serpent on a rock, a ship at sea, *"the way of a man with a maiden"* — and the adulterous
woman's way is set beside them: she eats, wipes her mouth, and says she has done nothing wrong
(18–20). Under four things the earth trembles: a servant who becomes king, a fool filled with food,
an unloved woman who is married, and a servant girl who becomes heir to her mistress (21–23). Four
small creatures are exceedingly wise: ants that store food in summer, conies that live in the rocks,
locusts that advance in ranks without a king, and a lizard found in kings' palaces (24–28). Four
things are stately in their going: the lion, the greyhound, the male goat, and the king whom no one
rises against (29–31).

The unit, and Agur's words, close with an instruction rather than a list. Whoever has exalted
himself foolishly or planned evil should put his hand over his mouth, because pressing produces
something: milk gives butter, the nose gives blood, and wrath gives strife (32–33).

## Notes

### Counting as a way of seeing

Four of the five lists use the *three … four* form, the *x, x+1* pattern Proverbs already used at
6:16 (*"six things … yes, seven"*). The Hebrew of 30:15, 18, 21, and 29 pairs *šālôš*/*šəlōšāh*
with *ʾarbaʿ*/*ʾarbāʿāh* (Sefaria, *Miqra according to the Masorah*, read 2026-10-27); the list
of 24–28 simply says *ʾarbāʿāh*, four. In each case four items follow, so the second number is the
real count and the first a rhetorical step towards it. The weight usually falls on the fourth: the
fire, the man with a maiden, the servant girl who inherits, the king. Amos uses the same counting
for the nations' transgressions, with a different purpose; see
[[Three Transgressions, Yes Four - Proverbs 30 and Amos 1-2]].

The lists do not explain themselves. They place things side by side and leave the reader to find
what they share. That is consistent with Agur's opening confession that he does not have wisdom as a
possession (30:2–3): here he looks at the world and reports what amazes him (18) without claiming to
master it. See [[The Words of Agur]].

### Appetite that never stops (15–17)

The leech (*ʿălûqāh*, read in MAM) has only one word, said twice. The four that never say
*"Enough"* (*hôn*) put Sheol beside the barren womb, so the grave that always takes and the womb that
longs to give life are listed together as forms of unfilled want. The barren womb is not a moral
fault here; it belongs to the same list as dry ground and fire, things whose emptiness is a fact.
The wiki's Genesis pages show what that want felt like from inside, in Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel.
See [[Barrenness]] and
[[Never Satisfied - Proverbs 27.20, 30.15-16, Ecclesiastes 1.8, and Habakkuk 2.5]].

Verse 17 breaks the counting with a curse on contempt for parents. The eye that mocks will itself
become food for birds of prey, left unburied. It continues the generation that *"curses their
father, and doesn't bless their mother"* (30:11), and stands with 20:20's lamp put out. It is a
warning about where contempt leads, not a statement about how any particular death occurred. See
[[Honor Your Father and Mother - Exodus 20 and Ephesians 6]].

### Four wonderful ways, and one that is not (18–20)

All four *ways* (*derek*, repeated four times in MAM) leave no trace: the eagle's flight, the
serpent's path on bare rock, a ship's wake, and the intimacy of a man with a young woman
(*ʿalmāh*). The text calls them *"too amazing"*; it does not condemn the fourth. Verse 20 then uses
*"so"* (*kēn*) to bring in a fifth way, the adulterous woman's, which also leaves no visible trace.
She eats, wipes her mouth, and denies any wrong. The comparison suggests why the passage chose
traceless things: what leaves no mark can be denied. Proverbs 9:17 gave Folly the same eating
image — *"Food eaten in secret is pleasant"*. The adulteress's guilt is not undone by the absence of
evidence; her own claim, not the act, is what the verse sets before the reader. See [[Sexual Sin]]
and [[Deception]].

The meaning of verse 19's fourth item is disputed. Readers take it as wonder at courtship and sexual
union, or as a darker comparison to the seduction in verse 20. The page does not decide between them;
the text's own word is *amazing*, and verse 20's *"so"* marks a link without stating what it is.

### When the order is turned over (21–23)

The earth *"trembles"* at four reversals. Two are about men: a servant who reigns and a fool with
plenty (compare 19:10, *"a servant to have rule over princes"*, and Ecclesiastes 10:7). Two are
about women. The *unloved* woman (*śənûʾāh*) who gets a husband and the servant girl (*šip̄ḥāh*)
who inherits from her mistress (*gəḇirtāh*) use, in MAM, the words Genesis uses of [[Leah]] at
29:31 (*śənûʾāh*, *"hated"* in the WEB) and of [[Hagar]]'s mistress at 16:4 (*gəḇirtāh*). See
[[The Unloved Wife and the Servant Heir - Proverbs 30.23, Leah, and Hagar]].

**Explicit tension of perspective.** The saying speaks from the established order's point of view:
the household cannot bear the reversal. Genesis tells those reversals from the other side. Yahweh
*"saw that Leah was hated, and he opened her womb"* (Genesis 29:31), and he meets Hagar in the
wilderness after her mistress dealt harshly with her (16:6–13). Proverbs 30:23 does not deny that
care; it records that a sudden rise in status, without the character to carry it, produces
contempt and conflict. The servant made king in 22 is likewise set beside [[Joseph]], a slave who
rose to rule in Egypt without the disorder the saying fears. Neither text cancels the other.

### Small and wise, stately and strong (24–31)

The four small creatures are wise because each makes up for weakness by a different means:
foresight (ants), a secure dwelling (conies), order without a ruler (locusts), and access through
persistence (the lizard in a palace). The ants repeat the lesson of 6:6–8; the locusts recall the
swarm of the eighth plague, though here they are an example of order rather than judgment. See
[[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]].

The animal names are not all certain. The WEB's *conies* (*šəp̄annîm*) are usually identified as
rock hyraxes. The *lizard* (*śəmāmîṯ*) has also been rendered *spider*. The *greyhound*
(*zarzîr moṯnayim*, literally something *girded in the loins*) is uncertain enough that translations
also give *strutting rooster* or *war horse*. The last line about the king is also hard: the WEB's
*"against whom there is no rising up"* renders *ʾalqûm*, a rare word read in several ways. These are
noted from the Hebrew read in MAM; no lexicon was consulted, so the alternative glosses are reported
as translation options, not as settled meanings.

The second animal list has the opposite emphasis to the first. The lion, which *"doesn't turn away
for any"*, and the confident king are stately in their strength; the small creatures were wise in
their weakness. Kings appear in both lists: the locusts have none, and the lizard lives in their
palaces. See [[Kingship and Empire]] and [[Creation]].

### The hand on the mouth (32–33)

The last saying turns from observation to command. Whether one has acted foolishly in lifting
oneself up or only *"thought evil"*, the response is the gesture of silence. Job used it before God
(Job 40:4). The reason is a triple wordplay in Hebrew: *mîṣ* (*churning*, *wringing*, *forcing*)
appears three times in verse 33, and *ʾap̄* means both *nose* and *anger*, with *ʾappāyim* in the
last line (MAM, read 2026-10-27). Pressing the nose draws blood; pressing anger draws strife. Agur's
words therefore end where Proverbs often ends a point, in [[Strife and Peacemaking]]: the way to
avoid the quarrel is to stop before the pressure is applied. See
[[Lay Your Hand on Your Mouth - Job 21.5, 29.9, and 40.4]].
