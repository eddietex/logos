# The Young Man Void of Understanding

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 7:1–27

## Summary

The strange woman's third and last lecture, and the only one told as a story. Proverbs 2:16–19
named her in one sentence, chapter 5 described her road, and 6:20–35 argued from the nature of the
act; chapter 7 lets the son watch it happen. **The charge** (7:1–5): keep the father's words and
commandments, guard his teaching *"as the apple of your eye"*, bind them on the fingers, write them
on the heart, and call wisdom *"my sister"*, so that they keep him from the strange woman. **The
scene from the window** (7:6–23): the father looks out through his lattice at dusk and sees a young
man *"void of understanding"* walk the street toward her corner as the night darkens. She meets him
dressed as a prostitute, seizes and kisses him, and speaks: she has peace-offering meat at home and
has paid her vows; she came out looking for him; her bed is spread with Egyptian linen and perfumed
with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon; her husband is away until the full moon. He follows her at once,
*"as an ox goes to the slaughter"*, a bird hurrying to the snare, not knowing it will cost his life.
**The verdict** (7:24–27): sons, do not turn aside to her paths; she has slain *"a mighty army"*, and
*"Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the rooms of death."*

The lecture is built so that **the son sees what the young man cannot**. The young man sees only
the woman, the street, and the open door; the father, and the son reading over his shoulder, see
the hour, the trap, the ox, and the end.

**7:1–5 — The charge.** *"My son, keep my words. Lay up my commandments within you. Keep my
commandments and live! Guard my teaching as the apple of your eye"* (7:1–2). Almost every phrase is
a phrase the father has used before, and the Hebrew (read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the
Masorah*) shows how close: *ûmiṣwōṯay tiṣpōn ʾittāḵ*, *"store up my commandments within you"*, is
2:1's second line word for word; *šəmōr miṣwōṯay weḥyēh*, *"Keep my commandments, and live"*, is the
line his own father taught him at 4:4. *Tiṣpōn*, *lay up* or *hide*, is the verb the gang of 1:11
used when they proposed to *"lurk secretly"* (*niṣpənâ*) for the innocent: they hide themselves to
ambush, and the son hides the commandments to be kept from ambush.

*"As the apple of your eye"* is *kəʾîšôn ʿênêḵā*, *the little man of your eye*, the pupil — the part
of the body a person protects by reflex. Deuteronomy 32:10 used the phrase of God guarding Israel in
the wilderness (*"He kept him as the apple of his eye"*), and Psalm 17:8 prays it (*"Keep me as the
apple of your eye"*). Here the direction is reversed: the son guards the teaching as God guards his
people, and the teaching guards him. *ʾÎšôn* then comes back two verses later in another sense: the
young man walks out *bəʾîšôn laylâ*, *"in the middle of the night"* (7:9). The father uses the same
word for the pupil the son must guard and for the black centre of the night the fool walks into.
See [[The Apple of the Eye - Deuteronomy 32.10, Psalm 17.8, and Proverbs 7.2]].

*"Bind them on your fingers. Write them on the tablet of your heart"* (7:3). The second line is
3:3's (*kāṯḇēm ʿal-lûaḥ libbeḵā*); the first moves 3:3's *neck* (*gargərôṯeḵā*) to the *fingers*
(*ʾeṣbəʿōṯeḵā*), and keeps its verb *qošrēm*. Deuteronomy 6:8 bound the words *"for a sign on your
hand"* (*ûqšartām … ʿal-yāḏeḵā*); fingers are the nearest thing Proverbs has to that sign. See
[[The Tablet of the Heart - Proverbs 3.3, Jeremiah 31.33, and 2 Corinthians 3.3]] and
[[When You Walk and When You Lie Down - Deuteronomy 6.6-9 and Proverbs 6.20-23]].

*"Tell wisdom, 'You are my sister.' Call understanding your relative"* (7:4, *ʾĕmōr laḥoḵmâ ʾăḥōṯî
ʾāt*). Wisdom is to be the woman of the son's own household, so that a strange one has no room. The
Song of Solomon calls the bride *"my sister, my bride"* (4:9, 10, *ʾăḥōṯî ḵallâ*), and the wiki
notes the overlap without claiming Proverbs means *sister* in that sense: the second line's
*relative* (*mōḏāʿ*, a kinsman or acquaintance) keeps the language in the family. In 4:6–8 the son
was told to love wisdom and embrace her; here he is told to claim her as kin. The purpose is then
given in the words of 2:16 almost exactly — *"that they may keep you from the strange woman, from
the foreigner who flatters with her words"* (7:5). Only the first verb changes: 2:16's
*ləhaṣṣîləḵā*, *to deliver you*, becomes *lišmorḵā*, *to keep you*, the verb of 7:1 and 7:2. The
whole charge is one word, *šāmar*, used three times: keep the teaching, and it will keep you.

**7:6–9 — The window.** *"For at the window of my house, I looked out through my lattice"* (7:6,
*bəʿaḏ ʾešnabbî nišqāp̄tî*). The verb *šāqap̄* in this stem, *to look down* or *look out*, with a
window, is the verb of two other watchers the wiki knows or will reach. Abimelech *"looked out at a
window"* (*wayyašqēp̄ … bəʿaḏ haḥallôn*) and saw Isaac caressing Rebekah, and knew she was his wife
(Genesis 26:8); Sisera's mother *"looked through the lattice"* (*nišqəp̄â … bəʿaḏ hāʾešnāḇ*, Judges
5:28) for a son who would not come home. Judges 5:28 has both of Proverbs' nouns, *window* and
*lattice*. **Each is a watcher at a window seeing something about a marriage or a son that the
people below do not know is being seen.** The father's window is the didactic version: he looks so
that his son may see too. See [[Through the Window - Proverbs 7.6, Genesis 26.8, and Judges 5.28]].

*"I saw among the simple ones. I discerned among the youths a young man void of understanding"*
(7:7). The *simple* (*pəṯāʾyim*) are the open-minded and unformed of 1:4 and 1:22, the readers
the book was written for; the young man is one of them. *Void of understanding*, *ḥăsar-lēḇ*,
*lacking heart*, is what 6:32 called the adulterer, and it is the phrase Folly will use for the
guest she calls into her house (9:16). The lecture's title is taken from it. He is not described as
wicked. He is described as missing the organ the father told him to guard (4:23).

*"Passing through the street near her corner, he went the way to her house"* (7:8). *Yiṣʿāḏ*, *he
paces*, is a deliberate step; the father had promised that *"When you go, your steps will not be
hampered"* (4:12, where the noun is *ṣaʿaḏ*, read in Sefaria for this pass) and warned that the
strange woman's *"steps lead straight to Sheol"* (5:5). The young man is not yet at her door; he is walking
*the way to her house*, the road 5:8 told the son to keep far from: *"Don't come near the door of
her house."*

*"In the twilight, in the evening of the day, in the middle of the night and in the darkness"* (7:9,
*bəneš ep̄-bəʿereḇ yôm bəʾîšôn laylâ waʾăp̄ēlâ*). Four time words, each darker than the last, and
they read as the young man's walk lengthening into night. Job 24:15 drew the same figure: *"The eye
also of the adulterer waits for the twilight"* (*nešep̄*). [[The Two Paths]] had the way of the
wicked *"like darkness"*, where they *"don't know what they stumble over"* (4:19); the lamp of 6:23
was given against exactly this hour. See [[Light and Darkness]] and
[[A Lamp and a Light - Proverbs 6.23 and Psalm 119.105]].

**7:10–13 — The woman.** *"Behold, there a woman met him with the attire of a prostitute, and with
crafty intent"* (7:10). *Šîṯ zônâ*, *a prostitute's dress*: she is not a prostitute — she has a
husband and a house (7:19) — but dresses as one. *Crafty intent* is *nəṣurat lēḇ*, *guarded of
heart*, from *nāṣar*, the verb of 4:23's *"Keep your heart"* (*nəṣōr libbeḵā*). **She has done what
the father told the son to do.** Her heart is guarded, its purpose hidden, while the young man's
is missing. Genesis 38 is the wiki's other case of a woman taken for a prostitute by her dress:
Tamar put off her widow's garments, veiled herself, and sat *"by the way"*, and Judah *"thought that
she was a prostitute, for she had covered her face"* (Genesis 38:14–15, *wayyaḥšəḇehā ləzônâ*).
Tamar covered her face; this woman makes it bold (7:13). See
[[The Attire of a Prostitute - Genesis 38 and Proverbs 7.10]].

*"She is loud and defiant. Her feet don't stay in her house. Now she is in the streets, now in the
squares, and lurking at every corner"* (7:11–12). *Loud* is *hōmiyyâ*, and 9:13 will use the same
word of the *"foolish woman"*, the figure of Folly, so the lecture prepares the portrait of chapter
9. *Her feet* are the feet of 5:5, which *"go down to death"*. *In the streets … in the squares*
(*baḥûṣ … bārəḥōḇôṯ*) are the two places where Wisdom cried out at 1:20 (*ḥoḵmôṯ baḥûṣ tārōnnâ
bārəḥōḇôṯ*): **the strange woman works the same public ground as Wisdom, and calls in the same
places.** 1:21 even placed Wisdom *"at the head of noisy places"*, *hōmiyyôṯ*, the root of *loud*.
*Lurking* is *teʾĕrōḇ*, the verb of the gang of 1:11, 18 (*neʾerbâ*, *"Let's lay in wait"*;
*yeʾĕrōḇû*) and of Job's oath, *"if … I have laid wait at my neighbor's door"* (Job 31:9,
*ʾārāḇtî*). See [[Wisdom Calls in the Street]], [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]], and
[[The Neighbor's Door - Proverbs 5 and Job 31.9-12]].

*"So she caught him, and kissed him. With an impudent face she said to him"* (7:13). *Heḥĕzîqâ bô*,
*she took hold of him*. Potiphar's wife also seized Joseph, with a different verb (*wattiṯpəśēhû
bəḇiḡdô*, *"She caught him by his garment"*, Genesis 39:12), and he *"ran outside"*; this young man
stays. *Hēʿēzâ p̄ānêhā*, *she made her face strong*, is the opposite of Tamar's covered one.

**7:14–20 — Her speech.** *"Sacrifices of peace offerings are with me. Today I have paid my vows"*
(7:14, *zibḥê šəlāmîm ʿālāy hayyôm šillamtî nəḏārāy*). **The seduction opens with worship.** A peace
offering, *šəlāmîm*, was the sacrifice whose meat the offerer and household ate (Exodus 20:24;
24:5); and Leviticus 7:16 adds that a vow offering *"shall be eaten on the day that he offers his
sacrifice; and on the next day what remains of it shall be eaten"*. So she has meat at home that
must be eaten now, and a feast to offer — *Today*. The pun is in the Hebrew: *šəlāmîm*, *peace
offerings*, and *šillamtî*, *I have paid*, are the same root. Whether the vow was real piety put to
bad use or a pretext, the text does not say; what it shows is that the language of sacrifice can be
spoken by the strange woman as easily as by anyone. See
[[Peace Offerings and Paid Vows - Proverbs 7.14 and Leviticus 7.16]], [[Sacrifice and Offering]],
and [[Oaths and Vows]].

*"Therefore I came out to meet you, to diligently seek your face, and I have found you"* (7:15,
*ləšaḥēr pānêḵā wāʾemṣāʾekkā*). *Šiḥēr*, *seek diligently*, and *māṣāʾ*, *find*, are Wisdom's pair:
those who refused her *"will seek me diligently, but they will not find me"* (1:28, *yəšaḥărunənî
wəlōʾ yimṣāʾunənî*), and *"Those who seek me diligently will find me"* (8:17, *ûməšaḥăray
yimṣāʾunənî*). **The strange woman says to the young man what Wisdom promises the son**, and she
says it first: she has already done the seeking. See [[Wisdom]].

*"I have spread my couch with carpets of tapestry, with striped cloths of the yarn of Egypt. I have
perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon"* (7:16–17). Luxury goods, imported. *ʾĒṭûn
miṣrāyim*, *Egyptian yarn* or linen, is costly cloth from [[Egypt]]. The spices are a bride's and a
king's: the Song of Solomon's garden has *"myrrh and aloes, with all the best spices"* and
*"calamus and cinnamon"* (4:14, *mōr waʾăhālôṯ … qāneh wəqinnāmôn*), and the royal wedding psalm's
garments *"smell like myrrh, aloes, and cassia"* (Psalm 45:8; 45:9 MT, *mōr-waʾăhālôṯ*). Two of the three — myrrh and
cinnamon — are also the first two ingredients of the holy anointing oil (*mor-dərôr … qinnəmān-beśem*,
Exodus 30:23), which *"shall not be poured on man's flesh"* (30:32). Proverbs does not draw the
connection. The wiki records the vocabulary: the strange woman's bed smells of the wedding and the
sanctuary. See [[Myrrh, Aloes, and Cinnamon - Proverbs 7.17, Song of Solomon 4.14, and Psalm 45.8]].

*"Come, let's take our fill of loving until the morning. Let's solace ourselves with loving"* (7:18,
*ləḵâ nirweh ḏōḏîm ʿaḏ-habbōqer niṯʿalləsâ bāʾŏhāḇîm*). *Ləḵâ*, *Come*, is the gang's first word in
1:11 (*ləḵâ ʾittānû*, *"Come with us"*). *Nirweh*, *let us drink our fill*, is the verb of 5:19,
where the son's own wife was to satisfy him (*yərawwuḵā*); *ḏōḏîm*, *loving*, is the Song of
Solomon's word (*"How beautiful is your love"*, 4:10, *ḏōḏayiḵ*). The father has already given the
son this language for his wife. The woman offers the same words for the wrong person. See [[Love]] and
[[Your Own Well - Proverbs 5.15-19, Song of Solomon 4.12-15, and Ecclesiastes 9.9]].

*"For my husband isn't at home. He has gone on a long journey. He has taken a bag of money with him.
He will come home at the full moon"* (7:19–20). She does not say *my husband*: the Hebrew is
*hāʾîš*, *the man*. *A bag of money*, *ṣərôr-hakkesep̄*, is the phrase of Joseph's brothers'
*"bundle of money"* (*ṣərôr-kaspô*, Genesis 42:35); the husband has taken enough for a long trip,
and *the full moon* (*keseʾ*) fixes the date of his return. She is offering safety: no witness, no
interruption. Proverbs 6:34–35 has already told the son what the husband does when he comes home.
See [[No Ransom for the Adulterer - Proverbs 6.29-35, Leviticus 20.10, and Deuteronomy 22.22]].

**7:21–23 — The ox, the noose, the bird.** *"With persuasive words, she led him astray. With the
flattering of her lips, she seduced him"* (7:21). *Persuasive words* is *rōḇ liqḥāh*, *her much
teaching*: *leqaḥ* is the book's word for what the wise man receives — *"that the wise man may hear,
and increase in learning (leqaḥ)"* (1:5), *"I give you sound learning (leqaḥ ṭôḇ)"* (4:2). **She is
a teacher too, and she has a pupil.** *The flattering of her lips*, *ḥēleq śəp̄āṯêhā*, is the
smoothness of 2:16, 5:3 and 6:24 again. See [[Language and Speech]] and [[Deception]].

*"He followed her immediately, as an ox goes to the slaughter, as a fool stepping into a noose.
Until an arrow strikes through his liver, as a bird hurries to the snare, and doesn't know that it
will cost his life"* (7:22–23). *Pittōʾm*, *suddenly*, after the long slow walk of 7:8–9. The ox
does not know where it is being led. The second simile is a textual difficulty: the Masoretic text
reads, literally, *and as an anklet* (*ʿeḵes*) *to the discipline of a fool*, which does not
construe easily, and translations differ — the WEB's *"a fool stepping into a noose"* is one
interpretation, and some commentators emend the line; the wiki has not checked a commentary here
and takes no position beyond noting the difficulty. *His liver* (*kəḇēḏô*) is the seat of life.
The bird recalls 1:17, where *"in vain is the net spread in the sight of any bird"*: the gang were
too foolish to see their trap, and now the young man is the bird. *Doesn't know*, *wəlōʾ-yāḏaʿ*,
is repeated exactly at 9:18 of Folly's guest, *"he doesn't know that the departed spirits are
there"*. *It will cost his life* is *ḇənap̄šô hûʾ*, *it is at the price of his soul* — as the
gang's ambush was set *"for their own lives"* (1:18, *lənap̄šōṯām*).

**7:24–27 — The verdict.** *"Now therefore, sons, listen to me. Pay attention to the words of my
mouth. Don't let your heart turn to her ways. Don't go astray in her paths"* (7:24–25). *Sons*,
plural, as at 4:1 and 5:7, and 7:24a repeats 5:7a (*wəʿattâ ḇānîm šimʿû-lî*). *Turn aside*,
*ʾal-yēśṭ*, is the verb of the law of the suspected wife in Numbers 5:12, *"If any man's wife goes astray"* (*kî-ṯiśṭeh ʾištô*): the woman *went astray*, and
the son is told not to go astray after her. *Heart* and *paths* again: the heart guarded (4:23) is
the one that does not turn.

*"For she has thrown down many wounded. Yes, all her slain are a mighty army. Her house is the way
to Sheol, going down to the rooms of death"* (7:26–27). The images are military — *ḥălālîm*, the
slain, and *ʿăṣumîm*, the numerous or the mighty — and the young man is one casualty among many. The
final line closes the three lectures on the same place: her house *"leads down to death"* (2:18),
her steps *"lead straight to Sheol"* (5:5), and now her house is *"the way to Sheol"*. *Rooms* is
*ḥaḏrê*, *chambers* — the word for the inner room or bedchamber, and the word the Song of Solomon
uses when *"The king has brought me into his rooms"* (1:4, *ḥăḏārāyw*). The bedroom she prepared in
7:16–17 is a room of death. See [[Sheol]] and [[Life and Death]].

**Reading it after Job.** [[Job's Oath of Innocence]] swore that Job's heart had not been enticed
to a woman and that he had not *"laid wait at my neighbor's door"* (Job 31:9). Proverbs 7 shows
the other side of the door: the woman herself lies in wait (7:12). The wiki keeps the reserve it
kept on chapters 5 and 6. Proverbs shows what happens to a man who goes in; it does not let the
reader say that a man who has lost everything must have gone in.

**God is not named.** As in 6:20–35, *Yahweh* does not appear in this chapter. The only religious
act in it is the woman's sacrifice (7:14). The father's charge rests on his words, his commandments,
and his teaching (7:1–2), and the vocabulary of Sinai (*miṣwâ*, *tôrâ*, *lûaḥ*) carries the rest.

## People

- The father, the young man, the woman, and her absent husband are all unnamed types. The woman is
  the strange woman of 2:16, chapter 5, and 6:24–35; as on [[If You Seek Her as Silver]],
  [[Sexual Sin]] is her page and she gets no person page.
- [[God]] — not named in this chapter; see the Summary.
- [[Tamar]], [[Potiphar's Wife]] — not named; the wiki's Genesis comparisons for the prostitute's
  dress (Genesis 38:14–15) and the seizing (Genesis 39:12).

## Places

- [[Sheol]] — *"Her house is the way to Sheol"* (7:27).
- [[Egypt]] — *"the yarn of Egypt"* on her couch (7:16), the only place named in the chapter.
