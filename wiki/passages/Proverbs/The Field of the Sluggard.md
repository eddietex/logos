# The Field of the Sluggard

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 24:23–34

## Summary

A short appendix with its own heading: *"These also are sayings of the wise"* (24:23). The first
*"words of the wise"* (22:17–24:22) ended on the fear of Yahweh and the king; this second, much
smaller collection follows before the Solomonic sayings resume at 25:1. It has two halves. Verses
23–29 are a run of sayings about the court, the neighbour, and the field, held together by speech —
what is said to the wicked, what answer is given, what is said against a neighbour, what the
wronged man says he will do. Verses 30–34 are a single first-person observation: a walk past a
ruined vineyard, and the lesson the walker took from it. Yahweh is not named anywhere in the twelve
verses, and no one in them is addressed as *"My son"*. The only place the collection mentions
*blessing*, *cursing*, and *deceit* is in the mouths and lives of people.

**24:23–25 — Partiality in judgment.** *"To show partiality in judgment is not good. He who says to
the wicked, 'You are righteous'; peoples will curse him, and nations will abhor him— but it will go
well with those who convict the guilty, and a rich blessing will come on them."* The phrase the WEB
renders *"show partiality"* is literally *to recognize faces* — the very words of the law's charge
to Israel's judges, *"You shall not show partiality in judgment"* (Deuteronomy 1:17), repeated
almost word for word at Proverbs 28:21 (see the Notes). The partial judge's sentence is quoted as
he speaks it: *"You are righteous"* — in Hebrew the single word *ṣaddîq*, the verdict *innocent*.
The penalty is public and wide: *peoples* and *nations* curse and abhor him, the language of a whole
society's disgust rather than a private grievance. The reward goes to *"those who convict"* — the
verb behind the book's *reproof* (*hôḵîaḥ*), here meaning to establish guilt in court. Proverbs
17:15 had called the same act an abomination to Yahweh; here it is an abomination to the peoples.
The two are not in tension: both say a verdict that acquits the guilty is not a private favour.
See [[A Bribe Blinds - Proverbs 17.23, Exodus 23.8, and Deuteronomy 16.19]].

**24:26 — The kiss.** *"An honest answer is like a kiss on the lips."* In Hebrew there is no *like*:
*lips he kisses, who returns right words*. Placed straight after the court, the *answer* is most
naturally a reply given there or in counsel — *right* (*nəḵōḥîm*) means straight, to the point —
and its effect is that of a kiss, the gesture of friendship and loyalty. Whether the one who kisses
is the speaker or the one he answers is left open by the grammar; either way, a straight answer is
treated as an act of love, not of hostility. The saying stands beside 27:6 ahead, which sets a friend's
wounds against an enemy's kisses — *"Faithful are the wounds of a friend; although the kisses of an
enemy are profuse"* — and against the flatterer of 26:28 and 29:5.

**24:27 — First the field.** *"Prepare your work outside, and get your fields ready. Afterwards,
build your house."* The one saying in the block that is pure agricultural advice, and it is about
order: the work outside, the field readied, and only *afterwards* the house. The verb *prepare* is
the *kûn* of 24:3, where the house was *established* by understanding; *build* is the same verb as
24:3's *built*. Coming six verses after the house built through wisdom, the saying gives that
house a foundation in sequence: a household is built on the field that will feed it. Read more
broadly — and the Masoretic text marks it off as its own paragraph — it is the wisdom of putting
the means before the comfort. Whether a marriage is meant by *build your house* (as later Jewish
interpretation sometimes took it) the text does not say, and the wiki does not decide.

**24:28–29 — The neighbour.** *"Don't be a witness against your neighbor without cause. Don't
deceive with your lips. Don't say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me; I will render to the man
according to his work.'"* Two prohibitions about the neighbour, the second of which supplies the
motive for the first. *Without cause* is *ḥinnām*, the gang's word at 1:11 (*"ambush the innocent
without cause"*) and the quarrel's at 3:30; a witness *without cause* may simply mean one who is
not needed, or one with no true case. Verse 29 then explains why someone would bear such witness:
because the neighbour wronged him first. The words forbidden to the man are the words of the law's
retaliation formula — *"as he has done, so shall it be done to him"* (Leviticus 24:19) — turned
from the passive of a court sentence into a private resolution in the first person. And their last
clause, *"I will render to the man according to his work"*, is word for word what 24:12 said God
does: *"Shall he not render to every man according to his work?"* Twelve verses apart, the same
phrase belongs to God and is forbidden to man. See
[[As He Has Done to Me - Proverbs 24.29 and the Law of Retaliation]],
[[According to His Work - Proverbs 24.12, Psalm 62.12, and Romans 2.6]], and
[[Wait for Yahweh - Proverbs 20.22 and the Refusal of Revenge]].

**24:30–31 — The vineyard.** *"I went by the field of the sluggard, by the vineyard of the man void
of understanding; Behold, it was all grown over with thorns. Its surface was covered with nettles,
and its stone wall was broken down."* The book's only extended first-person scene outside the
father's lectures, and a counterpart to the window scene of 7:6–23: there the speaker watched a
*"young man void of understanding"* walk into ruin, and here he walks past the ruin itself, the
property of *a man void of understanding* — the same phrase, *ḥăsar-lēḇ*, *lacking heart*. The
*field* of verse 27, which should have been made ready, is the one in front of him, untouched. Two
weeds cover it — *thorns* and *nettles*; the identifications are conventional, and Genesis 3:18's
*"thorns and thistles"* use different Hebrew words. What was not left undone was the decay: the
stone wall, built once by someone, has *fallen down*. The wall is the *gāḏēr* that Yahweh, in
Isaiah's song of the vineyard, says he will himself break down (Isaiah 5:5). There the ruin is a
judgment; here it is simply what happens when no one acts. See
[[The Vineyard Gone to Thorns - Proverbs 24.30-34, Genesis 3.18, and Isaiah 5.1-7]].

**24:32 — The lesson taken.** *"Then I saw, and considered well. I saw, and received instruction."*
Two verbs of seeing (*ʾeḥĕzeh*, *rāʾîṯî*), one of attention (*I set my heart*), one of learning (*I
took instruction*, *mûsār*). This is Proverbs' method stated outright: wisdom is learned by looking
at the world and reflecting on what one sees. The sluggard's field taught the passer-by more than
it ever taught its owner.

**24:33–34 — The saying.** *"a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to
sleep; so your poverty will come as a robber, and your want as an armed man."* The instruction he
received is not new: it is the saying of 6:10–11, which the father attached to the ant. The first
verse is identical letter for letter in Hebrew; the second differs only in spelling and one word
(see [[Go to the Ant - Proverbs 6.6-11, 24.30-34, and 30.25]]). In chapter 6 the saying was
learned from a creature that works; here, from a man who did not. Its point is the same in both:
the sluggard never decides to be poor, he only takes *a little* more rest, and the poverty arrives
not as a gradual decline but as an intruder — *a robber*, *an armed man*.

**What the block adds.** The appendix closes the *"words of the wise"* by bringing wisdom out of the
lecture and into the world: the court, the answer, the field, the witness stand, the wronged man's
private resolve, the walk past a neighbour's vineyard. Its sayings hang together more than they
first appear to. The judge who says *"You are righteous"* to the wicked (24) and the witness who
pays back his neighbour (28–29) both misuse speech in the place where speech decides outcomes; the
honest answer (26) is the alternative to both. The field readied before the house (27) and the field
left to thorns (30–31) are the two ends of the same advice. And the phrase *"according to his work"*
(29) closes the loop with 24:12: the son is not to make himself the one who renders. The next queue
item opens a new collection: *"These also are proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of
Judah copied out"* (25:1). See [[The Glory of Kings]].

## People

- No named person appears. [[God]] is not named in 24:23–34; the absence is noted above, and no
  person page is revised. The collection is attributed to *"the wise"* (24:23), not to [[Solomon]].
- The judge, the wicked, the honest answerer, the neighbour, the witness, the speaker, and the
  sluggard are generic figures; none receives an invented identity.

## Places

- No named place. *The field* and *the vineyard* (27, 30) are generic.

## Notes

**Boundary and title.** The planned 24:23–34 unit is retained. 24:23 opens with its own heading, and
25:1 opens the Hezekian collection with another; the whole appendix is one queue item. Within it the
Masoretic text marks paragraph breaks (*petuchah*) after 24:27, 24:29, and 24:34. The provisional
title, *The Field of the Sluggard*, matches the WEB of 24:30 and is kept. Exactly one queue item is
advanced.

**Language.** Read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* on 2026-10-26:
- 24:23 has *gam-ʾēlleh laḥăḵāmîm*, *these also are of the wise*, and *hakker-pānîm bəmišpāṭ
  bal-ṭôḇ*, *to recognize faces in judgment is not good*. Proverbs 28:21 has *hakker-pānîm lōʾ-ṭôḇ*.
  Deuteronomy 1:17 has *lōʾ-takkîrû p̄ānîm bammišpāṭ* and 16:19 *lōʾ ṯakkîr pānîm* — the same verb
  and noun. Proverbs 18:5, on the existing connection page, uses a different idiom, *śəʾēṯ pənê*,
  *lifting the face*.
- 24:24 has *ʾōmēr ləräšāʿ ṣaddîq ʾāttāh*, *who says to the wicked, "Righteous are you"*.
  *Curse* is *yiqqəḇuhû*, from *qāḇaḇ*; *abhor* is *yizʿāmûhû*, from *zāʿam*, a verb often used of
  indignation. That gloss of *zāʿam* is standard lexical knowledge, not checked against a lexicon in
  this pass.
- 24:25 has *wəlammôḵîḥîm yinʿām*, *to those who reprove* (or *convict*) *it will be pleasant*, and
  *birkaṯ-ṭôḇ*, *a blessing of good*. *Môḵîaḥ* is the participle of the verb Proverbs uses for
  reproof throughout.
- 24:26 is *śəp̄āṯayim yiššāq mēšîḇ dəḇārîm nəḵōḥîm* — no comparative particle; the WEB's *"like"*
  is interpretive.
- 24:27 has *hāḵēn baḥûṣ məlaʾḵteḵā … ʾaḥar ûḇānîṯā ḇêṯeḵā*. *Hāḵēn* is the imperative of *kûn*,
  the verb of 24:3's *yiṯkônān*; *ûḇānîṯā* is from *bānāh*, 24:3's *yibbāneh*.
- 24:28 has *ʿēḏ-ḥinnām bərēʿeḵā* and *wahăp̄ittîṯā biśp̄āṯeḵā*. *Ḥinnām* also stands at 1:11 and 3:30.
  *Deceive* is from *pāṯāh*, the verb of sinners *enticing* the son at 1:10 (*yəp̄attûḵā*).
- 24:29 has *kaʾăšer ʿāśāh-lî kēn ʾeʿĕśeh-lô ʾāšîḇ lāʾîš kəp̄oʿŏlô*. Leviticus 24:19 has *kaʾăšer
  ʿāśāh kēn yēʿāśeh lô*. 24:12 has *wəhēšîḇ ləʾāḏām kəp̄oʿŏlô*.
- 24:30 has *śəḏēh ʾîš-ʿāṣēl* and *kerem ʾāḏām ḥăsar-lēḇ*; 7:7 has *naʿar ḥăsar-lēḇ*.
- 24:31 has *qimmaśōnîm* (WEB *"thorns"*), *ḥărullîm* (WEB *"nettles"*), and *wəḡeḏer ʾăḇānāyw
  nehĕrāsāh*, *its wall of stones was torn down*. Genesis 3:18 has *qôṣ wəḏardar*; Isaiah 5:6 has
  *šāmîr wāšāyiṯ*; Isaiah 5:5 has *pārōṣ gəḏērô*, *break down its wall* — the same noun *gāḏēr* as
  Proverbs 24:31, a different verb. The botanical identifications of the plant words are uncertain,
  and the wiki does not attempt them.
- 24:32 has *wāʾeḥĕzeh ʾānōḵî ʾāšîṯ libbî rāʾîṯî lāqaḥtî mûsār*.
- 24:33–34 against 6:10–11: verse 33 is identical to 6:10; 24:34 has *miṯhallēḵ*, *rêšeḵā*, and
  *maḥsōreyḵā* where 6:11 has *kimhallēḵ*, *rēʾšeḵā*, and *maḥsōrəḵā*.
