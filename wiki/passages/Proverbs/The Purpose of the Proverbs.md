# The Purpose of the Proverbs

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 1:1–7

## Summary

Seven verses, and only the last of them is a proverb. The rest is a title and a statement of
purpose: the book tells its reader what it is, who wrote it, what it is for, and who it is for,
before it teaches a single thing. It is the nearest the Old Testament comes to a preface, and it
is built as one long sentence — a heading (1:1), a chain of purpose clauses each opening with
*to* (1:2–6), and a motto that closes the chain (1:7).

**The heading (1:1) names a king, his father, and his kingdom.** *"The proverbs of Solomon, the
son of David, king of Israel."* The Hebrew opens on the genre word — *mišlê*, the plural construct
of *māšāl*, which gives the book its Hebrew name — and only then names the man. [[Solomon]] is the
Old Testament's emblem of wisdom: he asked for *"an understanding heart to judge your people"*
(1 Kings 3:9), was told no one like him had come before or would come after (3:12), and *"spoke
three thousand proverbs"* (4:32). The heading claims that tradition for the book. It does not
claim every line of the book for Solomon, and the book's own later headings — *"the words of the
wise"* (22:17), Agur (30:1), Lemuel (31:1), and Hezekiah's copyists (25:1) — show that it was not
meant to. 1:1 is best read as the title of the whole anthology and the heading of chapters 1–9,
with 10:1 (*"The proverbs of Solomon"*) heading the next collection. See
[[The Wisdom of Solomon - 1 Kings 3-4 and the Headings of Proverbs]].

**The purpose clauses (1:2–6) pile up the vocabulary the book will use.** Wisdom (*ḥokmâ*),
instruction (*mûsār*), understanding (*bînâ*), wise dealing, righteousness, justice, equity,
prudence, knowledge, discretion, learning, sound counsel — a dozen near-synonyms in five verses.
The effect is deliberate saturation: wisdom in Proverbs is not one faculty but a field, and the
preface surveys its whole extent before the lectures begin. Three features of the list matter.

- **It is moral as well as practical.** Verse 3 sets *"wise dealing"* beside *"righteousness,
  justice, and equity"* — *ṣedeq ûmišpāṭ ûmêšārîm*, the vocabulary of the law court and the
  prophets. The book does not keep skill and goodness apart; being shrewd and being just are taught
  in one curriculum. See [[Righteousness]].
- **It is received before it is possessed.** Verse 3's verb is *"to receive"* (*lāqaḥat*), and
  verse 5's *"learning"* is *leqaḥ*, a noun from the same root — what is taken in. Wisdom in this
  preface is something handed on, which is why the next verse turns to *"My son, listen to your
  father's instruction"* (1:8). The tradition is a transmission before it is an achievement.
- **One of its gifts has a double edge.** *"Prudence"* for the simple (1:4) is *ʿormâ*. The same
  root gives the adjective for the serpent, *ʿārûm*, *"more subtle than any animal of the field"*
  (Genesis 3:1), and the noun in Eliphaz's *"He takes the wise in their own craftiness"*
  (*bəʿormām*, Job 5:13). Proverbs wants the simple to have what the serpent had, turned to good.
  Shrewdness is not a vice in this book; it is a capacity, and the preface claims it for wisdom.

**The audience is everyone, at every stage.** Verse 4 addresses *"the simple"* (*pətāʾyim*, the
untaught and impressionable — not yet fools, but open to anything) and *"the young man"*. Verse 5
turns at once to *"the wise man"* and *"the man of understanding"*: they too may *"hear, and increase
in learning"*. Proverbs is not a primer the wise outgrow. It is a book whose most advanced students
are told, in the preface, that they still have something to gain from it — which is the opposite of
the posture [[Eliphaz the Temanite]] takes when he cites the wisdom of the elders against Job. The
wise man of 1:5 *listens*.

**Verse 6 promises to teach the reader how to read.** *"To understand a proverb, and parables, the
words and riddles of the wise."* *Māšāl*, *məlîṣâ*, *ḥîdâ* — three kinds of indirect speech, the
last of them the word for the riddle Samson sets at his wedding (Judges 14:12) and for the
*"riddles"* God does not use with Moses (Numbers 12:8). Wisdom's language is oblique on purpose: a
proverb is compressed, a parable figurative, a riddle closed until it is solved. **The book does not
only contain wisdom; it trains the reader in the kind of attention wisdom requires.** That is why
the two-line sayings of chapters 10–29 so often set two clauses side by side without saying how
they relate — the reader is meant to do the relating.

**And then the motto (1:7).** *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge; but the foolish
despise wisdom and instruction."* After six verses of purpose, the first actual proverb, and the
first use of the divine name. Three things about it carry weight.

1. **It is placed at the head of the book and repeated at the end of the prologue.** *"The fear of
   Yahweh is the beginning of wisdom"* (9:10). Chapters 1–9 are bracketed by the same sentence, and
   it returns as *"The fear of Yahweh teaches wisdom"* at 15:33. A tradition that elsewhere argues
   from the ant and the sluggard puts Israel's covenant God, by name, at the root of the whole
   enterprise. See [[The Fear of God]].
2. **"Beginning" is *rēʾšît*** — the word of *bərēʾšît*, *"In the beginning"* (Genesis 1:1), and of
   Wisdom's claim that *"Yahweh possessed me in the beginning of his work"* (*rēʾšît darkô*, 8:22).
   It can mean first in time or first in rank — the starting point or the chief part — and both
   senses work here: the fear of Yahweh is where knowledge starts and what governs it after. **Job
   says something stronger.** Job 28:28 has no word for *beginning* at all: *"the fear of the Lord,
   that is wisdom."* Where Proverbs opens a curriculum, Job 28 offers the fear of God as the whole
   of what a human being receives. See
   [[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]].
3. **The second line names the alternative, and it is contempt, not ignorance.** *"The foolish
   despise wisdom and instruction."* The fool (*ʾĕwîl*) is not someone who has not been told; he has
   been told and *despises* it. And the object is the same pair verse 2 opened with — *ḥokmâ
   ûmûsār*, *wisdom and instruction* — so the preface closes where it began, with the one difference
   that what verse 2 offered, verse 7's fool refuses. The book's great contrast, wise and foolish,
   is set up as a choice before a single saying has been read. See [[Folly]].

**Read after Job, the preface is modest.** Proverbs promises to teach wisdom, not to explain the
world. Its motto puts a limit at the start of knowledge — knowledge begins in a fear directed at
someone else — and the book will go on to say, as its sentences accumulate, how life ordinarily
goes for the wise and the foolish. Job's friends turned that ordinary course into a verdict on a
particular man, and the book of Job showed what that cost. Proverbs, read on its own terms, is the
tradition they were drawing on; read after Job, it is the same tradition with its claims sized
correctly — sayings, not sentences passed. See [[Retribution]].

## Key verses

> **1** The proverbs of Solomon, the son of David, king of Israel:

> **2** to know wisdom and instruction; to discern the words of understanding;
> **3** to receive instruction in wise dealing, in righteousness, justice, and equity;
> **4** to give prudence to the simple, knowledge and discretion to the young man:
> **5** that the wise man may hear, and increase in learning; that the man of understanding may
> attain to sound counsel:
> **6** to understand a proverb, and parables, the words and riddles of the wise.

> **7** The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge; but the foolish despise wisdom and
> instruction.

## People

- [[Solomon]] — named in the first verse as the book's author and authority, by the same three
  terms the historical books use of him: son of David, king of Israel. He does not speak in this
  unit; the voice of 1:2–7 is the editor's, and the voice from 1:8 on is a father's.
- [[David]] — named only as Solomon's father, which is enough to place the book in the line of
  the dynasty and of the promise attached to it.
- [[God]] — *Yahweh*, once, in the motto (1:7). The book's first theological statement is not
  about what God does but about how a human being stands toward him, and it uses the covenant
  name rather than the generic *ʾĕlōhîm*. Proverbs is an international genre with an Israelite
  root.

## Places

- *Israel* is named in 1:1 as Solomon's kingdom, not as a location in the narrative. No place page
  is created for it here; a page for Israel as a place has not yet been needed in the wiki.

## Themes

- [[Wisdom]] — the book's subject, surveyed in its whole extent before the lectures begin: skill,
  discipline, discernment, justice, shrewdness, counsel, and the reading of indirect speech.
- [[The Fear of God]] — the beginning (*rēʾšît*) of knowledge, placed at the head of the book and
  repeated at the end of its prologue (9:10).
- [[Folly]] — introduced in the book's first antithesis: the fool despises what the preface has
  just offered.
- [[Righteousness]] — *"righteousness, justice, and equity"* (1:3), named as part of what wisdom
  teaches, not as a separate subject.
- [[Language and Speech]] — proverb, parable, riddle (1:6): a book about words that promises to
  teach its reader how to hear them.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — a king's book: wisdom attached to the throne at the outset (1:1),
  as 1 Kings 3 attaches it to the request for a heart to judge.
- [[Retribution]] — not stated in this unit, but the book it introduces is where the doctrine's
  classic sentences live; see the closing paragraph of the Summary.

## Notes

**Hebrew.** Claims above about the Hebrew wording — *mišlê* (1:1), *ḥokmâ ûmûsār* in both 1:2 and
1:7, *lāqaḥat* (1:3) and *leqaḥ* (1:5), *ṣedeq ûmišpāṭ ûmêšārîm* (1:3), *ʿormâ* and *pətāʾyim*
(1:4), *māšāl*, *məlîṣâ*, and *ḥîdâ* (1:6), *rēʾšît* and *ʾĕwîlîm* (1:7) — were checked against the
Masoretic text as given in Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* edition, as were the
comparisons with Genesis 1:1, Genesis 3:1 (*ʿārûm*), Job 5:13 (*bəʿormām*), Job 28:28, Psalm 111:10,
Proverbs 8:22 (*rēʾšît darkô*), and 9:10 (*təḥillat*). The glosses of the words' ranges (e.g. *pətî*
as "untaught, impressionable", *rēʾšît* as first in time or in rank) are standard lexical
descriptions, not readings of a particular edition.

**1:7's second line inverts the usual order.** It puts its object first: *wisdom and instruction —
fools despise*, so the line ends on the verb. Read with verse 2's *"to know wisdom and
instruction"*, the pair frames the whole preface.

**"Beginning" in 1:7 and 9:10 is two different words.** 1:7 has *rēʾšît*; 9:10 has *təḥillâ*, the
ordinary word for a start. The motto is repeated in sense, not verbatim.

**Who is speaking?** The preface has no *I* and no *you*. From 1:8 the voice is a parent's — *"My
son"* — and the father's and mother's instruction (1:8) is the first concrete form the received
wisdom of 1:3 and 1:5 takes.
