# The Worthy Woman

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 31:10–31

## Summary

The closing poem of [[Proverbs]] asks who can find a worthy woman, then praises a life whose
value surpasses precious goods. Her husband trusts her. She works wool and flax, provides food,
considers and buys a field, plants a vineyard, produces goods for sale, and strengthens herself
for the work. Her provision includes servant girls; the portrait assumes a household with
resources and help. Its hands extend outward to poor and needy people as well as inward to
those at home.

Clothing and preparedness lead to the image of strength and dignity as her clothing. She
speaks wisdom and faithful instruction, watches the household, and refuses idleness. The
ending shifts attention to those who owe her recognition: children call her blessed, her
husband praises her, and the hearers are commanded to give her the fruit of her hands and let
her works praise her at the gates. Fear of Yahweh, rather than charm or beauty, supplies the
final criterion.

This is a sustained praise portrait, not a named woman’s biography. The poem does not identify
her with Lemuel’s mother or wife, or assign every action to a single day. It closes the book
with wisdom expressed in work, relationships, giving, and speech.

## Key verses

> **31:10** Who can find a worthy woman? For her price is far above rubies.

> **31:11** The heart of her husband trusts in her. He shall have no lack of gain.

> **31:12** She does him good, and not harm, all the days of her life.

> **31:13** She seeks wool and flax, and works eagerly with her hands.

> **31:14** She is like the merchant ships. She brings her bread from afar.

> **31:15** She rises also while it is yet night, gives food to her household, and portions for her servant girls.

> **31:16** She considers a field, and buys it. With the fruit of her hands, she plants a vineyard.

> **31:17** She arms her waist with strength, and makes her arms strong.

> **31:18** She perceives that her merchandise is profitable. Her lamp doesn’t go out by night.

> **31:19** She lays her hands to the distaff, and her hands hold the spindle.

> **31:20** She opens her arms to the poor; yes, she extends her hands to the needy.

> **31:21** She is not afraid of the snow for her household; for all her household are clothed with scarlet.

> **31:22** She makes for herself carpets of tapestry. Her clothing is fine linen and purple.

> **31:23** Her husband is respected in the gates, when he sits among the elders of the land.

> **31:24** She makes linen garments and sells them, and delivers sashes to the merchant.

> **31:25** Strength and dignity are her clothing. She laughs at the time to come.

> **31:26** She opens her mouth with wisdom. Faithful instruction is on her tongue.

> **31:27** She looks well to the ways of her household, and doesn’t eat the bread of idleness.

> **31:28** Her children rise up and call her blessed. Her husband also praises her:

> **31:29** “Many women do noble things, but you excel them all.”

> **31:30** Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman who fears Yahweh, she shall be praised.

> **31:31** Give her of the fruit of her hands! Let her works praise her in the gates!

## Notes

### Form, boundary, and attribution

The planned Proverbs 31:10–31 boundary and title are retained. In the Masoretic Hebrew text
published by Mechon-Mamre, the initial letters of the twenty-two verses run from aleph through
tav: an alphabetic acrostic. This formal completeness supports reading a comprehensive praise
portrait; it does not establish a daily timetable. The Hebrew was checked on 2026-10-27 at
`https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt2831.htm`; the English quotations here use the repository’s WEB,
not that site’s English translation.

The question in verse 10 begins a different form from the direct royal instruction of 1–9.
The heading at 31:1 explicitly credits Lemuel’s mother with teaching him, but the poem supplies
no new personal identification of its subject or speaker. Its placement permits reading it
alongside that instruction; it does not prove that the described woman is his mother or wife.
No separate person page is created for an ideal type, and no historical identity is invented.

### Hands that produce, give, and receive their due

The poem repeatedly attends to hands: working fibers, planting from earnings, using spindle
and distaff, and reaching toward the needy. It grants the woman deliberation and initiative in
buying a field and trading; its household economy is not confined to an interior room. The
merchant ships are a comparison in verse 14, not a named voyage or foreign country.

Her household has resources: a field, a vineyard, merchandise, fine clothing, and servant girls.
Their presence matters when reading the night labor: this is neither a solitary worker’s
schedule nor a statement that rest is forbidden. Exodus 20:9–10 bounds labor for dependents
as well as household heads. The WEB’s servant girls (15) does not settle the precise terms or
legal status of their service.

The final imperative addresses other people: they must give her the fruit of her hands (31).
Praise is therefore accompanied by an obligation to recognize her work and its results. The
husband’s respected place among elders (23) is stated, but the poem neither names him as king
nor says that every aspect of his reputation was acquired through her alone.

### Wisdom lived rather than merely sought

The opening valuation above rubies recalls wisdom’s worth in Proverbs 3:15 and Job 28:18.
The house that Wisdom builds in Proverbs 9:1 and the wise woman’s building in 14:1 provide
literary context for this well-provided household. Verse 26 explicitly names wise speech;
verse 30 returns to the fear of Yahweh with which the book began (1:7). These are textual
parallels, not an assertion that the woman is literally the personification who speaks in
chapter 8. See
[[Wisdom Lived in the Household - Proverbs 1-9 and 31.10-31]].

The WEB’s worthy woman in Proverbs 12:4, 31:10, and Ruth 3:11 translates the shared phrase
*ʾēšet ḥayil*, verified in Mechon-Mamre’s Masoretic Hebrew text. Ruth 3:11 supplies a named
woman described with the phrase; it does not identify the subject of this poem. See
[[A Worthy Woman - Proverbs 12.4, 31.10, and Ruth 3.11]].

### What the praise does and does not promise

**Explicit tension with Job:** gain, household protection, and public esteem belong to this
portrait, while Job’s fear of God and integrity remain affirmed amid loss and humiliation
(Job 1:1, 13–22; 2:3, 7; 30:9–10). The poem cannot turn poverty, bereavement, illness, or lack
of public recognition into evidence that someone failed to live wisely.

**Explicit tension within Proverbs:** Agur asks for neither poverty nor riches (30:8–9), while
this household enjoys profitable trade and fine clothing. The poem values generosity and fear
of Yahweh within prosperity; Agur fears prosperity’s temptation to deny God. The anthology
retains both judgments. The woman’s confidence toward the future (31:25) also does not erase
the warning that tomorrow is unknown (27:1).

Verse 30 does not make beauty itself an offense; it rejects charm and beauty as sufficient
grounds for the praise that belongs to character and deeds. The children’s and husband’s
commendation fits Proverbs 27:2’s preference for another person’s praise over one’s own.
Her works praising her at the gates is a public image; it does not state that she holds an
elder’s office. The poem’s final command is directed to the community that should recognize her.
