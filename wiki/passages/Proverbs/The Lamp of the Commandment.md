# The Lamp of the Commandment

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 6:20–35

## Summary

The strange woman's second lecture, after the single sentence of 2:16–19 and the whole of
chapter 5. The Masoretic text divides it in two with a *petuḥah* after 6:26. **The teaching as
guardian** (6:20–26): keep the father's commandment and the mother's teaching, bind them on the
heart and around the neck, and they will lead the son when he walks, watch him when he sleeps, and
talk with him when he wakes — *"For the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light"* (6:23) — to
keep him from the married woman, whose beauty he must not covet and who *"hunts for your precious
life"* (6:26). **Fire in the lap** (6:27–35): no man carries fire against his chest without his
clothes burning, or walks on coals without scorching his feet, *"So is he who goes in to his
neighbor's wife"* (6:29). Then a comparison with the thief: a hungry thief is not despised, and
even he must repay sevenfold; the adulterer *"destroys his own soul"*, carries a reproach that will
not be wiped away, and faces a husband whose jealousy no ransom and no gift will buy off.

Chapter 5 argued from the end of the road — honey now, wormwood later. This lecture argues from
the nature of the act. It is fire, and touching it burns; it is theft that cannot be repaid.

**6:20–23 — The teaching that walks with you.** *"My son, keep your father's commandment, and don't
forsake your mother's teaching"* (6:20). The second line is 1:8's word for word (*wəʾal-tiṭṭōš
tôraṯ ʾimmeḵā*); the first changes 1:8's *"instruction"* (*mûsar ʾāḇîḵā*) to *"commandment"*
(*miṣwaṯ ʾāḇîḵā*), and the verb to *nəṣōr*, *guard*, the verb of 4:23's *"Keep your heart"*
(Hebrew read from Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*). The pair *miṣwâ* and *tôrâ* returns
in 6:23 without the possessives: *"the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light"*. Father and
mother still speak, but the words they use are the words for what Sinai gave. See
[[The Tablet of the Heart - Proverbs 3.3, Jeremiah 31.33, and 2 Corinthians 3.3]].

*"Bind them continually on your heart. Tie them around your neck"* (6:21). *Qošrēm*, *bind them*, is
the verb of 3:3 (*qošrēm ʿal-gargərôṯeḵā*, *"Bind them around your neck"*), and here it is the heart
that the teaching is bound on, with the neck in the second line. Deuteronomy 6:8 used the same verb
for the words Moses commanded: *"You shall bind them for a sign on your hand"* (*ûqšartām*). The
next verse runs closer to Deuteronomy still. *"When you walk, it will lead you. When you sleep, it
will watch over you. When you awake, it will talk with you"* (6:22). Deuteronomy 6:7 told Israel to
talk of the commandments *"when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise up"*;
Proverbs takes the same three times of day, and *bəšoḵbəḵā*, *when you lie down*, is the identical
word in both. **What changes is who does the talking.** In Deuteronomy, Israel speaks the words
(*wəḏibbartā bām*); in Proverbs the teaching speaks to the son (*hîʾ ṯəśîḥeḵā*, *it will talk with
you*). It guides like a shepherd (*tanḥeh*, the verb of Exodus 15:13 and Psalm 23:3) and keeps guard
(*tišmōr*) over him in his sleep — the promise of 3:23–24 (*"When you lie down, you will not be
afraid"*) given a cause. See
[[When You Walk and When You Lie Down - Deuteronomy 6.6-9 and Proverbs 6.20-23]].

*"For the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light. Reproofs of instruction are the way of life"*
(6:23, *kî nēr miṣwâ wəṯôrâ ʾôr*). Psalm 119:105 says the same thing in the first person: *"Your
word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path"* — *nēr* and *ʾôr* in the same order. In
[[The Two Paths]], the righteous walked in *"dawning light"* and the wicked did not know what they
stumbled over (4:18–19); here the light is the teaching itself, carried. Its purpose is not general
illumination but one danger in the dark. See
[[A Lamp and a Light - Proverbs 6.23 and Psalm 119.105]] and [[Light and Darkness]].

**6:24–26 — The woman who hunts.** The lamp is *"to keep you from the immoral woman, from the
flattery of the wayward wife's tongue"* (6:24). The WEB's *"immoral woman"* is *ʾēšeṯ rāʿ*,
literally *a woman of evil*. The same consonants can be vocalized *ʾēšeṯ rēaʿ*, *a neighbour's
wife* — the phrase of 6:29 — and some commentators read it so; the Masoretic vowels give *rāʿ*, and
the WEB follows them. The second line names her again as *nokriyyâ*, the *"another"* of 2:16 and
5:20, and her weapon is again her speech: *ḥelqaṯ lāšôn*, *smoothness of tongue*, from the root of
2:16's *heḥĕlîqâ* and 5:3's *"smoother than oil"*. The wiki keeps the reading of
[[If You Seek Her as Silver]]: she is another man's wife, and in this lecture the text says so
outright, twice (6:26, 29).

*"Don't lust after her beauty in your heart, neither let her captivate you with her eyelids"*
(6:25). The verb is *ʾal-taḥmōḏ*, the verb of the tenth commandment, *lōʾ-ṯaḥmōḏ ʾēšeṯ rēʿeḵā*,
*"You shall not covet your neighbor's wife"* (Exodus 20:17; MT 20:14). **Proverbs gives the
commandment against coveting its sexual case and puts it in the heart**, *bilḇāḇeḵā*. Her beauty is
*yopyāh*; Genesis 39:6 called Joseph *yəp̄ēh-ṯōʾar*, *well-built and handsome*, in the verse before
his master's wife *"set her eyes on Joseph"* (39:7). Here the eyes are hers, and the instrument is
*ʿap̄ʿappêhā*, *her eyelids*. *Let her captivate you* is *tiqqāḥăḵā*, *let her take you*. The
look is where Job 31:1 made a covenant and where Matthew 5:28 places adultery already committed
*"in his heart"*. See [[A Covenant with My Eyes - Job 31.1 and Matthew 5.28]] and
[[Murder, Adultery, and the Heart - Exodus 20 and Matthew 5]].

*"For a prostitute reduces you to a piece of bread. The adulteress hunts for your precious life"*
(6:26). The Hebrew is compressed and its first line is read more than one way. Literally: *for on
account of a woman, a prostitute, down to a loaf of bread; and a man's wife hunts a precious
soul*. The WEB takes it as a comparison of costs: the prostitute takes a man's money down to his
last loaf, while the married woman takes his life. An alternative reading has the prostitute's
price as only a loaf of bread, against the married woman's much higher price; either way **the
second line is the point, and it escalates**. *"The adulteress"* is *ʾēšeṯ ʾîš*, *a man's wife*,
and she *hunts* (*tāṣûḏ*), the verb of the hunter. The life she takes is *nep̄eš yəqārâ*,
*precious*, the adjective of the gang's *"valuable wealth"* (1:13, *hôn yāqār*); they hoped to
seize precious things, and she hunts a precious life. The wiki does not take this verse as approving prostitution by comparison:
it ranks two ruins, and the lesser still costs the man his bread.

**6:27–29 — Fire.** *"Can a man scoop fire into his lap, and his clothes not be burned? Or can one
walk on hot coals, and his feet not be scorched?"* (6:27–28). The Hebrew plays on sound: *ʾîš*,
*a man*, carries *ʾēš*, *fire*, and the woman is *ʾēšeṯ*, *the wife of*. *His lap* is *ḥêqô*, his
bosom — the word of 5:20, *"Why embrace the bosom (ḥēq) of another?"* — so the fire is held exactly
where the woman would be. The feet that walk on coals are the feet the father has been guarding
since 1:15 and 4:26–27. Job had called adultery *"a fire that consumes to destruction"* (Job 31:12,
*ʾēš hîʾ*); Proverbs makes the same fire a question nobody needs answered.

*"So is he who goes in to his neighbor's wife. Whoever touches her will not be unpunished"* (6:29).
*Haḇāʾ ʾel-ʾēšeṯ rēʿēhû* — *goes in to* is the ordinary Hebrew idiom for intercourse, and *ʾēšeṯ
rēʿēhû*, *his neighbour's wife*, is the phrase of Leviticus 20:10's death penalty for the
adulterer. *Touches* (*hannōḡēaʿ*) is the verb Genesis uses for the same danger: God told Abimelech
*"I didn't allow you to touch her"* (Genesis 20:6, *lingōaʿ*), and the next Abimelech decreed
death for *"He who touches this man or his wife"* (Genesis 26:11, *hannōḡēaʿ* again). *"Will not be
unpunished"* is *lōʾ yinnāqeh*, *will not be held innocent*. Genesis's clearest case of the man who
did not touch is [[Joseph in Potiphar's House]]: his master's wife seized him *"by his garment"*,
and *"He left his garment in her hand, and ran outside"* (39:12). The man in 6:27 keeps hold of the
fire and loses his clothes to it; Joseph let go of his clothes and was not burned, though he went
to prison for it. See
[[Fire in the Lap and the Garment Left Behind - Proverbs 6.27-29 and Genesis 39]].

**6:30–31 — The thief.** *"Men don't despise a thief, if he steals to satisfy himself when he is
hungry: but if he is found, he shall restore seven times. He shall give all the wealth of his
house"* (6:30–31). The argument is from the lesser to the greater. The thief has the best excuse a
thief can have — he was hungry, *ləmallēʾ nap̄šô*, *to fill his soul* — and people do not despise
him. Even so the law reaches him: *yəšallēm*, *he shall repay*, the verb of the restitution laws of
Exodus 22. **Sevenfold is not the Exodus rate**, which is four or five for an animal slaughtered or
sold and double for one found alive (Exodus 22:1, 4); and Exodus 22:3 says a thief who cannot pay
is sold. Proverbs' *šiḇʿāṯāyim* is the number of Cain's protection, *"vengeance will be taken on him
sevenfold"* (Genesis 4:15, *šiḇʿāṯayim yuqqām*), and is best read as the proverbial figure for full
and more than full repayment, explained by the second line: *"all the wealth of his house"* (*kol-hôn bêṯô*). The gang of 1:13 meant to *"fill our houses"*
(*nəmallēʾ bāttênû*) with plundered *hôn*; the hungry thief, who stole *to fill* (*ləmallēʾ*) his
stomach, pays out the *hôn* of his own house. The wiki records the difference and does not convert Proverbs into a legal tariff; see
[[Fourfold Restitution - Exodus 22, Nathan's Parable, and Zacchaeus]] and
[[Theft and Restitution]].

The comparison then turns. A thief, however heavy the cost, can pay and be done; he takes a
thing and gives back things. **The adulterer has taken what cannot be returned**, from a man who will
take nothing in exchange. The lecture never calls adultery theft; it lets the comparison do it.

**6:32–35 — No ransom.** *"He who commits adultery with a woman is void of understanding. He who
does it destroys his own soul"* (6:32). *Nōʾēp̄*, *the adulterer*, is the participle of the seventh
commandment's verb (*lōʾ ṯinʾāp̄*, Exodus 20:14; MT 20:13) and the word Leviticus 20:10 uses for him.
*"Void of understanding"* is *ḥăsar-lēḇ*, *lacking heart* — the description that 7:7 will give the
young man who walks toward the strange woman's house. The thief filled his soul (6:30); the
adulterer ruins it (*mašḥîṯ nap̄šô*). Verse 33: *"He will get wounds and dishonor. His reproach will
not be wiped away."* *Wounds* is *negaʿ*, a stroke or plague, from the root of *touches* in 6:29:
whoever touches her is touched back. *"Will not be wiped away"*, *lōʾ timmāḥeh*, uses the verb of
the flood, which *"destroyed"* (*wayyimaḥ*) every living thing (Genesis 7:23) — the verb of erasing
or blotting out. The thief's debt can be paid; this reproach stays.

*"For jealousy arouses the fury of the husband. He won't spare in the day of vengeance. He won't
regard any ransom, neither will he rest content, though you give many gifts"* (6:34–35).
*Jealousy* is *qinʾâ*, the word for envy and jealousy at once (see [[Envy]]); Song of Solomon 8:6
sets it beside love, *"Jealousy is as cruel as Sheol. Its flashes are flashes of fire"*, and the
fire of 6:27 comes back as the husband's fury, *ḥămaṯ-gāḇer*. *"The day of vengeance"*, *yôm
nāqām*, makes the injured husband an avenger, and the ransom he will not regard is *kōp̄er*, the
payment Exodus 21:30 allowed for a life when a goring ox killed (*"If a ransom is laid on him"*). The
gifts are *šōḥaḏ*, the word Exodus 23:8 forbids judges to take: *"You shall take no bribe"*. He
will not *lift the face* of any ransom (*lōʾ-yiśśāʾ pənê kol-kōp̄er*) — the idiom for showing
favour.

**Proverbs and the law of adultery.** The law prescribes death: *"the adulterer and the adulteress
shall surely be put to death"* (Leviticus 20:10), *"they shall both die"* (Deuteronomy 22:22). Numbers
35:31 forbids taking *kōp̄er* for a murderer's life. Proverbs 6 names no court, no witness, and no
sentence; what the son faces is a husband. **The two are not contradictory, but they are different
pictures, and the wiki records both rather than merging them.** Proverbs may assume the law and
describe the husband who prosecutes it, refusing any settlement that would let the case drop; or it
may describe what happened when the law was not enforced, and the husband's vengeance was all there
was. The text does not say which. What it does say agrees with the law at its sharpest point: there
is no price for this. See
[[No Ransom for the Adulterer - Proverbs 6.29-35, Leviticus 20.10, and Deuteronomy 22.22]],
[[Vengeance]], and [[Redemption]].

**Reading it after Job.** Job's oath (Job 31:9–12) swore he had never lain in wait at his
neighbour's door, called the act *"a fire that consumes to destruction"*, and named the judges who
would punish it. Proverbs 6 confirms every term of his oath — the neighbour's wife, the fire, the
punishment — and the wiki keeps the same reserve it kept on chapter 5: Proverbs describes what
adultery does, and does not license the inference that a man whose household was destroyed must
have committed it. See [[The Neighbor's Door - Proverbs 5 and Job 31.9-12]].

**The second table, finished.** Read with the seven abominations just before it (6:16–19), the
chapter has now named murder (*"innocent blood"*), false witness, adultery, theft, and coveting —
the commandments of Exodus 20:13–17, in a different order, none quoted as law. The wiki records the
overlap without claiming the editors arranged it. See
[[Six Things Yahweh Hates - Proverbs 6.16-19 and the Ten Commandments]].

**God is not named.** Yahweh, named once in chapter 5 (5:21) and once in 6:1–19 (6:16), does not
appear in 6:20–35. Like [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]], this lecture argues from the shape of the act
and the people it wrongs. The divine law is present only in its vocabulary — *miṣwâ*, *tôrâ*,
*taḥmōḏ*, *nōʾēp̄*, *kōp̄er*.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 6:20–35 unit is kept. *"My son"* opens it at 6:20 and opens the next
lecture at 7:1. Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks a *petuḥah* after 6:19, after 6:26,
and after 6:35; the internal break at 6:26 is treated as a division within one lecture, since 6:27's
fire continues the subject of 6:24–26 and 6:29 names the same woman. The title is taken from 6:23.
