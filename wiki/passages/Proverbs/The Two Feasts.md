# The Two Feasts

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 9:1–18

## Summary

The last chapter of the book's prologue, and the scene that the whole of chapters 1–9 has been
building toward. Two women, two houses, two invitations, one guest. **Wisdom's feast** (9:1–6):
she *"has built her house"*, carved its *"seven pillars"*, slaughtered and mixed and set her
table, sent out her maidens, and calls *"from the highest places of the city"*: *"Whoever is
simple, let him turn in here!"* To the one *"void of understanding"* she says, *"Come, eat some of
my bread, drink some of the wine which I have mixed! Leave your simple ways, and live."* **A middle
block of sayings** (9:7–12): correcting a mocker earns insult, reproving a wise man earns his love;
*"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of wisdom"* (9:10); *"by me your days will be multiplied"*;
and *"If you are wise, you are wise for yourself. If you mock, you alone will bear it."* **The
foolish woman's feast** (9:13–18): *"loud, undisciplined, and knows nothing"*, she sits at her
door *"in the high places of the city"* and calls the same words to the same passers-by —
*"Whoever is simple, let him turn in here"* — but her menu is *"Stolen water is sweet. Food eaten
in secret is pleasant."* The chapter, and the prologue, end on what the guest does not know: *"that
the departed spirits are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol"* (9:18).

Everything the son has been taught in eight chapters comes down to this choice. The father has
warned against the gang (1:10–19), the strange woman (2:16–19; 5; 6:20–35; 7), and the evil way
(4:14–19); Wisdom has spoken three times (1:20–33; 8:1–36). Now the father steps back and the two
women speak for themselves, with the same words, from the same height. **The son is the simple
man passing by, and both of them are calling him.**

**9:1–2 — The house and the table.** *Ḥoḵmôṯ bānəṯâ ḇêṯāh*, *"Wisdom has built her house"*. The
name is the plural form *ḥoḵmôṯ* — the form of her first speech, *ḥoḵmôṯ baḥûṣ tārōnnâ* (1:20) — with
a singular verb, as there. The same consonants open 14:1, *"Every wise woman builds her house"*
(*ḥaḵmôṯ nāšîm bānəṯâ ḇêṯāh*, vowelled differently in Sefaria's edition, *wise ones of women*), so
the image of a woman building her house is used twice in the book, of Wisdom and of the wise wife.
She *"has carved out her seven pillars"* (*ḥāṣəḇâ ʿammûḏeyhā šiḇʿâ*). What the seven pillars stand
for has been read many ways — a large house with a colonnaded court, the seven days of creation
looking back to 8:22–31, a temple, seven heavens or seven ages or seven sections of a book — and
the text gives no key. **The wiki takes them as the mark of a great and finished house**, built to
hold many guests, and records the other readings as readings.

*Ṭāḇəḥâ ṭiḇḥāh*, *"She has prepared her meat"*, is literally *she has slaughtered her slaughter*.
The word is the one the father used of the young man in chapter 7, who went after the strange woman
*"as an ox goes to the slaughter"* (7:22, *kəšôr ʾel-ṭeḇaḥ*). In one house the guest is fed a
slaughtered animal; in the other he is the one slaughtered. *Māsəḵâ yênāh*, *"She has mixed her
wine"* — wine mixed with water or spices for serving. *ʿĀrəḵâ šulḥānāh*, *"set her table"*. The
four perfect verbs say the feast is already ready; nothing is left but for the guest to come.

**9:3–6 — The invitation.** *"She has sent out her maidens. She cries from the highest places of the
city"* (9:3). *Naʿărōṯeyhā*, her *maidens*, go out with the message, and she herself calls *ʿal-gappê
mərōmê qāreṯ*, *on the heights of the high places of the city* — where she stood in 8:2 (*bərōʾš-
mərōmîm*, *"on the top of high places"*). *Qāreṯ*, *city*, is a poetic word, used again at 8:3 and
9:14.

*"Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!"* (9:4, *mî-p̄eṯî yāsur hēnnâ*). The *pəṯî*, the *simple*
or *open* one, is the reader the book named in its first lines (1:4) and the ones Wisdom first rebuked
(*"How long, you simple ones, will you love simplicity?"*, 1:22). *Ḥăsar-lēḇ*, *"him who is void of
understanding"* — literally *lacking heart* — is the young man of 7:7 (*naʿar ḥăsar-lēḇ*). Wisdom
invites exactly the one the strange woman caught.

*"Come, eat some of my bread, drink some of the wine which I have mixed!"* (9:5, *ləḵû laḥămû
ḇəlaḥmî*). Verb and noun are from the same root, *lāḥam* and *leḥem*: *eat my bread*. The father used
the same pairing for the wicked, who *"eat the bread of wickedness, and drink the wine of violence"*
(4:17, *lāḥămû leḥem rešaʿ wəyên ḥămāsîm*). Two tables, two breads, two wines.

*"Leave your simple ways, and live. Walk in the way of understanding"* (9:6). *ʿIzḇû pəṯāʾyim*: the
WEB's *simple ways* renders a word that elsewhere means *simple ones*, people — the plural of 7:7,
*bappəṯāʾyim*, *"among the simple ones"*. Read that way, Wisdom tells the guest to leave the company
of the simple, as the father told the son not to go with the gang (1:10–15). Either way the choice is
stated with a single verb: *wiḥyû*, *and live*. *Wəʾišrû*, *walk* or *go straight*, is from the verb
usually connected with *ʾašrê*, *blessed* (8:32, 34) — a lexical association the wiki has not checked
against a lexicon.

**9:7–12 — The sayings between the feasts.** Six verses interrupt the two scenes and change the form
to short sayings. They are not out of place, though, because they say who will accept the invitation.
*"He who corrects a mocker invites insult"* (9:7); *"Don't reprove a scoffer, lest he hate you.
Reprove a wise man, and he will love you"* (9:8). The *lēṣ*, *mocker* or *scoffer*, is the third
figure of 1:22 — *"How long will mockers delight themselves in mockery?"* — and the book will define
him later: *"A scoffer doesn't love to be reproved"* (15:12). *Hôḵaḥ*, *reprove*, is the verb of
3:12: *"whom Yahweh loves, he reproves"* (*yôḵîaḥ*). Reproof is a form of love, and the wise man
returns it with love (*wəyeʾĕhāḇekkā*); the scoffer returns it with hatred.

*"Instruct a wise man, and he will be still wiser. Teach a righteous man, and he will increase in
learning"* (9:9). *Wəyôsep̄ leqaḥ*, *increase in learning*, is the phrase of the book's opening
purpose: *"that the wise man may hear, and increase in learning"* (1:5, *wəyôsep̄ leqaḥ*). The
prologue ends, as it began, on the wise man who keeps learning.

*"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of wisdom. The knowledge of the Holy One is understanding"*
(9:10). This is the motto of 1:7 — *"The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge"* — repeated at
the end of the prologue, so that chapters 1–9 open and close on it. The Hebrew changes two words.
1:7 has *rēʾšîṯ dāʿaṯ*, *the beginning of knowledge*; 9:10 has *təḥillaṯ ḥoḵmâ*, *the beginning of
wisdom*, with *təḥillâ*, a word for a beginning in time or a first step. And *daʿaṯ qədōšîm*, *"the
knowledge of the Holy One"*, has a plural noun — *holy ones* — which the WEB, like most translations,
reads as a plural of majesty for God; the same phrase recurs in Agur's confession, *"neither do I
have the knowledge of the Holy One"* (30:3, *wədaʿaṯ qədōšîm*). See
[[The Fear of the Lord Is Wisdom - Job 28.28 and the Wisdom Motto]] and [[The Fear of God]].

*"For by me your days will be multiplied. The years of your life will be increased"* (9:11). *By me*
puts Wisdom's voice back into the sayings: she is speaking. *Wəyôsîp̄û ləḵā šənôṯ ḥayyîm* repeats the
words of the father's promise in 3:2, *ûšənôṯ ḥayyîm … yôsîp̄û lāḵ*, *"years of life … will they add
to you"*. What the father's commandments promised, Wisdom now promises in her own name.

*"If you are wise, you are wise for yourself. If you mock, you alone will bear it"* (9:12).
*Ləḇaddəḵā tiśśāʾ*, *you alone will carry it*. The verse is the book's plainest statement that the
choice belongs to the one who makes it: the wise man's wisdom profits himself, and the mocker's
mockery falls on himself. It is the same lesson as 1:18–19 and 8:36 — the sinner *"wrongs his own
soul"* — said in the second person. The Greek translation is commonly reported to add lines after
9:12 and again after 9:18; the wiki has not checked a Septuagint edition and does not quote them.

**9:13–15 — The foolish woman.** *"The foolish woman is loud, undisciplined, and knows nothing"*
(9:13). *ʾĒšeṯ kəsîlûṯ* is literally *a woman of folly*; *kəsîlûṯ* is formed from *kəsîl*, *fool*,
the word of 1:22, *"fools hate knowledge"* (*ûḵəsîlîm*). *Hōmiyyâ*, *loud*, is the word used of the
strange woman at 7:11 — *"She is loud and defiant"* (*hōmiyyâ hîʾ*). The WEB's *undisciplined*
renders *pəṯayyûṯ*, a noun from the same root as *pəṯî*: **she is simpleness itself**, and she calls
to the simple. *Ûḇal-yāḏəʿâ mâ*, *and does not know anything* — against Wisdom, whose subject is
knowledge.

*"She sits at the door of her house, on a seat in the high places of the city"* (9:14). Wisdom built
a house and went out with her maidens; the foolish woman sits. *Ləp̄eṯaḥ bêṯāh*, *at the door of her
house*, is the father's word for the strange woman's door — *"Don't come near the door of her
house"* (5:8, *petaḥ bêṯāh*) — and against Wisdom's blessed man *"waiting at my door posts"* (8:34).
*ʿAl-kissēʾ mərōmê qāreṯ*: the same *high places of the city* as Wisdom's (9:3), but on a *kissēʾ*,
a *seat* — the word also used for a throne. She has set herself up at Wisdom's height.

*"To call to those who pass by, who go straight on their ways"* (9:15). *Hamyaššərîm ʾōrḥôṯām*, *who
make their paths straight*, uses the phrase of 3:6: *"he will make your paths straight"* (*yəyaššēr
ʾōrḥōṯeḵā*). Her target is not the man already on the wrong road but the one going straight, and the
call is to *turn in* (*yāsur*, *turn aside*) — off the path. The young man of chapter 7 was
*"passing through the street near her corner"* (7:8) when she met him.

**9:16–17 — The same words, a different menu.** *"Whoever is simple, let him turn in here."* (9:16)
The Hebrew of 9:16 repeats 9:4 word for word, adding only two conjunctions (*wa-ḥăsar-lēḇ wə-ʾāmərâ
lô*). **Folly's invitation is Wisdom's, copied exactly.** The difference is in what follows.
*"Stolen water is sweet. Food eaten in secret is pleasant"* (9:17, *mayim-gənûḇîm yimtāqû wəleḥem
sətārîm yinʿām*). Wisdom offered her own bread and her own mixed wine; Folly offers water that
belongs to someone else and bread eaten where no one sees. The water is the father's image for a man's
wife: *"Drink water out of your own cistern, running water out of your own well"* (5:15). Stolen
water is another man's well — the adultery of chapters 5–7 — and the *secret* bread is the strange
woman's hour, *"in the twilight, in the evening of the day, in the middle of the night"* (7:9). The book says
the same of all gain taken by fraud: *"Fraudulent food is sweet to a man, but afterwards his mouth is
filled with gravel"* (20:17). The sweetness is real; that is the point of the warning. The father
said as much at 5:3–4: her lips *"drip honey"*, but her end is *"as bitter as wormwood"*.

**9:18 — What the guest does not know.** *"But he doesn't know that the departed spirits are there,
that her guests are in the depths of Sheol."* *Wəlōʾ-yāḏaʿ*, *and he does not know* — she *"knows
nothing"* (9:13), and her guest does not know either. *Rəp̄āʾîm*, *the departed spirits* or *shades*,
are the dead in Sheol: the strange woman's paths lead *ʾel-rəp̄āʾîm*, *"to the departed spirits"*
(2:18), and in Job *"The departed spirits tremble, those beneath the waters"* (Job 26:5,
*hārəp̄āʾîm*). *Bəʿimqê šəʾôl*, *in the depths of Sheol*. *Qəruʾeyhā*, *her guests*, is literally
*her called ones*, from *qārāʾ*, *to call*, the verb of her calling in 9:15 (*liqrōʾ*) and of
Wisdom's in 9:3 (*tiqrāʾ*). **Both women call; whoever answers becomes one of the called of the house
he enters.** Wisdom's guests are told *"live"* (9:6); Folly's are already among the dead.

The prologue's three endings now line up. *"Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the rooms of
death"* (7:27). *"All those who hate me love death"* (8:36). *"Her guests are in the depths of
Sheol"* (9:18). Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks a *petuḥah* after 9:18, and 10:1
opens the next collection with a new heading. See [[Sheol]] and [[Life and Death]].

**The shape of the chapter.** The two scenes mirror each other closely:

| | Wisdom (9:1–6) | the foolish woman (9:13–18) |
|---|---|---|
| **herself** | builds, carves, slaughters, mixes, sets, sends (9:1–3) | loud, simpleness, knows nothing; sits (9:13–14) |
| **where** | *"the highest places of the city"* (9:3) | *"a seat in the high places of the city"* (9:14) |
| **the call** | *"Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!"* (9:4) | the same words (9:16) |
| **the meal** | *"my bread … the wine which I have mixed"* (9:5) | *"Stolen water … food eaten in secret"* (9:17) |
| **the end** | *"Leave your simple ways, and live"* (9:6) | *"her guests are in the depths of Sheol"* (9:18) |

The middle sayings (9:7–12) sit between the two calls and divide the hearers before either woman has
been answered: the scoffer will not take correction, the wise man will. **Which feast a man goes to
depends on what he already is** — the same point the father made in insisting the son keep his words
in the heart (4:23; 7:1–3), because the two invitations themselves cannot be told apart by their
words.

**Who are the two women?** Wisdom is read here as on the earlier pages: God's own wisdom personified,
now the hostess of a house. The foolish woman is read as Folly personified, the counterpart Wisdom has
lacked until now. She takes on the strange woman's features — *loud* (7:11), at her door (5:8),
offering secret pleasure, leading to Sheol (2:18; 5:5; 7:27) — so the adulteress of chapters 2–7
becomes, at the end, the figure of all folly. **The wiki does not decide whether the foolish woman is
the strange woman or a wider figure that includes her**: the text gives her the strange woman's words
and destination but calls her only *a woman of folly*. See [[Folly]] and
[[Two Voices in the Street - The Strange Woman and Wisdom in Proverbs 7-9]].

**The feast in later Scripture.** Wisdom's invitation — come, eat my bread, drink my wine, and live —
has close parallels in later texts, none of which quotes Proverbs 9. Isaiah 55:1–2: *"Come, everyone
who thirsts, to the waters! … buy wine and milk without money and without price … Listen diligently
to me, and eat that which is good"*, followed in 55:3 by *"Hear, and your soul will live"*. Isaiah 25:6
promises *"a feast of choice meat, a feast of choice wines"* for all peoples. In the Gospels, the
parables of the great supper and the wedding feast have a host who prepares a meal and *sends out*
servants with an invitation (Luke 14:16–17, 21; Matthew 22:2–4) — in Luke, into *"the streets and
lanes of the city"*. Jesus says *"I am the bread of life"* (John 6:35), and Matthew 11:19 ends a
saying about his eating and drinking with *"wisdom is justified by her children"*. Whether any of
these writers had Proverbs 9 in mind is a question of interpretation; the wiki records the pattern and
not a claim of dependence. See
[[Come, Eat of My Bread - Proverbs 9, Isaiah 55, and the Gospel Banquets]].

**Reading it after Job.** 9:11 promises multiplied days, and 9:12 says each person carries the result
of his own choice. Job was wise and righteous by the narrator's own verdict (Job 1:1), and his days
were not multiplied until after the loss of his children (Job 42:16). The promise stands as the book's
general rule; the wiki keeps Job's case beside it. See [[Retribution]].

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 9:1–18 unit is kept: the chapter is one composition, the two feasts framing
the sayings of 9:7–12, and it ends the prologue before the heading at 10:1. Sefaria's *Miqra according
to the Masorah* marks a *petuḥah* only after 9:18. The middle sayings were not split off: 9:11's *"by
me"* keeps them in Wisdom's voice, and they divide the hearers of the two invitations.
