# A False Balance

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 11:1–31

## Summary

A collection of short sayings brings righteousness into the marketplace, the neighborhood, the city,
and the household. Accurate weights delight Yahweh; pride gives way to shame; integrity guides;
speech can protect a confidence or destroy a community. Generosity and the release of grain benefit
others, while wealth proves unable to secure life against judgment. These are related observations,
not a single narrated event.

**11:1–8 — The measure and the person.** The false balance of verse 1 is a concrete commercial
wrong, not merely a metaphor for being unfair. Yahweh’s delight in accurate weights gives ordinary
exchange religious significance. Humility accompanies wisdom (2); integrity and righteousness guide
and deliver (3–6), whereas treachery, wickedness, and evil desires bring ruin. Riches cannot profit
in the day of wrath (4), and death ends the wicked person’s expectation of power (7). Verse 8
pictures a reversal: the righteous person escapes trouble and the wicked takes that person’s place.
It supplies no mechanism and should not be converted into a doctrine that every rescue requires a
substitute victim.

**11:9–14 — Neighbors, cities, and counsel.** Destructive speech expands from the neighbor (9) to
the whole city (11). The city’s joy at the success of the righteous, and at the wicked person’s fall
(10), describes a community affected by its members’ conduct; it is not a narrated command to
celebrate a particular death. The wise person knows when to hold peace and protect a confidence
(12–13), but wisdom is not permanent silence: a nation needs guidance and a multitude of counselors
(14). No city or nation is named. Compare the public benefit of shared judgment in
[[Jethro's Counsel]].

**11:15–23 — Risk, honor, and moral consequence.** The warning against becoming collateral for a
stranger (15) compresses Proverbs 6:1–5. It stands beside praise for generosity later in the
chapter: caution about binding oneself to another’s obligation need not cancel giving from one’s own
resources. A gracious woman gains honor while violent men gain riches (16); the verse acknowledges
violent acquisition without making wealth the highest good. Mercy benefits its giver and cruelty
harms its practitioner (17). Sowing righteousness promises reward, life, and deliverance for one’s
offspring, while perverse hearts and evil face Yahweh’s rejection (18–21). Beauty without discretion
is as incongruous as a gold ring in a pig’s snout (22). The comparison criticizes the absence of
judgment, not beauty itself; it identifies no particular woman. Verse 23 returns to desire and
expectation.

**11:24–29 — Giving, grain, and the limits of possession.** Scattering can increase what one has;
withholding more than is appropriate can produce poverty (24). The generous person prospers and the
one who waters is watered (25). The WEB’s language of being made fat is an image of flourishing.
Verse 26 makes the communal stakes concrete: people curse withheld grain and bless the seller who
makes it available. Selling is praised here, so the lesson cannot be reduced to giving everything
away. Neither famine nor price manipulation is explicitly specified. Seeking good seeks favor,
whereas pursuing evil brings evil back (27). Trust in riches falls; righteousness flourishes as a
green leaf (28). Troubling one’s own house ends with an inheritance of wind, and folly with
subordination to the wise (29); the proverb does not explain every case of poverty or servitude.

**11:30–31 — Fruit that gives life, and repayment on earth.** The fruit of the righteous is a tree
of life (30), extending the image used for Wisdom in Proverbs 3:18. The WEB’s second line, “He who
is wise wins souls,” places beneficial influence beside that fruit. Reading it as a later Christian
evangelism slogan is an application, not a setting supplied by this proverb. This page follows the
WEB without claiming to settle the underlying Hebrew’s meaning or alternative renderings. Verse 31
reasons from the righteous being repaid in the earth to the greater consequence facing the wicked
and sinner. First Peter 4:17–19 has a related argument, with markedly different English wording and
an explicit setting of suffering while doing good. See
[[Repaid in the Earth - Proverbs 11.31 and 1 Peter 4.18]].

**The conflict with Job must stay visible.** Proverbs 11:8 describes righteous deliverance and 11:21
promises that righteous offspring will be delivered. Job 1:1, 18–22 nevertheless describes an
upright man whose children die, and Job 2:3 expressly denies that his ruin had a cause in his
misconduct. Job 21:7–13 further observes prosperous wicked people with secure offspring. Verse 16 of
Proverbs 11 itself grants that violent men obtain riches, while verse 28 denies that such riches are
dependable. Reading the promises as general moral instruction rather than a diagnostic rule is the
wiki’s interpretation; the verses do not state their own exception clauses. See
[[The Prosperity of the Wicked]], [[Retribution]], and [[Suffering]].

## Key verses

> **11:1** A false balance is an abomination to Yahweh, but accurate weights are his delight.

> **11:2** When pride comes, then comes shame, but with humility comes wisdom.

> **11:3** The integrity of the upright shall guide them, but the perverseness of the treacherous shall destroy them.

> **11:4** Riches don’t profit in the day of wrath, but righteousness delivers from death.

> **11:11** By the blessing of the upright, the city is exalted, but it is overthrown by the mouth of the wicked.

> **11:13** One who brings gossip betrays a confidence, but one who is of a trustworthy spirit is one who keeps a secret.

> **11:14** Where there is no wise guidance, the nation falls, but in the multitude of counselors there is victory.

> **11:15** He who is collateral for a stranger will suffer for it, but he who refuses pledges of collateral is secure.

> **11:16** A gracious woman obtains honor, but violent men obtain riches.

> **11:17** The merciful man does good to his own soul, but he who is cruel troubles his own flesh.

> **11:21** Most certainly, the evil man will not be unpunished, but the offspring of the righteous will be delivered.

> **11:24** There is one who scatters, and increases yet more. There is one who withholds more than is appropriate, but gains poverty.

> **11:25** The liberal soul shall be made fat. He who waters shall be watered also himself.

> **11:26** People curse someone who withholds grain, but blessing will be on the head of him who sells it.

> **11:28** He who trusts in his riches will fall, but the righteous shall flourish as the green leaf.

> **11:30** The fruit of the righteous is a tree of life. He who is wise wins souls.

> **11:31** Behold, the righteous shall be repaid in the earth; how much more the wicked and the sinner!

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh rejects false weights and perverse hearts, and delights in accurate weights and
  blameless ways (1, 20).
- [[Solomon]] — collection-level attribution from Proverbs 10:1; not named or narrated in chapter
  11.
- The gracious and beautiful women, neighbors, counselors, traders, strangers, and righteous and
  wicked people are unnamed types, not separately identifiable figures.

## Places

- No named place. City, nation, household, and earth describe the scope of these sayings without
  identifying a geographical location.

collection are the book page’s ingestion convention; the clusters above are reading aids rather than
newly asserted literary divisions. Only this pericope is advanced.

25:13–16, 2 Corinthians 9:6–11, and 1 Peter 4:17–19 were verified through bible-api.com with

to First Peter are stated where those claims are discussed.
