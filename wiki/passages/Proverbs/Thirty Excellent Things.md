# Thirty Excellent Things

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 22:17–23:11

## Summary

The sentence collection headed *"The proverbs of Solomon"* (10:1) ended at 22:16. Verse 17 changes
the voice and the form at once: *"Turn your ear, and listen to the words of the wise. Apply your
heart to my teaching."* The speaker is a teacher again, as in chapters 1–9, addressing *"you"*
directly; and what follows is no longer a string of two-line observations but a series of
commands, nearly every one of them a *don't* with a reason attached. This pericope takes the
collection's preface and its first block of commands, ending at 23:11. Verse 12 opens with a fresh
call to attention — *"Apply your heart to instruction, and your ears to the words of knowledge"* —
which echoes 22:17 and starts the next queue item.

**22:17–21 — The preface: why the words are written.** The teacher states four purposes in five
verses. The words are to be kept *"within you"* and *"ready on your lips"* (18), so that they can be
said as well as known. They are taught *"today, even you"*, *"So that your trust may be in Yahweh"*
(19): the stated end of this whole collection of practical counsel is not success but trust. They
are *"thirty excellent things of counsel and knowledge"*, written down (20). And they are meant
*"To teach you truth, reliable words, to give sound answers to the ones who sent you"* (21) — the
pupil pictured as an envoy or messenger who must report back accurately to those who sent him.
The book's motto had made the fear of Yahweh the beginning of knowledge (1:7); this preface makes
trust in Yahweh its goal (22:19).

**22:22–23 — The poor in court.** *"Don't exploit the poor, because he is poor; and don't crush the
needy in court; for Yahweh will plead their case, and plunder the life of those who plunder them."*
The first command names the motive it forbids: the poor man is exploited *because he is poor*,
because nobody will stop it. The answer is that somebody will. Yahweh takes the poor man's side as
his advocate, and the penalty matches the crime word for word: the plunderers are plundered. This is
the courtroom form of what 14:31 and 22:2 had said about the Maker of the poor (see
[[Contempt for His Maker - Proverbs 14.31 and Job 31.13-15]]).

**22:24–25 — Company.** *"Don't befriend a hot-tempered man, and don't associate with one who harbors
anger: lest you learn his ways, and ensnare your soul."* The reason is not that he will hurt you but
that you will become like him. It is the same lesson as the book's first lecture, where the father
warns the son off the gang's path (1:10, 15; see [[Do Not Consent to Sinners]]), now applied to a
temperament rather than a crime. The hot temper itself is one the collection had already judged:
*"A wrathful man stirs up contention"* (15:18; see [[Slow to Anger - Proverbs 14.29 and Exodus 34.6]]).

**22:26–27 — Surety and the bed.** *"Don't you be one of those who strike hands, of those who are
collateral for debts. If you don't have means to pay, why should he take away your bed from under
you?"* The fifth warning against standing surety in the book (6:1–5; 11:15; 17:18; 20:16), and the
first to say exactly what is lost: the bed. Exodus 22:26–27 had required a creditor to return a
poor man's pledged garment by sundown, because *"What would he sleep in?"*; this verse supposes a
creditor who takes the bed itself. The law limits the creditor; the proverb advises the debtor not
to put himself in that creditor's hands. See
[[I Will Be Surety for Him - Judah's Pledge and the Guarantor in Scripture]] and
[[Lending Without Interest - Exodus 22.25 and the Poor Brother]].

**22:28 — The ancient boundary stone.** *"Don't move the ancient boundary stone, which your fathers
have set up."* A one-verse command, given its reason in the second line: the stone is old and the
fathers set it. Moving it is a silent theft of land, done at night with a lever rather than in
daylight with a sword. The command returns at 23:10, there with a victim named.

**22:29 — The skilled worker.** *"Do you see a man skilled in his work? He will serve kings. He won't
serve obscure men."* The only verse in the block that is not a command: an observation, set like a
hinge between the boundary stone and the ruler's table. Competence brings a person into the
presence of power — which is exactly where the next three verses find him.

**23:1–3 — At the ruler's table.** *"When you sit to eat with a ruler, consider diligently what is
before you; put a knife to your throat, if you are a man given to appetite. Don't be desirous of his
dainties, since they are deceitful food."* The skilled man who serves kings is now eating with one,
and the danger is the meal. *"Put a knife to your throat"* is hyperbole for drastic self-restraint,
not an instruction. The reason the food is *"deceitful"* is not stated: it may be that the ruler's
hospitality is a means of obligation, or a test of the guest's discretion, or simply that rich food
dulls judgment where judgment is most needed. The saying leaves all three open. The noun for the
ruler's *dainties* is the word Genesis 27 uses six times for the *"savory food"* Isaac asked for and
Rebekah prepared to deceive him; see
[[Deceitful Food - The Savory Food of Genesis 27 and the Ruler's Delicacies]].

**23:4–5 — Riches with wings.** *"Don't weary yourself to be rich. In your wisdom, show restraint. Why
do you set your eyes on that which is not? For it certainly sprouts wings like an eagle and flies in
the sky."* Not a condemnation of wealth but of exhausting oneself for it. The reason is the image:
wealth *"is not"* — it has no permanence — and it leaves the way a bird leaves, without asking. See
[[Riches Sprout Wings - Proverbs 23.4-5 and the Uncertainty of Riches]].

**23:6–8 — The stingy host.** *"Don't eat the food of him who has a stingy eye, and don't crave his
delicacies: for as he thinks about the cost, so he is. 'Eat and drink!' he says to you, but his heart
is not with you. The morsel which you have eaten you shall vomit up, and lose your good words."* A
second table, and the reverse of 22:9's *"generous eye"* that shares food with the poor. The host's
words invite; his heart counts. When the guest realises it, the meal turns in his stomach and the
compliments he paid were wasted. The figure of vomiting up what was eaten is Zophar's at Job 20:15,
there of riches swallowed by the wicked; see
[[Sweet Food and Gravel - Proverbs 20.17 and Job 20]].

**23:9 — The fool's ears.** *"Don't speak in the ears of a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your
words."* After two warnings about wasted meals, one about wasted words. Verse 21 had sent the pupil
out to *"give sound answers"*; this verse names the one audience not worth answering.

**23:10–11 — The fatherless and their Defender.** *"Don't move the ancient boundary stone. Don't
encroach on the fields of the fatherless: for their Defender is strong. He will plead their case
against you."* The block ends where 22:28 began, with the boundary stone, and where 22:22–23 began,
with Yahweh pleading the case of the weak. The two commands are fused: moving the stone is now
explicitly a crime against the fatherless, whose field has no father to walk its edge. The
fatherless have a *Defender* all the same, and he is *strong*. The word behind the WEB's *Defender*
is the Hebrew *gōʾēl*, the kinsman-redeemer of Leviticus 25 and Ruth and the *"Redeemer"* of Job
19:25 (see Notes for the source). See
[[The Widow's Borders - Proverbs 15.25 and the Ancient Boundary Stone]] and
[[The Redeemer and the Avenger of Blood]].

**The shape of the block.** Read as a whole, 22:22–23:11 is framed by the weak and their advocate
(22:22–23; 23:10–11) and by the boundary stone (22:28; 23:10). Inside the frame stand three
warnings about entanglement — with an angry friend, with another man's debt, and with a powerful or
grudging host's table — and two about wasted effort, on riches and on fools. The preface's promise
that the pupil's *"trust may be in Yahweh"* (22:19) is answered at the edges: the poor and the
fatherless, who have nothing else to trust, are the ones Yahweh is said to defend.

**Tensions kept explicit.** Yahweh pleads the poor man's case (22:23) and the fatherless's
(23:11); Job, at 24:2–3, watched the landmarks moved and the fatherless's donkey driven off and
asked why God did not visibly act. The wiki keeps both, as it has throughout the sentence
collection: the proverb states God's character and his stated intent, Job testifies that the
intervention is not always visible in time (see [[Retribution]]). The warnings about the ruler's
table are cautions, not a rule against dining with the powerful: 22:29 expects the skilled to stand
before kings, and 23:1 assumes the pupil will sit at the ruler's table. The concern is appetite and
discretion there, not presence.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh, named twice (22:19, 23): the object of the pupil's trust, and the advocate who
  pleads the poor man's case. He is also the *Defender* of 23:11, unnamed there but doing the same
  thing in the same words.
- [[Solomon]] — not named, and not the attributed author. The collection headed at 10:1 under his
  name ended at 22:16; 22:17 attributes what follows to *"the wise"*.
- The poor, the needy, the hot-tempered man, the creditor, the fathers who set the stone, the
  skilled worker, the ruler, the stingy host, the fool, and the fatherless are generic figures;
  none receives an invented identity.

## Places

- None. The court (22:22) and the fields (23:10) are settings in general, not named locations.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 22:17–23:11 unit is retained. Verse 17 begins a new collection with a
new heading; 23:12 opens with a new call to attention that echoes 22:17, so the next queue item
(*Do Not Envy Sinners*, 23:12–24:2) begins there. Exactly one queue item is advanced.

**"Thirty."** The WEB's *"thirty excellent things"* (22:20) is a rendering choice over a disputed
word. In Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* (read 2026-10-26), the written consonantal text
(*ketiv*) is *šlšwm*, the consonants of a word usually meaning *formerly* (literally *the day
before yesterday*), and the reading tradition (*qere*) is *šālîšîm*, a word whose sense here is
disputed — it is commonly linked to a term for officers, and often rendered *excellent things*. These
lexical senses are from standard reference knowledge, not checked against a lexicon in this pass. Neither is the ordinary Hebrew for *thirty*, *šəlōšîm*. Translations that
print *thirty* revocalize the consonants to that number; translations that print *excellent things*
follow the *qere*. The WEB combines both. Where the count of thirty is accepted, commentators divide
22:17–24:22 into thirty units in different ways; the wiki's queue does not attempt the division.

**An Egyptian comparison, unverified here.** Modern scholarship widely compares 22:17–24:22 with the
Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope*, a teaching in thirty chapters, and reports close parallels with
this pericope in particular — the poor not to be robbed, the boundary stone not to be moved, wealth
flying away like a bird, care at a ruler's table, the skilled scribe before kings. This pass did not
consult an edition or translation of Amenemope, so no wording from it is given, and nothing is
claimed about the direction or nature of any dependence. If verified later, it belongs in a
connection page with its source named.

**Language.** Four further claims rest on the same Sefaria edition, read 2026-10-26: 23:11's
*Defender* is *gōʾălām*, *their gōʾēl*; 22:23 and 23:11 share the phrase *yārîb rîbām*, *he will plead
their case*; 22:26 uses both surety idioms of Proverbs 6:1, *tōqəʿê-kāp*, *strikers of the hand*, and
*ʿōrəbîm*, *those who stand surety*; and 23:3 and 23:6 use *maṭʿammōt*, *dainties*, the noun Genesis
27 uses six times (27:4, 7, 9, 14, 17, 31, checked in the same edition). 22:9's *generous eye* is
literally *good of eye* (*ṭôb-ʿayin*) and 23:6's *stingy eye* is *evil of eye* (*raʿ ʿāyin*). 22:22's
*in court* is literally *in the gate*, the place of a town's courts. 22:28 and 23:10 open with the
identical clause.
