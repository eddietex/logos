# The Words of King Lemuel

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 31:1–9

## Summary

A new heading introduces King [[Lemuel]] and the instruction taught him by
[[Lemuel's Mother]]. She addresses him as her son, the son of her womb and of her vows, before
warning him against spending his strength on women and ways that destroy kings. The next warning
is explicitly public: kings and princes must not drink and forget the law, because the justice
due to afflicted people would be perverted.

Verses 6–7 turn from the ruler to the person near death or bitter in soul. Strong drink and wine
are assigned to that sufferer so that poverty and misery can be forgotten. The passage ends by
returning to the ruler’s responsibility: twice the mother tells him to open his mouth, first for
the mute and desolate, then to judge righteously for the poor and needy. Royal self-restraint is
therefore part of an obligation to people whose cases depend on a competent hearing.

No response from Lemuel is narrated. This is instruction, not evidence that he was drunken,
sexually reckless, or a successful defender of the poor. His kingdom and date are not supplied.

## Key verses

> **31:1** The words of king Lemuel; the revelation which his mother taught him.

> **31:2** “Oh, my son! Oh, son of my womb! Oh, son of my vows!

> **31:3** Don’t give your strength to women, nor your ways to that which destroys kings.

> **31:4** It is not for kings, Lemuel; it is not for kings to drink wine; nor for princes to say, ‘Where is strong drink?’

> **31:5** lest they drink, and forget the law, and pervert the justice due to anyone who is afflicted.

> **31:6** Give strong drink to him who is ready to perish; and wine to the bitter in soul:

> **31:7** Let him drink, and forget his poverty, and remember his misery no more.

> **31:8** Open your mouth for the mute, in the cause of all who are left desolate.

> **31:9** Open your mouth, judge righteously, and serve justice to the poor and needy.”

## Notes

### A mother’s voice and a separate attribution

The heading calls these Lemuel’s words and immediately says his mother taught him. This is not
an inconsistency: the heading can associate the transmitted words with the recipient while
crediting their teacher. The direct address in verse 2 is emphatically maternal. Her vows are
mentioned, but their occasion and terms are absent. Neither her personal name nor a connection
to Solomon’s family is established by this passage. The wiki therefore creates two distinct
person pages without identifying Lemuel with Solomon or his mother with Bathsheba.

The WEB’s heading calls the teaching a revelation. No scene of revelation or prophetic
commission follows. This page makes no claim about the precise Hebrew term or an alternative
geographical reading of the heading.

### Women, strength, and the work of ruling

Verse 3 warns of the ruinous use of royal strength. It does not specify the number or status of
the women, the form of a relationship, or a historical incident. The comparison with
Deuteronomy 17:17 is thematic: both constrain a king’s dealings with women, but Deuteronomy
specifically prohibits multiplying wives, which this verse does not explicitly say. No direct
quotation of Deuteronomy is claimed. The woman who teaches these words has authority in the
text; treating verse 3 as a condemnation of women as such contradicts the role the passage
gives its own teacher.

### Two kinds of forgetting

The explanation in verse 5 makes the drinking warning more than a rule of private decorum.
Forgetting the law can deprive an afflicted person of justice. The king must retain awareness
because he holds responsibility for others. Verses 6–7 use forgetting differently: the sufferer
is allowed relief from the memory of poverty and misery. Neither poverty nor its causes are
said to disappear.

**Explicit tension within Proverbs:** 20:1 condemns being led astray by drink, and 23:29–35
portrays woe, confused perception, and craving; 31:6–7 directs drink toward a distressed person.
Different recipients and purposes explain the local contrast with the king, but do not erase
the wider tension about drink’s effects. The passage does not identify a customary treatment,
state a dosage, or explain whether the contrast is deliberately stark. Its wording can be
read as relief for the sufferer; it should not be turned into a general remedy for poverty.
The final demand to serve justice remains in force.

Leviticus 10:8–11 provides a related restriction: priests must abstain when entering the Tent
of Meeting, in a setting of distinguishing holy from common and teaching the statutes. The
parallel concerns fitness for responsibility; it does not make Lemuel a priest or claim that
the two rules have identical scope.

### Opening the mouth for another person

The repeated command in verses 8–9 moves from self-restraint to action. The mute person lacks
speech; the wider context of a cause, the desolate, and the poor makes effective representation
the concern. The passage does not name a particular litigant or authorize falsifying evidence.
Exodus 23:3, 6 forbids both favoritism for a poor person and denial of justice to the poor.
Advocacy and impartial judgment belong together.

Job’s remembered search into an unknown person’s cause (Job 29:16) shows what such attention can
look like. His later rejection (Job 30:9–10) also prevents turning the ethic into a guarantee
that an advocate will always retain power or receive the same help in return. Proverbs 31:8–9
commands the king’s conduct; it does not promise a particular outcome for every faithful ruler.
