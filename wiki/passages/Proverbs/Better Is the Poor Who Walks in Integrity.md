# Better Is the Poor Who Walks in Integrity

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 19:1–29

## Summary

The chapter opens by ranking integrity above dishonest speech and returns to that ranking when it
places the poor man above a liar (19:1, 22). Between those judgments it describes a world in which
money attracts friends and poverty drives even relatives away (4, 6–7). Verse 17 answers the
abandonment: pity for the poor is lending to Yahweh, who undertakes the reward. The chapter
therefore distinguishes how society distributes attention from what makes a person worth honoring.

As in the preceding sentence collections, this is a set of sayings rather than one continuous
speech. The groupings below help read their intersections; they do not claim an original discourse
structure.

**19:1–9 — Integrity, knowledge, and the friends money attracts.** The upright poor person is better
than a fool with perverse lips (1); the second figure is not explicitly called rich. Zeal without
knowledge and hasty steps are dangerous (2), and folly can wreck a person's course before he blames
Yahweh for it (3). That is a diagnosis of self-caused trouble, not permission to diagnose every
sufferer. Wealth gathers friends (4), gifts gather favor (6), and the poor man's relatives and
friends leave him pleading after them (7). These observations do not endorse abandonment: verses 1
and 17 give the poor moral dignity and a claim on compassion. Acquiring wisdom benefits one's own
soul (8). Verses 5 and 9 repeat the first sentence about the false witness; their second sentences
move from the liar not going free to his perishing. The repetition places accountability amid the
account of social advantage.

**19:10–14 — Rank, restraint, and the household.** Delicate living is unsuitable for a fool, and a
servant ruling princes is treated as still more inappropriate (10). This defends a hierarchy that
17:2 complicates by placing a wise servant above a shameful son. The settings differ, and 19:10 does
not identify the servant as wise or foolish; the tension between rank and competence should remain
visible. Discretion delays anger, and overlooking an offense is honorable (11). The king's wrath
roars like a lion while his favor refreshes like dew (12), a variation on the spring rain of 16:15.
A foolish son and a quarrelsome wife damage the household (13). Yet a prudent wife is from Yahweh,
distinguished from the house and riches that parents can leave as an inheritance (14). The two wife
sayings describe contrasting conduct; they do not identify particular women or make every wife
quarrelsome.

**19:15–19 — Hunger, preservation, generosity, and correction.** Idleness brings hunger (15);
keeping the commandment preserves one's soul (16). The chapter does not identify a particular
commandment here. Pity for the poor is pictured as a loan to Yahweh (17): aid goes to someone unable
to guarantee a return, while Yahweh promises the reward. No amount, payment date, or financial
return is specified. Correction of a son is motivated by hope and the refusal to be a willing party
to his death (18). The WEB does not mention a rod in this verse; its connection to 13:24 is
thematic. The hot-tempered man needs repeated rescue (19), a warning that intervention alone does
not change the disposition producing the trouble. The text does not specify the penalty or rescuer.

**19:20–24 — Counsel, kindness, and the promise of safety.** Listening and receiving instruction
prepare a person for wisdom in later life (20). Human plans are many, but Yahweh's counsel prevails
(21). Read together, these verses commend deliberation while denying that it controls the outcome.
Kindness makes a person desirable, and the poor man ranks above the liar (22, following the WEB).
Fear of Yahweh leads to life, contentment, and freedom from trouble (23), an especially strong
promise that must be read beside Job. The sluggard then caricatures failed action: his hand is in
the dish, but he will not bring it back to his mouth (24). Food is available in this picture, so it
cannot explain every case of hunger.

**19:25–29 — Who learns, and who mocks justice.** Flogging the scoffer teaches the simple observer
prudence; rebuking an understanding person teaches that person knowledge (25). The first clause does
not say the scoffer has become wise. A son who robs his father and drives away his mother brings
shame (26), the opposite of the parental honor commanded at Exodus 20:12. Instruction must keep
being heard or the son strays (27). The corrupt witness mocks justice and consumes iniquity (28);
penalties and beatings for scoffers and fools close the unit (29). These sayings assume physical
punishment but do not supply a legal procedure or a method of household discipline.

**Tensions kept explicit.** Proverbs 19:23's promise that the God-fearing person is untouched by
trouble cannot be an exceptionless guarantee alongside Job 1:1, 8; 1:13–19; 2:3, 7: Job fears God, loses
children and property, and suffers sores, while God describes his ruin as without cause. The proverb
is retained as a general wisdom assurance, not used to deny Job's testimony. Equally, 19:15's hunger
through idleness cannot be reversed into a diagnosis of all poverty: 19:1 honors the poor person's
integrity, and 13:23 names injustice destroying the poor person's food. The servant's elevation in
17:2 and resistance to his elevation in 19:10 remain distinct judgments, not silently harmonized.
None of these comparisons claims that one book directly quotes the other.

## Key verses

> **19:1** Better is the poor who walks in his integrity than he who is perverse in his lips and is a fool.

> **19:3** The foolishness of man subverts his way; his heart rages against Yahweh.

> **19:7** All the relatives of the poor shun him: how much more do his friends avoid him! He pursues them with pleas, but they are gone.

> **19:9** A false witness shall not be unpunished. He who utters lies shall perish.

> **19:11** The discretion of a man makes him slow to anger. It is his glory to overlook an offense.

> **19:14** House and riches are an inheritance from fathers, but a prudent wife is from Yahweh.

> **19:17** He who has pity on the poor lends to Yahweh; he will reward him.

> **19:18** Discipline your son, for there is hope; don’t be a willing party to his death.

> **19:21** There are many plans in a man’s heart, but Yahweh’s counsel will prevail.

> **19:22** That which makes a man to be desired is his kindness. A poor man is better than a liar.

> **19:23** The fear of Yahweh leads to life, then contentment; he rests and will not be touched by trouble.

> **19:25** Flog a scoffer, and the simple will learn prudence; rebuke one who has understanding, and he will gain knowledge.

## People

- [[God]] — Yahweh is named in verses 3, 14, 17, 21, and 23: blamed by the fool, giver of the
  prudent wife, rewarder of mercy, possessor of prevailing counsel, and the one to be feared.
- [[Solomon]] — attribution from the collection heading at 10:1; not named in this chapter.
- [[Job (person)]] — a comparison, not a participant: his integrity, abandonment, and affliction
  test how the sayings are read.
- The poor, relatives, friends, witnesses, fools, rulers, servant, princes, son, parents, wives,
  hot-tempered man, sluggard, scoffer, and learners are unnamed types, not identifiable individuals.

**Sources.** All new Bible quotations in this pass are copied from the stored WEB. Supporting

23:1–9; 34:6–7. No unreached book was fetched, and existing raw files were unchanged.

**Language and scope.** Analysis follows the stored WEB English, including 19:7's final clause,
19:18's warning about the son's death, and 19:22's account of kindness. No new claim about Hebrew
wording, textual variants, lexical identity, or direct literary dependence is made. Existing
connection pages retain their own earlier source documentation; their new sections here use only the
local comparisons stated above. Physical punishments are described as features of the sayings, not
converted into a contemporary disciplinary procedure.
