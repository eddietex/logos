# Hope Deferred

**Book:** [[Proverbs]]
**Reference:** Proverbs 13:1–25

## Summary

The chapter opens where chapter 10 did, with a son and his father: the wise son *listens* to his
father's instruction, and the scoffer does not listen to rebuke (13:1). It closes on the father's
side of the same relationship — *"One who spares the rod hates his son"* (13:24) — so that hearing
correction and giving it frame twenty-five sayings about mouths, desire, money, hope, and company.
Between those frames the chapter returns three times to *desire*: the sluggard's that comes to
nothing (4), hope deferred and longing fulfilled (12), and longing fulfilled again, sweet to the
soul (19). It is a collection of related sayings, not a continuous argument; the groupings below
are reading aids.

**Proverbs 13 is the first chapter of the Solomonic collection in which neither *Yahweh* nor *God*
appears.** Chapters 10–12 each name Yahweh (10:3, 22, 27, 29; 11:1, 20; 12:2, 22). Here the moral
order runs without a divine name: righteousness *guards* (6), wickedness *overthrows* (6),
misfortune *pursues* (21), prosperity *rewards* (21). The chapter does not say who does the
guarding and rewarding. Reading these as Yahweh's acts is consistent with the surrounding chapters,
but it is the reader's inference, not this chapter's statement.

**13:1–6 — The mouth and the desire.** Listening (1) leads into speaking: the fruit of a man's lips
feeds him (2), and *"He who guards his mouth guards his soul"* (3) — the word *guard* that returns
at 6, where righteousness guards the way of integrity. Verse 4 sets the sluggard's desire against
the diligent's: both want, and only one is satisfied. The righteous man *hates lies* (5); the line
is about what a man loves and hates, not only about what he says.

**13:7–11 — What riches are worth.** Verse 7 is an observation, not a command: some *pretend* to be
rich and have nothing, some pretend to be poor and have great wealth. Appearances in money are
unreliable in both directions. Verse 8 is double-edged: riches can ransom a man's life, which means
they also make him worth threatening, while the poor *"hear no threats"*. Then the lamp (9; see
below), the quarrels that pride breeds against the wisdom of those who *take advice* (10), and
wealth gained dishonestly that dwindles while what is gathered *by hand* grows (11). The WEB's *"by
hand"* is followed here without a claim about the Hebrew idiom behind it.

**13:12–19 — Hope, instruction, and longing.** Verse 12 is the chapter's most quoted line and gives
this page its title: *"Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a
tree of life."* It is a saying about the experience of waiting, and unlike most of its neighbours it
makes no moral contrast — the sick heart in the first half belongs to no fool and no sinner. Its
tree of life is the image of 3:18 and 11:30, now attached to fulfilled desire. The verses that
follow contrast despising and respecting instruction (13), call the teaching of the wise *"a spring
of life, to turn from the snares of death"* (14), and set good understanding that wins favor
against the hard way of the unfaithful (15), the prudent against the fool who exposes folly (16),
and the wicked messenger against the trustworthy envoy who *"gains healing"* (17). Refusing
discipline brings poverty and shame (18). Verse 19 returns to longing — *"sweet to the soul"* — and
then turns sharply: *"fools detest turning from evil."* The fool also has a desire, and what he
cannot bear to give up is the evil itself.

**13:20–25 — Company, inheritance, injustice, and the rod.** *"One who walks with wise men grows
wise"* (20) is the positive form of the father's first warning about company (1:10–19). Misfortune
pursues sinners and prosperity rewards the righteous (21). A good man leaves an inheritance to his
grandchildren, and *"the wealth of the sinner is stored for the righteous"* (22). Then the chapter's
one admission that the system can fail: **the poor man's field yields an abundance of food, and
*"injustice sweeps it away"* (23).** The poverty in this verse is not the sluggard's (4) or the
undisciplined man's (18); the field was productive and someone else's wrong emptied it. The rod
follows (24), and the chapter ends where the sluggard began, with appetite: the righteous eats to
satisfaction, *"but the belly of the wicked goes hungry"* (25).

**The lamp and the inheritance — two sayings Job's dialogue has already used.** Proverbs 13:9 is the
sentence [[Bildad the Shuhite]] applies to Job at Job 18:5–6, *"the light of the wicked shall be put
out"*, and that Job puts to a count at 21:17: *"How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put
out"*. See [[The Lamp of the Wicked Put Out]]. Proverbs 13:22's transfer of the sinner's wealth to
the righteous is the same transfer Job 27:16–17 describes — the wicked man heaps up silver, *"but
the just shall put it on, and the innocent shall divide the silver"* — in a section whose speaker
is disputed. See [[Stored for the Righteous - Proverbs 13.22 and Job 27.16-17]]. In both cases the
wiki records the same thing it has recorded for chapters 10–12: the sayings state an outcome as
general instruction, and the book of Job shows what happens when they are read backwards as a
verdict on one man.

**The promises and their counterexamples.** Proverbs 13:21 says *"prosperity rewards the
righteous"*; Job 1:1, 13–19 narrates an upright man stripped of herds, servants, and children in a
day, and Job 21:7–13 describes the wicked growing old in prosperity with their children established.
These contradict an exceptionless reading of 13:21–22, and the verses supply no exception clause.
Reading them as general moral instruction is the wiki's interpretation, as on the pages for
chapters 10–12. **Proverbs 13:23 itself is the book's own qualification**: it sits two verses after
13:21 and records a poor man whose loss is caused by injustice, not by sloth or folly. 13:18's
"poverty … to him who refuses discipline" cannot therefore be reversed into a diagnosis of every
poor man; 13:23 forbids it within the same chapter. See [[The Prosperity of the Wicked]],
[[Retribution]], and [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]].

**The rod.** Verse 24 says that withholding discipline is a form of hatred and that love is
*"careful to discipline"*. The father's correction of the son is the chapter's frame, and 3:11–12
has already modelled it on Yahweh's reproof of the son he loves. The rod is named again at 22:15;
23:13–14; and 29:15, which the wiki will reach in later pericopes. Proverbs states the rod as a
means of correction and says nothing about its limits; this page records what the text says and
does not use it to settle modern questions about physical punishment of children, which lie outside
what the verse addresses. See [[The Rod of Discipline - Proverbs 13.24 and the Father's Correction]].

## Key verses

> **13:1** A wise son listens to his father’s instruction, but a scoffer doesn’t listen to rebuke.

> **13:3** He who guards his mouth guards his soul. One who opens wide his lips comes to ruin.

> **13:4** The soul of the sluggard desires, and has nothing, but the desire of the diligent shall be fully satisfied.

> **13:7** There are some who pretend to be rich, yet have nothing. There are some who pretend to be poor, yet have great wealth.

> **13:8** The ransom of a man’s life is his riches, but the poor hear no threats.

> **13:9** The light of the righteous shines brightly, but the lamp of the wicked is snuffed out.

> **13:10** Pride only breeds quarrels, but with ones who take advice is wisdom.

> **13:12** Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a tree of life.

> **13:14** The teaching of the wise is a spring of life, to turn from the snares of death.

> **13:19** Longing fulfilled is sweet to the soul, but fools detest turning from evil.

> **13:20** One who walks with wise men grows wise, but a companion of fools suffers harm.

> **13:22** A good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children, but the wealth of the sinner is stored for the righteous.

> **13:23** An abundance of food is in poor people’s fields, but injustice sweeps it away.

> **13:24** One who spares the rod hates his son, but one who loves him is careful to discipline him.
