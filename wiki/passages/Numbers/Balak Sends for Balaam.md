# Balak Sends for Balaam

**Book:** [[Numbers]]
**Reference:** Numbers 22:1–21

## Summary

Israel arrives in the plains of Moab opposite Jericho. Alarmed by its numbers and victory over the
Amorites, Balak seeks Balaam’s curse as preparation for driving the people out. Moabite and
Midianite elders carry rewards; Balaam waits overnight for Yahweh’s answer. God refuses both the
journey and the curse because Israel is blessed (1–14).

Balak sends a larger, more distinguished delegation with promises of honor. Balaam says even the
king’s house full of wealth cannot let him exceed Yahweh’s word, but asks the envoys to stay for
another answer. God now permits travel under the restriction that Balaam do only what he is told.
Balaam saddles his donkey and departs (15–21).

## Notes

### Reports become narrower

God forbids travel and cursing because Israel is blessed (12). Balaam tells the princes that
Yahweh refuses him permission to go (13); their report says Balaam refuses to come (14). The
successive reports omit the blessed status that explains the refusal. Balak answers the reported
obstacle with a greater embassy and greater honor, although the reader knows the obstacle is God’s
instruction.

### Permission and the coming anger

The prohibition of travel in verse 12 and permission in verse 20 both stand. Verse 20 restricts
what Balaam may do; it does not rescind Israel’s blessed status. The next unit opens with God’s
anger because he went (22:22). This is an explicit narrative tension, not resolved here by
supplying an unstated motive or silently rewriting the permission. Balaam’s willingness to ask
again may invite suspicion, but this unit does not explicitly disclose his inner motive.

### Scope and source

The boundary remains 22:1–21: camp, two embassies, and departure. The donkey confrontation is not
ingested here. Beor and Zippor receive minimal identity pages; unnamed delegations remain
collective. All quotations above are copied from the stored WEB Numbers text. River and Pethor are
not assigned unverified modern locations. No claim about exact Hebrew wording is needed.
