# Sihon and Og

**Book:** [[Numbers]]
**Reference:** Numbers 21:21–35

## Summary

Israel requests safe transit through Sihon’s land. He refuses and attacks at Jahaz; Israel defeats
him, takes the territory between the Arnon and Jabbok up to Ammon, and settles in the Amorite
cities. The narrator explains that Heshbon had previously belonged to Moab and quotes a poem
recalling its conquest. Moses sends scouts to Jazer, whose villages are then taken. Og comes to
battle at Edrei; Yahweh assures Moses of victory, and Israel destroys Og’s people and possesses his
land.

## Key verses

Complete unit, copied verbatim from stored WEB `raw/bible/Numbers.md`.

> **21** Israel sent messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites, saying,

> **22** “Let me pass through your land. We will not turn away into field or vineyard. We will not
drink of the water of the wells. We will go by the king’s highway, until we have passed your
border.”

> **23** Sihon would not allow Israel to pass through his border, but Sihon gathered all his people
together, and went out against Israel into the wilderness, and came to Jahaz. He fought against
Israel.

> **24** Israel struck him with the edge of the sword, and possessed his land from the Arnon to the
Jabbok, even to the children of Ammon; for the border of the children of Ammon was strong.

> **25** Israel took all these cities. Israel lived in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon,
and in all its villages.

> **26** For Heshbon was the city of Sihon the king of the Amorites, who had fought against the
former king of Moab, and taken all his land out of his hand, even to the Arnon.

> **27** Therefore those who speak in proverbs say, “Come to Heshbon. Let the city of Sihon be built
and established;

> **28** for a fire has gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon. It has devoured Ar of
Moab, The lords of the high places of the Arnon.

> **29** Woe to you, Moab! You are undone, people of Chemosh! He has given his sons as fugitives,
and his daughters into captivity, to Sihon king of the Amorites.

> **30** We have shot at them. Heshbon has perished even to Dibon. We have laid waste even to
Nophah, Which reaches to Medeba.”

> **31** Thus Israel lived in the land of the Amorites.

> **32** Moses sent to spy out Jazer. They took its villages, and drove out the Amorites who were
there.

> **33** They turned and went up by the way of Bashan. Og the king of Bashan went out against them,
he and all his people, to battle at Edrei.

> **34** Yahweh said to Moses, “Don’t fear him, for I have delivered him into your hand, with all
his people, and his land. You shall do to him as you did to Sihon king of the Amorites, who lived at
Heshbon.”

> **35** So they struck him, with his sons and all his people, until there were no survivors; and
they possessed his land.

## People
- [[God]] — Yahweh tells Moses not to fear Og and promises delivery of his people and land, using the defeat of Sihon as the precedent (34). The subsequent victory fulfills that assurance within the narrative (35).
- [[Moses]] — Moses sends scouts to Jazer (32) and receives Yahweh’s assurance before the battle with Og (34). His leadership in these victories does not revoke the Meribah sentence concerning entry into the land.
- [[Sihon]] — King of the Amorites, based at Heshbon. He refuses Israel transit, attacks at Jahaz, and loses his territory to Israel (21–26). He had previously taken this territory from Moab; the quoted poem recalls that earlier victory (27–30).
- [[Og]] — King of Bashan who brings his people to fight Israel at Edrei (33). Yahweh assures Moses of victory; Og, his sons, and his people are struck until no survivors remain, and Israel possesses the land (34–35). This unit gives no physical description of Og.
- [[King of Moab (before Sihon's conquest)]] — Unnamed former ruler from whom Sihon took Heshbon and territory as far as the Arnon (26). He belongs to the retrospective account, not the embassy or battle at Jahaz. The text does not identify him with Balak.
- [[Chemosh]] — The deity whose people Moab is called in the quoted Heshbon poem (29). The poem portrays him as giving sons into flight and daughters into captivity to Sihon. This is speech within the cited poem, not an independent assertion that Chemosh acts alongside Yahweh.

## Places
- [[Heshbon]] — Sihon’s city and an earlier possession of Moab (25–26). Israel occupies it after defeating him; the poem celebrates its strength and then speaks of its ruin (27–30). The historical account supplies a different setting from the pools imagery in Song of Solomon 7:4.
- [[The Arnon]] — Southern limit of the territory Israel takes from Sihon (24), also the limit of Sihon’s earlier conquest from Moab (26). Its high places occur in the quoted poem (28).
- [[The Jabbok]] — Northern endpoint in the description of Israel’s acquisition from Sihon, from the Arnon to the Jabbok and up to Ammon (24). The verse gives a territorial boundary, not a new episode at Jacob’s ford.
- [[Ammon]] — Israel’s acquired territory reaches to the children of Ammon, whose border the WEB describes as strong (24). Ammon is not said to be conquered here. The older opening description of Ammon across the Arnon should not obscure the Amorite territory between Moab’s Arnon boundary and the Jabbok in Numbers 21:13, 24–26.
- [[Moab (place)]] — Sihon had previously taken Heshbon and land down to the Arnon from Moab’s former king (26). Israel now takes that land from Sihon, not from the Moabite king. The poem laments Moab’s defeat and captivity (28–29), keeping historical possession distinct from present control.
- [[Ar]] — Ar of Moab is devoured by the fire from Heshbon in the quoted poem (28). This is an account of Moab’s defeat, not a newly narrated Israelite campsite or an assertion that Israel attacked Ar.
- [[Jahaz]] — Place where Sihon gathers his people and fights Israel after refusing passage (23). Israel wins and takes his territory; no modern identification is supplied here.
- [[Dibon]] — Named as the extent of Heshbon’s ruin in the quoted poem (30). The passage does not separately narrate a battle at Dibon.
- [[Nophah]] — Named in the poem’s description of devastation reaching to Medeba (30). The WEB spelling is retained; the poem does not establish a precise modern location.
- [[Medeba]] — Named at the end of the poem’s description of devastation (30), in relation to Nophah. It is not presented as an additional stop in an itinerary.
- [[Jazer]] — Moses sends scouts here; Israel takes its villages and drives out their Amorite inhabitants (32). This success follows the settlement in Sihon’s cities.
- [[Bashan]] — Og’s kingdom, approached after the Jazer campaign (33). Following battle at Edrei, Israel kills Og, his sons, and his people and possesses his land (35).
- [[Edrei]] — Site of the battle to which Og king of Bashan comes with his people (33). Yahweh’s assurance to Moses precedes Israel’s victory (34–35).

## Themes
- [[War]] — The request for transit becomes battle when Sihon attacks (21–24). Og also comes out to fight (33); Yahweh promises victory and the account ends with no survivors and possession of the land (34–35). These are lethal conquests, not merely diplomatic successes.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — Israel moves from requesting transit to occupying Amorite cities (22–25, 31). The narrator distinguishes Sihon’s earlier seizure of Moabite territory from Israel’s present conquest (26); Ammon’s strong border remains a limit (24).
- [[Faith]] — Yahweh’s command not to fear Og grounds confidence in promised delivery and the already completed victory over Sihon (34). The text records the assurance and outcome without describing Moses’ inner emotional response.
- [[Judgment]] — Og’s defeat includes his sons and all his people, leaving no survivors (35). Divine delivery is explicit (34), but this unit supplies no catalogue of their sins; one should not invent one to soften or explain the severity.
- [[Strife and Peacemaking]] — Israel offers limited transit without using fields, vineyards, or wells (22). Sihon refuses and attacks (23). Unlike the withdrawal before Edom in Numbers 20:21, this refusal leads to conquest.
- [[Praise and Song]] — The Heshbon poem is introduced as the speech of those who speak in proverbs (27), not as Israel singing to Yahweh. Its celebration of Sihon and lament over Moab stand within an account of Sihon’s own defeat; this preserved poetry differs from the nearby well song.
- [[Remembrance]] — The poem preserves the memory of Moab’s defeat and Sihon’s power (27–30), while the surrounding prose records his fall. The text does not identify its author or securely assign every poetic speaker.
- [[Idolatry]] — Moab is called the people of Chemosh in the quoted poem (29). Its portrayal of the deity surrendering his people belongs to that poetic taunt; no Israelite worship of Chemosh is narrated here.

## Notes

**A change from transit to possession.** The request in verse 22 is not initially a claim to settle.
Sihon’s armed attack leads to occupation, whereas the earlier Edom refusal led Israel to turn away.
The narrator carefully identifies the present opponent as the Amorite king, even though the
territory formerly belonged to Moab. Ammon is a boundary, not another conquered kingdom in this
unit.

**Poem and narrator.** Verses 27–30 are quoted speech, introduced through speakers of proverbs. The
prose securely supplies Sihon’s earlier victory over Moab (26); the exact speaker and historical
perspective of every line, especially the first-person plural in verse 30, are not independently
identified. The apparent movement from celebrating Heshbon to announcing its ruin should be left
visible. No reconstructed Hebrew wording or hypothetical author is presented as established fact.

**The severity of victory.** Verse 35 expressly includes Og’s sons and all his people, with no
survivors. The passage should not be softened into a bloodless transfer of land, nor supplemented
with an invented account of particular crimes. Yahweh’s assurance is explicit; Moses’ inner feelings
and Og’s physical stature are not described here.

**Scope and identities.** The planned boundary is retained. Israel and the Amorites are peoples
here, not individual ancestors. The former king of Moab receives a narrative qualifier and is not
identified with Balak. Chemosh is indexed as a deity within the existing person-page convention,
with the poem’s perspective preserved. The highway needs no speculative route map. No modern
identification is imposed on the named sites. All quotations derive from stored WEB, and all
comparisons in the new connection pages were checked there.
