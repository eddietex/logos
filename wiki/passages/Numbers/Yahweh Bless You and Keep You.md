# Yahweh Bless You and Keep You

**Book:** [[Numbers]]
**Reference:** Numbers 6:22–27

## Summary

The camp has been counted, ordered, and cleared, and the laws of the last two chapters have ended on
a curse drunk in water and a vow kept in the body. This short unit gives the priests something to
say. Yahweh speaks to Moses, Moses is to speak to Aaron and his sons, and the words are fixed: *This
is how you shall bless the children of Israel* (23).

The blessing has three lines, and each names Yahweh once. He is asked to bless and keep (24), to make
his face shine and be gracious (25), and to lift up his face and give peace (26). Every action in
those lines belongs to Yahweh. The priest says the words, but no line asks anything of the priest or
of the people. There is no command in it and no condition.

Verse 27 then says what the speaking does and who does what. *So they shall put my name on the
children of Israel; and I will bless them.* The priests put the name on. Yahweh blesses. The text keeps
those two acts apart in one sentence.

This is the first time the Bible supplies the words of a priest's blessing. When Aaron blessed the
people at Leviticus 9:22–23 and Moses at Exodus 39:43, no words were given. Numbers does not say that
those blessings used these words, and it does not say how often or when this one was to be spoken.

## Notes

**Fixed words.** *Kōh təbārăkû* (23, WLC) tells the priests how to bless, and the next words are given
to them. Most of the priestly speech in Numbers so far has been ritual action, with the oath of 5:21–22
the one recorded exception. Here the priest's duty is a form of words. The text does not say that
these are the only words a priest could use, and it does not say that the people answer.

**Plural and singular.** The address in verse 23 is plural: Aaron and his sons are to bless, and to
speak *lāhem*, to them. Every *you* in the blessing is singular (*-kā*, 24–26, WLC). Verse 27 returns to
the plural, *ʾăbārăkēm*. English hides this, since WEB's *you* covers both. So a plural people is
addressed as one *you*. The text does not say whether that means Israel as a body or each person in
it, and either reading fits the grammar.

**Three, five, and seven.** In WLC, counting by words as morphhb tags them, the three lines run to
three, five, and seven Hebrew words. Each line is two words longer than the one before, and each opens
with a verb and then *YHWH*. That is an observation about form. The text attaches no meaning to the
numbers, and the wiki does not assign one.

**Keep.** *Yišmərekā* (24) is *šāmar*, and in this book so far that verb has belonged to the people
who guard. The Levites *keep* the charge and the furnishings (3:7–8), and Moses, Aaron, and his sons
camp at the door *keeping* the sanctuary's charge, with death for the outsider who comes near (3:38,
WLC). Now the same verb has Yahweh as its subject, with the people as the one kept. The chapter does not
draw the comparison. God promised this verb to Jacob on the road (Genesis 28:15). Cain denied it
(Genesis 4:9).

**His face.** *Yāʾēr pānāyw* (25) uses the verb for giving light. The same verb has lamps as its
subject at Exodus 25:37 and at Numbers 8:2, the second unit after this one. For the use with God's face, see
[[Let Your Face Shine - Numbers 6 in Psalms 4, 67, 80, and 119]]. Moses' shining face at Exodus
34:29–35 is a different verb, *qāran* (WLC), so the two cannot be joined by vocabulary. They can only
be compared as pictures. The blessing asks for what Exodus 33:20 seems to rule out, and it does not
explain how the two fit: *You cannot see my face*. A face turned toward someone and a face seen by
someone are not the same thing, and the blessing asks only for the first. That is the wiki's reading.

**Lift up his face.** The expression needs care, because the usual idiom is not the same as this one.
With another person's face as object, *nāśāʾ pānîm* means to accept or favor someone: *perhaps he will
accept me* (Genesis 32:20) and *I will accept him* (Job 42:8). The same idiom is what God does not do
at Deuteronomy 10:17, *who doesn’t respect persons*, and what judges must not do at Leviticus 19:15.
Verse 26 is not that form. Here Yahweh lifts *his own* face *toward* the one blessed. Humans lift their
own faces in that way at 2 Kings 9:32 and Job 22:26. The tag search run for this pass (lemmas H5375 and
H6440 within a few words, with H413) found no other verse with Yahweh as subject in that form, but a
search of that kind can miss a variant, and this pass consulted no lexicon. So the wiki does not treat
26 as a plain instance of the partiality idiom, and it does not call the two unrelated.

**Be gracious.** *Wîḥunnekkā* (25) is the verb of Exodus 33:19, *I will be gracious to whom I will be
gracious*. By Strong's tags, the first wish in this shape is Joseph's to Benjamin, *God be gracious to
you, my son* (Genesis 43:29), said by a man who is holding back tears and has not yet told his brother
who he is.

**Peace.** *Wəyāśēm ləkā šālôm* (26) is literally *and may he set for you peace*. WEB's *give* is a fair
rendering, but it hides a link in the Hebrew. The verb in 26 is *śîm*, and so is the verb in 27, *śāmû*,
*they shall put*. Yahweh sets peace and the priests set the name, one verb across the join between the
blessing and its explanation. *Šālôm* covers more than the absence of war, as its use at Genesis 37:14
for the welfare of Joseph's brothers and their flocks shows. The word is left in English as *peace*,
and nothing in the verse limits it.

**Put my name.** *Wəśāmû ʾet-šəmî ʿal-bənê yiśrāʾēl* (27, WLC). Elsewhere, *śîm* with Yahweh's *šēm*
usually has a place as its object, the chosen place of Deuteronomy 12:5 and 1 Kings 9:3. Here the object
of the same phrase is a people. How the name is put on them is not stated. The natural reading is
that the speaking does it, since the name is said three times, and *so* (27) ties the verse to the words
before it. Nothing in the text says any physical mark is involved. Exodus 28:36–38 has *HOLY TO YAHWEH*
engraved on Aaron's forehead, and the two passages can be compared, but neither mentions the other. See
[[His Name on Them, Their Names on Him - Numbers 6 and Exodus 28]].

**I will bless them.** *Waʾănî ʾăbārăkēm* (27) has an expressed pronoun that the verb does not need. The
wiki reads it as stress on the speaker, *and I, I will bless them*, as the plain force of the construction,
though no grammar was consulted. Whether a priest's blessing has any force of its own is a question this
verse answers by keeping the priest's act and Yahweh's act apart. Malachi 2:2 shows the other side:
Yahweh says he will curse the blessings of priests who do not honor his name.

**Placement.** The unit ends the run of laws that began at 5:1. The next verse goes back in time:
*On the day that Moses had finished setting up the tabernacle* (7:1). So the order of the book is not
the order of events, and the placement of the blessing at the end of the purity and vow laws is a
literary fact and not a date. Reading it as the seal on the camp as now ordered is the wiki's inference.

**Tensions left standing.**

1. **Face seen and face turned.** Exodus 33:20 against this blessing, and against Exodus 33:11's *face to
   face*. The note above offers a distinction, but the text does not make it.
2. **No partiality, and a lifted face.** Deuteronomy 10:17 against any reading of 6:26 as favoritism. The
   grammar differs, and the tension is real only if the idioms are the same.
3. **Unconditional words in a conditional law.** The blessing names no condition, and Leviticus 26 and
   Deuteronomy 28 name many. Deuteronomy 31:17–18 has Yahweh hide the same face. Numbers does not
   reconcile them. See [[The Lifted Face and the Hidden Face - Numbers 6 and Deuteronomy 31]].
4. **Who is blessed.** The whole people are named, and the unclean have just been sent out of the camp
   (5:1–4). The text does not say whether the blessing reaches them.
