# Balaam's Donkey

**Book:** [[Numbers]]
**Reference:** Numbers 22:22–35

## Summary

God’s anger burns as Balaam travels with two servants. Yahweh’s angel blocks the road with a
drawn sword. The donkey sees him and turns into a field, presses against a vineyard wall and
crushes Balaam’s foot, then lies down where neither side offers an escape. Balaam strikes her
each time, mistaking life-preserving resistance for mockery (22–27).

Yahweh opens the donkey’s mouth. She challenges the beatings and appeals to her past service;
Balaam admits that this behavior is unusual. Yahweh then opens his eyes. The angel rebukes his
way and explains that the donkey has saved his life. Balaam confesses sin and offers to return,
but is told to continue with the princes, speaking only the word given him (28–35).

## People

- [[Balaam]] — Balaam strikes the donkey three times without seeing the angel she sees.
- [[God]] — God is angry at Balaam’s journey, yet opens the donkey’s mouth and Balaam’s eyes, exposing the danger and preserving the traveler.
- [[The Angel of Yahweh]] — The angel blocks the road with a drawn sword, is seen first by the donkey, and tells Balaam that her turning aside saved his life.
- [[Balak]] — Balak does not appear in person in this scene.
- Two unnamed servants travel with Balaam; the princes remain a collective delegation.

## Places

The road passes a field, a walled path between vineyards, and a place too narrow to turn aside.
No named site or exact location is supplied in 22:22–35; these are not separate place pages.
The princes are identified with Moab in the preceding unit, but this scene does not locate the
encounter within a named territory.

## Notes

### Permission and opposition

The anger of verse 22 follows the permission of verse 20. The angel calls Balaam’s way perverse
in verse 32, then renews permission to travel in verse 35. This is a narrative tension: neither
the permission nor the condemnation may be erased. The text here does not state that Balaam
accepted payment, secretly planned a curse, or failed a new summons condition. Those proposals
cannot be substituted for the narrated explanation.

### The rider’s sword and the angel’s sword

Balaam wishes for a sword to kill the donkey (29), while the reader already knows of the drawn
sword threatening him (23). His complaint of mockery reverses the actual relation: the animal
he beats has kept him alive. The narrowing road repeats the conflict while removing each route
of escape. Her speech appeals to their shared history before his sight is restored.

### What the episode does and does not establish

The narration attributes the donkey’s speech and Balaam’s restored sight to Yahweh. It gives no
account of the servants’ or princes’ reactions. Balaam’s confession is recorded, but this unit
alone does not settle his later character or identify the angel with a later theological figure.
