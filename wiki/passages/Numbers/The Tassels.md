# The Tassels

**Book:** [[Numbers]]
**Reference:** Numbers 15:37–41

## Summary

Yahweh tells Moses to command Israel to make fringes on the borders of their garments, with a
blue cord on each border, throughout their generations (37–38). Looking at the fringe is to
lead to remembering and doing all his commandments, instead of following their own heart and
eyes (39). The purpose is holiness to their God (40). The speech closes by identifying Yahweh
as the one who brought them out of Egypt to be their God (41).

## Notes

**Seeing is redirected, not abolished.** Verse 39 opposes following one's own eyes while
commanding a deliberate act of looking. The fringe is an aid to obedience, not a substitute
for it or an object said to protect its wearer automatically. Remembering and doing are paired
in both verses 39 and 40.

**A preventive command after a sentence.** Following the distinction between error and
defiance and the Sabbath execution, the chapter supplies a recurring reminder of all the
commandments. That literary progression is an interpretation of the placement; the speech
does not say the fringes were instituted because of that man's act.

**A shared calling.** The command addresses Israel across generations, without limiting the
fringes to priests. Holiness in verse 40 is the people's vocation. The blue cord is specified,
but these verses do not explain its symbolism, prescribe a knot count, or identify a dye source.
Those details should not be inferred from the color alone.

**Unfaithfulness and desire.** The prostitution language of verse 39 depicts following heart
and eyes as disloyalty to God. Unlike Job 31:1, this is not an explicit case about looking at a
particular woman; neither a named idol nor a particular sexual act is specified. The comparison
with Job concerns disciplined desire, without collapsing the contexts.

**Deliverance has a purpose.** Verse 41 identifies belonging to Yahweh as the goal of the
exodus. Leviticus 22:31–33 similarly brings commandments, holiness, and deliverance together.
The command to be holy does not erase that passage's attribution of sanctification to God;
the texts place divine action and commanded response beside each other.
