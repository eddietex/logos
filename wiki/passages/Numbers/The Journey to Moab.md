# The Journey to Moab

**Book:** [[Numbers]]
**Reference:** Numbers 21:10–20

## Summary

Israel travels through Oboth, Iyeabarim, Zered, and the Arnon border region. The narrator
cites a geographical fragment from the Book of the Wars of Yahweh. At Beer, Yahweh
tells Moses to gather the people and promises water. Israel sings of the well dug by
princes and nobles. The itinerary resumes through Mattanah, Nahaliel, and Bamoth to
the valley in Moab and the top of Pisgah overlooking the desert.

The short unit changes the sound of the journey: after complaint and deadly snakes,
the reader hears a communal song. It does not say that all future rebellion is ended.

## People
- [[God]] — Yahweh promises to give the assembled people water at Beer (16). The divine gift stands beside the song’s attribution of digging to the leaders, without explaining the physical process.
- [[Moses]] — Yahweh addresses Moses with the command to gather the people at Beer (16). Moses is not named as the singer or digger in verses 17–18; his role here is receiving the instruction.

## Themes
- [[Providence]] — At Beer Yahweh explicitly promises water (16), and Israel sings of the leaders’ digging (17–18). Unlike hidden providence in the Joseph narratives, provision here is openly announced; divine gift and human work stand together.
- [[Praise and Song]] — Israel sings to the well and celebrates the princes’ digging (17–18). Unlike the Song of the Sea, the short song does not directly address Yahweh; its surrounding narrative identifies him as the giver of water (16).
- [[The Wilderness]] — The journey names camps east of Moab and along the Arnon before turning to the well and the heights of Pisgah (10–20). Wilderness is a traversed region with water and remembered places, not only a setting of rebellion.
- [[Land and Sojourning]] — The Arnon is explicitly a boundary between Moab and the Amorites (13). Israel’s movement is narrated through named territories without a conquest being reported within this pericope.
- [[Remembrance]] — The narrator cites the Book of the Wars of Yahweh for the Arnon landscape (14–15), then records Israel’s well song (17–18). Written citation and communal song preserve memory; the fragment does not disclose the cited book’s full contents or author.
- [[Work]] — The well song credits princes and nobles with digging, using the scepter and their poles in the WEB wording (18). Divine provision in verse 16 does not erase human labor, though the song is not a technical description of excavation.

## Notes

**A source named, not reconstructed.** Verses 14–15 explicitly cite the Book of the Wars
of Yahweh. This fragment gives neither its author nor its date, and the wiki does not
reconstruct a battle at Vaheb from the book’s title. The song in verses 17–18 is introduced
separately; this unit does not explicitly attribute it to that book.

**Geographical limits.** The itinerary’s relative order is clear in the stored WEB, but
precise modern sites are not established here. Vaheb, Suphah, and Ar occur in the quoted
fragment, not as three additional narrated camps. Ar is distinguished from Moab as a
whole, correcting the existing Moab page’s overly broad alias. The Arnon’s stated boundary
is with the Amorites (13), not Ammon; the earlier Moab page’s different description is
explicitly flagged there. No exact Hebrew wording or etymology is asserted.

**Gift and digging.** The song credits leaders with digging using the scepter and poles;
the prose credits Yahweh with the promise of water. Neither explanation displaces the
other. The text is not a technical account of how the well was excavated.

**Scope.** The planned boundary is retained. Israel is the people, not the individual
Jacob; the unnamed princes and nobles are not identified with the twelve named census
princes. Their role is recorded here without inventing personal identities. The Amorites
are the neighboring people at the boundary, not a newly named individual. The named book
is discussed here and under Remembrance without inventing a new page type. All quotations
in this ingest come from stored raw WEB; no later-book quotation is supplied from memory.
