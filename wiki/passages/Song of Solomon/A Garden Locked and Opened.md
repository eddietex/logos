# A Garden Locked and Opened

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 4:8–5:1

## Summary

The man goes on speaking, and for the first time he calls the woman *"my bride"*. He calls her down
from the far north: *"Come with me from Lebanon, my bride, with me from Lebanon"*. She is to look
from the tops of Amana, Senir, and Hermon, *"from the lions' dens, from the mountains of the
leopards"* (4:8). The heights are high, wild, and far off, and he wants her with him.

Then he says what she has done to him. *"You have ravished my heart, my sister, my bride"*, with one
glance of her eyes and one chain of her necklace (4:9). He praises her love and her perfume, in
words that return the woman's own words of 1:2–3: *"How much better is your love than wine! The
fragrance of your perfumes than all kinds of spices!"* (4:10). Her lips drip honey; *"Honey and milk
are under your tongue"*; her garments smell like Lebanon (4:11).

At 4:12 the picture becomes a garden. *"A locked up garden is my sister, my bride; a locked up spring,
a sealed fountain."* The garden is full: pomegranates and precious fruits, henna and spikenard,
saffron, calamus, cinnamon, frankincense trees, myrrh, aloes, *"all the best spices"* (4:13–14). The
locked spring is also a fountain for gardens, *"a well of living waters, flowing streams from
Lebanon"* (4:15). Locked does not mean dry. It means kept for one person.

At 4:16 the woman answers, and the garden opens. She calls on the winds, *"Awake, north wind; and come,
you south! Blow on my garden, that its spices may flow out"*, and then gives the invitation: *"Let my
beloved come into his garden, and taste his precious fruits."* The garden she calls *my garden* in one
line is *his garden* in the next.

He answers at once, in the past tense: *"I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride"* (5:1). He
has gathered his myrrh and spice, eaten his honeycomb and honey, drunk his wine and milk. The wine,
honey, milk, and spice he praised in 4:10–14 are now his to enjoy. A last voice, not identified,
tells the lovers or their guests to eat and drink: *"Eat, friends! Drink, yes, drink abundantly,
beloved"* (5:1).

This is the Song's most complete scene of union. The word *bride*, *kallâ*, is used six times in the
book, and all six fall in this unit (4:8, 9, 10, 11, 12; 5:1). The unit follows the man's first
description of her, [[You Are All Beautiful]], and the wedding procession before it,
[[Solomon's Wedding Procession]], so many readers take 4:8–5:1 as the wedding night. The text names no
wedding in this unit. It does call her *bride*, and it ends where the procession ended, in gladness.
What follows is a second night scene at 5:2, where she opens to him and he is gone (stored WEB, not yet
ingested; see [[The Two Night Searches - Song of Solomon 3.1-5 and 5.2-8]]).

The garden also has a long life outside the Song. A planted garden with fruit, water, and spices
invites comparison with [[The Garden of Eden]], and many readers have heard the Song's garden as
paradise reopened. The Song never names Eden. See
[[A Garden Locked - Song of Solomon 4.12-5.1, Eden, and the Royal Parks]].

## People

- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — the speaker of 4:8–15 and of 5:1a. He addresses a feminine *you*
  throughout (Sefaria). He calls her *bride* and *my sister, my bride*. He is not named and not called
  king.
- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — addressed and praised in 4:8–15. She speaks 4:16, at least its
  second half, where *"my beloved"* (*dôḏî*) is her word for him throughout the Song.
- *Friends* (5:1b) — an unidentified voice, or a group addressed; the WEB prints *"Friends"* as a
  speaker cue inside the verse. No page.

## Places

- [[Lebanon]] — three times here: where she is called from (4:8, twice), the smell of her garments
  (4:11), and the source of the garden's streams (4:15).
- [[Amana]] — a summit she is to look from (4:8). Its first appearance in the wiki.
- [[Hermon]] — *"Senir and Hermon"* (4:8). Deuteronomy 3:9 says Senir is the Amorite name for Hermon;
  the Song names them side by side. See the place page.
- *The lions' dens* and *the mountains of the leopards* (4:8) — not named places. No page.

## Themes

- [[Marriage]] — *kallâ*, *bride*, all six of its uses in the Song (4:8–5:1); the garden kept for one
  and given to him.
- [[Love]] — *"How beautiful is your love"* (4:10); *dôḏîm*, the word for love that opened the Song
  (1:2).
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — eating, drinking, honey, wine, and milk (4:10–11; 5:1); *"drink
  abundantly"*.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The unit runs from *"Come with me from Lebanon, my bride"* (4:8) to the invitation to eat
and drink (5:1). Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks a section break (*{ס}*) after 4:7
and the next one after 5:1, so it treats 4:8–5:1 as one section. The chapter break at 5:1 falls in the
middle of the scene: 4:16 invites him into the garden and 5:1 says he has come. At 5:2 a new scene
begins, *"I was asleep, but my heart was awake"*. The planned boundary held, and no box was redrawn.

**Bride and sister.** *Kallâ*, *bride*, is in 4:8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 5:1, and nowhere else in the
Song (stored WEB, six uses of *bride*; Hebrew read at each from Sefaria). The word can also mean
*daughter-in-law*, as [[Tamar]] is to Judah (Genesis 38:11, *kallāṯô*; Sefaria), but here it is
plainly the man's bride. *ʾĂḥōṯî ḵallâ*, *my sister, my bride*, is said four times (4:9, 10, 12; 5:1), and *ʾăḥōṯî*, *my sister*,
again with other names at 5:2. *Sister* is a term of affection, not a family claim. In 8:1 the woman
wishes the man were *"like my brother"*, so that she could kiss him in the street without anyone
despising her (stored WEB). Calling a lover *sister* or *brother* is also known from ancient Egyptian
love songs; that is a common scholarly comparison and this page has not checked those texts. It is
unrelated to the patriarchs' claim that a wife was a sister, in [[The Wife-Sister Episodes]].

**From the mountains.** *ʾIttî millǝḇānôn kallâ ʾittî millǝḇānôn tāḇôʾî* (4:8; Sefaria). *Tāḇôʾî* is
*you will come* or *come*; the WEB gives *"Come with me"*. *Tāšûrî*, the WEB's *"Look"*, is from a verb
that can mean *look* and also, in some analyses, *come down* or *travel*. This page follows the WEB
and has not checked a lexicon. Amana, Senir, and Hermon are heights of the Anti-Lebanon and Hermon
range north of Israel. Lions and leopards live there in the poem. Whether the bride is pictured as
living on the heights, or the heights stand for what is remote and dangerous, the verse does not
say.

**Ravished.** *Libbaḇtinî* (4:9, twice; Sefaria) is a verb made from *lēḇ*, *heart*. The WEB's
*"You have ravished my heart"* takes it as *you have taken my heart*; others render *you have made my
heart beat faster*. Both readings are offered in translations and this page does not decide between
them. The Masoretic text has a written form *bǝʾeḥād* and a read form *bǝʾaḥaṯ* in *"with one of your
eyes"*, a matter of gender agreement (Sefaria marks them as written and read). *ʿĂnāq* is a
neck-chain or necklace. One glance and one bead are enough.

**Her words returned.** The woman opened the Song with *kî-ṭôḇîm dōḏeyḵā miyyāyin*, *your love is
better than wine*, and *lǝrêaḥ šǝmāneyḵā ṭôḇîm*, *your oils have a pleasing fragrance* (1:2–3; Sefaria).
Here he says *mah-ṭṭōḇû ḏōḏayiḵ miyyayin*, *how much better is your love than wine*, and *rêaḥ
šǝmānayiḵ mikkol-bǝśāmîm*, *the fragrance of your perfumes than all spices* (4:10; Sefaria). The words
are the same, with the suffixes changed from masculine to feminine. What she said of him, he now says
of her. See [[Wine, Oil, and the Joy of Love]].

**Honey and milk.** *Dǝḇaš wǝḥālāḇ taḥaṯ lǝšônēḵ* (4:11; Sefaria). The promised land is *ʾereṣ zāḇaṯ
ḥālāḇ ûḏǝḇāš*, *a land flowing with milk and honey* (Exodus 3:8; stored WEB and Sefaria). The Song has
the same two nouns in the other order. Readers often hear the land in the bride. The verse has no word
for *land* or *flowing*, and the wiki records the shared pair without claiming an allusion. Gilead and
David's tower in 4:1–4 already drew the woman's body in pictures of the country; see
[[You Are All Beautiful]]. *Nōp̄eṯ*, the WEB's *honeycomb*, is honey that drips from the comb.

**Locked garden, sealed fountain.** *Gan nāʿûl ʾăḥōṯî ḵallâ gal nāʿûl maʿyān ḥāṯûm* (4:12; Sefaria).
*Nāʿûl* is *locked* or *barred*, and *ḥāṯûm* is *sealed*, from the root of *ḥôṯām*, the seal of 8:6,
*"Set me as a seal on your heart"* (stored WEB; Sefaria). The second phrase in Sefaria's text is *gal
nāʿûl*. *Gal* usually means a *heap* or a wave; the WEB renders *spring*, matching *maʿyān*, *spring*,
beside it. Some manuscripts and ancient versions are commonly said to read *gan*, *garden*, a second
time; this page has not checked them. The picture is the same either way: she is closed to everyone
else and kept for him. Proverbs uses the same idea of a wife as one's own spring; see
[[Your Own Well - Proverbs 5.15-19, Song of Solomon 4.12-15, and Ecclesiastes 9.9]].

**The orchard.** *Šǝlāḥayiḵ pardēs rimmônîm* (4:13; Sefaria), *your shoots are an orchard of
pomegranates*. *Šǝlāḥayiḵ* is from *šālaḥ*, *send out*: what she sends out, her growth, or perhaps her
channels of water; the WEB has *shoots*. *Pardēs* is the word Ecclesiastes uses for the Preacher's
*parks* (*gannôṯ ûp̄ardēsîm*, Ecclesiastes 2:5; Sefaria). It is generally taken as a loanword from
Persian for an enclosed park, and its Greek form *paradeisos* gave English *paradise*. The loanword
claim is standard, and this page has not verified it in a lexicon. Pomegranates also appeared in her
temples at 4:3.

**The spices.** *Kǝp̄ārîm ʿim-nǝrāḏîm nērd wǝḵarkōm qāneh wǝqinnāmôn ʿim kol-ʿăṣê lǝḇônâ mōr waʾăhālôṯ ʿim
kol-rāʾšê ḇǝśāmîm* (4:13–14; Sefaria). Henna and nard are the woman's comparisons of 1:12 and 1:14; myrrh
and frankincense are the spices of 3:6 and 4:6. Most of the rest are imported luxuries. The holy
anointing oil of Exodus 30:23 also used myrrh, cinnamon, and cane, *qǝnēh-ḇōśem*, the WEB's *"fragrant
cane"* (stored WEB; Sefaria). The Song's calamus is *qāneh*, the same noun. The overlap shows what the
spices were worth; nothing in the Song points to the sanctuary. See
[[Myrrh, Aloes, and Cinnamon - Proverbs 7.17, Song of Solomon 4.14, and Psalm 45.8]].

**Living water.** *Maʿyan gannîm bǝʾēr mayim ḥayyîm wǝnōzǝlîm min-lǝḇānôn* (4:15; Sefaria). *Bǝʾēr mayim
ḥayyîm*, *a well of living waters*, is exactly the phrase at Genesis 26:19, where Isaac's servants dig
in the valley *"and found there a well of springing water"* (stored WEB; Sefaria). *Living* water is
water that moves, from a spring, not stored in a cistern. Locked and sealed (4:12), she is also a
spring that waters other gardens (4:15). The text gives both images without reconciling them.

**Awake, and blow.** *ʿÛrî ṣāp̄ôn ûḇôʾî ṯêmān hāp̄îḥî ḡannî* (4:16; Sefaria). *ʿÛrî*, *awake*, is from the
same root, *ʿ-w-r*, as the refrain's *taʿîrû … tǝʿôrǝrû*, *stir up … awaken* (2:7; 3:5; Sefaria). There
she forbade the daughters to awaken love before its time. Here she tells the wind to awake. *Hāp̄îḥî*,
*blow*, is from *p-w-ḥ*, the verb of *ʿaḏ šeyyāp̄ûaḥ hayyôm*, *until the day breathes* (2:17; 4:6). These
root identifications are standard and are read here from the consonants in Sefaria's text; this page
has not checked a lexicon. The north and south winds are named as a pair; together they blow from
every side.

**My garden, his garden.** Who says *"my garden"* in 4:16a is not marked, and readers differ. If she
says it, she first calls it hers and then gives it to him: *yāḇōʾ ḏôḏî lǝḡannô*, *let my beloved come
into his garden* (Sefaria). If he says the first half, she answers in the second. In 5:1 he answers in
her exact words, *bāʾṯî lǝḡannî*, *I have come into my garden*, then says *my* seven more times in the
Hebrew: my sister, my myrrh, my spice, my honeycomb, my honey, my wine, my milk. (*Kallâ*, *bride*,
has no suffix; the WEB's *"my bride"* supplies it.) *Yaʿrî*, *my honeycomb*,
is usually a word for *forest* or *thicket*; here it is taken as the comb. This page has not checked a
lexicon on that sense.

**Eat, friends.** *ʾIḵlû rēʿîm šǝṯû wǝšiḵrû dôḏîm* (5:1; Sefaria). *Šiḵrû* is from *šāḵar*, *drink
deeply* or *become drunk*; the WEB has *"drink abundantly"*. *Dôḏîm* can be a vocative, *beloved ones*,
as in the WEB. It is also the plural *ḏôḏîm*, *love* or *lovemaking*, the word of 1:2, 4, and 4:10; on
that reading the line means *drink deeply of love*. Who speaks it is not said. It may be guests at a
wedding, the daughters of Jerusalem, or the poet, and it may be addressed to the lovers or to the
guests. The WEB's *"Friends"* in the verse is an edition's speaker cue, not part of the Hebrew. The same
noun is in the adulteress's invitation of Proverbs 7:18, *nirweh ḏōḏîm*, *let's take our fill of
loving*; see [[Myrrh, Aloes, and Cinnamon - Proverbs 7.17, Song of Solomon 4.14, and Psalm 45.8]].

**Near the middle.** The Song has 117 verses in the WEB numbering, and 4:16–5:1 are verses 61 and 62
(counted in the stored WEB). The scene of union falls near the centre of the book. Some readers make
much of that; the wiki notes it and builds nothing on it.

**Readings.** Jewish and Christian allegory read the garden as Israel kept for God, or the church or the
soul kept for Christ; the *"sealed fountain"* has also been applied to Mary in Christian tradition. The
wiki records these as readings and follows the plain sense: a man and a woman, a bride, and their
union.

**Pages decided.** Two new place pages: [[Amana]] and [[Hermon]]. Senir has no page of its own; it is
recorded on Hermon with the tension between Deuteronomy 3:9 and the Song noted there. The lions' dens
and the mountains of the leopards are images, not places. No new person page: the *friends* of 5:1 are
unidentified. One new connection page, for the garden.
