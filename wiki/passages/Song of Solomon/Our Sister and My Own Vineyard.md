# Our Sister and My Own Vineyard

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 8:8–12

## Summary

A plural family voice considers what to do for a little sister when she is spoken for.
They imagine building silver on her if she is a wall, or enclosing her with cedar if
she is a door (8–9). A woman answers with her own assessment: she is a wall, her breasts
are like towers, and she found peace in his eyes (10). The contrast between their
immature sister and her mature self-description is deliberate; its chronology is not
explained. This may recall an earlier family conversation, rather than introducing
another sister or a sequence of events happening now.

The poem then describes Solomon's vineyard at Baal Hamon, leased to keepers for a
thousand shekels each (11). The first-person voice places her own vineyard before
herself and addresses Solomon concerning the thousand and the two hundred for those
who tend the fruit (12). Read as the woman's continued speech, this puts her own
vineyard beside the king's revenue-producing property. It can assert personal agency
without supplying a narrated land transfer or proving a rejection of Solomon as a suitor.

## Key verses

> **8** We have a little sister. She has no breasts. What shall we do for our sister in the day when she is to be spoken for?

> **9** If she is a wall, we will build on her a turret of silver. if she is a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar.

> **10** I am a wall, and my breasts like towers, then I was in his eyes like one who found peace.

> **11** Solomon had a vineyard at Baal Hamon. He leased out the vineyard to keepers. Each was to bring a thousand shekels of silver for its fruit.

> **12** My own vineyard is before me. The thousand are for you, Solomon; two hundred for those who tend its fruit.

## Notes

**Speakers and maturity.** The stored WEB has no speaker labels in 8:8–12. The brothers'
identification draws on 1:6 and the plural family address; the little sister's identity
with the woman who answers is a reading of the contrast, not a named identification.
The man of verse 10 is not named Solomon. No precise age or elapsed time is supplied.

**Wall and door.** These images can suggest firmness and accessibility, with the family
promising adornment or enclosure. A reading about sexual boundaries is plausible in
this context, but the verses do not explain the code or establish a universal rule
for controlling a sister. Her answer adopts the wall image while asserting maturity
and peace. The family assessment and her self-assessment remain distinct voices.

**Revenue and agency.** Verse 11 assigns a thousand shekels to each keeper; verse 12
allocates a thousand to Solomon and two hundred to the tenders. It does not explicitly
say that the woman pays these amounts, narrate a sale, or state a general wage ratio.
The personal-vineyard metaphor gains force beside 1:6 and 4:12–16, but the economics
of the king's vineyard should not be erased. The contrast with 8:7 is not a contradiction:
fruit has a stated financial return, whereas love cannot be purchased.

**Boundary and sources.** The planned 8:8–12 unit is retained. The family and vineyard
exchange follows the seal poem; 8:13 begins a new request to hear a voice. All quotations
are copied from the stored WEB Song of Solomon. Comparisons use its 1:6; 4:12–5:1;
8:5–14. No source-language assertion, historical site identification, or outside
quotation is needed. The final two verses remain un-ingested.
