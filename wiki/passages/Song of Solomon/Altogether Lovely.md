# Altogether Lovely

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 5:9–6:3

## Summary

The questioners ask what makes the woman's beloved better than another, recalling her request
that they carry a message to him (5:9; 5:8). She answers with a sustained description of his
body: gold, black hair, dove-like eyes, spices, lilies, gems, ivory, marble, and Lebanon's
cedars. The catalogue ends in a personal declaration: he is her beloved and her friend
(5:10–16). The audience then offers to seek him with her (6:1). She says he has gone down to
his garden and closes with mutual belonging and the lilies (6:2–3).

The poem answers absence with praise and assurance. It does not narrate how she learned his
whereabouts or say that she found and brought him back. The confidence of 6:2–3 stands beside
the failed search of [[I Opened, but He Had Gone]], without an invented intervening event.

## People

- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — describes the beloved to the audience (5:10–16), identifies
  his garden as his destination, and declares mutual belonging (6:2–3).
- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — the described beloved and friend; he has no direct speech here.
- [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] — addressed explicitly at 5:16; the questioners of 5:9 and 6:1
  are understood as this group from the surrounding dialogue.

## Places

- [[Lebanon]] — a comparison for his appearance and excellence, with its cedars (5:15).
- [[Jerusalem]] — named in the audience's title (5:16), not identified as the scene's location.
- His garden, gardens, and beds of spices (6:2) — unnamed poetic spaces, continuous with the
  garden imagery of [[A Garden Locked and Opened]]; no separate geographical page is created.

## Themes

- [[Love]] — the beloved's singular value, friendship, and mutual belonging (5:10, 16; 6:3).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — the woman describes the man's body as he previously described hers.
- [[Praise and Song]] — a question about his worth elicits extended praise before others.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — precious materials become comparisons for a body (5:11, 14–15).
- [[Oaths and Vows]] — the question of 5:9 explicitly responds to the adjuration of 5:8.

## Notes

**Boundary and voices.** The existing queue is retained. The audience's question at 5:9 begins
this unit; the belonging formula at 6:3 closes it before renewed praise of the woman at 6:4.
The stored WEB has no speaker labels within these eleven verses. Assigning the questions to
the daughters follows the address to them in 5:8 and 5:16 and their plural offer in 6:1;
it is a reading of the dialogue, not an independently supplied identification.

**The answer is a person, not a list of accomplishments.** The description moves from head and
hair through eyes, cheeks, lips, hands, body, and legs, then to overall appearance and mouth.
Its materials evoke value, fragrance, solidity, and sweetness. They are poetic comparisons,
not literal anatomy or evidence that the beloved owns gold and gems. The praise of his
white and ruddy appearance (5:10) does not cancel her earlier self-description as dark and
lovely (1:5). The two speakers are praised in different images; no racial hierarchy is stated.

**Reciprocal praise.** His eyes are like doves (5:12), as hers were in 1:15 and 4:1. His lips
are lilies with myrrh (5:13), taking up the poem's flowers and fragrance. Her conclusion about
his entire loveliness (5:16) answers his praise of her whole beauty in 4:7. This comparison
uses the stored English text and makes no claim of identical Hebrew wording.

**Beloved and friend.** Her last identification joins desire and companionship (5:16).
Proverbs 17:17 describes a friend's enduring love, while 27:9 brings perfume and a friend's
counsel together. Those are useful thematic neighbors; this poem describes no act of counsel
or test of loyalty. The daughters' offer to seek him with her (6:1) changes their role from
questioners to prospective helpers. Their opening question need not be read as hostility.

**The garden after the search.** The woman who did not find him in 5:6 now identifies his
whereabouts (6:2). That shift is explicit; its explanation is not. Since the woman herself
was compared to a garden in 4:12 and invited him into it in 4:16, the return to garden and
lilies can express renewed intimacy. It does not prove a literal itinerary or narrated
physical reunion. The singular garden and plural gardens both remain in the quotation.

**Mutual belonging.** In 2:16 she begins with his belonging to her; in 6:3 she begins with hers
to him. Both statements are reciprocal. The changed order alone does not establish a change
in legal status, a stage of marriage, or a psychological progression. Later 7:10 remains a
stored cross-reference, awaiting its own ingest.
