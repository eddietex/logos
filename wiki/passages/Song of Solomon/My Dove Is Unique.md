# My Dove Is Unique

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 6:4–10

## Summary

The man again praises the woman, comparing her beauty to Tirzah and Jerusalem and her
presence to an army with banners (6:4). Her eyes overcome him. His description of her hair,
teeth, and veiled temples takes up the images of 4:1–3 (6:5–7). The many queens, concubines,
and virgins give way to the singular beloved: his dove is unique, her mother's only daughter
and favorite. Other women see her, call her blessed, and praise her (6:8–9). The closing
question pictures her through morning, moon, sun, and the army with banners again (6:10).

The movement is from personal address to recognition by others. It follows the woman's
assurance in [[Altogether Lovely]], but supplies no narrated reunion, apology, or explanation
of the preceding absence. The poem lets renewed praise stand without filling in that gap.

## People

- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — addressed as beautiful, described as unique, and praised
  by others; she has no clearly assigned speech here.
- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — the voice understood to praise her in 6:4–9; not named as a king.
- [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] — a possible identification of the daughters in 6:9 or the
  questioners of 6:10, not an explicit naming in this unit.
- Her mother, queens, concubines, and virgins — unnamed figures or groups. The mother's
  relation to her is stated; no individual royal woman's identity is recoverable.

## Places

- [[Tirzah (city)]] and [[Jerusalem]] — measures of her beauty (6:4), not stated destinations
  or the scene's location.
- [[Gilead]] — the goats along its side supply the hair comparison again (6:5).

## Themes

- [[Love]] — one beloved's singular value amid many women (6:8–9).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — her eyes overcome him; others see and admire her (6:5, 9–10).
- [[Praise and Song]] — intimate praise becomes acknowledgment by others (6:9).

## Notes

**Boundary and voices.** The planned 6:4–10 unit is retained. Direct praise begins at 6:4;
the repeated army-with-banners image closes the frame at 6:10, before the grove scene of
6:11. The stored WEB supplies no speaker labels in 6:4–10. Reading 6:4–9 as the man's speech
follows the surrounding exchange and the repeated bodily praise of chapter 4. Verse 10 may
continue his praise or voice the admiration attributed to the women in 6:9. Neither option
is marked conclusively by this English source.

**Beauty that overcomes the observer.** The gaze is not simply his inspection of her.
He asks her to turn her eyes away because of their effect on him (6:5), continuing the
power of one glance in 4:9. Goats, ewes, and pomegranate recur from 4:1–3; here the description
is surrounded by cities and a formidable army. The army image frames praise, not a narrated
battle, and the beloved is not declared hostile. The city names do not locate the lovers.

**Many and one.** The numbers belong to queens and concubines (6:8); the lover then singles
out his dove (6:9). The WEB says there are such women, not that all belong to the speaker.
The unit names neither Solomon nor any other king. A royal setting is possible, but a
biography of Solomon's household cannot be reconstructed from these numbers. Likewise,
being uniquely loved does not itself establish that the speaker has only one wife.
The passage neither legislates polygyny nor records its abolition.

**Mother and daughters.** The WEB calls her her mother's only daughter and favorite (6:9).
That does not contradict the mother's sons in 1:6: a woman can have brothers and no sisters.
Her mother remains unnamed; no separate biography is created. The daughters who praise her
are not explicitly called daughters of Jerusalem here. Their identification with the
recurring audience is possible, and is kept conditional on that group's page. The queens
and concubines join in praise; rivalry or humiliation is not narrated.

**Praise and its limits.** In Proverbs 31:28–30 the children call their mother blessed,
her husband ranks her above many women, and fear of Yahweh outranks charm and beauty.
Here beauty receives sustained celebration. This is a real difference of emphasis, not
an excuse to replace one poem with the other. Proverbs questions beauty as the measure of
lasting worth; Song praises a beloved without making beauty proof of virtue or divine favor.
The connection page keeps both emphases visible. The perfect one of 6:9 is a lover's praise,
not an assertion of sinlessness or qualification as a sacrifice.

**Radiance without an invented transformation.** Morning, moon, and sun widen the imagery
of beauty at 6:10. They do not cancel her dark and lovely self-description in 1:5–6, narrate
a change of complexion, or make her a heavenly being. Nor does morning here supply a
chronological dawn after the night scene of 5:2–8: it is a comparison.
