# Dark and Lovely

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 1:5–8

## Summary

The woman turns from the beloved to an audience, the daughters of Jerusalem, and speaks
about herself (5). She is dark and also lovely, and gives two comparisons: the tents of
Kedar and the curtains of Solomon. She asks not to be stared at, and says why she is dark:
the sun has scorched her (6). Her mother's sons were angry with her and made her keeper
of the vineyards, so she has not kept her own vineyard. Then she addresses the one her soul
loves and asks where he grazes his flock and rests it at noon. She does not want to be like
someone veiled beside his companions' flocks (7). The answer calls her most beautiful among
women and tells her to follow the sheep's tracks and graze her young goats beside the
shepherds' tents (8).

The unit therefore has three movements. First comes self-description to onlookers (5–6),
then a question to the beloved (7), then a reply (8). The opening unit valued the
beloved. Here the woman is the one looked at, and she answers the look herself. The *but*
of verse 5 joins dark and lovely without retracting either word. She explains the
darkness by outdoor labor and the sun, not by ancestry. She makes no claim about anyone
else's complexion, and the page does not supply one.

The scene has moved from the king's rooms of 1:4 to vineyards and pasture. The unit
gives no explanation of that shift. The beloved is now addressed as a shepherd with a
flock and companions, and the woman can graze young goats. Pastoral roles and royal
images stand side by side in the Song, and the poem does not resolve them into one plot.

## Key verses

> **5** I am dark, but lovely, you daughters of Jerusalem, like Kedar’s tents, like Solomon’s curtains.

> **6** Don’t stare at me because I am dark, because the sun has scorched me. My mother’s sons were angry with me. They made me keeper of the vineyards. I haven’t kept my own vineyard.

> **7** Tell me, you whom my soul loves, where you graze your flock, where you rest them at noon; For why should I be as one who is veiled beside the flocks of your companions?

> **8** If you don’t know, most beautiful among women, follow the tracks of the sheep. Graze your young goats beside the shepherds’ tents.

## People

- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — describes herself, names her brothers' anger and her
  assigned work, and seeks the beloved at noon (5–7).
- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — addressed as *"you whom my soul loves"* and pictured with a
  flock and companions (7). The edition supplies no speaker cue for verse 8, so who gives
  the answer is not settled. It may be him, or the daughters or other voices.
- [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] — the onlookers addressed in 5 and the first appearance of
  the Song's recurring audience.
- [[Solomon]] — named only in the comparison *"Solomon's curtains"* (5).
- *My mother's sons* (6) — the woman's brothers, unnamed and given no page. The plural
  *"our sister"* exchange at 8:8–9 may involve the same family voices; that is not yet ingested.
- The companions (7) and the shepherds (8) are unnamed groups.

## Places

- [[Jerusalem]] — named in the address *"daughters of Jerusalem"* (5). Nothing is located in
  the city in this unit.
- [[Kedar]] — the comparison *"like Kedar's tents"* (5). Kedar's people, not a setting.
- The vineyards and pasture of 6–8 are unnamed.

## Themes

- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — *"Don't stare at me"* (6). The woman under others' gaze names
  herself lovely first.
- [[Love]] — *"you whom my soul loves"* (7) and *"most beautiful among women"* (8).
- [[Brotherhood]] — brothers who are angry and set their sister to work (6).
- [[Work]] — the sun-scorched keeper of vineyards and the shepherds at noon (6–8).

## Notes

**Boundary.** Verse 5 opens with a new address and self-description, as recorded at the
close of [[Love Better Than Wine]]. Verse 9 begins a sequence of compliments, *"I have
compared you, my love"*, that runs into 2:7. This unit is 1:5–8 as planned, so no queue
boundary was redrawn.

**Dark, and the sun.** The poem explains the darkness itself: *"the sun has scorched me"*
(6), after forced work in the vineyards. Some later readings treat verse 5 as a statement
about ethnicity, and some English versions have rendered the conjunction as *and* rather
than *but*. This wiki follows the WEB's *but*. It makes no claim about the Hebrew
conjunction or about the woman's ancestry, and the text supplies neither. What the
verses give is a woman answering the stare of others with her own self-assessment
(*lovely*) and with a reason (*the sun*).

**Tents and curtains.** Kedar's tents and Solomon's curtains are parallel images. One
belongs to a tent-dwelling Arabian people and the other to a king. Jeremiah 49:29 lists
*"their tents"* and *"their curtains"* among the goods to be taken from Kedar (WEB, checked
at bible-api.com). That matches the Song's pairing, but it does not explain it. The usual
reading takes dark goat-hair tents as the point of the comparison. That is a probable
reading, not a statement of the text. The wiki also does not adopt a proposed textual
emendation of *Solomon* here.

**Her own vineyard.** Literally, she kept others' vineyards and not her own. Many readers
also take *"my own vineyard"* as a figure for herself, since the Song elsewhere uses
vineyard and garden imagery of the woman and her love (8:12; 4:12–5:1, both un-ingested).
In this unit the double sense is possible but unconfirmed. The literal statement about
forced work already makes sense without it.

**Veiled.** The WEB has *"as one who is veiled beside the flocks of your companions"* (7).
Interpreters understand the line in different ways. Some suggest a woman thought to be a
prostitute, as when Judah takes a covered Tamar for one (Genesis 38:15). Others suggest a
woman who has to wander or search among other men's flocks. This page follows the WEB
wording. It makes no claim about the Hebrew word, and neither reading is presented as
settled. Rebekah's veil at Genesis 24:65 marks modesty before her bridegroom, so a veil in
Genesis does not carry one meaning.

**Who answers?** Verse 8 could be the beloved's playful reply or the daughters' response.
The fetched WEB gives no speaker cue here, unlike in 1:4. A similar address, *"you
fairest among women"* in the WEB, returns at 5:9 and 6:1, which are not yet ingested. In
5:9 it answers an adjuration of the daughters of Jerusalem (5:8). That makes it possible
to assign the line here to the same voices, but the English wording differs, so it is no
proof. The role pages leave it open.

**The daughters get a page.** The daughters of Jerusalem are addressed at 1:5; 2:7; 3:5;
5:8, 16; and 8:4, and are referred to at 3:10. The daughters of Zion are summoned at
3:11. Most of these are refrains or exchanges that carry the book's structure. So, unlike
the opening's virgins and Friends, this collective gets a page:
[[The Daughters of Jerusalem]]. It records each address as it is ingested.

**Kedar gets a place page.** The name appears as a people and territory, with no act of
its own. As with [[Tema]] and [[Midian]], the place takes the bare page name. If the person
Kedar, son of Ishmael, ever needs a page, it must be `Kedar (son of Ishmael)`, following the
standing note on [[The Death of Abraham and the Line of Ishmael]].
