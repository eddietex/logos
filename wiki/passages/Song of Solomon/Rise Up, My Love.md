# Rise Up, My Love

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 2:8–17

## Summary

The woman hears him before she sees him: *"The voice of my beloved!"* (2:8). He comes leaping
over mountains and skipping over hills, like a roe or a young deer. Then he is close. He stands
behind *"our wall"*, looks in at the windows, and glances through the lattice (9). She is inside;
he is outside, looking for her.

She reports what he said, and the report fills the middle of the unit (10–13). *"Rise up, my
love, my beautiful one, and come away."* The reason is the season. Winter is past and the rain is
gone. Flowers appear, *"the time of the singing has come"*, and the turtledove is heard *"in our
land"*. The fig tree ripens its green figs, and the blossoming vines give off their fragrance. The
invitation is repeated word for word at the end, so the spring sits inside a frame of *"Arise …
and come away"*.

His voice goes on (14). He calls her *"my dove in the clefts of the rock"*, hidden on the
mountainside, and asks to see her face and hear her voice. The asking runs the other way from the
opening. She heard his voice first; now he wants to hear hers. Then a short line with a plural
voice: *"Catch for us the foxes, the little foxes that plunder the vineyards"*, because the
vineyards are in blossom (15).

The woman closes the unit. *"My beloved is mine, and I am his. He browses among the lilies"* (16).
She asks him to turn, until the day is cool and the shadows flee, and to be like a roe or a young
deer on the mountains of Bether (17).

The unit is built of movement and calls. He comes over the hills; he calls her out; she calls
him back to the hills. The spring gives the reason to come out now, which fits beside the refrain
of 2:7 just before it. That refrain said love must not be awakened before it desires. Here the
season itself says the time has come. Whether the poem means that as an answer to 2:7 is not
stated, and the wiki does not assume a story connecting the two units.

The unit also does not say whether she goes out. The invitation is given twice and never
answered by a narrated act. What she says instead is the statement of belonging (16) and a
summons of her own (17).

## Key verses

> **8** The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes, leaping on the mountains, skipping on the hills.

> **9** My beloved is like a roe or a young deer. Behold, he stands behind our wall! He looks in at the windows. He glances through the lattice.

> **10** My beloved spoke, and said to me, “Rise up, my love, my beautiful one, and come away.

> **11** For, behold, the winter is past. The rain is over and gone.

> **12** The flowers appear on the earth. The time of the singing has come, and the voice of the turtledove is heard in our land.

> **13** The fig tree ripens her green figs. The vines are in blossom. They give out their fragrance. Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away.”

> **14** My dove in the clefts of the rock, In the hiding places of the mountainside, Let me see your face. Let me hear your voice; for your voice is sweet, and your face is lovely.

> **15** Catch for us the foxes, the little foxes that plunder the vineyards; for our vineyards are in blossom.

> **16** My beloved is mine, and I am his. He browses among the lilies.

> **17** Until the day is cool, and the shadows flee away, turn, my beloved, and be like a roe or a young deer on the mountains of Bether.

## People

- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — the narrating voice (2:8–10a), the *"dove"* addressed
  (2:14), and the speaker of the belonging statement and the closing summons (2:16–17).
- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — the *"beloved"* who comes over the hills, looks through the
  lattice, and calls her out (2:8–14). His speech is reported by her; he browses among the lilies
  (2:16), the shepherd image of 1:7 again.
- *Us* (2:15) — the plural voice that asks for the foxes to be caught. It may be the woman
  speaking for both lovers, or a group. The text does not say.

## Places

- [[Bether]] — *"the mountains of Bether"* (2:17), where she sends him to roam. Whether *Bether*
  is a place name at all is uncertain; see Notes.
- *Our land* (2:12) and *our wall* (2:9) are unnamed. Neither is located.
- The mountains and hills of 2:8 and the rock and mountainside of 2:14 are unnamed.

## Themes

- [[Love]] — invitation, the reported speech of the beloved, and the statement of mutual
  belonging (2:10–16).
- [[Seeing and Being Seen]] — he looks in through the windows and the lattice (2:9); *"Let me see
  your face"* (2:14).
- [[Work]] — vineyards to protect from foxes (2:15); the beloved who *"browses among the lilies"*
  (2:16).

## Notes

**Boundary.** The unit runs from *"The voice of my beloved!"* (2:8) to the summons to the mountains
of Bether (2:17). 3:1 begins a new scene, at night on her bed, with a search. The planned boundary
held, and no box was redrawn. Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks section breaks
(*{ס}*) after 2:13, 2:14, and 2:17, so the Masoretic divisions fall inside the unit too. The wiki
keeps 2:8–17 together because the invitation, the dove, the foxes, and the reply belong to one
scene of calling.

**Who speaks.** The WEB gives no speaker cues here, but the text is clearer than in 1:9–2:7. The
woman says *"My beloved spoke, and said to me"* (2:10) and then quotes him, with the WEB's
quotation marks closing at the end of 2:13. In 2:14 the Hebrew suffixes are feminine,
*marʾayiḵ* and *qôlēḵ*, *your appearance*, *your voice* (Sefaria, *Miqra according to the
Masorah*), so the voice addressing the dove is his. In 2:17 the command *sōḇ* and *dəmēh-ləḵā*,
*turn, be like*, are masculine, so she is speaking to him. Who says 2:15 is not fixed by the forms:
*"for us"* (*lānû*) and *"our vineyards"* are plural.

**The roe and the young deer.** *Roe* (2:9, 17) renders *ṣəḇî*, and *young deer* renders *ʿōp̄er
hāʾayyālîm*, *the young of the deer* (Sefaria). These are the same animals by which the woman
adjured the daughters in 2:7 (*ṣəḇāʾôt*, *ʾaylôt*), so the creatures of the oath now picture the
beloved himself. 8:14, the Song's last verse, has the same Hebrew pair (Sefaria), though the WEB
there gives *"a gazelle or a young stag"*. The difference is in the English, not the Hebrew.

**Windows and lattice.** The windows of 2:9 are *ḥallōnôt*, and the lattice is *ḥărakkîm*. The
verbs are *mašgîaḥ*, *looking*, and *mēṣîṣ*, *glancing* or *peering* (Sefaria). This is not the
stock window vocabulary of Genesis 26:8, Proverbs 7:6, and Judges 5:28, which share *šāqap̄* and,
in two cases, *ʾešnāḇ* for *lattice*. The scene differs too: those watchers look out from inside;
here the watcher stands outside and looks in, and he is the lover, not a judge.

**"Come away."** Both invitations end *ûləḵî-lāḵ* (2:10, 13; Sefaria), the feminine counterpart of
the *lek-ləḵā* spoken to Abram at Genesis 12:1 and Genesis 22:2. The Masoretic text of 2:13 reads
*lāḵ* where the written consonants have *lḵy* (a *ketiv*/*qere* note in Sefaria's edition); the WEB's
English is the same either way. Some readers hear an echo of Abram's call in the invitation to leave
and go. The wiki records the shared idiom and does not claim the Song alludes to Genesis. A
reflexive *for yourself* after *go* is ordinary Hebrew, and nothing else in the unit points to
Abram.

**"The time of the singing."** The WEB's *singing* renders *zāmîr* (Sefaria). The root *z-m-r* is
also the verb for pruning a vineyard: *"you shall prune your vineyard six years"* (Leviticus 25:3,
*tizmōr*), and the judged vineyard of Isaiah 5:6 *"won't be pruned"* (*yizzāmēr*). So *ʿēt
hazzāmîr* can be read as *the time of pruning*, which fits the vines and figs of 2:13. *Singing*
fits the turtledove's voice in the same verse. The two readings are both in circulation; the wiki
follows the WEB and notes the other. The Hebrew of Leviticus 25:3–4 and Isaiah 5:6 was read in
Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah*; Leviticus 25:3–4 WEB was checked at bible-api.com.

**"Until the day is cool."** The Hebrew is *ʿaḏ šeyyāp̄ûaḥ hayyôm*, more literally *until the day
breathes*, and *the shadows flee* (Sefaria). The phrase has been taken both ways: the evening
breeze, when shadows lengthen and vanish into dark, or the dawn, when night's shadows flee. The WEB's
*cool* points to evening. With evening, *turn* asks him to stay or return until night ends the day;
with dawn, it asks him to spend the night with her and leave at morning. The text does not decide,
and the wiki adopts neither. The same clause opens 4:6 (stored WEB, not yet ingested).

**"Turn."** *Sōḇ* (2:17) can mean *turn back* to her or *turn away* toward the mountains. The
second half, *be like a roe … on the mountains of Bether*, can be an invitation to roam over her
(if the mountains are a figure for her body) or a sending-away. Readers divide on this, and the
page leaves it open.

**Bether.** The WEB treats *bāter* as a proper name, *"the mountains of Bether"*. The same root
*b-t-r* means *to cut in two*. At Genesis 15:10 Abram *wayəḇattēr*, *divided*, the animals and
laid each *biṯrô*, *its half*, opposite the other (Sefaria). So *hārê ḇāter* may be *the
mountains of division*, *cleft* or *rugged* mountains, rather than a named range. No location is
given, and the wiki makes no identification. The parallel in 8:14 is *hārê ḇəśāmîm*, *mountains of
spices* (Sefaria). See [[Bether]].

**Foxes.** *Šuʿālîm* (2:15; Sefaria) may be foxes or jackals; the WEB has foxes. The line is the
only one in the unit with a plural speaker and a practical request, and it is often read as a
figure for whatever threatens love while it is still in blossom. The text gives no explanation,
and the page does not supply one. It does show the vineyards in blossom, which repeats 2:13: the
same spring that makes the invitation makes the vineyard worth protecting.

**Pages decided.** [[Bether]] gets a place page under the [[Kedar]], [[En Gedi]], and [[Sharon]]
precedent for named places in images, with the uncertainty about the name stated on the page. The
plural *us* of 2:15 gets no page. No theme page is created for spring or the seasons. The
seasonal imagery belongs to this unit and is described here; a theme page can be added if it
recurs.
