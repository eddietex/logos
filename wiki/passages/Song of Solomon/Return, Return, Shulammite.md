# Return, Return, Shulammite

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 6:11–7:9

## Summary

A speaker goes down to a nut tree grove to look for budding vines and flowering
pomegranates, then describes an unanticipated movement among royal chariots (6:11–12).
Onlookers call the Shulammite back so that they may gaze at her; a reply questions their
wish to look at her as at the dance of Mahanaim (6:13). Praise then moves from her feet
in sandals upward through her body to her head and hair (7:1–5). Crafted jewels, produce,
fawns, towers, pools, and Carmel supply the comparisons; even a king is captive in her hair.
The closing lines turn admiration toward touch and taste: she is a palm tree whose fruit
the lover desires, her breath like apples and her mouth like wine (7:6–9).

The sequence continues the praise of [[My Dove Is Unique]], but changes its shape and
intensity. The grove speaker and the precise transitions between voices remain uncertain.
The poem supplies neither a continuous travel itinerary nor an identified royal household.

## Notes

**Boundary and voices.** The planned 6:11–7:9 unit is retained. The grove and summons lead
into sustained bodily praise; 7:10 begins a new declaration of belonging and invitation.
This is an editorial boundary, not a claim that the voices divide precisely at verse ends.
The stored WEB does not label the speaker of 6:11–12. Assigning that descent confidently
to either lover, or making 6:12 a report of abduction, goes beyond this source. Its royal
chariot wording is preserved without substituting a personal name from another translation.
At 6:13 the WEB's Lover label assigns the reply, but the callers remain unidentified.
At 7:9 the turn from your mouth to my beloved can be read as the woman taking up the wine
image. No speaker label marks that point in the stored text, so the handoff is possible,
not certain. The final reference to sleepers is retained without correcting it from memory.

**Shulammite and royalty.** Shulammite is treated as a designation of the existing woman,
not a second character or a recovered personal name. The poem does not explain it here.
No derivation from Solomon, identification with a named historical woman, or hometown is
asserted. Prince's daughter and the captive king belong to the royal register of the
praise; they do not establish her parentage or identify the man as Solomon. The king's
captivity is in her tresses, an image of attraction, not a narrated political imprisonment.

**The gaze and the dance.** The summons to return is repeated four times in 6:13. Its
purpose is to gaze at her; the reply puts that desire into question. The dance comparison
can suggest a performance, but does not supply its choreography, audience, or occasion.
The earlier refusal of the stare in 1:6 is not erased: the Song contains both a woman's
objection to being looked at and delighted contemplation of her beauty. Different voices
and contexts prevent either from becoming a blanket permission or prohibition.

**A body praised through the world.** Chapter 4 moved from eyes downward; 7:1–5 begins
with feet and rises to head and hair. Twin fawns recur, but the tower is now ivory and
the eyes are pools rather than doves. These are poetic valuations, not anatomical diagrams.
Named places enlarge the comparisons without placing the lovers in those places. The
craftsman's work at 7:1 praises skilled form; it does not narrate the creation of the woman.
The palm and fruit imagery in 7:7–8 expresses erotic desire explicitly, while the text
states an intention rather than narrating each desired action as accomplished.
