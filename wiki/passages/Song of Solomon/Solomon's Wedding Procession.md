# Solomon's Wedding Procession

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 3:6–11

## Summary

The scene changes without warning. Someone asks a question: *"Who is this who comes up from the
wilderness like pillars of smoke"* (3:6)? Whatever is coming is seen from far off, rising like smoke
and scented with myrrh, frankincense, and *"all spices of the merchant"*. The question is answered at
once: *"Behold, it is Solomon's carriage!"* (3:7).

Sixty mighty men surround it, *"of the mighty men of Israel"*. Each of them holds a sword, is trained
for war, and has his sword on his thigh, *"because of fear in the night"* (3:7–8). The guard is
there because the night is dangerous. The poem does not say what the danger is.

Then the carriage itself is described (3:9–10). King Solomon made it for himself from the wood of
Lebanon. Its pillars are silver, its base gold, its seat purple. Its inside is *"paved with love, from
the daughters of Jerusalem"*. That last phrase can be read in more than one way; see Notes.

The unit ends with a summons. *"Go out, you daughters of Zion, and see king Solomon"* (3:11). He wears
the crown his mother crowned him with *"in the day of his weddings, in the day of the gladness of his
heart"*. This is the only time the Song names a wedding. The bride is not named, described, or seen.

This is the first time Solomon is present in the Song rather than named in a heading (1:1) or in a
comparison (1:5). He makes a carriage and wears a crown, but he does not speak. Neither lover speaks
either. The unit has no *I* and no *you* except the daughters of Zion, who are told to look. Who
asks and answers the opening question is not said.

The unit stands between the two halves of the opening of the Song. Before it, two scenes of the woman
alone with or without her beloved (2:8–3:5); after it, the man's long praise of her body and his
invitation to *"my bride"* (4:1–5:1, not yet ingested). Whether the procession is the lovers' wedding,
someone else's, or a picture of royal splendor set beside the couple's love is the main question
readers ask of it. The poem does not answer it.

## Key verses

> **6** Who is this who comes up from the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, with all spices of the merchant?

> **7** Behold, it is Solomon’s carriage! Sixty mighty men are around it, of the mighty men of Israel.

> **8** They all handle the sword, and are expert in war. Every man has his sword on his thigh, because of fear in the night.

> **9** King Solomon made himself a carriage of the wood of Lebanon.

> **10** He made its pillars of silver, its bottom of gold, its seat of purple, the middle of it being paved with love, from the daughters of Jerusalem.

> **11** Go out, you daughters of Zion, and see king Solomon, with the crown with which his mother has crowned him, in the day of his weddings, in the day of the gladness of his heart.

## People

  crowned on his wedding day (3:11). Present, but silent.
- [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] — named in 3:10 as the source of the love the carriage is *"paved
  with"*; the *"daughters of Zion"* summoned in 3:11 are probably the same group. See Notes.
- Solomon's mother (3:11) — she crowned him. She is not named. 1 Kings names Solomon's mother as
  Bathsheba, but the Song does not. No page; see Notes.
- The sixty mighty men (3:7–8) — the armed escort. No page.
- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] and [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — neither speaks in the unit, and
  neither is clearly present. Whether either is part of the procession is the open question of the
  passage.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The unit runs from the new question at 3:6 to the summons of 3:11. 3:5 closes the
previous unit with the refrain, and 4:1 begins the man's direct praise, *"Behold, you are
beautiful, my love"*. Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks a section break (*{ס}*) after
3:5, after 3:8, and after 3:11. So the Masoretic text divides this unit in two: the approach and
escort (3:6–8), and the carriage and the summons (3:9–11). The wiki keeps them together because the
second half describes the carriage the first half announces. The planned boundary held, and no box
was redrawn.

**"Who is this?"** The Hebrew is *mî zōʾt ʿōlâ min-hammidbār* (3:6; Sefaria). *Zōʾt*, *this*, and
*ʿōlâ*, *coming up*, are feminine. The WEB's *"Who is this"* does not show the gender. Two readings
follow from it. The word for the carriage in 3:7, *miṭṭâ*, is also feminine, so *this* may be the
carriage, seen from far off before anyone knows what it is. Or *this* may be a woman, perhaps a bride
carried in the carriage, as at 8:5, where the same four Hebrew words are followed by *"leaning on her
beloved"*. The wiki records both readings. The text, as stored, does not settle it. See
[[Who Is This Coming Up from the Wilderness - Song of Solomon 3.6, 6.10, and 8.5]].

**"Pillars of smoke."** The Hebrew is *kǝṯîmǝrôṯ ʿāšān* (3:6; Sefaria). *Tîmǝrôṯ* is not the usual
word for *pillar* (*ʿammûḏ*, used of the pillar of cloud at Exodus 13:21 and of the carriage's
*"pillars"* at 3:10). The same phrase, *wǝṯîmǝrôṯ ʿāšān*, appears at Joel 3:3 in the Hebrew numbering
(Joel 2:30 in English): *"blood, fire, and pillars of smoke"* (Sefaria; WEB checked at
bible-api.com). It is commonly said that these are the only two uses of the word in the Hebrew
Bible. This page has not verified that by a concordance search. The usual explanation is that it
is related to *tāmār*, *palm tree*, so it pictures smoke rising in columns like palm trunks. That
etymology is a lexical proposal, not something this page has checked. See
[[Pillars of Smoke - Song of Solomon 3.6, Joel 2.30, and the Wilderness Pillar]].

**Perfumed.** *Mǝquṭṭereṯ*, *perfumed* (3:6; Sefaria), is from the root *q-ṭ-r*, the root of
*qǝṭōreṯ*, *incense*, the word used of the altar incense in Exodus 30:1 (*qǝṭōreṯ*, Sefaria). The verb means *made to smoke*
or *made fragrant with smoke*. So the smoke and the perfume belong together: the rising columns are
fragrant. The two named spices, *mōr* and *lǝḇônâ*, myrrh and frankincense, are both in the holy
incense and anointing oil of Exodus 30 (myrrh at 30:23, frankincense at 30:34; stored WEB). The
Song uses them for a king's carriage, not for worship, and the wiki does not draw a cultic meaning
from the vocabulary.

**Frankincense, Lebanon, and the moon.** *Lǝḇônâ*, frankincense (3:6), *lǝḇānôn*, Lebanon (3:9),
and *lǝḇānâ*, *the moon* (6:10, *"beautiful as the moon"*), all have the consonants *l-b-n*, the root
for *white* (Sefaria for the spellings). Readers often see wordplay among them across the Song. The
wiki notes the shared letters. It does not claim the poet intended a link.

**The carriage.** Two different Hebrew words lie behind the WEB's one English word. In 3:7 it is
*miṭṭâ* (Sefaria), the ordinary word for a *bed* or *couch*; here it is probably a litter carried by
bearers. In 3:9 it is *ʾappiryôn* (Sefaria), a word found only here in the Hebrew Bible, as is
commonly stated (not verified by this page). Many scholars suggest it is a loanword, perhaps Persian
or Greek (compare Greek *phoreion*, *litter*). That proposal is scholarly opinion, and this page does
not depend on it. The wiki does not decide whether the *miṭṭâ* and the *ʾappiryôn* are one object or
two. The simplest reading is one carriage, first seen moving (3:7) and then described (3:9–10).

**"Solomon's."** *Miṭṭāṯô šellišlōmōh*, literally *his litter, that which is Solomon's* (3:7;
Sefaria). The relative *še-* matches the Song's heading, *ʾăšer lišlōmōh* (1:1), in meaning, but
the heading uses *ʾăšer*. 3:7, 9, and 11 all name Solomon.

**The guard.** *Šiššîm gibbōrîm*, sixty mighty men, *miggibbōrê yiśrāʾēl* (3:7; Sefaria). *Gibbôr*,
*mighty man*, is the word for David's heroes: *"the mighty men whom David had"* (2 Samuel 23:8;
WEB checked at bible-api.com; Hebrew *haggibbōrîm*, Sefaria). Each
of them is *ʾăḥuzê ḥereḇ*, literally *held of the sword* (3:8). This is the same verb, *ʾ-ḥ-z*, as
*ʾăḥaztîw*, *I held him*, in 3:4 (Sefaria). The guard is *mippaḥaḏ ballêlôṯ*, *because of fear in
the nights* (3:8), the same plural *ballêlôṯ* as the woman's *"By night"* in 3:1. So two words of
the night search return in the procession. The wiki notes the repetition and does not make it a
plot.

**The carriage's materials.** Cedar is not named; the WEB's *"wood of Lebanon"* (3:9) translates
*ʿăṣê hallǝḇānôn*, *trees of Lebanon* (Sefaria). Lebanon timber is what 1 Kings says Solomon used for
the temple and his palaces: *"command that cedar trees be cut for me out of Lebanon"* (1 Kings 5:6),
and *"the House of the Forest of Lebanon"* (1 Kings 7:2; both WEB checked at bible-api.com, 1 Kings
not fetched). *ʾArgāmān*, purple (3:10; Sefaria), is a costly dye. It is used for the tabernacle's
curtains and veil in Exodus 26:1, 31 (stored WEB). Silver, gold, and purple make the carriage a
display of royal wealth.

**"Paved with love, from the daughters of Jerusalem."** The Hebrew is *tôḵô rāṣûp̄ ʾahăḇâ mibbǝnôṯ
yǝrûšālāim* (3:10; Sefaria). *Rāṣûp̄* is *inlaid* or *fitted*, as with a pavement. There are at least
three readings. (1) The daughters of Jerusalem made the inlay out of love, as a gift. (2) The inside
is decorated with scenes or images of love, made by the daughters. (3) *ʾahăḇâ*, *love*, is a
misreading of a similar word for a material (often suggested: *ʾaḇānîm*, *stones*, or a word for
leather). That last reading is a textual emendation; the Hebrew text as read here says *ʾahăḇâ*. The
wiki follows the text and leaves the first two readings open.

**The daughters of Zion.** *Bǝnôṯ ṣiyyôn* (3:11; Sefaria) appears only here in the Song. The phrase
stands in parallel with *"the daughters of Jerusalem"* in 3:10, as Zion stands in parallel with
Jerusalem in later poetry. The wiki treats them as probably the same group, so they are recorded on
[[The Daughters of Jerusalem]], whose page already lists *daughters of Zion* as another name. It is a
reading, and it is marked as one on that page. The command is a double imperative, *ṣǝʾênâ ûrǝʾênâ*,
*go out and see* (Sefaria), both feminine plural.

**The crown and his mother.** *Bāʿăṭārâ šeʿiṭṭǝrâ-lô ʾimmô*, *with the crown with which his mother
crowned him* (3:11; Sefaria). The noun and the verb are from the same root, *ʿ-ṭ-r*. The Song does not
name his mother. 2 Samuel 12:24 names Solomon's mother as Bathsheba, as does 1 Kings 2:19, where he
sets a throne for her at his right hand (WEB checked at bible-api.com; Samuel and Kings not fetched).
No narrative of Kings describes his mother crowning him; in 1 Kings 1:39, *"Zadok the priest … anointed
Solomon"* (WEB, bible-api.com). So the crown of 3:11 is likely a wedding garland or crown rather
than the royal crown of his accession. The wiki does not connect the verse to any recorded event, and
Bathsheba has no page.

**"The day of his weddings."** The Hebrew is singular: *bǝyôm ḥăṯunnāṯô*, *in the day of his wedding*
(3:11; Sefaria). The WEB's plural *weddings* is the translation's choice and does not mean several
weddings. *Ḥăṯunnâ* is from the root of *ḥāṯān*, *bridegroom*. The word is commonly said to appear
only here in the Hebrew Bible; this page has not verified that by a concordance search. The second
phrase, *bǝyôm śimḥaṯ libbô*, *in the day of the gladness of his heart*, uses *śimḥâ*, the ordinary
word for joy.

**Is this the lovers' wedding?** The man is called *king* at 1:4 and 1:12, and the next unit (4:8–5:1)
calls the woman *"my bride"*, so many readers take 3:6–11 as the wedding of the two lovers, with the
man as Solomon or playing the part of Solomon. Others read it as a contrast: the king's splendor
against the shepherd lover of 1:7. Others read it as a separate wedding song placed here in an
anthology. The wiki has not equated the man with Solomon before this unit, and it does not do so now.
The unit names Solomon and a wedding, but it never names a bride and never uses the man's or the
woman's voice.

**Pages decided.** New place pages for [[Lebanon]] (it recurs at 4:8, 11, 15; 5:15; 7:4) and for
[[Zion]] (it will recur through the Psalms and the Prophets). No page for the sixty mighty men, the

Bathsheba's page will be made when Samuel is ingested. The daughters of Zion are filed under
