# Love Is Strong as Death

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 8:5–7

## Summary

An unidentified voice asks about a woman coming up from the wilderness, leaning on
her beloved. An intimate address recalls awakening under an apple tree, at the place
of the addressee's conception and birth. The request to be set as a seal on heart and
arm opens a declaration of love's power: strong as death, fierce in its jealousy,
fiery, resistant to floods, and beyond the purchase of an entire household's wealth.

## Key verses

Complete pericope, copied from the stored WEB:

> **8:5** Who is this who comes up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved? Under the apple tree I aroused you. There your mother conceived you. There she was in labor and bore you.

> **8:6** Set me as a seal on your heart, as a seal on your arm; for love is strong as death. Jealousy is as cruel as Sheol. Its flashes are flashes of fire, a very flame of Yahweh.

> **8:7** Many waters can’t quench love, neither can floods drown it. If a man would give all the wealth of his house for love, he would be utterly scorned.

- [[Love]] — the desire for enduring attachment and love's unquenchable power.
- [[Life and Death]] — death supplies love's measure of strength, not an enemy defeated here.
- [[Envy]] — this existing theme also tracks jealousy; the poem stresses its fierce force.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — total household wealth cannot purchase love.

## Notes

**Seal and attachment.** The request places the speaker on the beloved's heart and arm
as a seal. It expresses a desire for lasting closeness and recognition. The image
alone does not establish a wedding rite, a legal transfer, or a literal tattoo.

**Strong as death.** The comparison says as strong, not stronger. It does not narrate
resurrection or promise that lovers cannot die. Likewise the fire belongs to the
love-and-jealousy comparison, not to a description of fire burning inside Sheol.

**Jealousy and money.** The force celebrated here is not a license for possessive
violence. Proverbs 6:30–35 places jealousy beside a husband's refusal of compensation;
this poem places it beside love that cannot be bought. The contexts remain distinct.
The scorn in 8:7 falls on an attempted purchase, not on poverty or ordinary gifts.

**Yahweh in the WEB.** The stored translation reads “a very flame of Yahweh.” This
page follows that rendering; it does not decide the Hebrew expression's derivation
or alternative translation. Naming Yahweh in a simile does not make him a lover in
the poem or establish an allegorical identification.

**Speakers and sequence.** The question in 8:5 recalls 3:6, but leaning on the beloved
replaces the earlier royal display. Assigning the following address to the woman
is a reading, not an explicit WEB speaker label. Birth imagery anchors intimacy in
a remembered place without establishing the lovers' literal birth histories. The
8:5–7 boundary is retained; the family exchange beginning at 8:8 remains queued.
