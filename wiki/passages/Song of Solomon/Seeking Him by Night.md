# Seeking Him by Night

**Book:** [[Song of Solomon]]
**Reference:** Song of Solomon 3:1–5

## Summary

The woman is alone, at night, on her bed. She looks for the one she loves and does not find him
(3:1). The previous unit ended with her sending him off to the mountains, or calling him back,
*"until the day is cool, and the shadows flee away"* (2:17). Now it is night, and he is not there.
The poem does not say how the two scenes are related, and the wiki does not supply a story to join
them.

She decides to get up and go through the city, through its streets and squares, to look for him.
The verse ends exactly as the one before it did: *"I sought him, but I didn't find him"* (3:2). The
watchmen who go around the city find her instead. She asks them, *"Have you seen him whom my soul
loves?"* (3:3). They do not answer, or the poem does not report it. The search ends with them.

Then, almost at once, she finds him. *"I had scarcely passed from them"* (3:4). Nothing is said about
where he was or what he says. She holds him and will not let go until she has brought him into her
mother's house, *"into the room of her who conceived me"*. The finding is told in a single clause;
the holding takes the rest of the verse.

The unit closes with the refrain from 2:7, word for word in the WEB and in the Hebrew: the daughters
of Jerusalem are put under oath, by the roes and the hinds of the field, not to stir up or awaken
love *"until it so desires"* (3:5). As at 2:7, it follows an embrace.

The unit is built on one phrase. *"Him whom my soul loves"* is said four times in four verses (3:1,
2, 3, 4), and each time the verb around it changes: *sought*, *will seek*, *have you seen*,
*found*. The phrase first appeared at 1:7, where she asked *"you whom my soul loves"* where he
grazes his flock. Here she never names him, describes him, or calls him *beloved*. He is defined
only by her love for him.

The woman speaks the whole unit. She is the one who lies awake, gets up, searches, asks, finds,
holds, and brings him home. The man does not speak and does nothing that is narrated. In the
previous unit he came looking for her; here she goes looking for him.

## Key verses

> **1** By night on my bed, I sought him whom my soul loves. I sought him, but I didn’t find him.

> **2** I will get up now, and go about the city; in the streets and in the squares I will seek him whom my soul loves. I sought him, but I didn’t find him.

> **3** The watchmen who go about the city found me; “Have you seen him whom my soul loves?”

> **4** I had scarcely passed from them, when I found him whom my soul loves. I held him, and would not let him go, until I had brought him into my mother’s house, into the room of her who conceived me.

> **5** I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, or by the hinds of the field, that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires.

## People

- [[The Woman (Song of Solomon)]] — the only speaker. She searches, asks the watchmen, finds him,
  and brings him to her mother's house (3:1–4), then adjures the daughters (3:5).
- [[The Man (Song of Solomon)]] — *"him whom my soul loves"*, sought and found (3:1–4). He is
  silent and passive throughout.
- [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] — adjured a second time, in the same words as 2:7 (3:5).
- [[The Watchmen (Song of Solomon)]] (3:3) — *"who go about the city"*. They find her, and she
  questions them. No reply is recorded. Their page was created at 5:2–8; see Notes.
- Her mother (3:4) — named only as the owner of the house and *"her who conceived me"*. No page.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The unit runs from her bed at night (3:1) to the refrain (3:5). 3:6 opens a new scene
with a new question, *"Who is this who comes up from the wilderness"*, and Solomon's carriage. The
planned boundary held, and no box was redrawn. Sefaria's *Miqra according to the Masorah* marks a
section break (*{ס}*) after 2:17 and after 3:5 and none inside 3:1–5, so the Masoretic division
matches the wiki's.

**Who speaks.** The whole unit is first person, and nothing in it suggests a change of voice. In
3:1–4 the beloved is referred to in the third person masculine: *biqqaštîw*, *I sought him*;
*mǝṣāʾtîw*, *I found him*; *ʾăḥaztîw*, *I held him* (Sefaria, *Miqra according to the Masorah*).
The speaker who brings him into *"my mother's house"* is the woman.

**"By night."** The Hebrew is *ballêlôt*, a plural, *in the nights* (Sefaria). The WEB's *By night*
is a fair English rendering. The plural can be read as *night after night*, a search that is
repeated, or simply as poetic usage for *at night*. The text does not decide, and neither does
the wiki.

**Dream or event.** Many readers take 3:1–4 as a dream, because it begins on her bed at night, the
search ends with improbable ease, and the parallel at 5:2 begins *"I was asleep, but my heart was
awake"*. Others read it as a real search, or as a poem that is neither. The unit does not say she
slept or woke. The page reports the reading and does not adopt it.

**"Him whom my soul loves."** The Hebrew is *ʾēt šeʾāhăḇâ napšî* each time (3:1, 2, 3, 4; Sefaria),
with the relative particle *še-* rather than *ʾăšer*. *Napšî* is *my soul*, or *my life*, *my
self*: the whole person, not a part of her. The same words without *ʾēt* address him at 1:7
(Sefaria). In this unit the phrase takes the place of a name and of *dôḏî*, *my beloved*, which she
uses throughout 2:8–17.

**Going about.** The verbs of movement share a root. She says *ʾăsôḇǝḇâ ḇāʿîr*, *I will go about
the city* (3:2), and the watchmen are *hassōḇǝḇîm bāʿîr*, *who go about the city* (3:3). Both are
forms of *s-b-b*, *to go around* (Sefaria). So she and the watchmen are doing the same thing in the
same streets, and it is they who find her. The same root gave *sōḇ*, *turn*, in her last word to
him at 2:17. The wiki notes the repetition of the root; it does not claim the poet meant the link
with 2:17.

**Streets and squares.** *Šǝwāqîm* and *rǝḥōḇôt* (3:2; Sefaria). *Šûq* is a rare word. It is used
at Proverbs 7:8, of the young man *"passing through the street"* to the forbidden woman's house, and
at Ecclesiastes 12:4–5, where the doors are shut *"in the street"* and the mourners *"go about the
streets"*, *wǝsāḇǝḇû baššûq*, the same verb as here (Sefaria). How many times *šûq* occurs in all
is a standard observation (usually four: Proverbs 7:8, Ecclesiastes 12:4, 12:5, Song 3:2) that this
page has not verified by a full concordance search. *Rǝḥōḇôt* is common; it is the *squares* of
Proverbs 1:20 and 7:12. See
[[Seeking in the Streets and Squares - Song of Solomon 3.1-4 and Proverbs 1, 7, and 8]].

**The watchmen.** *Haššōmǝrîm* (3:3; Sefaria), *the ones who keep watch*. They return at 5:7, in
the same words, and there they beat her and take her cloak. Here they do nothing but find her. She
asks them a question without any greeting, and the poem gives no answer. Whether that silence
means they did not know, did not care, or simply did not matter to the story is not said. The
watchmen get no page for now, under the same rule that gave [[The Daughters of Jerusalem]] one
only because they recur and act. The decision was reconsidered at 5:2–8, when they act again, and
they now have a page: [[The Watchmen (Song of Solomon)]]. See [[I Opened, but He Had Gone]].

**Holding on.** *ʾĂḥaztîw wǝlōʾ ʾarpennû*, *I held him and would not let him go* (3:4; Sefaria). The
second verb is from *r-p-h*, *to let drop*, *to let go slack*. The force of the verse is in the
holding. He was hard to find, and she does not intend to lose him again.

**"My mother's house."** *Bêt ʾimmî*, and *ḥeder hôrātî*, *the room of the one who conceived me*
(3:4; Sefaria). *Hôrātî* is from *h-r-h*, *to conceive*. In Israel's ordinary usage a family
household is a *father's house*. A *mother's house* is rare, and each case the wiki has checked
belongs to a woman's speech or a woman's story (the list is not from a full concordance search):
Rebekah runs to *"her mother's house"* (Genesis 24:28), Naomi sends her
daughters-in-law back each *"to her mother's house"* (Ruth 1:8), and the Song uses it twice (3:4;
8:2). Isaac brings Rebekah into *"his mother Sarah's tent"* (Genesis 24:67), the nearest
counterpart from a man's side. The wiki does not decide what the mother's house means here: a
literal family home, a place of marriage arrangements, or the place of her own origin, which the
second phrase points to. See
[[Into My Mother's House - Song of Solomon 3.4, 8.2, Genesis 24, and Ruth 1.8]].

**The refrain.** The Hebrew of 3:5 is identical to 2:7, letter for letter, including the Masoretic
section mark after it (Sefaria). The refrain returns a third time at 8:4 without the roes and the
hinds and with *mah*, *why*, in place of *ʾim*, *that … not*, so it reads *why would you stir up …?*
(Sefaria; stored WEB 8:4 renders it *"that you not stir up"*). Another adjuration at 5:8 asks the
daughters something else entirely: to tell him she is faint with love. Here, as at 2:7, the refrain
follows a scene in which the lovers are together. Whether it marks the end of a cycle, or warns
against the very haste the search showed, is not said.

**Pages decided.** No page for the watchmen yet (see above). No page for her mother, who is named
only as the owner of the house and the one who bore her; the same applies at 8:2 and 8:5 unless a
later unit gives her more. No theme page for seeking or searching. [[Light and Darkness]] and
[[City and Civilization]] are existing themes that take this unit as a short entry. The unnamed
city is not identified with Jerusalem.
