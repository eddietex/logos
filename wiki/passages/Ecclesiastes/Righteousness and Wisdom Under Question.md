# Righteousness and Wisdom Under Question

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 7:15–29

## Summary

The Preacher returns to first-person report: in his days of vanity he has seen a righteous
man perish in his righteousness and a wicked man live long in his evildoing (15). From that
observation he draws counsel in both directions. Do not be overly righteous or make yourself
overly wise — why destroy yourself? Do not be too wicked or foolish — why die before your
time? (16–17). It is good to take hold of the one without withdrawing the hand from the
other, *"for he who fears God will come out of them all"* (18).

Wisdom is a strength to the wise more than ten rulers in a city (19), yet there is not a
righteous man on earth who does good and does not sin (20). The counsel turns practical: do
not take heed of every word spoken, or you will hear your servant curse you, since your own
heart knows you have cursed others (21–22).

Then the inquiry turns on itself. The Preacher has proved all this in wisdom; he resolved to
be wise, but wisdom was far from him. What is, is far off and exceedingly deep: who can find
it out? (23–24). He turned to seek wisdom and the scheme of things, and to know wickedness as
stupidity and foolishness as madness (25). What he finds is bitter: more bitter than death is
the woman whose heart is snares and traps and whose hands are chains; the one who pleases God
escapes her, the sinner is caught (26). Counting one thing to another to find the scheme, he
has found one man among a thousand but not a woman among all those (27–28). One finding he
offers without qualification: God made man upright, but they search for many schemes (29).

## Themes

- [[Righteousness]] — the righteous man who perishes (15), the warning against being
  overly righteous (16), and the denial that anyone righteous does good without sin (20).
- [[Retribution]] — the observation of 7:15, which reverses expected outcomes.
- [[Wisdom]] — overly wise (16), a strength more than ten rulers (19), resolved upon and far
  off (23–24), sought with the scheme of things (25).
- [[Folly]] — foolishness that brings death before one's time (17); wickedness as stupidity,
  foolishness as madness (25).
- [[Sin]] — no one who does good without sin (20); the sinner ensnared (26); many schemes (29).
- [[The Fear of God]] — the one who fears God comes out of them all (18).
- [[Language and Speech]] — words not to take to heart; the servant's curse and one's own (21–22).
- [[Sexual Sin]] — the woman whose heart is snares and traps (26), read against Proverbs'
  adulteress; see the note below on what the verse does and does not say.
- [[Image of God]] — God made man upright (29), set beside the making of humanity in Genesis 1.
- [[Life and Death]] — the righteous who perish, the wicked who live long, dying before one's
  time (15, 17); a woman more bitter than death (26).
- [[Vanity]] — *"my days of vanity"* (15).

## Notes

**Boundary.** Retain the planned 7:15–29. 7:15's *"All this I have seen in my days of
vanity"* opens a first-person report after the *better than* sayings of 7:1–14, and the
closing quotation mark at 7:29 ends the speech the Preacher is credited with at 7:27. 8:1
opens a new question — *"Who is like the wise man?"* — and turns to the king's command.

**What 7:16–17 does and does not counsel.** Verse 16 warns against being *overly* righteous
and *overly* wise, and verse 17 against being *too* wicked or foolish. Read side by side, they
can sound like a counsel of moderation between virtue and vice. The text does not say that,
and the wiki does not supply it: verse 17 does not commend a little wickedness, verse 20 says
no one escapes sin anyway, and verse 18 grounds the counsel in the fear of God, not in a
mean. What *overly* righteous means is not defined. Readings include self-righteousness (a
claim to righteousness beyond the truth, compare Job 32:1–2), excessive rigor, or
righteousness pursued as a guarantee against the outcome of 7:15. Each is interpretation;
the verse supplies only the warning and its question, *"Why should you destroy yourself?"*

**The observation of 7:15 is not withdrawn.** The Preacher does not explain the righteous
man's perishing or the wicked man's long life, and nothing in 7:16–29 reverses it. It stands
beside 3:17's affirmation that God will judge the righteous and the wicked, and ahead of
8:12–14, where the tension will be stated again. The wiki keeps both claims.

**Wisdom's strength and wisdom's distance.** 7:19 credits wisdom with more strength than ten
rulers; 7:23–24 reports that the wisdom he resolved upon was far from him. The two are not
contradictory — the first is about practical advantage, the second about comprehending *that
which is* — but the passage sets them close together without comment.

**The woman of 7:26 and the count of 7:28.** These verses are the hardest in the unit.
What the text says: the Preacher finds *more bitter than death* a particular woman — one
*"whose heart is snares and traps, whose hands are chains"* — whom the one who pleases God
escapes (26). He then says he found one man among a thousand but not a woman among all those
(28). What the text does not say: verse 26 does not call all women snares; the relative
clause defines which woman is meant, and its language of snares, bitterness, death, and the
sinner caught matches Proverbs' adulteress (5:3–5; 7:22–27; 22:14). Verse 28 does not say
what was being sought in the man or woman; the immediate object of the search is *the scheme*
(27). Verse 29 then attributes the upright making and the many schemes to *man* without
dividing by sex, and the Hebrew noun there is *hā'ādām* with a plural verb following
(*they search*). Readings of 7:28 range from a statement about the Preacher's own limited
experience, to a reflection of a royal court, to a quotation he goes on to qualify by 7:29.
The text does not settle which. The wiki records the verse as written and does not soften it
or generalize it.

**Source language, verified.** The Hebrew of Ecclesiastes 7:25–29 was read from Sefaria's
*Miqra according to the Masorah*, 2026-10-27. It shows *ḥešbôn* (WEB *scheme*) at 25 and 27
and the plural *ḥiššəbōnôt* (WEB *schemes*) at 29, visibly from the same consonants; the word
the Preacher seeks is related to the word for what humanity has gone after. *'ādām* (WEB
*man*) stands at 28 and *hā'ādām* at 29. The verb attributing 7:27 to the Preacher is
printed *'āmərāh*, a form usually read as feminine; this is commonly noted, and some
interpreters redivide the letters to match the masculine *'āmar haqqōhelet* of 12:8. This
page makes no claim about which is original and does not build on the form. Genesis 1:27's
*'et-hā'ādām* was read from the same edition, 2026-10-27.

**Upright making, many schemes.** 7:29 is the only finding the Preacher presents without
qualification (*"I have only found this"*). It places the source of the crooked in human
searching, not in God's making; yet 7:13 has just said God made the crooked. The two verses
are kept side by side: 7:13 speaks of what no one can straighten in God's work; 7:29 of what
humanity has done with an upright making. Neither verse explains how the two relate.
