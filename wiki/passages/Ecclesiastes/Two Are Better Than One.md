# Two Are Better Than One

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 4:4–12

## Summary

The Preacher looks at all labor and achievement and finds that it is the envy of a man's
neighbor (4). Rivalry, not need or delight, is named as what drives the work he observes, and
the verdict is vanity and a striving after wind. Two short sayings then mark out the extremes.
The fool folds his hands and ruins himself (5): refusing work is no escape. A handful with
quietness is better than two handfuls with labor and chasing after wind (6): more labor
bought at the cost of rest is not the better portion.

He turns again (7) to a single case. A man is alone, without son or brother, yet his labor has
no end and his eyes are not satisfied with wealth (8). Only now does the man ask the question
that should have stopped him: *"For whom then, do I labor, and deprive my soul of enjoyment?"*
The Preacher calls this vanity and a miserable business.

The answer to solitary toil is companionship. Two are better than one because they have a good
reward for their labor (9). Three plain images follow: one lifts the other who falls, while the
one who falls alone has no one to lift him (10); two lying together keep warm (11); and two can
withstand an attacker who would overpower one (12). The unit closes with a proverb that goes
one step beyond its own count: a threefold cord is not quickly broken.

## Key verses

All nine verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:4–12.

> **4** Then I saw all the labor and achievement that is the envy of a man’s neighbor. This also is vanity and a striving after wind.

> **5** The fool folds his hands together and ruins himself.

> **6** Better is a handful, with quietness, than two handfuls with labor and chasing after wind.

> **7** Then I returned and saw vanity under the sun.

> **8** There is one who is alone, and he has neither son nor brother. There is no end to all of his labor, neither are his eyes satisfied with wealth. “For whom then, do I labor, and deprive my soul of enjoyment?” This also is vanity. Yes, it is a miserable business.

> **9** Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labor.

> **10** For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow; but woe to him who is alone when he falls, and doesn’t have another to lift him up.

> **11** Again, if two lie together, then they have warmth; but how can one keep warm alone?

> **12** If a man prevails against one who is alone, two shall withstand him; and a threefold cord is not quickly broken.

## People

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — the observer who reports the envy, the solitary laborer,
  and the advantages of two.

The neighbor, the fool, the man alone, and the two companions are types, not named people. God
is not mentioned in these nine verses.

## Places

None named. *Under the sun* (7) gives the scope of the observation, not a location.

## Themes

- [[Work]] — labor driven by rivalry, refused by the fool, and multiplied without end by the man alone.
- [[Envy]] — named as what all labor and achievement amount to in 4:4.
- [[Vanity]] — pronounced over envious labor (4) and solitary accumulation (7–8).
- [[Folly]] — the fool who folds his hands and ruins himself (5).
- [[Wisdom]] — the handful with quietness ranked above two handfuls with labor (6).
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — eyes not satisfied with wealth, and no heir to receive it (8).
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — the laborer who deprives his soul of enjoyment (8).
- [[Brotherhood]] — neither son nor brother (8); the companion who lifts his fellow (10).

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 4:4–12 unit is retained. Verse 4 opens a new observation after the
comparison of the living and the dead (4:1–3), and verses 4–12 move from labor driven by
rivalry, through labor without anyone to share it, to the advantage of two. Verse 13 begins a
separate comparison of a poor wise youth and an old foolish king, left for the next pericope.

**Envy as motive.** Verse 4 is a sweeping statement — *all* the labor and achievement — and the
wiki keeps its scope rather than softening it to "some labor." It is still an observation under
the sun, not a command against skill, and verse 5 at once rules out idleness as the
alternative. The Hebrew noun in 4:4 is *qinʾaṯ*, from the root used for jealousy and envy
elsewhere in the wiki (see [[Envy]]); Hebrew read from Mechon-Mamre's Masoretic text,
Ecclesiastes 4, on 2026-10-27 (`https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt3104.htm`).

**The fool's folded hands.** The WEB's *"ruins himself"* (5) renders a Hebrew phrase that reads
literally *"eats his own flesh"* (*ʾōḵēl ʾeṯ-bəśārô*), in the same Mechon-Mamre text. The image
is self-consumption: the idle man lives off himself until nothing is left. The verb for folding
the hands is the one Proverbs uses of the sluggard (6:10; 24:33); see the connection page.
Verse 5 speaks of self-inflicted ruin and says nothing about poverty in general; 4:1 has just
shown people powerless because of their oppressors, and the two cases are not to be merged.

**Two sayings in tension.** Verse 5 condemns too little work; verse 6 cautions against too much.
The handful *with quietness* is the middle the two sayings define. Verse 6 resembles the
better-than sayings of Proverbs (15:16–17; 17:1), but its contrasting term is the Preacher's own
refrain — *"chasing after wind"* — not the fear of Yahweh, love, or the absence of strife.

**The man alone.** The text notes his lack of son and brother first, and his endless labor
second; the question *"For whom then, do I labor?"* links the two. The WEB punctuates the
question as the man's own words; the Preacher then supplies the verdict. Unlike 2:18–21, where
the anxiety is that an unknown successor may be a fool, here there is no successor at all. The
Hebrew of 4:8 reads *lōʾ-ṯiśbaʿ ʿōšer*, *is not satisfied with wealth*, the verb of 1:8 and
Proverbs 27:20; the Mechon-Mamre text records a written *ʿênāyw* (his eyes) read as *ʿênô* (his
eye). The WEB follows the plural.

**Two, not specifically a married couple.** Verse 11's lying together for warmth is often read
of husband and wife, but the unit never names marriage: verse 8's lack is son and brother,
verse 10 speaks of a *fellow* (*ḥăḇērô*, companion), and verse 12 of withstanding an attacker,
which suits travelling companions or allies. The wiki therefore files the passage under
companionship and [[Brotherhood]] and does not add it to [[Marriage]]. Genesis 2:18's *"not
good for the man to be alone"* is a real comparison, but it is not cited here and its context is
the making of the woman; see the connection page for both sides.

**The threefold cord.** Verse 12 counts one, then two, then three. The text does not say who or
what the third strand is. Readings that make it God, or a husband and wife with God, go beyond
anything verses 4–12 state; the Hebrew phrase is *haḥûṭ haməšullāš*, *the thread that is
tripled* (same Mechon-Mamre text), a proverbial image of strength through multiplication. The
wiki records the later readings as interpretation, not as the passage's claim.

**Enjoyment again.** The man who deprives his soul of enjoyment (8) is the negative image of the
commendation in 2:24 and 3:12–13, 22. Read beside 4:1–3, the passage does not return to the
preference for the dead; it finds a concrete good among the living — another person to lift,
warm, and defend.
