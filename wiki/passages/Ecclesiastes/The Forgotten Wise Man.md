# The Forgotten Wise Man

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 9:13–10:4

## Summary

The Preacher reports one more thing he has seen under the sun, an instance of wisdom that
seemed great to him (9:13). A little city with few men is besieged by a great king who builds
great bulwarks against it (14). A poor wise man found in it delivers the city by his wisdom,
yet no man remembers that poor man (15).

From the story he draws a ranking and its limit at once: wisdom is better than strength,
*nevertheless* the poor man's wisdom is despised and his words are not heard (16). Two further
sayings extend both halves. Quiet words of the wise are better than the cry of a ruler among
fools (17); wisdom is better than weapons of war, but one sinner destroys much good (18).

Chapter 10 opens with the same arithmetic in an image: dead flies make the perfumer's oil
stink, and a little folly outweighs wisdom and honor (10:1). The wise man's heart is at his
right hand and the fool's at his left (2); the fool, walking along the way, lacks
understanding and tells everyone that he is a fool (3). The unit closes with counsel for the
one under authority: if the ruler's spirit rises against you, do not leave your place, for
gentleness lays great offenses to rest (4).

The movement is from a story to its moral to practical advice. Wisdom does deliver — a city,
and in 10:4 an endangered subordinate — but it does not secure credit, a hearing, or
protection against the disproportionate damage of a little folly.

## Key verses

The complete unit is copied verbatim from immutable stored WEB Ecclesiastes 9–10.

> **9:13** I have also seen wisdom under the sun in this way, and it seemed great to me.

> **9:14** There was a little city, and few men within it; and a great king came against it, besieged it, and built great bulwarks against it.

> **9:15** Now a poor wise man was found in it, and he by his wisdom delivered the city; yet no man remembered that same poor man.

> **9:16** Then I said, “Wisdom is better than strength.” Nevertheless the poor man’s wisdom is despised, and his words are not heard.

> **9:17** The words of the wise heard in quiet are better than the cry of him who rules among fools.

> **9:18** Wisdom is better than weapons of war; but one sinner destroys much good.

> **10:1** Dead flies cause the oil of the perfumer to produce an evil odor; so does a little folly outweigh wisdom and honor.

> **10:2** A wise man’s heart is at his right hand, but a fool’s heart at his left.

> **10:3** Yes also, when the fool walks by the way, his understanding fails him, and he says to everyone that he is a fool.

> **10:4** If the spirit of the ruler rises up against you, don’t leave your place; for gentleness lays great offenses to rest.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 9:13–10:4 unit is retained. Verse 13 opens a new observation with a
story, and its sayings run on into 10:1–3, which apply the same imbalance — a little folly
outweighing much wisdom — that 9:18 states. 10:4 is advice to a subordinate and could begin
the next unit; it is kept here because its ground, gentleness laying offenses to rest, is a
case of quiet wisdom outweighing a ruler's rising anger (9:17). 10:5 opens with *"There is an
evil which I have seen under the sun,"* a fresh observation formula, and begins the next unit.

**An example story, not a historical report.** The city, the king, and the poor man are all
unnamed, and no date or people is supplied. Attempts to identify the episode with a known
siege are not supported by the text, and none is made here. A frequently compared narrative
is the wise woman of Abel who, by her counsel, ends a siege of her city in 2 Samuel 20 (around
vv. 14–22). That book is not yet stored in `raw/`, so the comparison is given only as a
paraphrased reference, its verse range is approximate, and no literary relationship is
asserted.

**Was he forgotten, or never consulted?** The WEB of 9:15 says that the poor man *"by his
wisdom delivered the city."* Read that way, he saved it and was then forgotten. Some
interpreters instead take the verse as a hypothetical — he *could have* delivered the city,
but no one thought of him — which would fit 9:16's *"his words are not heard."* This is a
question about the Hebrew verb's sense that has not been checked here against an identified
Hebrew edition, so it is recorded as an interpretive possibility only. This page follows the
WEB: the deliverance happened, and the remembrance did not.

**The two halves of 9:16.** *"Wisdom is better than strength"* is not withdrawn by the
following *"Nevertheless."* Both stand: wisdom achieves more than force, and the poor man's
wisdom is despised. The story's lesson is about wisdom's standing, not its effectiveness.
The book's earlier pattern recurs — wisdom ranked above folly (2:13) beside its lack of
lasting advantage (2:16), and the poor and wise youth above the old king (4:13) beside a
following that does not last (4:16).

**Disproportion.** 9:18 and 10:1 state the same asymmetry twice: one sinner destroys much
good; a little folly outweighs wisdom and honor. The perfumer's oil is valuable and laborious
to make; a few dead flies spoil it. The sayings warn about how easily good work is undone,
without ranking folly above wisdom in worth.

**Right hand and left.** 10:2 sets the wise heart at the right hand and the fool's at the
left. Elsewhere in the stored WEB the right hand is associated with power (Exodus 15:6) and
with the greater blessing (Genesis 48:13–18), which suggests the heart that inclines toward
what succeeds and what fails. The saying uses that
contrast as an image; it makes no claim about left-handed people, and no Hebrew idiom is
asserted here.

**The fool on the road.** 10:3 can be read two ways in English: the fool tells everyone that
*he* is a fool (self-exposure), or he calls everyone *else* a fool. The WEB's *"he says to
everyone that he is a fool"* most naturally reads as self-exposure, matching Proverbs 12:23
and 13:16. The other reading is noted, not adopted, and the Hebrew has not been checked.

**Gentleness before the ruler.** 10:4 does not tell the subordinate to flatter or to flee. It
tells them to hold their post and answer anger with gentleness. Whether *"don’t leave your
place"* means a literal post at court or one's composure is not decided by the text; both
fit, and 8:3's counsel not to be hasty to leave the king's presence supports at least the
first. The counsel follows 9:17: quiet words above a ruler's cry.

**God is not named.** This unit mentions neither God nor his judgment. Its scale is entirely
human: siege, reputation, speech, and court. That silence is retained rather than filled.
