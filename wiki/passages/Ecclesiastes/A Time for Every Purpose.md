# A Time for Every Purpose

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

## Summary

The poem opens with a season for everything under heaven (1), then unfolds fourteen pairs
of times (2–8). Birth and death, planting and uprooting, destruction and restoration, grief
and joy, holding and releasing, silence and speech, love and hate, war and peace all belong
to its range. It is a catalogue of contrasting experiences and actions, not a dated schedule
or a set of instructions identifying when a reader should perform each one.

The question about the worker's profit returns at verse 9. The Preacher sees the burden God
has given humanity (10), and affirms that God has made everything beautiful in its time (11).
Yet the same verse limits human understanding: even with eternity set in their hearts,
people cannot find out God's work from beginning to end. The order asserted is not an order
the observer can completely explain.

The response is neither inactivity nor a promise of mastery. The Preacher commends rejoicing
and doing good during life (12), and calls eating, drinking, and enjoying good in labor God's
gift (13). God's work, unlike the worker's uncertain gain, endures without addition or
subtraction, so that people should fear before him (14). Verse 15 returns to recurrence, now
naming God as the one who seeks again what has passed away.

## Key verses

All fifteen verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:1–15.

> **1** For everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven:

> **2** a time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted;

> **3** a time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time to build up;

> **4** a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance;

> **5** a time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing;

> **6** a time to seek, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to cast away;

> **7** a time to tear, and a time to sew; a time to keep silence, and a time to speak;

> **8** a time to love, and a time to hate; a time for war, and a time for peace.

> **9** What profit has he who works in that in which he labors?

> **10** I have seen the burden which God has given to the sons of men to be afflicted with.

> **11** He has made everything beautiful in its time. He has also set eternity in their hearts, yet so that man can’t find out the work that God has done from the beginning even to the end.

> **12** I know that there is nothing better for them than to rejoice, and to do good as long as they live.

> **13** Also that every man should eat and drink, and enjoy good in all his labor, is the gift of God.

> **14** I know that whatever God does, it shall be forever. Nothing can be added to it, nor anything taken from it; and God has done it, that men should fear before him.

> **15** That which is has been long ago, and that which is to be has been long ago: and God seeks again that which is passed away.

- [[God]] — gives the burden and enjoyment; his enduring work evokes fear (10–15).

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 3:1–15 unit is retained. Verses 9–15 interpret the poem through
labor, knowledge, enjoyment, and God's work. Verse 16 begins a new observation about
wickedness in the place of justice; that pericope is now ingested as [[From Dust to Dust]].

**Times and moral judgment.** Naming a time to kill, hate, or wage war does not identify a
permitted victim, an object of hatred, or a justified campaign. The tension with moral
commands matters: Exodus 20:13 prohibits murder, whereas Ecclesiastes 3:3 includes killing
in its catalogue. Description and prohibition have different functions; the poem supplies
no exemption from the command. This observation compares the WEB's English, without a
claim about the Hebrew verbs. Doing good in verse 12 also resists reading the whole unit
as moral indifference.

**Beauty, burden, and limited knowledge.** The WEB's affirmation that everything is beautiful
in its time (11) stands beside the burden (10) and the inability to know God's entire work
(11). These claims remain together rather than becoming a promise that every painful event
will look beautiful to the person experiencing it. The earlier hatred of life and labor
(2:17–18) is not silently retracted by the invitation to rejoice (3:12–13).

**Eternity in their hearts.** This page follows the stored WEB's wording. Within its sentence,
eternity does not give people comprehensive knowledge; the qualification immediately says
the opposite. The phrase alone does not settle a doctrine of the afterlife. No Hebrew
edition or competing translation has been used to assert a lexical solution here.

**Stones, tearing, and what passed away.** The stones of verse 5 are not explained. Agricultural,
building, or other symbolic readings would go beyond the description, so none is made the
page's settled meaning. Verse 7 does not identify what is torn or sewn. Verse 15's statement
that God seeks again what has passed away is kept in the WEB's wording: its precise mechanism
is not stated. This page does not substitute an unverified alternative translation.
