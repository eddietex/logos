# Folly in High Places and Everyday Work

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 10:5–20

## Summary

The Preacher reports one more evil he has seen under the sun: an error that proceeds from the
ruler (10:5). Folly is set in great dignity while the rich sit low; servants ride on horses
while princes walk on the ground like servants (6–7). The disorder is described, not explained,
and no remedy is offered for it.

Four short sayings about ordinary work follow (8–11). Digging a pit, breaking through a wall,
quarrying stones, and splitting wood each carry their own danger. A blunt ax demands more
strength, *"but skill brings success"* (10); a charmer who is too late to stop the bite gains
nothing from his skill (11). Wisdom helps, but only when it is applied in time.

The sayings then turn to speech (12–15). The wise man's words are gracious, but the fool is
swallowed by his own lips; his talk begins in foolishness and ends in mischievous madness, and
he multiplies words although no one knows what will be (12–14). His labor wears him out, for he
does not even know the way to the city (15).

The last part returns to rulers and households (16–20). A land is pitied when its king is a
child and its princes feast in the morning, and called happy when its king is the son of nobles
and its princes eat at the proper time, for strength and not for drunkenness (16–17). Slothful
hands let the roof sink and the house leak (18). A feast is made for laughter, wine gladdens
life, and money answers all things (19). The unit closes with a warning: do not curse the king
or the rich even in private, for a bird may carry the word (20).

The unit's two ends match. It opens with folly placed in high office and closes with the
subject's need for caution before kings and the rich. Between them, the sayings on tools,
speech, and housekeeping show the same folly at a smaller scale.

## Key verses

The complete unit is copied verbatim from immutable stored WEB Ecclesiastes 10.

> **10:5** There is an evil which I have seen under the sun, the sort of error which proceeds from the ruler.

> **10:6** Folly is set in great dignity, and the rich sit in a low place.

> **10:7** I have seen servants on horses, and princes walking like servants on the earth.

> **10:8** He who digs a pit may fall into it; and whoever breaks through a wall may be bitten by a snake.

> **10:9** Whoever carves out stones may be injured by them. Whoever splits wood may be endangered thereby.

> **10:10** If the ax is blunt, and one doesn’t sharpen the edge, then he must use more strength; but skill brings success.

> **10:11** If the snake bites before it is charmed, then is there no profit for the charmer’s tongue.

> **10:12** The words of a wise man’s mouth are gracious; but a fool is swallowed by his own lips.

> **10:13** The beginning of the words of his mouth is foolishness; and the end of his talk is mischievous madness.

> **10:14** A fool also multiplies words. Man doesn’t know what will be; and that which will be after him, who can tell him?

> **10:15** The labor of fools wearies every one of them; for he doesn’t know how to go to the city.

> **10:16** Woe to you, land, when your king is a child, and your princes eat in the morning!

> **10:17** Happy are you, land, when your king is the son of nobles, and your princes eat in due season, for strength, and not for drunkenness!

> **10:18** By slothfulness the roof sinks in; and through idleness of the hands the house leaks.

> **10:19** A feast is made for laughter, and wine makes the life glad; and money is the answer for all things.

> **10:20** Don’t curse the king, no, not in your thoughts; and don’t curse the rich in your bedroom: for a bird of the sky may carry your voice, and that which has wings may tell the matter.

## People

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — the observer who has seen this evil under the sun (10:5)
  and servants on horses (7).

The ruler, the servants and princes, the workmen, the charmer, the fool, the child king, and
the son of nobles are unnamed figures of observations and general sayings. None is identified
with a historical individual, and none receives a person page.

## Places

No named place appears. The *land* addressed in 10:16–17 and the *city* of 10:15 are unnamed and
are not identified with any known land or city.

## Themes

- [[Folly]] — Folly set in great dignity; the fool swallowed by his own lips and lost on the way to the city.
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — The ruler's error, the child king and the noble one, and the king not to be cursed.
- [[Wisdom]] — Skill that brings success, and gracious words, but only when applied in time.
- [[Work]] — The hazards of digging, quarrying, and woodcutting; the blunt ax; the fool's wearying labor.
- [[Language and Speech]] — Gracious words, the fool's multiplied talk, the curse a bird may carry.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — Servants on horses and princes walking like servants.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — The rich in a low place; money answers all things; the rich not to be cursed.
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — Feasting for laughter and wine that makes life glad, set beside drunken princes.
- [[Retribution]] — The digger who may fall into his own pit, read as risk rather than verdict.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 10:5–20 unit is retained. 10:5 opens with a fresh observation
formula, *"There is an evil which I have seen under the sun,"* after the counsel of 10:4. The
unit is a chain of sayings rather than a single argument, but it is framed by rulers at both
ends (5–7, 16–20) and held together by folly throughout. 11:1 begins a series of imperatives
about acting under uncertainty, and opens the next unit. The planned title is also kept.

**An error from the ruler.** The WEB's *"the sort of error which proceeds from the ruler"*
renders *kišḡāḡâ*, *like an error*, with the noun *šəḡāḡâ* that 5:6 uses for the excuse
*"this was a mistake"* (5:5 in Hebrew numbering; MAM, read 2026-10-27). Elsewhere in the Hebrew
Bible, notably in the Levitical laws, the word denotes unintentional wrongdoing. Ecclesiastes
does not say whether the ruler's error is willful; the comparative *k-* (*like*) lets the
reader see it as an unintentional mistake that is nonetheless far-reaching. The broader legal
sense is cited from common lexical usage and was not checked against a lexicon here.

**Disorder, not a program.** 10:6–7 describes the world turned upside down from the point of
view of a settled social order: rich people and princes in low places, servants mounted. It
does not say that rank should never change. 4:13–14 approved a poor and wise youth who came out
of prison to reign, and Joseph rose from slave to ruler in Genesis. What 10:5–7 laments is the
promotion of *folly* (*hassekel*, 10:6, with the article). The same perspective appears in
Proverbs 19:10 and 30:22. In 10:7 and Proverbs 19:10 the Hebrew pairs *ʿeḇeḏ* (*servant*)
with *śar* (*prince*), in the plural in Ecclesiastes and with *ʿeḇeḏ* singular in Proverbs;
the shared pair does not establish that one text quotes the other.

**May, not shall.** 10:8's *"He who digs a pit may fall into it"* and Proverbs 26:27's
*"Whoever digs a pit shall fall into it"* use the same verb form, *yippôl*, a Hebrew imperfect
that can express either futurity or possibility. The nouns for *pit* differ: *šaḥaṯ* in
Proverbs, *gûmmāṣ* in Ecclesiastes (MAM, read 2026-10-27). The WEB's difference between
*shall* and *may* is therefore an interpretation of context, not something the verb itself
decides. In context, the next three lines (9–11) are about workmen's hazards rather than about
the wicked, and *may* suits them. The connection page, which had left this unchecked, is
updated.

**Skill and advantage.** In 10:10, *"skill brings success"* renders *wəyiṯrôn haḵšêr ḥoḵmâ*;
*ḥoḵmâ* is the ordinary word for *wisdom*, and *yiṯrôn* is the *gain* the book asks about in
1:3 (*mah-yyiṯrôn lāʾāḏām*). The ax saying is therefore a small answer to the book's large
question: wisdom does yield an advantage, in sharpening before chopping. 10:11 immediately
limits it with the same noun: *wəʾên yiṯrôn ləḇaʿal hallāšôn*, *no advantage to the master of
the tongue* (WEB *"the charmer"*) if the snake bites before the charm. The WEB reading is kept;
the word-level observations are from MAM, read 2026-10-27, and the reading of *hakšêr* as
*success* is the WEB's, not independently checked.

**Snake and charm.** 10:11 sets *nāḥāš* (*snake*) beside *lāḥaš* (*charm*), words that sound
alike in Hebrew (MAM). The WEB cannot keep the sound-play. Snake charming is named elsewhere in
WEB Psalm 58:4–5 and Jeremiah 8:17, verified at bible-api.com on 2026-10-27; neither book is
stored in `raw/`.

**The fool's words and the fool's road.** 10:12–15 pairs speech with direction. The fool's
lips swallow him (12), and his multiplied words claim knowledge no one has (14): *"Man doesn’t
know what will be"*, echoing 6:12 and 8:7. The fool who *"doesn’t know how to go to the city"*
(15) recalls the fool of 10:3 whose understanding fails him *"when the fool walks by the
way"*. Whether *going to the city* is a proverbial phrase for the obvious and easy, or refers
more particularly to the road to market or court, is not decided by the text, and no Hebrew
idiom is asserted here.

**Child and noble.** 10:16's *child* is *naʿar*, a word that covers a range from infant to
young man; 4:13's *youth* is a different word, *yeleḏ* (MAM). Whether 10:16 means literal youth
or immature conduct is not decided here; the contrast in 10:17 is with *ben-ḥôrîm*, the *son of
nobles* (WEB), so the pair sets character against birth and age as a single picture of a
well-ordered court. The princes who *"eat in the morning"* begin feasting before the day's work.
Isaiah 3:4 and 5:11 offer comparable images (see
[[Woe to the Land - Proverbs 31.4-5, Isaiah 3.4, 5.11, and Ecclesiastes 10.16-17]]).

**What money answers.** 10:19, *"money is the answer for all things"*, can be read as cynical
observation, as the voice of the drunken princes of 10:16, or as a sober remark that money
makes provision possible. The verb is *yaʿăneh*, rendered *answer* by the WEB. In 5:20 (5:19
in Hebrew) the WEB's *"God occupies him with the joy of his heart"* renders the participle
*maʿăneh*. The two forms share the consonants *ʿ-n-h* (MAM), and the WEB translates them with
different verbs; whether they are the same verb, and whether Ecclesiastes intends an echo, is
not determined here. The page does not adopt one reading of 10:19. It notes that 10:19's feast,
wine, and money sit just after 10:18's sloth and just before 10:20's warning about the rich, so
the sentence can be read as the reason both diligence and caution matter.

**Cursing the king.** 10:20 uses *təqallēl* twice — *"Don’t curse the king"* and *"don’t curse
the rich"*. Exodus 22:28 (22:27 in Hebrew) uses this verb for God (WEB *"blaspheme"*) and a
different verb, *ʾārar*, for the ruler (MAM, read 2026-10-27). The Exodus law and the
Ecclesiastes counsel therefore agree in substance without sharing their wording. Their reasons
also differ: Exodus gives a commandment, and Ecclesiastes gives prudence — *"a bird of the sky
may carry your voice"*. The bird is an image for how private words reach the powerful, not a
claim about birds; the *"thoughts"* of the WEB renders *maddāʿ*, *knowledge* or *mind*.

**Wine, twice.** 10:17 commends princes who eat *"for strength, and not for drunkenness"*, and
10:19 says that wine makes life glad. These sayings are compatible: Ecclesiastes elsewhere
commands drinking wine with a merry heart (9:7). Proverbs 31:4–5 keeps wine from kings because
it makes them forget the law. Ecclesiastes 10:17 makes the narrower complaint about drunkenness
and timing.

**God is not named.** As in 9:13–10:4, the unit never mentions God. Its scale is entirely
human: rulers, tools, speech, houses, money. That silence is retained.
