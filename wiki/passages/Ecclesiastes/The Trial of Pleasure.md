# The Trial of Pleasure

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 2:1–11

## Summary

Having reported that much wisdom brings much grief (1:18), the Preacher turns to the opposite
instrument. He speaks to his own heart, *"Come now, I will test you with mirth"* (2:1), and gives
the verdict at once, before any of the evidence: *"this also was vanity."* Laughter is called
foolishness, and of mirth he asks what it accomplishes (2). The rest of the unit is the evidence
that earned the verdict, reported in order.

The experiment is controlled. He tries to cheer his flesh with wine and to lay hold of folly,
*"my heart yet guiding me with wisdom"* (3). The goal is stated as a question about everyone, not
about himself alone: what is good for the sons of men to do under heaven all the days of their
lives. Pleasure is not abandonment here; it is a method of inquiry, and wisdom stays at the
controls.

Then comes the catalogue, built almost entirely on *"I made myself … I built myself … I planted
myself"* (4–8). Houses, vineyards, gardens and parks with fruit trees of every kind, pools to water
a forest of young trees; male and female servants bought and born in the house; herds and flocks;
silver and gold and the treasure of kings and provinces; male and female singers and musical
instruments of all sorts. He was great and increased beyond all before him in Jerusalem, and his
wisdom remained with him (9). He refused his eyes nothing and his heart no joy, and his heart did
rejoice in all his labor: *"this was my portion from all my labor"* (10).

The reckoning comes when he looks back at all of it (11). The works his hands made, the labor he
spent: *"all was vanity and a chasing after wind, and there was no profit under the sun."* The
book's opening question — *"What does man gain from all his labor"* (1:3) — receives its first
direct answer, and it is no. Yet verse 10 is not withdrawn. The joy was real and was his
portion; what failed was profit, something that would remain. The next unit,
[[The Wise and the Fool Share One End]] (2:12–17), turns back to compare wisdom with folly in the light
of death.

## Key verses

All verses below are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:1–11.

> **1** I said in my heart, “Come now, I will test you with mirth: therefore enjoy pleasure”; and behold, this also was vanity.

> **2** I said of laughter, “It is foolishness”; and of mirth, “What does it accomplish?”

> **3** I searched in my heart how to cheer my flesh with wine, my heart yet guiding me with wisdom, and how to lay hold of folly, until I might see what it was good for the sons of men that they should do under heaven all the days of their lives.

> **4** I made myself great works. I built myself houses. I planted myself vineyards.

> **5** I made myself gardens and parks, and I planted trees in them of all kinds of fruit.

> **9** So I was great, and increased more than all who were before me in Jerusalem. My wisdom also remained with me.

> **10** Whatever my eyes desired, I didn’t keep from them. I didn’t withhold my heart from any joy, for my heart rejoiced because of all my labor, and this was my portion from all my labor.

> **11** Then I looked at all the works that my hands had worked, and at the labor that I had labored to do; and behold, all was vanity and a chasing after wind, and there was no profit under the sun.

  before him there (7, 9).

## Themes

- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — the theme's first Ecclesiastes test: mirth tried deliberately (1–2),
  joy unwithheld and called a portion (10), and still no profit (11).
- [[Vanity]] — the verdict announced first (1) and confirmed last (11), with *"a chasing after
  wind"*.
- [[Work]] — great works built and planted (4–6); the heart rejoicing in labor (10); labor without
  profit (11).
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — houses, flocks, silver, gold, and royal treasure accumulated
  beyond all predecessors (7–9).
- [[Wisdom]] — the guide of the experiment (3) that *"remained with me"* (9).
- [[Folly]] — laughter called foolishness (2) and folly laid hold of deliberately, under wisdom's
  guidance (3).
- [[Kingship and Empire]] — the treasure of kings and provinces (8) and greatness above all in
  Jerusalem (9): a royal capacity for experiment no ordinary person has.
- [[Slavery and Servitude]] — servants bought and born in the house (7), listed among
  possessions.

## Notes

**Boundary.** 2:1 opens a new experiment with a new address to the heart; 2:11 closes it with
the full verdict formula and the first explicit answer to 1:3. 2:12 turns to a fresh comparison
of wisdom and folly. The planned boundary held.

**Verdict before evidence.** The unit is framed by its verdict: vanity is announced in verse 1
and repeated with its fuller formula in verse 11. The catalogue in between is not suspense; it is
the reason. This is the same order as 1:2, where *"all is vanity"* precedes the poem that
illustrates it.

**Wisdom kept at the controls.** Verse 3's *"my heart yet guiding me with wisdom"* and verse 9's
*"My wisdom also remained with me"* make this an inquiry and not a lapse. The experiment cannot
be dismissed as a fool's verdict on pleasure, which is what gives the conclusion its weight. It
also means that folly (3) is laid hold of *as an object of study*, continuing 1:17.

**Joy affirmed, profit denied.** Verse 10 says his heart rejoiced and that this was his portion.
Verse 11 says there was no profit. Both stand. A reading that makes 2:11 cancel 2:10 loses half
the text; a reading that makes 2:10 a recommendation loses the verdict. The book will return
to enjoyment repeatedly (2:24; 3:12–13; 5:18–19; 8:15; 9:7), and there the portion is named as
God's gift. Here, in 2:1–11, God is not mentioned at all. That absence is recorded, not
interpreted.

**The first person.** *"Myself"* or *"my"* recurs through 4–10 in the WEB. This page notes the
English pattern only; no Hebrew analysis was done for this ingest, and no claim is made here
about the underlying forms.

**A royal experiment.** The catalogue presupposes a king's resources: provinces, the treasure of
kings, servants born in the house. The conclusion is offered to *"the sons of men"* (3), but the
evidence is gathered at a scale they cannot reach. One reading is that this is the point: if the
one who could afford everything found no profit, the rest need not try. Ecclesiastes does not
say this outright; it is an inference.

**Solomon.** The resemblance to the splendor 1 Kings 4–10 assigns to Solomon — building, gold,
servants, and wealth above other kings — is often noted. Those chapters are not stored and are
paraphrased here without quotation. As with 1:16, *"all who were before me in Jerusalem"* (9)
implies more predecessors than a Solomonic identification easily allows. Solomon is not named.

**Servants as possessions.** Verse 7 lists bought and house-born servants between building
projects and herds. The text does not comment on it. The listing is recorded here without
deriving either approval or critique from it.
