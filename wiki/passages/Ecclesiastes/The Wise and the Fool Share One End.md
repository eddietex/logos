# The Wise and the Fool Share One End

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 2:12–17

## Summary

The trial of pleasure is over, and the Preacher turns back to the three objects he named at 1:17:
*"I turned myself to consider wisdom, madness, and folly"* (2:12). The verse then asks a question
that seems to step sideways — *"for what can the king's successor do? Just that which has been done
long ago."* Whoever comes after the king can only repeat what the king has already tried. The
successor will return in 2:18–19, where he inherits the labor, so the question looks forward as
well as back.

The first finding is a ranking, stated without qualification: *"wisdom excels folly, as far as light
excels darkness"* (13). The wise man sees where he is going; the fool walks in darkness (14). Nothing
that follows withdraws this. The Preacher does not say wisdom is no better than folly.

The second finding stands beside the first: *"one event happens to them all"* (14). The Preacher
applies it to himself: *"As it happens to the fool, so will it happen even to me; and why was I then
more wise?"* (15). Verse 16 names the event twice over. Neither the wise man nor the fool will be
remembered for ever, because all will be forgotten in the days to come, and *"the wise man must die
just like the fool!"* The forgetting of 1:11, which fell on all generations alike, now falls on the
wise in particular.

The unit ends in the strongest emotional statement the book has made so far: *"So I hated life"*
(17). The reason given is that the work done under the sun was grievous to him, and the verdict
returns in full: *"all is vanity and a chasing after wind."* The next unit, 2:18–26, turns the same
hatred onto his own labor and the man who will inherit it.

## Key verses

All verses below are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:12–17.

> **12** I turned myself to consider wisdom, madness, and folly: for what can the king’s successor do? Just that which has been done long ago.

> **13** Then I saw that wisdom excels folly, as far as light excels darkness.

> **14** The wise man’s eyes are in his head, and the fool walks in darkness—and yet I perceived that one event happens to them all.

> **15** Then I said in my heart, “As it happens to the fool, so will it happen even to me; and why was I then more wise?” Then I said in my heart that this also is vanity.

> **16** For of the wise man, even as of the fool, there is no memory for ever, since in the days to come all will have been long forgotten. Indeed, the wise man must die just like the fool!

> **17** So I hated life, because the work that is worked under the sun was grievous to me; for all is vanity and a chasing after wind.

## Notes

**Boundary.** 2:12 opens with a new turn, *"I turned myself to consider"*, after 2:11 closed the
pleasure experiment with its verdict. 2:17 closes this unit with the full verdict formula. 2:18
begins again with *"I hated all my labor"*, which picks up 2:17's *"I hated life"* but moves to a
new subject: inheritance. That makes 2:17 a hinge. It is kept here because its reason, the common
end and the vanity of all, belongs to 2:14–16. The planned boundary held.

**Two findings, both kept.** 2:13–14a ranks wisdom above folly as light above darkness. 2:14b–16
says the two share one end and one forgetting. The text gives both in consecutive breaths and
reconciles neither with the other. A reading that makes the common end cancel wisdom's advantage
has to discard verse 13, which the Preacher calls something he *saw*; a reading that makes verse 13
the last word has to discard verses 15–17, where he draws from the common end the conclusion that
he hated life. This page keeps both. The tension is the unit's content, not a problem it hands on
to be solved.

**Light without destination.** In Proverbs 4:18–19, light is a path that shines *"more and more
until the perfect day"*; the image carries a direction and an end. In Ecclesiastes 2:13–14 the wise
man's light lets him see where he walks, and the walk ends where the fool's does. The image is the
same contrast. What it promises is different. See [[The Two Paths]].

**The question about himself.** 2:15 is the first time the Preacher applies the verdict to himself
as a person who will die, rather than to works or experiments. *"And why was I then more wise?"*
treats his own lifetime of wisdom as an investment whose return death cancels. It continues the
book's language of gain (1:3) and profit (2:11) without using those words here.

**"So I hated life."** The book records the feeling without correcting it. Later units commend
eating, drinking, and enjoying one's labor (2:24) and say that *"a living dog is better than a dead
lion"* (9:4). Those statements are not yet ingested; they are noted here so that 2:17 is not read
as the book's last word on life. Equally, 2:17 is not softened by them. It is reported as what the
Preacher felt when he faced the common end.

**The king's successor.** 2:12b is short, and its connection to 2:12a is not explained. The WEB
reads it as a question about what anyone coming after the king could add: only *"that which has
been done long ago."* This page interprets the WEB wording only; it has not checked the Hebrew or
compared other translations, and makes no claim about how the clause should be rendered. The
successor reappears as *"the man who comes after me"* in 2:18 (stored WEB), not yet ingested.

**Remembrance narrowed.** 1:11 spoke of former and later generations in general. 2:16 speaks of the
wise man and the fool, and so contradicts not only Proverbs 10:7's distinction between righteous and
wicked memory, but any hope that wisdom specifically would secure a name. The contradiction with
Proverbs 10:7 is recorded on [[Profit and Remembrance - Proverbs and Ecclesiastes 1]], not resolved.

**No mention of God.** As in 2:1–11, God is not named in 2:12–17. He returns in 2:24–26. The absence
is recorded, not interpreted.

**Hebrew.** No Hebrew analysis was done for this ingest. Observations about repeated words (*one
event*, *vanity*, *hated*) are about the WEB English only, and no claim is made about the forms
behind them.
