# Remember Your Creator

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 11:7–12:8

## Summary

Light and the sight of the sun are pleasant. The speaker invites a person to rejoice through
many years, remembering the many days of darkness, then addresses the young man directly:
rejoice, follow heart and eyes, and know that God will bring all these things into judgment
(11:7–9). The counsel to put away sorrow and evil does not make youth permanent; youth too
receives the verdict of vanity (11:10).

Remembering the Creator belongs in youth, before years without pleasure (12:1). The poem
then darkens the lights, stills the household, and fills the way with fear. Keepers tremble,
strong men bend, grinders become few, windows darken, and music is brought low (2–4).
Blossoming, burden, and failing desire lead to the everlasting home and mourners in the
streets (5). Severed cord, broken bowl, pitcher, and wheel give the ending an irreversible
character (6).

Dust returns to earth and the spirit returns to God who gave it (7). The Preacher's verdict
of vanity returns (8), echoing the opening of the book. The poem thus joins enjoyment,
accountability, creaturely dependence, and mortality; none alone supplies its whole meaning.

## Key verses

All twelve verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB; chapter labels distinguish repeated numbers.

### Ecclesiastes 11:7–10

> **7** Truly the light is sweet, and a pleasant thing it is for the eyes to see the sun.

> **8** Yes, if a man lives many years, let him rejoice in them all; but let him remember the days of darkness, for they shall be many. All that comes is vanity.

> **9** Rejoice, young man, in your youth, and let your heart cheer you in the days of your youth, and walk in the ways of your heart, and in the sight of your eyes; but know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment.

> **10** Therefore remove sorrow from your heart, and put away evil from your flesh; for youth and the dawn of life are vanity.

### Ecclesiastes 12:1–8

> **1** Remember also your Creator in the days of your youth, before the evil days come, and the years draw near, when you will say, “I have no pleasure in them”;

> **2** Before the sun, the light, the moon, and the stars are darkened, and the clouds return after the rain;

> **3** in the day when the keepers of the house shall tremble, and the strong men shall bow themselves, and the grinders cease because they are few, and those who look out of the windows are darkened,

> **4** and the doors shall be shut in the street; when the sound of the grinding is low, and one shall rise up at the voice of a bird, and all the daughters of music shall be brought low;

> **5** yes, they shall be afraid of heights, and terrors will be on the way; and the almond tree shall blossom, and the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail; because man goes to his everlasting home, and the mourners go about the streets:

> **6** before the silver cord is severed, or the golden bowl is broken, or the pitcher is broken at the spring, or the wheel broken at the cistern,

> **7** and the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it.

> **8** “Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher. “All is vanity!”

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 11:7–12:8 unit is retained. Sweet light introduces the counsel to
rejoice; the repeated attention to youth and the days ahead joins the chapter boundary.
12:8 closes with the opening refrain. The evaluation of the Preacher beginning at 12:9
belongs to the following pericope, [[The End of the Matter]].

**Joy and judgment.** The invitation to rejoice is positive, not explicitly marked as
sarcasm. Judgment follows in the same sentence (11:9), so the ways of heart and eyes are
not exempted from accountability. Conversely, the verse does not call every pleasure a
sin. The WEB's instruction to put away evil from the flesh (11:10) is retained without
substituting an unverified alternative translation. Neither this verse nor 12:1 promises
that all physical suffering can be removed by choice.

**An image of aging, without an anatomical key.** Youth and the coming difficult years
make bodily decline a well-supported reading of 12:2–5. The text nevertheless speaks of
keepers, strong men, grinders, windows, doors, birds, and music; it does not label each
one with an organ. Reading the few grinders as lost teeth and the darkened windows as
failing sight is an inference from the image, not a literal statement of the WEB.
The almond tree, grasshopper, silver cord, and golden bowl are left as poetic images
without a claimed medical identification. Their cumulative force is fading capacity
and the breaking of life's supports, not a clinical schedule everyone must follow.

**Remembering before loss.** 11:8 asks for remembrance of darkness and 12:1 for remembrance
of the Creator. The two commands make the future limit and present dependence part of
rejoicing. This differs from the failures of public memory in 1:11 and 9:15. The tension
with 5:20 remains: there joy occupies the person so that life's days are not often
reflected on; here the person is instructed to remember the dark days.

**Dust and spirit: an explicit tension.** In the WEB, 3:21 asks who knows whether the
human spirit goes upward; 12:7 affirms that it returns to God who gave it. The earlier
question is not rewritten as certainty, and the later affirmation is not reduced to
another unanswered question. 9:5–10 also describes the dead's lost knowledge and portion
under the sun. 12:7 gives a destination and a giver without describing conscious
experience, resurrection, or the animal spirit's destination. A fuller account cannot
be inferred from this verse alone. See
[[Dust and Breath - Genesis 2-3 and Ecclesiastes 3.18-21]].

**Joy, mourning, and vanity remain together.** 7:2–4 prefers mourning and sorrow;
11:9–10 commands joy and removal of sorrow. 6:2 describes enjoyment withheld by God;
11:8 calls for rejoicing in all one's years. These tensions remain explicit. Reading
the commands as counsel within a limited life, rather than a report that everyone
can enjoy every day, respects their form without erasing the different judgments.
The final refrain does not retract remembrance of the Creator or judgment.
