# From Dust to Dust

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 3:16–22

## Summary

The Preacher observes wickedness in the very places associated with justice and righteousness
(16). His inward response is that God will judge both righteous and wicked, with a time for
every purpose and work (17). The promised judgment stands beside the observed corruption;
no corrected verdict or date for its correction is narrated.

He then describes God testing humanity so that people recognize their likeness to animals
(18). Humans and animals alike die, share breath, and return to dust (19–20). In this respect,
humanity has no advantage, and the verdict of vanity returns. The WEB presents the next
sentence as a question about who knows whether the human spirit goes upward and the animal
spirit downward (21), rather than as a declaration that the two destinations are established.

The conclusion commends rejoicing in one's works as one's portion (22). It does not solve the
corruption of justice or supply knowledge of what follows a person. The inability to be
brought to see what will be afterward is the reason given for receiving this present portion.

## Key verses

All seven verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 3:16–22.

> **16** Moreover I saw under the sun, in the place of justice, that wickedness was there; and in the place of righteousness, that wickedness was there.

> **17** I said in my heart, “God will judge the righteous and the wicked; for there is a time there for every purpose and for every work.”

> **18** I said in my heart, “As for the sons of men, God tests them, so that they may see that they themselves are like animals.

> **19** For that which happens to the sons of men happens to animals. Even one thing happens to them. As the one dies, so the other dies. Yes, they have all one breath; and man has no advantage over the animals: for all is vanity.

> **20** All go to one place. All are from the dust, and all turn to dust again.

> **21** Who knows the spirit of man, whether it goes upward, and the spirit of the animal, whether it goes downward to the earth?”

> **22** Therefore I saw that there is nothing better, than that a man should rejoice in his works; for that is his portion: for who can bring him to see what will be after him?

## People

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — observes, reflects, questions, and commends enjoyment.
- [[God]] — will judge and tests humanity (17–18).

Humanity and animals are collective subjects; no new individual is named.

## Places

None named. The places of justice and righteousness are institutional settings; the earth
and dust do not identify a new geographical location. Sheol is not named in this unit.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 3:16–22 unit is retained. Verse 16 begins a new observation after
the poem of times; verse 17 takes up its language of time, purpose, and work. Verse 22 closes
with an inference about enjoyment. The next observation is now recorded in [[The Tears of the Oppressed]] (4:1–3).

**Justice promised, injustice observed.** Verse 17 does not deny verse 16. Nor does the shared
death of humans and animals in 19–20 erase the moral distinction between righteous and wicked
in 17. The passage affirms divine judgment without locating it explicitly before or after
death. Reading the common death as proof that God never judges would suppress verse 17;
reading judgment as already visible in every court would suppress verse 16.

**Human dignity and the animal comparison.** Genesis 1:26–28 distinguishes humanity by God's
image and dominion, while Ecclesiastes 3:19 denies human advantage in the immediate context of
shared breath and death. The tension is real, but the claims concern different aspects of
creaturely life. This unit does not expressly revoke the image or say that humans and animals
are identical in every respect. Genesis 2:7, 19 already gives humans and animals a connection
to the ground; Genesis 3:19 joins toil to returning to dust. Ecclesiastes applies the dust
observation to both, without retelling the disobedience or assigning animals a personal sin.

**What is and is not known.** The question in verse 21 is retained as the stored WEB gives it.
It is neither an unqualified affirmation of different spiritual destinations nor a definitive
denial of any life beyond death. Ecclesiastes 12:7, verified in stored WEB but not yet ingested,
later affirms the spirit's return to God. The later affirmation and the present question must
remain distinguishable; importing the later answer would change this unit's questioning voice.
Verse 22 asks about seeing what follows a person, without spelling out whether this concerns
later earthly events, an experience after death, or both. No Hebrew lexical or grammatical
solution is asserted here; breath and spirit describe the WEB's English renderings.

**Testing and enjoyment.** The test in verse 18 is explained in terms of what humans come to
see about themselves. No individual ordeal, named victim, or successful performance is given.
The pleasure experiment in 2:1–11 and the hatred of life in 2:17 remain beside the present
commendation. Rejoicing is a portion within mortality, not a promise of enduring profit.
