# The Lover of Silver and the Gift of Enjoyment

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 5:8–20

## Summary

The Preacher moves from words before God to injustice and possessions under the sun. The
oppression of the poor and the violent removal of justice are not to be met with amazement:
one official is watched by another, with further officials above them (8). The WEB then
says the earth's profit is for all and that the king profits from the field (9). The hierarchy
is visible; no official is shown repairing the injustice.

More possessions do not necessarily give their owner more good. The lover of silver is not
satisfied by silver (10); increasing goods bring increasing consumers, leaving the owner
looking at what he owns (11). The worker sleeps sweetly with little or much to eat, while
abundance keeps the rich awake (12). The contrast concerns the experience of possessing,
not only the amount possessed.

The speaker has seen wealth retained to its owner's harm (13). It disappears through
misfortune, leaving nothing in hand when there is a son (14). The example broadens to the
boundary of every life: a person leaves as naked as he arrived, carrying none of his labor
away (15). This is called a grievous evil, followed by another question about profit (16).
Eating in darkness, frustration, sickness, and wrath describe the life consumed by this
predicament (17).

Yet the unit ends with a positive judgment. Eating, drinking, and enjoying one's labor during
the life God gives are good and proper, one's portion (18). Wealth and the power to enjoy it
are both attributed to God (19). God occupies the recipient with joy rather than frequent
reflection on life's days (20). The grievous evil is not withdrawn, and the gift is not a
promise that every possessor of wealth experiences it.

## Key verses

All thirteen verses are copied verbatim from immutable stored WEB Ecclesiastes 5:8–20.

> **8** If you see the oppression of the poor, and the violent taking away of justice and righteousness in a district, don’t marvel at the matter: for one official is eyed by a higher one; and there are officials over them.

> **9** Moreover the profit of the earth is for all. The king profits from the field.

> **10** He who loves silver shall not be satisfied with silver; nor he who loves abundance, with increase: this also is vanity.

> **11** When goods increase, those who eat them are increased; and what advantage is there to its owner, except to feast on them with his eyes?

> **12** The sleep of a laboring man is sweet, whether he eats little or much; but the abundance of the rich will not allow him to sleep.

> **13** There is a grievous evil which I have seen under the sun: wealth kept by its owner to his harm.

> **14** Those riches perish by misfortune, and if he has fathered a son, there is nothing in his hand.

> **15** As he came out of his mother’s womb, naked shall he go again as he came, and shall take nothing for his labor, which he may carry away in his hand.

> **16** This also is a grievous evil, that in all points as he came, so shall he go. And what profit does he have who labors for the wind?

> **17** All his days he also eats in darkness, he is frustrated, and has sickness and wrath.

> **18** Behold, that which I have seen to be good and proper is for one to eat and to drink, and to enjoy good in all his labor, in which he labors under the sun, all the days of his life which God has given him; for this is his portion.

> **19** Every man also to whom God has given riches and wealth, and has given him power to eat of it, and to take his portion, and to rejoice in his labor—this is the gift of God.

> **20** For he shall not often reflect on the days of his life; because God occupies him with the joy of his heart.

## Notes

**Boundary.** Retain the planned 5:8–20 unit. Verses 8–9 turn to public administration and
agricultural profit before 10–17 test private accumulation. Verses 18–20 close with enjoyment.
The next unit, [[Riches Without Enjoyment]], opens with another observed evil (6:1):
wealth given without the power to eat of it (6:2). Both units are now ingested.

**Officials and the field.** Verse 8's higher supervision does not explicitly promise
redress or identify the highest official as God. Reading the tiers as machinery of
exploitation fits the surrounding oppression; reading them as possible oversight is also
compatible with the isolated image. The WEB does not settle how the supervision works.
Likewise, verse 9 says that all share the earth's profit and that the king profits from the
field; it does not explain how fairly the produce is distributed. Agricultural dependence
and royal extraction are possible emphases, not an established account of a particular
administration. No Hebrew syntax or alternative translation is asserted in this pass.

**Loss before death.** Verse 14 names misfortune, not a particular speculative investment,
crime, or divine sentence. Its empty hand precedes the empty-handed departure of verse 15.
The text does not specify whether the hand in verse 14 is the father's or the son's; in
either case the family has no retained wealth. The son is not the uncertainly wise heir
of 2:19, and is not accused of wasting an inheritance.

**A real tension about wealth.** Proverbs 10:22 says that Yahweh's blessing brings wealth
without added trouble in the WEB. Ecclesiastes 5:13–17 describes harmful wealth, loss, and
distress, while 5:19 attributes both wealth and enjoyment to God. Distinguishing riches
alone from riches enjoyed helps explain the juxtaposition, but does not authorize a rule
that every prosperous person is blessed or every sufferer has acquired wealth wrongly.
The loss is not explicitly explained as punishment. The now-ingested 6:2
sharpens the distinction by attributing wealth to God without enjoyment.

**Enjoyment beside oppression.** Verses 18–20 do not undo the injustice of verse 8 or the
uncomforted victims of 4:1–3. Nor is the rich person's sleeplessness in verse 12 a statement
that every wealthy person is excluded from joy: verse 19 expressly permits enjoyed wealth.
The page preserves both experiences. Receiving a portion rather than securing permanent
profit is a useful interpretation of the sequence, not a distinction the text defines
as a formal doctrine.

**Less reflection, not a longer life.** The WEB of verse 20 speaks of not often reflecting
on life's days because God occupies the heart with joy. It promises neither immortality
nor permanent remembrance by others, and does not command forgetfulness of the oppressed.
Its focus is how given days are experienced.
