# The Tears of the Oppressed

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 4:1–3

## Summary

The Preacher turns again to what happens under the sun and sees oppression through the tears
of its victims. Twice he says they have no comforter. Between those statements he locates
power on the oppressors' side (1). The absence of comfort belongs to an unequal social
situation, not simply to a private feeling of sadness.

From this observation he praises the long-dead above the still-living (2), then judges the
one who has not yet existed better off than either, because that person has not seen the
evil work done under the sun (3). The comparison grows more severe at each step. No rescue,
consoling speech, or correction of the oppressors is narrated in these three verses.

This follows immediately after rejoicing in one's works was commended as a portion (3:22).
Both evaluations must remain visible. Here the reason given for preferring nonexistence is
exposure to evil; the passage neither retracts every affirmation of enjoyment nor allows
those affirmations to silence the victims' tears.

## Key verses

All three verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:1–3.

> **1** Then I returned and saw all the oppressions that are done under the sun: and behold, the tears of those who were oppressed, and they had no comforter; and on the side of their oppressors there was power; but they had no comforter.

> **2** Therefore I praised the dead who have been long dead more than the living who are yet alive.

> **3** Yes, better than them both is him who has not yet been, who has not seen the evil work that is done under the sun.

## People

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — the observer and speaker of the comparative judgments.

The oppressed, their oppressors, the living, and the dead are unnamed groups. The absent
comforter is a role, not a newly identified person. God is not mentioned in this unit.

## Places

None named. The repeated setting under the sun describes the scope of the observation;
it does not identify a particular country, court, or city.

## Themes

- [[Suffering]] — tears under oppression, with no relief narrated.
- [[The Poor and the Vulnerable]] — power belongs to the oppressors; poverty itself is not specified.
- [[Compassion and Mercy]] — comfort absent precisely where it is needed.
- [[Mourning and Lament]] — tears and the speaker's severe response, without a funeral rite.
- [[Life and Death]] — the dead preferred to the living, and the never-existent to both.
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — the preceding commendation of enjoyment remains in tension with this verdict.
- [[Judgment]] — 3:17's affirmed judgment is not shown accomplished in 4:1–3.
- [[Retribution]] — suffering is not explained as punishment deserved by these victims.
- [[Sin]] — the evil observed concerns oppression, not a charge against the oppressed.
- [[Work]] — evil work in 4:3 contrasts with the works enjoyed in 3:22.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 4:1–3 unit is retained. The renewed observation in verse 1 leads
to the two comparative judgments in verses 2–3. Verse 4 turns to labor, achievement, and envy;
that next pericope is [[Two Are Better Than One]] (4:4–12).

**Power and comfort.** The repeated lack of a comforter encloses the statement about the
oppressors' power. This is stronger than saying the victims feel lonely. Yet the text does
not identify the institution, the perpetrators, or a failed attempt at intervention. It
supplies no grounds for naming a Pharaoh or placing the scene in Jerusalem. Comfort is
absent; the verse does not say that an explanation of suffering would have supplied it.

**Death and never having been.** Verse 2 is the speaker's evaluation after seeing oppression,
not a narrated command to die. Verse 3 goes further: the one who has not yet been has not
seen evil. It does not expressly teach a conscious existence before birth or the condition
of the dead afterward. Ecclesiastes 6:3–5 explicitly compares a stillborn child with a
long-lived but unsatisfied man; importing that wording into 4:3 would collapse two distinct
comparisons. Job 3:13–19 imagines rest from trouble, including the taskmaster, in death.
The Preacher instead describes victims he sees and does not narrate his own bereavement.

**Tensions retained.** Ecclesiastes 3:12–13 and 3:22 commend enjoyment; 4:2–3 prefers death
and never having been in the face of evil. Later, 9:4 assigns hope to the living. These are
different evaluations with different stated grounds, not a single ranking to be silently
imposed on every situation. Likewise, 3:17 affirms God's judgment, while 4:1 leaves power
with oppressors and the victims without comfort. Exodus's heard cry and promised rescue
provide a contrast, not an event that this pericope itself reports. No guilt of the oppressed
is asserted, and no claim that God never hears is stated.
