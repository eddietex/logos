# Riches Without Enjoyment

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 6:1–9

## Summary

The Preacher sees an evil heavy on people: a man receives riches, wealth, and honor from
God, lacking none of what he desires, yet God gives him no power to eat of it. An alien
enjoys it instead (1–2). The problem is not simply losing wealth or acquiring it wrongly.
The giver of the possessions is named, while the reason for the withheld enjoyment is not.
This reverses the enjoyed gift described immediately before, in 5:19.

A second case pushes outward success to an extreme. A man may father a hundred children
and live many years, yet remain unfilled with good and have no burial (3). The Preacher
ranks a stillborn child above him: it comes in vanity, departs in darkness, has its name
covered with darkness, and has neither seen nor known the sun, yet has more rest (4–5).
Even two thousand years without enjoyment would not solve the problem, since all go to
one place (6). The comparison concerns a life without good; it does not make long life
or many children a sufficient measure of flourishing.

The unit turns from extreme examples to ordinary appetite. Human labor serves the mouth,
yet appetite is not filled (7). The speaker questions the wise person's advantage over
the fool and what the poor person gains from knowing how to conduct himself among the
living (8). He prefers what the eyes see to desire's wandering, but ends again with vanity
and chasing after wind (9). A relative good remains possible without mastery of desire.

## Key verses

All nine verses are copied verbatim from immutable stored WEB Ecclesiastes 6:1–9.

> **1** There is an evil which I have seen under the sun, and it is heavy on men:

> **2** a man to whom God gives riches, wealth, and honor, so that he lacks nothing for his soul of all that he desires, yet God gives him no power to eat of it, but an alien eats it. This is vanity, and it is an evil disease.

> **3** If a man fathers a hundred children, and lives many years, so that the days of his years are many, but his soul is not filled with good, and moreover he has no burial; I say, that a stillborn child is better than he:

> **4** for it comes in vanity, and departs in darkness, and its name is covered with darkness.

> **5** Moreover it has not seen the sun nor known it. This has rest rather than the other.

> **6** Yes, though he live a thousand years twice told, and yet fails to enjoy good, don’t all go to one place?

> **7** All the labor of man is for his mouth, and yet the appetite is not filled.

> **8** For what advantage has the wise more than the fool? What has the poor man, that knows how to walk before the living?

> **9** Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the desire. This also is vanity and a chasing after wind.

## Notes

**Boundary.** Retain the planned 6:1–9 unit. The observed evil of 1–2, the comparison of
3–6, and appetite's limits in 7–9 develop wealth without enjoyment. Verse 9 closes with
the familiar vanity refrain. The questions of 6:10–12 are now treated in [[Who Knows What Is Good]].

**Given possessions, withheld enjoyment.** The WEB expressly attributes both sides of
6:2 to God. Reducing the difficulty to a miser's refusal to enjoy life would omit the
second attribution. Verse 4:8 describes self-deprivation; 6:2 describes no power given.
Neither the man's sin nor the alien's virtue is stated. The alien is not identified as
an invading army, a relative, or a beneficiary favored for righteousness. Ecclesiastes
2:26 distinguishes the sinner from the person pleasing God; this verse does not repeat
that moral classification, so it cannot be supplied as the missing explanation.

**Tension with blessing and enjoyment.** Ecclesiastes 5:19 joins wealth and power to eat
of it as God's gift; 6:2 separates them. These are contrasting cases, not the same man's
story told inconsistently. Yet they challenge any universal claim that wealth God gives
must carry enjoyment. Proverbs 10:22's blessing without added trouble stands beside this
evil without being silently rewritten. The comparison does not prove the wealthy man
wicked, or explain why God withholds power. Both sources remain explicit on the linked
connection and affected theme pages.

**A hundred children and two thousand years.** The numbers intensify the hypothetical
case rather than identify a historical patriarch. Having no burial is a further deprivation;
the text does not say that the hundred children refused to bury their father, or specify
how he died. The stillborn child's relative rest is not a full account of its consciousness,
afterlife, or moral standing. Darkness here is not an accusation of wickedness. The
comparison neither commands death nor states that every life is worse than nonexistence.
The same book commends enjoyment in 3:22 and gives hope to the living in 9:4 (checked in
stored WEB; see [[One Event Comes to All]]). That tension stays visible.

**One place.** Verse 6's shared destination follows the stillborn comparison and the
hypothetical long life. Death is the natural contextual reading, supported by 3:19–20's
shared dust and Job 21:23–26's common end. Verse 6 itself does not name Sheol or describe
what follows death. The fact that the father has no burial also prevents equating a
shared mortal end with identical funeral circumstances.

**Seeing and wandering.** Taking 6:9 as a preference for available good over endless
craving fits 6:7's unfilled appetite. It is an interpretation of the image, not a promise
that sight alone satisfies: 1:8 has already said the eye is not satisfied with seeing.
The closing vanity verdict limits a triumphal reading of the better-than saying; its
precise scope is not explained. Verse 8 similarly asks about advantage rather than
retracting 2:13's explicit preference for wisdom over folly. In the WEB its second question
concerns a poor man who knows how to walk before the living; no biography or specific
profession is supplied.
