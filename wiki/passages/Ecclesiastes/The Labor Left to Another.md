# The Labor Left to Another

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 2:18–26

## Summary

The Preacher moves from hating life (2:17) to hating his labor (18). Everything he has made
must pass to someone else, and he cannot know whether that successor will be wise or foolish
(19). His own wisdom cannot govern what happens after him. He despairs at the thought of a
person laboring with wisdom, knowledge, and skillfulness only to leave the result to someone
who has not worked for it (20–21). He calls this vanity and a great evil; the successor is
not accused of theft, and the worker is not accused of laziness or wrongdoing.

The question about gain returns in verse 22, now with the cost borne by the worker's heart.
His days bring sorrow and grief, and even at night his heart does not rest (23). The problem
is both what happens to the labor afterward and what the labor does to him now.

Verses 24–26 turn toward a good that can be received within this predicament: eating, drinking,
and enjoying good in one's labor. The Preacher attributes this to God's hand (24). In the
stored WEB, verse 25 asks who can eat or have enjoyment more than the speaker himself. God
gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy to the person who pleases him, while the sinner gathers and
heaps up for that person (26). Yet the unit ends with vanity and chasing after wind. The
commendation of enjoyment stands alongside that closing verdict; neither is omitted.

## Key verses

All nine verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 2:18–26.

> **18** I hated all my labor in which I labored under the sun, because I must leave it to the man who comes after me.

> **19** Who knows whether he will be a wise man or a fool? Yet he will have rule over all of my labor in which I have labored, and in which I have shown myself wise under the sun. This also is vanity.

> **20** Therefore I began to cause my heart to despair concerning all the labor in which I had labored under the sun.

> **21** For there is a man whose labor is with wisdom, with knowledge, and with skillfulness; yet he shall leave it for his portion to a man who has not labored for it. This also is vanity and a great evil.

> **22** For what has a man of all his labor, and of the striving of his heart, in which he labors under the sun?

> **23** For all his days are sorrows, and his travail is grief; yes, even in the night his heart takes no rest. This also is vanity.

> **24** There is nothing better for a man than that he should eat and drink, and make his soul enjoy good in his labor. This also I saw, that it is from the hand of God.

> **25** For who can eat, or who can have enjoyment, more than I?

> **26** For to the man who pleases him, God gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy; but to the sinner he gives travail, to gather and to heap up, that he may give to him who pleases God. This also is vanity and a chasing after wind.

## People

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — hates the labor he must leave, describes despair and
  sleepless grief, and recognizes enjoyment as coming from God.

The successor, the skilled worker, the sinner, and the person who pleases God are unnamed
figures or types. No successor is identified with a historical king. God is the giver in
verses 24 and 26; divine action is traced under [[Providence]] and [[Retribution]].

## Places

None named. The repeated phrase *under the sun* describes the inquiry's scope, not a new
geographical location.

## Themes

- [[Work]] — labor's product passes to another; its present cost includes a restless heart.
- [[Wealth and Possessions]] — accumulation does not secure control of the eventual recipient.
- [[Wisdom]] — skill cannot secure the legacy, yet wisdom and knowledge are also God's gifts.
- [[Folly]] — the successor may be a fool; the text leaves that possibility unresolved.
- [[Vanity]] — the verdict recurs at 19, 21, 23, and 26, even after enjoyment is commended.
- [[Suffering]] — despair, sorrow, grief, and a heart without rest at night (20–23).
- [[Joy and Enjoyment]] — eating, drinking, and enjoying labor come from God's hand (24–26).
- [[Providence]] — God gives both enjoyment and the sinner's task of gathering for another.
- [[Retribution]] — the sinner's accumulation benefits the person who pleases God (26).
- [[Life and Death]] — mortality from 2:16 frames the laborer's loss of control over his legacy;
  these verses describe the successor rather than narrating a death.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 2:18–26 unit holds. Hatred turns from life to labor in verse 18;
verse 26 ends with the full vanity verdict before chapter 3's poem about times. Verses 24–26
belong with the preceding complaint because they address good in the very labor under dispute.

**Inheritance as good and evil.** Proverbs 13:22 commends a good man's inheritance reaching his
grandchildren. Ecclesiastes 2:21 calls the skilled worker's transfer to a nonworker a great
evil. These are contrasting evaluations of leaving goods behind, although Ecclesiastes does
not identify the recipient as a grandchild or say that every bequest is wrong. Its point is
that the laborer's competence does not ensure the successor's. The wiki preserves the tension
on the Stored for the Righteous connection instead of making Proverbs' blessing or the
Preacher's despair the only account.

**Two transfers.** At 2:19 the successor's character is unknown. At 2:26 the recipient pleases
God and the gatherer is a sinner. The second case does not identify the despairing speaker of
verses 18–23 as that sinner, or establish that every transfer rewards goodness. Job 21:7–8's
wicked people growing old with their children established remain a counterexample to an
exceptionless reading of the transfer rule. The connection records both claims.

**Enjoyment and vanity.** Verse 24 neither promises that work will outlive its worker nor
retracts verses 18–23. Receiving present enjoyment as a gift rather than securing lasting gain
is a useful reading of the turn, supported by the attribution to God's hand. It remains an
interpretation of the relation between the statements, not an explanation expressly supplied
by the passage. Verse 26 still ends in vanity. The final *this* may focus on the sinner's
fruitless accumulation or summarize the wider situation; the WEB does not explicitly identify
its scope. It should not be made to cancel verse 24 without argument.

**Verse 25 and wording.** This page follows the stored WEB's *“more than I?”* exactly and reads
it as the speaker's appeal to his experience. It does not silently substitute a different
translation or draw a conclusion about Hebrew wording. No source-language claim is made.
