# The Youth and the Old King

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 4:13–16

## Summary

The Preacher gives a better-than saying about rulers: a poor and wise youth is better than an
old and foolish king who no longer knows how to receive admonition (13). Age, office, and wealth
are all on the king's side; wisdom and teachability are on the youth's. The saying ranks the
second pair above the first.

A short story follows (14). Someone came out of prison to be king, and was born poor. The
Preacher then sees all the living who walk under the sun gathered around *"the youth, the
other, who succeeded him"* (15). The people are without number (16).

The last line takes the success away again: *"those who come after shall not rejoice in him."*
The youth who rose from prison draws the crowd and then becomes the next thing a later
generation leaves behind. The Preacher's verdict is the familiar one: *"Surely this also is
vanity and a chasing after wind"* (16).

## Key verses

All four verses are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 4:13–16.

> **13** Better is a poor and wise youth than an old and foolish king who doesn’t know how to receive admonition any more.

> **14** For out of prison he came out to be king; yes, even in his kingdom he was born poor.

> **15** I saw all the living who walk under the sun, that they were with the youth, the other, who succeeded him.

> **16** There was no end of all the people, even of all them over whom he was—yet those who come after shall not rejoice in him. Surely this also is vanity and a chasing after wind.

## People

- [[The Preacher (Ecclesiastes)]] — the observer who gives the saying, tells the story, and
  pronounces the verdict.

The poor youth, the old king, and the successor are unnamed figures in an exemplary story. The
wiki creates no pages for them and does not identify them with any historical king. God is not
mentioned in these four verses.

## Places

None named. The *prison* (14) is part of the story, not an identified location, and *under the
sun* (15) gives the scope of the observation.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The planned 4:13–16 unit is retained. Verse 13 begins a new comparison after
4:4–12's companionship, and verse 16 closes with the vanity refrain. Ecclesiastes 5:1 then
turns to direct commands about going to God's house, which begins the next pericope.

**The saying's terms.** In the Mechon-Mamre Masoretic text (Ecclesiastes 4, read 2026-10-27,
`https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt3104.htm`), verse 13 opens with *ṭôḇ*, *better*, as 4:6 does;
4:9 opens with the plural *ṭôḇîm*. The youth is *yeleḏ
miskēn wəḥāḵām*, a poor and wise child or youth. The king's fault is that he *lōʾ-yāḏaʿ
ləhizzāhēr ʿôḏ*, does not know *to be warned* any longer; the WEB renders it *"receive
admonition"*. The word *ʿôḏ*, *any more*, implies that he could once take warning. The
saying does not condemn old age. It condemns a king who has stopped listening.

**Who came out of prison?** Verse 14 has no named subject. Most readers take it as the youth of
verse 13, which is the reading the summary follows. The second clause, *"even in his kingdom he
was born poor"*, can be read two ways: the youth was born poor in the realm he later ruled, or
he was born poor during the old king's reign. Verse 14 calls him *rāš*, *poor*, a different
word from verse 13's *miskēn*; this is the same Mechon-Mamre text. The WEB's *"prison"* renders
*bêṯ hāsûrîm*, *house of the bound*; in that text the participle is spelled without an aleph.

**One youth or two?** Verse 15's Hebrew reads *hayyeleḏ haššēnî ʾăšer yaʿămōḏ taḥtāyw*, *the
second youth, who will stand in his place* (same Mechon-Mamre text). The WEB's *"the youth,
the other, who succeeded him"* takes him as the youth already introduced, called *second* as
the old king's successor. Others take him as a further youth who replaces the first. In that
reading the cycle runs again, and the former prisoner becomes the old king. Either way verse 16
is the same: the crowd that gathers will not last. The wiki records both readings and does not
resolve the grammar.

**Before them.** The WEB's *"all them over whom he was"* (16) renders *ləḵōl ʾăšer-hāyâ
lip̄nêhem*, literally *all who were before them* (same Mechon-Mamre text). *Before* can mean
*in front of*, a ruler leading the people, which is how the WEB takes it. It can also mean
*earlier*, the generations who came first. In the second reading verse 16 runs from the
earlier generations to *those who come after*, as 1:11 does. The wiki keeps the WEB's
reading in the summary and records the other here.

**Historical identifications.** Readers have connected the story to Joseph, David and Saul, or
later kings of Israel and the nations. The text names no one. It also calls the youth a king,
which Joseph never was: Pharaoh keeps the throne (Genesis 41:40). See
[[Out of Prison to Reign - Joseph and Ecclesiastes 4.13-14]]. The wiki keeps the story
exemplary and adds no identification. The Preacher's own royal role (1:12) does not make this
story autobiography either.

**Wisdom ranked, then relativized.** Verse 13 says wisdom with poverty is better than folly with
a crown, and the passage never withdraws that. The vanity verdict in verse 16 is about what
follows. Popular favor, even when the wiser candidate wins it, does not last beyond its
generation. This is the pattern of 2:13–16: wisdom excels folly, and it still cannot secure
memory. Read beside 4:9–12, where two are better than one, this unit shows the limit of a
crowd. *"No end of all the people"* still does not secure the rejoicing of those who come
after.
