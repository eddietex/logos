# Vanity of Vanities

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 1:1–11

## Summary

The book introduces the Preacher as the son of David, king in Jerusalem, and immediately places
his verdict of vanity beside a question: what does a person gain from all the work done under
the sun? The opening does not tell a royal success story. It sets the most elevated human
position against the same limits faced by every generation.

Generations come and go while the earth remains. The sun rises and sets, the wind turns and
returns, and rivers keep flowing into a sea that is never full. Continual movement does not
produce a final arrival. The poem then turns to its observers: things are weary beyond telling,
the eye is not satisfied, and the ear is not filled. The inability to finish extends to speech
and perception as well as labor.

The closing lines challenge claims of novelty and the security of human memory. What seems new
has predecessors beyond the observer's recollection; later generations will also be forgotten.
These observations give the opening question its force. Human activity continues, but its
capacity to secure lasting gain or remembrance is under examination.

## Key verses

All eleven verses below are copied verbatim from stored WEB Ecclesiastes 1:1–11.

> **1** The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem:

> **2** “Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher; “Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.”

> **3** What does man gain from all his labor in which he labors under the sun?

> **4** One generation goes, and another generation comes; but the earth remains forever.

> **5** The sun also rises, and the sun goes down, and hurries to its place where it rises.

> **6** The wind goes toward the south, and turns around to the north. It turns around continually as it goes, and the wind returns again to its courses.

> **7** All the rivers run into the sea, yet the sea is not full. To the place where the rivers flow, there they flow again.

> **8** All things are full of weariness beyond uttering. The eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing.

> **9** That which has been is that which shall be; and that which has been done is that which shall be done: and there is no new thing under the sun.

> **10** Is there a thing of which it may be said, “Behold, this is new?” It has been long ago, in the ages which were before us.

> **11** There is no memory of the former; neither shall there be any memory of the latter that are to come, among those that shall come after.

## Notes

**Boundary.** The heading and opening poem form 1:1–11. Verse 12 starts the first-person
royal inquiry, ingested as [[The Burden of Wisdom]].

**Attribution.** The heading's royal description resembles Proverbs 1:1 but does not name
Solomon. The person page records the Preacher as a textual speaker, not as proof of a second
historical king or a decision against traditional Solomonic attribution.

**Vanity is not simply self-admiration.** In the WEB passage, the word is explained by the
question about labor's gain, recurrence, unsatisfied senses, and forgotten generations. This
page treats futility and transience as contextual readings, not as an exhaustive lexical
translation or a claim that every action is morally worthless.

**Under the sun.** The phrase marks the sphere of the poem's human observations. Nothing in
1:1–11 defines it as a technical expression meaning life without faith. God is not named in
this unit; that absence must not be turned into a declaration that the entire book excludes God.

**Tensions, not corrections.** Proverbs 14:23 affirms profit in hard work, and Proverbs 31:31
requires recognition of its fruit. Ecclesiastes 1:3 questions the gain from all labor. The
contrast may involve practical benefit versus enduring advantage, but that is an interpretive
distinction, not an explanation supplied by either verse. Likewise, Proverbs 10:7's blessed
memory and Ecclesiastes 1:11's forgetting must both remain visible.

**Nature and novelty.** Genesis calls creation good and promises continuing seasons; here its
recurrence is set against human weariness. These are different evaluations of human experience,
not an assertion that Genesis has been revoked. The poem gives no theory of the sun's physical
motion, no full water-cycle account, and no list of inventions; those are not what its
observations demonstrate. Its denial of novelty should retain its force without being recast
as a scientific inventory.
