# The House of Mourning

**Book:** [[Ecclesiastes]]
**Reference:** Ecclesiastes 7:1–14

## Summary

After the unanswered question of 6:12 — who knows what is good for man in life? — the
Preacher answers with a run of *better than* sayings. A good name is better than fine
perfume, and the day of death better than the day of birth (1). The house of mourning is
better than the house of feasting, because death is the end of all, and the living should
take it to heart (2). Sorrow is better than laughter, for the sad face makes the heart good
(3); the wise heart is in the house of mourning, the fool's in the house of mirth (4).

The comparisons turn to speech and character. The rebuke of the wise is better than the
song of fools, whose laughter is the crackling of thorns under a pot — *"This also is
vanity"* (5–6). Extortion makes the wise man foolish, and a bribe destroys understanding
(7): wisdom itself can be corrupted. The end of a thing is better than its beginning, the
patient in spirit better than the proud (8). Anger is not to be hasty, because it rests in
the bosom of fools (9), and nostalgia for better former days is not a wise question (10).

Wisdom is as good as an inheritance and a defense, as money is a defense; its excellence is
that it preserves the life of the one who has it (11–12). The unit closes on God: consider
his work, for no one can straighten what he has made crooked (13). In prosperity be joyful;
in adversity consider. God has made the one beside the other, so that man should not find
out anything after him (14). The closing purpose clause returns to the limit stated in
6:12 about what comes after a person.

## Key verses

All quotations are copied verbatim from immutable stored WEB Ecclesiastes 7.

> **1** A good name is better than fine perfume; and the day of death better than the day of one’s birth.

> **2** It is better to go to the house of mourning than to go to the house of feasting: for that is the end of all men, and the living should take this to heart.

> **3** Sorrow is better than laughter; for by the sadness of the face the heart is made good.

> **7** Surely extortion makes the wise man foolish; and a bribe destroys the understanding.

> **10** Don’t say, “Why were the former days better than these?” For you do not ask wisely about this.

> **12** For wisdom is a defense, even as money is a defense; but the excellency of knowledge is that wisdom preserves the life of him who has it.

> **13** Consider the work of God, for who can make that straight, which he has made crooked?

> **14** In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity consider; yes, God has made the one side by side with the other, to the end that man should not find out anything after him.

## Notes

**Boundary.** Retain the planned 7:1–14. The sequence of *better* sayings opens at 7:1 and
the unit closes on God's work and the purpose clause of 7:14. At 7:15 the Preacher returns
to first-person observation (*"All this I have seen in my days of vanity"*) with a new
subject, the righteous man who perishes, which begins the next pericope.

**An answer of a kind to 6:12.** The juxtaposition invites reading 7:1–14 as a response to
*who knows what is good?* — it is a sequence of judgments about what is *better*. The text
does not say it is answering 6:12; the link is a reading of the sequence. It does not
claim to know what comes *after* a person, and 7:14 restates that limit.

**Mourning beside enjoyment — a tension, not a reversal.** Ecclesiastes has commended
eating, drinking, and enjoying labor as God's gift (2:24; 3:12–13; 5:18), and 7:14 itself
commands joy in prosperity. Yet 7:2–4 prefers the house of mourning and sorrow to
laughter. Verse 2 gives the reason: death is the end of all, and the living should take
it to heart. One reading holds that the house of mourning teaches the mortality that
makes present enjoyment a gift rather than a possession. That is an interpretation; the
text sets the sayings side by side without reconciling them, and the wiki keeps both.
The laughter faulted in 7:6 is specifically the fool's.

**The day of death better than birth.** Verse 1 makes a comparison, not a wish for death
or a command to seek it. It stands beside 4:2–3's praise of the dead and 6:3–5's stillborn
child, both responses to observed evil, while 7:12 counts the preservation of life as
wisdom's excellence. No explanation of why the day of death is better is supplied beyond
the reason in verse 2.

**Wisdom's worth and its limits.** Verse 7 concedes that extortion and a bribe can
corrupt the wise; verse 12 claims that wisdom preserves the life of its possessor. Against
2:14–16, where the wise man dies like the fool, 7:12 is a qualified claim of advantage, not
an exemption from death. The text does not say how wisdom preserves life or for how long;
the wiki does not supply a mechanism.

**God made the crooked.** 1:15 states that the crooked cannot be made straight without
naming who made it. 7:13 attributes the crooked to God's making, and 7:14 attributes both
prosperity and adversity to him. Neither verse calls the crooked evil, gives a reason for
any particular adversity, or explains the moral status of what God made crooked. The
purpose clause — so that man should not find out anything after him — is the only purpose
given. No claim is made here about the Hebrew vocabulary of *crooked*, *adversity*, or
*evil*; the comparison with Job 2:10 is a comparison in English (WEB), not an assertion
that the same Hebrew word stands in both verses.

**Former days.** Verse 10 forbids asking why the former days were better, not as a false
claim, but as an unwise question. It fits 1:9–11's account of forgotten predecessors and
recurring things; the verse itself does not say the former days were not better.

**Anger and patience.** Verse 8 commends the *patient in spirit*, not the phrase *slow to
anger* used in the WEB of Proverbs 14:29 and Exodus 34:6, so the comparison on
[[Slow to Anger - Proverbs 14.29 and Exodus 34.6]] is thematic, not verbal.
